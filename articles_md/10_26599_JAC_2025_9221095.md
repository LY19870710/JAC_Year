## 1 Introduction

Cellular ceramic structures (CCSs) fabricated by three-dimensional (3D) printing technologies are promising candidates for structural components, as they are lightweight and possess exceptional strength simultaneously [1]. Although notable achievements have been made in CCSs, achieving superior strength and damage resistance simultaneously remains a challenge for CCSs [2−4]. This is because the load-bearing capacity and damage tolerance of ceramics are mutually exclusive. Once overloaded, CCSs fail to dissipate excess energy and abruptly lose load-bearing capacity without warning. Such catastrophic failure severely restricts the application of CCSs. Therefore, enhancing the energy-absorbing ability of CCSs is the top priority to make them compelling candidates for structural components.

Second-phase toughening and structural optimization are common approaches to toughen CCSs [5−7]. However, improvements in the toughness of CCSs are limited. Their energy-absorbing ability is overshadowed by cellular metal structures (CMSs) and cellular polymer structures (CPSs). Hence, discovering a creative way to enhance the energy-absorbing ability without damaging the load-bearing capacity is urgent and critical for CCSs. Natural materials, such as the pearlite layer in red abalone shells, conch shells, and the mantis shrimp cheliped, simultaneously possess remarkable strength and toughness, protecting them from attackers and harsh environments. The superior mechanical properties of natural materials originate from their ingenious structures [8−10]. For example, the pearlite layer in red abalone shells is composed of stiff CaCO3 platelets and tough chitin in a dual-phase interpenetrating architecture (Fig. 1(a)). When subjected to loading, tough chitin distributes locally concentrated stress throughout the pearlite through the interpenetrating network to reduce damage caused by stress concentration. Moreover, well-connected interfaces between stiff CaCO3 platelets and tough chitin tend to separate and move, consuming considerable energy. Furthermore, the tough chitin network constrains the position adjustment of CaCO3 platelets, allowing connected CaCO3 platelets to withstand increased loading [11]. The same dual-phase interpenetrating architecture can be observed in conch shells and mantis shrimp chelipeds, allowing natural materials to withstand multiple attacks [12,13].

The ingenious dual-phase interpenetrating architecture of natural materials provides an elegant and creative way to mitigate the adverse effects of the brittleness of CCSs. Munch et al.* [14] mimicked the dual-phase interpenetrating architecture to arrange Al2O3 and polymethyl methacrylate in such a structure, aiming to optimize the mechanical properties of Al2O3. The Al2O3/polymethyl methacrylate composite exhibited a higher yield strength (~200 MPa) and fracture toughness (~30 MPa·m1/2) than its constituent materials. Li *et al.* [15] also fabricated an Al2O3/epoxy composite with a dual-phase interpenetrating architecture. The damage resistance of the Al2O3/epoxy composite was even comparable to that of the aluminum alloy. Numerous investigations have demonstrated that the use of a dual-phase interpenetrating architecture is a promising approach to enhance the damage tolerance of CCSs under quasi-static loading. However, investigations of CCSs-based dual-phase interpenetrating composites under dynamic loading have rarely been conducted. In fact, structural components are subjected to not only quasi-static loading but also dynamic impact in application. It is significant to elucidate the mechanical properties of CCSs-based dual-phase interpenetrating composites subjected to both quasi-static and dynamic loading.

Furthermore, structural parameters play determinant roles in the mechanical properties of dual-phase interpenetrating composites. Their mechanical properties can be tuned by tailoring their structural parameters. For example, the impact toughness values of dual-phase interpenetrating Ti6Al4V/Mg composites with brick-and-mortar, Bouligand, and crossed-lamellar structures were 8.9, 11.3, and 13.0 kJ/m2, respectively [10]. Jia and Wang [16] revealed how the structural configuration altered the crack propagation paths of dual-phase interpenetrating verowhite/tangoplus composites. However, how structural parameters affect the mechanical properties of CCSs-based dual-phase interpenetrating composites remains unclear.

Inspired by natural materials, unique polyurea-toughened Al2O3 CCSs (P/CCSs), whose constituents are arranged in a dual-phase interpenetrating architecture, were fabricated via digital light processing (DLP) technology and infiltration, aiming to achieve superior load-bearing capacity and energy absorption capacity simultaneously (Figs. 1(b)–1(d)). The performances of P/CCSs under both quasi-static and dynamic compressions were investigated. The effects of the structural configuration and relative density on the mechanical properties of P/CCSs were also explored. This work demonstrates that the bioinspired dual-phase interpenetrating architecture effectively mitigates the adverse effects of the brittleness and poor energy absorption of CCSs, thereby improving their application prospects.

### 2.1 3D printing

The pristine Al2O3 CCSs in P/CCSs were prepared via DLP technology. First, 3D models of these CCSs were created. As shown in Figs. 1(b)–1(d), body-centered cubic (BCC), octahedral truss (Octet), and modified body-centered cubic (MBCC) structures were used in this work to investigate the effects of structural configuration on the mechanical properties of P/CCSs. Octet and BCC structures are representative stretching-dominated and bending-dominated structures, whose struts undergo stretching and bending states under compression, respectively. The MBCC is designed based on the BCC structure, aiming to alleviate the rotational tendency and enhance the stability of joints in the BCC [17,18]. Pristine Al2O3 CCSs with three relative densities (20%, 30%, and 40%) were designed to investigate how the relative density influences the performance of P/CCSs under quasi-static and dynamic compression. Unit cells with dimensions of 6 mm × 6 mm × 6 mm were arranged in a 5×5×5 array. Then, files of models were imported into 10dim software (Beijing 10 Dimensions Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China) to slice them with a thickness of 100 μm. The files of sliced models were subsequently imported into a DLP-based 3D printer (Beijing 10 Dimensions Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China) for printing.

The Al2O3 stereolithographic slurry for DLP was composed of Al2O3, MgO, and TiO2 powders, 1,6-hexanediol diacrylate, 1,1,1-trimethylol propane triacrylate, diphenyl(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl)phosphine oxide, and KOS110. Al2O3 powders (Shanghai Zhongzhou Special Alloy Materials Co., Ltd., China) were the most important constituents for Al2O3 CCSs. MgO powders (Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) and TiO2 powders (Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) acted as sintering aids to lower the sintering temperature and facilitate densification of CCSs. 1,6-Hexanediol diacrylate (Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) and 1,1,1-trimethylol propane triacrylate (Changshu Hengrong Trading Co., Ltd., China) were photosensitive monomers for 3D printing. Diphenyl(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl)phosphine oxide (Guangzhou Lihou Trading Co., Ltd., China) served as the photoinitiator to trigger the polymerization of monomers. KOS110 (Guangzhou Kangoushuang Trade Co., Ltd., China) was used as a dispersant to ensure uniform dispersion of Al2O3 powder in the slurry. The proportions of each component are listed in previous work [17]. The weighed constituents were then mixed by ball mixing with zirconia balls. The Al2O3 stereolithographic slurry for printing was ready after ball milling at 400 r/min for 20 h.

Afterwards, the Al2O3 stereolithographic slurry was poured into the vat of the 3D printer. The exposure intensity and time were 12,000 μW/cm2 and 4 s, respectively. After the printer started, green bodies were gradually formed at the bottom of the platform layer by layer. Once the entire process was completed, the green bodies were removed from the workbench and cleaned thoroughly with ethanol. Subsequently, green bodies were placed in a muffle furnace (Facerom Thermal Equipment Co., Ltd., China) at 550 °C for 2 h (heating rate: 0.5 °C/min) to remove the cured resins. The sintering process was also executed in a muffle furnace at 1650 °C for 3 h (heating rate: 5 °C/min) to densify the loose ceramic powders. Pristine CCSs were obtained after sintering.

### 2.2 Infiltration

The target P/CCSs were fabricated by infiltrating polyurea into Al2O3 CCSs. Polyurea was synthesized through the reaction of a modified isocyanate prepolymer (ingredient A, Qingdao Air++ New Materials Co., Ltd., China) and 3,3’-dichloro-4,4’-diaminodiphenylmethane (ingredient B, pale yellow powder, Qingdao Air++ New Materials Co., Ltd., China). The mass ratio of ingredients A to B was set at 10 : 1.5. In the preparation process, ingredient B was first transferred to a beaker and heated at 120 °C until completely melted. The molten ingredient B was then poured into liquid ingredient A and stirred thoroughly. The Al2O3 CCSs were immersed in the resulting mixture to ensure full infiltration. After complete polymerization of ingredients A and B, the P/CCSs were sequentially cured in an oven at 80 °C for 72 h and aged under standard conditions for an additional 72 h to optimize the properties of polyurea. Finally, excess polyurea on the sample periphery was removed by grinding, ensuring the dimensional consistency of P/CCSs and CCSs as much as possible.

### 2.3 Characterization

The mechanical properties of P/CCSs under quasi-static compressive loading were tested using an LD23 universal testing machine (Shenzhen Labsans Testing Machine Co., Ltd., China) at a loading speed of 1 mm/min. The force and displacement recorded by the sensors were used to calculate the engineering stress and engineering strain of P/CCSs during quasi-static compression. Moreover, a camera recorded the whole compressive process to analyze the failure modes of P/CCSs. A split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB) system was used to assess the dynamic mechanical properties of P/CCSs. The impact speed was 12±0.7 m/s. A high-speed camera was employed to record the deformation process of P/CCSs under dynamic compression. The images were acquired at a rate of 50,000 frames per second. Detailed information about the dynamic mechanical tests was presented in our previous work [18].

#### 3.2.1 Mechanical property definitions

The response of P/CCSs under quasi-static compressive loading, as shown in Fig. 2, was extremely different from that of pristine Al2O3 CCSs, with the former being much closer to the response of CPSs and CMSs [18]. Hence, we need to define the mechanical property parameters of P/CCSs before analyzing by referencing those of CPSs and CMSs. Here, we take the quasi-static compressive stress–strain curve of P/MBCC20 as an example. Based on the variation trend of the stress–strain curve of P/MBCC20, as shown in Fig. 2(a), the compressive process is divided into three successive stages, i.e., linear elastic stage, plateau stage, and densification stage, which are marked with yellow, blue, and green areas, respectively.

*
Fig. 2Stress–strain curves and energy absorption–strain and energy absorption efficiency–strain curves of (a) P/MBCC20 and (b) P/BCC20 under quasi-static compression. Yellow, blue, and green areas represent the linear elastic stage, plateau stage, and densification stage, respectively. Star and ε*1 represent the strain at the maximum energy-absorbing efficiency.

Linear elastic stage refers to the stage from the start of the test to the strain corresponding to the first maximal stress. At the initial stage of quasi-static compression, the stress P/MBCC20 rapidly climbed in an almost linear manner, similar to the behavior of elastic deformation in components. Hence, this stage is referred to as the linear elastic stage. Notably, the elastic deformation of P/CCSs mentioned here is significantly different from the conventional elastic deformation. It is well-known that stress has linear dependence on strain within the elastic range. After the applied load is removed, the materials return to their initial states if deformation is within the elastic range. In other words, linear deformation and recoverability are common characteristics of the elastic range. However, a different phenomenon occurred in P/CCSs. Although the stress experienced by P/CCSs increased linearly with strain, pristine CCSs in P/CCSs were gradually damaged before the stress reached the first maximum. Damaged CCSs cannot return to their original states. Additionally, the maximum stress in the linear elastic stage is defined as the quasi-static compressive strength (QS).

As the stress reached the first peak, compression stepped into the plateau stage. There were two different variation trends of stress during the plateau stage. For the stress–strain curve of P/MBCC20, the force applied to it decreased dramatically at the start of the plateau stage and then rose smoothly, which is a typical characteristic of a strain-softening component (Fig. 2(a)). The second one was the strain-hardening component, such as P/BCC20. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the stress that P/BCC20 experienced continuously increased without an obvious decline at the initial plateau stage. In addition, the plateau stress is introduced to represent the average strength of P/CCSs in the plateau stage, which is calculated by dividing the energy absorbed by the strain in the plateau stage [19,20]. The quasistatic energy absorption (QE) of P/CCSs represents the energy that P/CCSs absorb in the linear elastic stage and plateau stage.

As the tests proceeded, the compression entered the densification stage. The quasi-static stress–strain curve of P/CCSs rose at an accelerating rate, as broken struts were forced to connect with each other, providing a continuously increasing load-bearing capacity. The plateau stage and densification stage are separated by the strain corresponding to the maximum energy absorbing efficiency, which is denoted as *ε*1 as shown in Fig. 2 [21]. The energy absorbing efficiency, *μ*, represents the quotient of the energy absorbed per unit volume at a given strain and the energy absorbed per unit volume by an ideal energy absorber up to the same strain, which is calculated by Eq. (1) [22,23]:

(1) μ=QE(ϵ)σmax(ε)

where *ε* is the strain; *σ*max(*ε*) is the maximum stress until a given *ε*; QE(*ε*) is the energy that P/CCSs absorb during compression, which is calculated by Eq. (2):

(2) QE=∫0ϵσ(ϵ)dϵ

Figures 2(a) and 2(b) also display the curves of stress, energy absorption, and *μ* versus strain for P/MBCC20 and P/BCC20, respectively. The stress of strain-softening P/CCSs, such as P/MBCC20, at *ε*1 was the same as that at QS, which can be easily deduced from Eq. (1). However, the stress of strain-hardening P/BCC20 at *ε*1 was always greater than that at QS.

#### 3.2.2 Mechanical properties of P/CCSs

(1) Quasi-static compressive strength

Figure 3(a) displays representative stress–strain curves of P/BCC20, P/Octet20, and P/MBCC20 under quasi-static compression. Each quasistatic stress–strain curve could be clearly divided into a linear elastic stage, a plateau stage, and a densification stage, rather than just a linear elastic stage and an abrupt decline like that of pristine CCSs. This finding indicates that P/CCSs no longer fracture catastrophically. Furthermore, the stress–strain curve of P/MBCC20 was clearly greater than those of the other samples, whereas that of P/BCC20 was the lowest. This aligned with the observation that the QSs of P/MBCC20 and P/BCC20 were the highest (20.29±1.04 MPa) and the lowest (2.09±0.10 MPa), respectively, among the three samples (Fig. 4). The QS of P/Octet20 (6.69±1.15 MPa) fell midway, which is consistent with previous research [17]. Figure 3(b) displays stress–strain curves of P/CCSs with a 30% relative density of pristine CCSs. The QS of P/MBCC30 remained the highest. The QS of P/Octet30 exceeded that of P/BCC30 but remained lower than that of P/MBCC30. The same trend was observed in P/CCSs with a 40% relative density of pristine CCSs (Fig. 3(c)). This indicates that structural configurations have a significant effect on the QS of P/CCSs. Octet structure, a representative stretching-dominated structure, is capable of withstanding higher compressive loads than bending-dominated structures, such as the BCC structure. The MBCC structure is also a bending-dominated architecture characterized by joint connectivity, but a higher diameter-to-length ratio of struts enables MBCCs to rank among the top three in QS [17]. Furthermore, although the CCSs were fully infiltrated with polyurea, the QS of P/CCSs was governed by the structural configuration because the strength of polyurea before fracture (3.97±0.20 MPa) was much lower than that of Al2O3 (251.18±23.74 MPa). Consequently, MBCC P/CCSs exhibited the highest QS, BCC P/CCSs were the weakest, and Octet P/CCSs fell in the middle.

In addition to the structural configuration, the relative density of pristine CCSs also plays a key role in the QS of P/CCSs. As shown in Figs. 3(d)–3(f) and 4, the QS of P/CCSs improved with increasing relative density, which was not altered by structural configurations. It has been extensively proven that an increase in relative density has a positive effect on the load-bearing capacity of cellular structures, regardless of whether they are composed of ceramics, metals, or polymers [4,17,18]. The higher the relative density is, the closer the QS of CCSs is to that of dense bulk materials. Although the CCSs were fully filled with polyurea, the relationship between the relative density and QS of P/CCSs remained unchanged. Notably, polyurea enhanced the QS of CCSs, enabling P/CCSs to exhibit improved load-bearing capacity (Table 2). This occurred because polyurea filled the spaces between interconnected struts, thereby restricting the movement of broken struts. Broken struts were forced to connect with their neighboring struts and thus continued to provide support. Thus, the QS of P/CCSs was 1.22–3.64 times that of CCSs.

In addition, an obvious peak was observed in the stress–strain curves of the BCC P/CCS samples, which was attributed to the larger diameter of the struts in the pristine Al2O3 CCSs. The struts of BCCs were larger in diameter than those of Octets and MBCCs. For example, the strut diameters of BCC20, Octet20, and MBCC20 were 1.01, 0.64, and 0.86 mm, respectively. As compression continued, the broken struts rotated. Their contact areas grew steadily, increasing the load-bearing capacity of BCC P/CCSs accordingly. Owing to the thinner struts, the stress enhancement from the contact area of fractured struts in Octet P/CCSs and MBCC P/CCSs was relatively small compared with the maximum stress value in the linear elastic stage. Hence, the the stress–strain curves of the Octet P/CCS and MBCC P/CCS samples exhibited a smooth, steady increases in the plateau stage.

(2) Quasi-static energy absorption

Polyurea not only increased the QS of P/CCSs but also increased their QE. It was concluded from the comparison, as listed in Table 2, that P/CCSs exhibited more remarkable energy absorption capacities than pristine CCSs. The energy consumed by P/CCSs in the quasi-static compressive process was more than two orders of magnitude greater than that consumed by CCSs. Although pristine CCSs still cracked under loading, polyurea prevented the disconnection of broken struts, allowing them to provide support even when the strain exceeded 0.6. The superior load-bearing capacity and large deformation of P/CCSs were comparable to those of tough CMSs and CPSs.

In addition, there was a significant difference in the QE of P/CCSs with different structural parameters. For example, the QEs of P/MBCC20, P/MBCC30, and P/MBCC40 were 6.15±0.21, 7.40±1.02, and 15.60±2.86 MJ/m3, respectively (Fig. 4). That is, the QE of MBCC P/CCSs increased with increasing relative density of pristine CCSs, which was ascribed to the ε*1 and plateau stress. Notably, although the plateau stress represents only the average stress in the plateau stage, the energy P/CCSs dissipated in the plateau stage because of the crushing of fractured CCSs, and the compression of polyurea was much greater than that consumed in the linear elastic stage because of the cracking of pristine CCSs. Thus, the plateau stress in the plateau stage can represent the average load-bearing capacity of P/CCSs to some extent. As shown in Fig. 5, both the plateau stress and *ε*1 of MBCC P/CCSs increased with increasing relative density of pristine CCSs, contributing to the improved QE. Although the *ε*1 of Octet P/CCSs slightly decreased with increasing relative density of pristine CCSs, the improved plateau stress played a dominant role. Hence, the QE of Octet P/CCSs also improved with increasing relative density. However, the QE of BCC P/CCSs did not show a positive correlation with the relative density of pristine CCSs. The energy-absorbing ability of P/BCC30 was the weakest, dissipating only 4.37±0.46 MJ/m3. The QEs of P/BCC20 and P/BCC40 were 5.19±0.64 and 5.40±1.46 MJ/m3, respectively. This occurred because dramatically decreased *ε*1 failed to compensate for the weak plateau stress of P/BCC30.

The structural configuration has been proven to be another key to determining the QE of P/CCSs. There was no doubt that the QE of the MBCC P/CCSs was the highest among the three P/CCSs because both ε*1 and the plateau stress of the MBCC P/CCSs were greater than those of Octet P/CCSs and BCC P/CCSs. The QE of Octet P/CCSs was lower than that of BCC P/CCSs, which was contrary to the trend observed in QS. As depicted in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b), the *ε*1 values of P/BCC20, P/BCC30, and P/BCC40 were 0.54±0.01, 0.48±0.05, and 0.44±0.05, respectively, higher than those of P/Octet20 (0.45±0.04), P/Octet30 (0.43±0.08), and P/Octet40 (0.41±0.03), respectively. Similarly, the plateau stresses of P/BCC20, P/BCC30, and P/BCC40 were 10.00±1.30, 10.13±0.82, and 12.83±3.20 MPa, respectively, which were also higher than those of Octet P/CCSs. Owing to the combined effect of *ε*1 and the plateau stress, BCC P/CCSs exhibited a superior QE compared with that of Octet P/CCSs.

In brief, by incorporating polyurea, P/CCSs overcome the brittleness and limited deformation of CCSs and exhibit outstanding load-bearing capacity and excellent energy-absorbing ability, making them promising candidates for components requiring both strength and energy absorption simultaneously.

#### 3.2.3 Failure mode of P/CCSs

It has been proved that the mechanical properties of P/CCSs under quasi-static compressive loading are completely different from those of CCSs. Their failure modes, naturally, are also distinctive from those of CCSs. Hence, it is important to clarify the failure modes of P/CCSs under quasi-static compressive loading.

Representative states and deformation diagrams of P/BCC20, P/Octet20, and P/MBCC20 under quasi-static compression are displayed in Figs. 6 and 7, respectively. Although P/CCSs seemed to be unchanged during the linear elastic stage, a small number of joints in pristine CCSs cracked before the stress reached its maximum, which was confirmed by X-ray computed topography results [22]. However, polyurea limited the movement of broken parts, and the space between them was too narrow to be noticed [18]. Therefore, P/CCSs remained intact at the macro level, rather than disintegrating like CCSs.

When the deformation increased to 2 mm, compression occurred in the early plateau stage. Owing to insufficient deformation, cracks were still difficult to observe. As the deformation increased to 5 mm, adjacent broken struts in P/CCSs were forced to split apart, and cracks could be easily observed. Notably, fracture always occurs around joints of pristine CCSs inside P/CCSs, as joints are sites where stress concentrations occur due to their sharp geometry. Additionally, inevitable defects introduced at the joints of pristine CCSs during the printing process weakened the strength around these regions. Furthermore, the cracking modes of pristine CCSs within P/CCSs differed significantly. As shown in Fig. 7(a), pristine BCC20 in P/BCC20 cracked at joints in an intersecting pattern. The joints of Octet20 inside P/Octet20 were too strong to be destroyed, so the struts fractured around the joints, as shown in Fig. 7(b). The cracking mode of MBCC20 in P/MBCC20 differed from that of P/BCC20 and P/Octet20. As revealed in Fig. 7(c), pristine MBCC20 inside P/MBCC20 cracked in a lateral-vertical-inclined mixed mode [18]. Furthermore, fractured joints are always concentrated on specific planes, resulting in localized deformation. After the first fracture, adjacent joints were forced to bear more load due to stress redistribution, causing them to break earlier than others. Thus, broken joints tended to concentrate on a specific plane, which was called shear bands, a behavior analogous to the responses of cellular structures under compressive loading [17,22−24].

Under subsequent compression, the broken struts embedded in polyurea were forced to move farther apart in the lateral direction because P/CCSs expanded laterally under compression, as shown in Figs. 7(a)(v), 7(b)(v), and 7(c)(v). However, the broken struts were compressed more tightly in the vertical direction. That is why P/CCSs can withstand increasingly high loads in the densification stage. As P/CCSs were compressed increasingly tightly, localized deformation gradually disappeared. Consequently, P/CCSs tended to deform as a whole.

The failure modes of P/CCSs with relative densities of 30% and 40% were the same as those of P/CCSs with a relative density of 20%. No obvious cracks appeared in the linear elastic stage. Shear bands were generated and propagated before P/CCSs were fully compressed. Moreover, the orientations of shear bands in BCC P/CCSs and Octet P/CCSs were 45°. However, fractured parts in MBCC P/CCSs slipped along a 63.43° plane. In other words, the orientation of shear bands in P/CCSs was dependent on the inclination angle of struts.

Investigations into the mechanical properties and failure modes of P/CCSs have proved that polyurea tremendously improves the performance of CCSs, endowing them with excellent load-bearing capacity and energy-absorbing ability under quasi-static compressive loading.

#### 3.3.1 Mechanical properties of P/CCSs

P/CCSs not only undergo quasi-static compression but also withstand impacts for structural components. Hence, it is crucial to consider their performance under dynamic loading. We examined the dynamic mechanical properties of P/CCSs at a loading rate of 12±0.7 m/s. P/BCC40, P/Octet40, and P/MBCC40 were used to analyze how the structural configuration of pristine CCSs affected the dynamic mechanical properties of P/CCSs. The effect of the relative density of pristine CCSs on the performance of P/CCSs under dynamic compressive loading was discussed based on the test results of P/MBCC20, P/MBCC30, and P/MBCC40. In this work, the dynamic compressive strength (DS) of P/CCSs is defined as the highest value of stress in the dynamic compressive stress–strain curve. The energy-absorbing ability of P/CCSs under dynamic compression (DE) is defined as the area enclosed by the dynamic compressive stress–strain curve and the *x*-axis before the stress first drops to zero.

Figure 8(a) displays the dynamic compressive stress–strain curves of P/BCC40, P/Octet40, and P/MBCC40. The dynamic compressive stress–strain curves of P/MBCC40 were located above those of P/Octet40 and P/BCC40. The DS and DE of P/MBCC40 were 125.70±11.25 MPa and 5.10±0.39 MJ/m3, respectively, which were the highest among those of P/CCSs (Fig. 8(b)). The dynamic compressive stress–strain curve of P/Octet40 is in the middle, with a DS of 41.05±1.12 MPa and a DE of 2.02±0.17 MJ/m3. The dynamic mechanical properties of P/BCC40 were the weakest among the three P/CCSs. The DS and DE of P/BCC40 were 21.68±0.56 MPa and 1.75±0.10 MJ/m3, respectively. Figures 8(c) and 8(d) display the dynamic mechanical properties of P/MBCC20, P/MBCC30, and P/MBCC40. The dynamic mechanical properties of MBCC P/CCSs clearly increased with increasing relative density, which is consistent with the trend observed under quasi-static compressive loading.

#### 3.3.2 Failure mode of P/CCSs

As the compression progressed, P/BCC40 expanded in the direction perpendicular to the loading direction. Broken struts embedded in polyurea moved with the deformed polyurea and gradually separated from each other. The cracks in P/BCC40 were clearly distinguishable. Movie S2 in the ESM and Fig. 9(b) reveal the deformation process of P/Octet40 at a loading rate of 12±0.7 m/s. Both P/BCC40 and P/Octet40 were compressed as a whole. However, there were differences in the crack modes between P/Octet40 and P/BCC40. As shown in Figs. 9(b) and 10(b), the struts of pristine Octet40 inside P/Octet40 cracked around joints rather than fracturing at joints in the form of intersections, similar to those of P/BCC40. The difference in their crack modes was ascribed to the structural configuration of pristine CCSs. Octet and BCC are representative stretching-dominated and bending-dominated structures, respectively. Their connectivities are 12 and 8, meaning that 12 and 8 struts meet at each joint in the Octet and BCC structures, respectively. Higher connectivity strengthens joints. Thus, the struts of P/Octet40 cracked around the joints rather than at the joints. Pristine MBCC40 within P/MBCC40 also cracked at joints in the form of an intersection (Fig. 9(c)). This was because MBCC40 was also a bending-dominated structure with an average connectivity of 6.86. As revealed in Fig. 10(c), the joints of pristine MBCC40 in P/MBCC40 cracked similarly to those of BCC40. Notably, localized deformation at the edge of P/MBCC40 was observed due to the unique structural configuration of pristine MBCC40.

The relative density of pristine CCSs significantly altered the mechanical properties of P/CCSs. Thus, it was essential to clarify how the relative density affected the deformation modes of P/CCSs under dynamic compression. Figure 11(a) and Movie S4 in the ESM show the deformation process of P/MBCC20. At initial impact, minimal strut displacement was observed due to limited deformation. When the strain of P/MBCC20 exceeded 8%, a shear band emerged. The broken joints were mainly concentrated on the shear bands (labeled with dotted lines in Figs. 11(a) and 10(d)), with a specific plane inclined at 63.43° relative to the loading direction. P/MBCC20 was divided into two parts by the emerging shear band. The left part was compressed and moved towards the right part. The deformation process of P/MBCC30, as shown in Fig. 11(b) and Movie S5 in the ESM, mirrored that of P/MBCC20. However, the extent of localized deformation decreased as the relative density of pristine MBCCs increased. When the relative density of pristine MBCCs rose from 30% to 40%, localized deformation further diminished. This finding indicates that the relative density of pristine MBCCs alters the failure mode of MBCC P/CCSs, transitioning the failure mode from localized deformation to global fracture (Fig. 10).

Ceramics are typically characterized by high strength but poor toughness. Thus, CCSs can withstand high compressive loading but absorb minimal energy during deformation. Consequently, data points for CCSs are positioned at the bottom of the Ashby map [17,18,26−29,39,40]. In contrast, by leveraging the excellent strength and toughness of metals, CMSs possess superior specific compressive strength and specific energy absorption capacity [30−34,41−44]. Although polymers have inferior mechanical properties compared with those of metals, their low density endows CPSs with significant advantages in specific mechanical properties, making them even comparable to CMSs [35−38,45,46].

Notably, P/CCSs demonstrate exceptional specific mechanical properties. On one hand, the polyurea network restricts the displacement of broken struts, enabling P/CCSs to dissipate more energy under the same deformation. On the other hand, the dual-phase interpenetrating P/CCSs allow a maximum compressive strain up to 0.6. Under the synergistic effect of enhanced strength and deformability, P/CCSs exhibit superior load-bearing capacity and energy absorption capability simultaneously. Importantly, the added polyurea does not compromise the mechanical properties of pristine CCSs but rather achieves broad tuning of the specific compressive strength and energy absorption, outperforming most reported CMSs and CPSs. Although the energy dissipated by P/CCSs under impact is less than that absorbed by CMSs and CPSs, their energy absorption is still significantly greater than that of CCSs. Thus, P/CCSs offer advantageous mechanical performance and are lightweight under both quasi-static compression and dynamic impact conditions.

## 4 Conclusions

Inspired by the dual-phase interpenetrating architecture of natural materials, we proposed to fabricate P/CCSs to address the inherent brittleness and limited energy absorption capacity of CCSs. It was concluded that polyurea not only strengthened CCSs but also tremendously improved the energy absorption capacity of CCSs by more than an order of magnitude under quasi-static compressive loading, and P/CCSs were still in contact even when the strain reached 0.6. The load-bearing capacity and energy-absorbing ability of P/CCSs under impact were 1.07–1.85 and 3.31–10.94 times those of CCSs without polyurea. The height retention rates of P/CCSs after impact ranged from 98.35%±0.18% to 100.39%±1.08%. Furthermore, the mechanical properties of P/CCSs were closely related to the structural configurations and relative densities of pristine Al2O3 CCSs, which also altered the cracking patterns and deformation modes of P/CCSs under compression. This work provides an effective solution to reduce the adverse effects of the brittleness and poor energy-absorbing ability of CCSs. The approach provided in this work can be extensively used for CCSs, thereby positioning P/CCSs as promising candidates for structural components.
