## 1 Introduction

Ce3+-activated materials have attracted considerable attention because of their multicolor luminescence characteristics [1]. The tunable broadband emission (400–700 nm) of Ce is attributed to spin-allowed 5d→4f transitions, which are closely associated with Ce3+ occupancy and host structure [2]. For example, Yuan *et al*. [3] successfully synthesized a novel BaLu2Al2Ga2SiO12:Ce phosphor, which emits cyan light centered at 485 nm with ultrahigh internal quantum efficiency (IQE = 92.2%). Sun *et al*. [4] developed a YAG:Ce-based yellow (520−580 nm) phosphor-glass emitter, which possesses high quantum efficiency (98.4%) and absorption coefficient (86.8%). Wang *et al*. [5] achieved wide-range emission (400−600 nm) tuning from blue to green in Ce-doped Ba3Y1−*y*Lu*y*Al2O7.5 phosphors. Zhu *et al*. [6] implemented strategic lattice-site occupation control of Ce, realizing broadband orange emission across the cover visible spectrum (450−800 nm). To our knowledge, although some Ce3+-activated high-quality n-UV emissions (300–400 nm) in the halide family have been reported, such as (DFPD)CeCl4·2MeOH (luminescence efficacy = 60%) [7], Cs3CeBr6 (60% intensity retention at 423 K) [8], and Cs2ZnCl4:Ce [9], excellent n-UV-emitting inorganic oxide phosphors activated with Ce3+ are scarce and have low quantum efficiency and reabsorption loss. Notably, the low cost, facile synthesis, structural stability, and environmental stability of phosphates make them promising n-UV-emitting phosphor hosts. Exploiting Ce3+-activated n-UV emission from the structural benefit of phosphate is a fascinating research topic.

The Stokes shift (Δ*S*) is a vital parameter that describes the energy difference between excitation and emission. It is closely related to the structure of host material. Blasse [10] found that a phosphor with a small Δ*S* typically corresponded to high structural rigidity of the host. A large Δ*S* may induce luminescence quenching, whereas a small Δ*S* benefits luminescence efficiency and thermal stability [11]. For example, Liu’s group [12] employed the rigid structure of SrSc4Si5O17 to achieve a small Δ*S* (~30 nm), reducing energy loss. The blue–violet luminescence of Ce with a high IQE (73.4%) peaked at 390 nm. Conversely, the reported red-emitting SrLaScO4:Ce phosphor presented a large ΔS (~210 nm), leading to low luminescence efficiency and poor thermostability due to increased nonradiative transitions [13]. As a result, host structure engineering is a feasible route for realizing efficient and stable n-UV emission of Ce.

Double phosphates with the general formula of ABLn(PO4)2 (A/B = alkali/alkaline earth, Ln = lanthanide) have emerged as promising phosphor hosts for rare-earth activator doping owing to their exceptional structural rigidity, photochemistry stability, and strong absorption in the n-UV spectral region [14]. Recently, Zhang *et al*. [15] employed solid-state synthesis under a reducing atmosphere to obtain a KSrLu(PO4)2:Ce phosphor with 78% quantum efficiency, emitting bright bluish-violet light peaking at 384 nm in the 340−460 nm range. Notably, the double phosphate family possesses a three-dimensional rigid framework structure, which is composed of [PO4], [AO*n*], and [B/LnO*n*] polyhedra (*n* = 6−8) [16]. The stable interconnection of these structural units can effectively suppress phonon-assisted nonradiative transitions, which can increase the luminescence efficiency and stability [17]. The synergistic combination of structural rigidity and chemical bonding makes the double phosphate an ideal phosphor host.

Based on the above expectations, we designed a new double-phosphate NaSrY(PO4)2 (NSYP) with high structural rigidity, which was supported by the high Debye temperature (*Θ*D = 695 K) calculated by the density functional theory (DFT). In this study, a NSYP:Ce nanophosphor was first prepared by microwave-assisted hydrothermal synthesis. The highly efficient n-UV luminescence of Ce was systematically investigated based on its rigid structure-induced small Δ*S*. Moreover, the temperature-sensitive characteristics and scintillating luminescence behaviors of the title material were utilized for advanced optical thermometry and X-ray imaging.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

A microwave-assisted hydrothermal method was employed to synthesize NaSrY1−*x*(PO4)2:*x*Ce (*x* = 0.3%, 0.5%, 0.7%, 1%, 3%, 5%) nanophosphors. All raw materials were purchased from Huawei Ruike Chemical Co., Ltd., China. According to the specified molar ratio, sodium nitrate (NaNO3, AR), strontium nitrate (Sr(NO3)2, AR), yttrium nitrate (Y(NO3)3, AR), and cerium nitrate (Ce(NO3)3, AR) were dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water. A suitable amount of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (C16H33N(CH3)3Br, CTAB) was added to the solution, stirring until a clear solution was formed. Next, 2 mL of 85% phosphoric acid (slightly excess) was added to the solution, and the pH was adjusted to 3 with NaOH. The solution was stirred for 20 min and transferred to a 50 mL reaction vessel (Teflon-lined), which was filled to 70% capacity. The heating rate was 5 (°)·min−1 at a frequency of 2 GHz, and the power output was 220 W. Subsequently, the reaction vessel was heated at 220 °C for 20 min, after which the furnace was turned off and cooled to room temperature. The resulting product was washed several times with water and alcohol and then dried in a vacuum oven, yielding a white powder sample.

### 2.2 Characterizations

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) for phase purity identification was performed using a diffractometer (D/Max-3B, Rigaku) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.54056 Å), 30 kV, and 30 mA. Nano-sized morphology characteristics were obtained by field emission transmission electron microscopy (FETEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) (JEM-F200, JEOL). An electron spectrometer (K-Alpha+, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with Mono Al Kα (1486.6 eV) excitation was used for X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis. An ultraviolet–visible (UV/vis) spectrophotometer (UV2600, SHIMADZU) was employed for diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS). A spectrophotometer (HITACHI F-7100) with xenon lamp excitation was used for photoluminescence excitation (PLE) and photoluminescence (PL) data. The fluorescence decay time and quantum efficiency (QE) were tested using an Edinburgh FLS980 fluorescence spectrometer. A JC2000C contact angle meter was employed to measure the static contact angle. The liquid used for the measurement was ethyl alcohol, while a hanging drop method was employed with a droplet volume of 5 μL. X-ray excited optical luminescence (XEOL) spectra were measured by a spectrometer (Omni-Fluo960) with an X-ray tube (target material: W, voltage 50 kV, tube current 200 μA) and a photon counter (DCS210PC-9S). X-ray imaging was performed using a homemade setup comprising a miniature X-ray tube and camera (ORCA-Fusion BT, C15440-20UP).

### 2.3 Calculation method

The Vienna *ab-initio* simulation package (VASP) code was employed to perform the DFT calculations. The structural relaxation and electronic structure calculations were conducted using the plane-wave scheme with a cut-off energy of 500 eV. The projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotentials and generalized gradient approximation (GGA) were chosen. A 1 × 1 × 2 supercell was constructed for the calculations. The Monkhorst–Pack 6 × 6 × 3 k-point mesh was used for sampling the Brillouin zone. The convergence criteria for electronic energy and structural relaxation were 10−6 eV and −0.02 eV·Å−1, respectively. For *Θ*D calculation, an increased plane wave cut-off energy of 520 eV and an explicit k-mesh of 8 × 8 × 4 were employed.

## 3 Results and discussion

Microwave-assisted rapid hydrothermal synthesis was employed to construct a series of NSYP:*x*Ce (*x* = 0−5%) nanopowder phases. The XRD patterns of the typical NSYP and NSYP:0.5%Ce nanophosphors were refined using the Rietveld method, as shown in Figs. 1(a) and 1(b), respectively. The results yield acceptable values for the refinement parameters (*R*wp, *R*p, and *χ*2), revealing the reliability of phase purity (Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). All the diffraction peaks for the NSYP-based phosphors incorporated with various Ce concentrations agree with the standard card (PDF #00-051-1632) for KCaY(PO4)2 reported previously [18], verifying their hexagonal structure with *P*6222 space group (Fig. S1 in the ESM). Subsequently, the structural characteristics of NSYP are illustrated using the VESTA software, as depicted in Fig. 1(c). The octahedral [Sr/YO8] and tetrahedral [PO4] units are arranged to form [Sr/YO8]–[PO4]–[Sr/YO8] chains along the *c* axis. The octahedral [NaO8] units are bridged with these chains, constructing a stable three-dimensional framework. The radii of eight-coordinated Na, Sr, Y, and Ce ions are 1.18, 1.26, 1.019, and 1.143 Å, respectively. Considering the matching ionic radius, Ce is expected to occupy the Na, Sr, or Y sites in the NSYP matrix. For example, the NSYP:0.5%Ce nanophosphor shows a near-spherical morphology with a mean size of 63.6 nm (Fig. 1(d)). The corresponding HRTEM and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) results indicate high crystallinity, with a lattice spacing of 2.05 Å from the (211) crystal plane (Fig. 1(e)), corresponding to the XRD peak at 42.4° in Fig. 1(b). In addition, the interplanar spacings of 2.37 and 2.21 Å belong to the (112) and (202) crystal planes, respectively. The XPS spectrum displays the photoelectron peaks of Na 1s, Sr 3p, Y 3d, P 2p, O 1s, and Ce 3d (Fig. 1(f)), confirming that Ce3+ elements have been successfully incorporated into the NSYP nanophosphor. The Ce3+ XPS spectrum exhibits the doublet peaks at 884.99/881.27 eV (3d5/2) and 903.37/899.46 eV (3d3/2), corresponding to the binding energy separation of 18.4/18.2 eV. This result agrees well with the data reported previously [8], indicating the presence of trivalent cerium. Moreover, the elemental mappings conﬁrm that Na, Sr, Y, P, O, and Ce elements are uniformly distributed throughout the measured nanoparticles (Fig. 1(g)).

The precise mapping of the energy band structure of host materials provides fundamental insights into the processes that govern the luminescent behavior of rare-earth-activated phosphors. Figure 2(a) displays the energy band structure and density of states (DOS) of NSYP, which presents an indirect bandgap (E*g) of 4.17 eV. DOS can be used to analyze the composition of the band structure further. The bottom of the conduction band and the top of the valence band are mainly composed of Y and O orbitals, respectively. The optical absorption is attributed to the electronic transitions from O2− to Y3+/Sr2+. The experimental *E*g values of NSYP and NSYP:0.5%Ce3+ are 5.64 and 5.43 eV, respectively, as depicted in Fig. 2(b). The discrepancy between the experimental and calculated values is due primarily to the inherent limitations of the DFT method [3]. The slight decrease in *E*g is attributed to Ce doping, which creates new energy levels between the valence and conduction bands [19]. Such a large *E*g effectively reduces self-absorption. Notably, the *E*g (4.17 eV) obtained by DFT calculations is smaller than that (5.64 eV) obtained by experiment, which is due to the conventional underestimation of *E*g by the GGA method. Figure 2(c) illustrates the PLE and PL curves of NSYP:0.5%Ce. When monitored at 380 nm, the obtained PLE bands peaking at 256 and 325 nm are assigned to the transitions from the 4f ground state to the 5d excited states of Ce. Under 256 nm (4.84 eV, ~39,060 cm−1) excitation, the n-UV luminescence peak at 3.44 eV (~27,745 cm−1) corresponds to Δ*S* = 1.40 eV (~11,315 cm−1). Notably, the n-UV luminescence under 325 nm (3.81 eV, ~30,730 cm−1) excitation peaks at 3.28 eV (~26,455 cm−1) present a small Δ*S* of 0.53 eV (~4280 cm−1). Compared with the recently reported Ce-activated luminescent materials, the investigated NSYP:0.5%Ce material has a smaller Δ*S* (Table S2 in the ESM), which is beneficial for efficient luminescence. A small Δ*S* is closely associated with a stable host framework structure (structural rigidity), which can be evaluated using *Θ*D parameter [20]. Hence, we calculated the *Θ*D parameter based on the quasi-harmonic Debye model via the DFT method. The obtained *Θ*D of NSYP is 695 K, which far surpasses those of traditional phosphor hosts (Table S3 in the ESM), indicating its high structural rigidity. This high structural rigidity effectively suppresses the vibrations of the Ce excited states within the lattice, leading to a minimal Δ*S* between excitation and emission [20]. On the other hand, in an asymmetric crystal field, the 5d level of Ce splits into 5d1 and 5d2, corresponding to different emission peaks. The 5d1 level contains a single electron in the 5d orbital, resulting in weaker electron interactions and less level splitting, typically resulting in an asymmetric broadband under 325 nm excitation [21,22]. For the 5d2 level, two electrons occupy the 5d orbital, and their interaction leads to greater level splitting, making the emission spectrum more asymmetric and exhibiting a characteristic of double peak superposition under 256 nm excitation [23].

As shown in Fig. 2(d), the PL band (*λ*ex = 325 nm) can be separated into four Gaussian sub-bands, centered at 27,097 cm−1 (I), 26,371 cm−1 (II), 25,565 cm−1 (III), and 24,597 cm−1 (IV). The energy difference between bands (I) and (III) is 1774 cm−1, while the energy difference between bands (Ⅱ) and (Ⅳ) is 1532 cm−1. Similarly, the energy difference of the Gaussian sub-peaks under 256 nm excitation is calculated. Two sets of PL bands, 29,762 cm−1 (I) and 27,745 cm−1 (III), as well as 28,633 cm−1 (II) and 26,536 cm−1 (IV), correspond to energy differences of 2017 cm−1 and 2097 cm−1, respectively. These values are close to the theoretical energy diﬀerence (2000 cm−1) between the 2F5/2 and 2F7/2 ground levels [24,25]. Ce occupies the sites of [Sr/YO8] and [NaO8], both with the same coordination number. The crystal field splitting of the 5d emission level and the electron cloud distribution effect of Ce are directly influenced by the average bond length of coordination [26−28]. A smaller bond length leads to a greater energy decrease of 5d→4f transitions, resulting in a longer emission wavelength. Since the average bond length of [Sr/YO8] (2.436 Å) is smaller than that of [NaO8] (2.894 Å) (Table S4 in the ESM), the II and IV PL bands result from the Ce activators in the [Sr/YO8] sites, whereas the I and III emissions come from those in the [NaO8] sites. To confirm the above conclusion, luminescence decay measurements were conducted on the four Gaussian sub-bands using 256 and 325 nm excitations (Fig. 2(e)). The biexponential function is suitable for fitting all the decay curves (Eq. (S1) in the ESM). The II emission presents decay time similar to that of the IV. A similar result is obtained for the I and III emissions. Moreover, the decay differences for the II/IV and I/III emissions clearly appear, further revealing the incorporation of Ce into the [Sr/YO8] and [NaO8] sites. The PL concentration quenching under 325 and 256 nm excitation was analyzed (Fig. S2 in the ESM). With increasing dopant concentration, the emissive shapes and positions remain unchanged, and the optimal doping concentration is 0.5% Ce. The nonradiative energy transfer process depends on the distance between the Ce-emitting centers [29]. The critical distance (*R*c) of Ce in the NSYP host is determined by Eq. (S2) in the ESM. The calculated *R*c value (47.3 Å) is much greater than 5 Å, indicating that concentration quenching is caused by multipolar interactions. The specific multipolar interaction is confirmed according to Eqs. (S3) and (S4) in the ESM. The log(*I*/χ) versus log(*χ*) plots for the 5d1→4f and 5d2→4f transitions exhibit linear relationships with slopes of −1.21 and −1.37, respectively. The calculated *θ* values are close to 6, indicating that dipole–dipole interactions are directly responsible for concentration quenching [30].

Free Ce ions exhibit a large energy difference between the 4f ground and 5d excited states [31,32]. However, the 4f→5d electronic configuration of Ce undergoes crystal-field splitting within the NSYP host lattice, resulting in stark deviations from the energy level structure of free Ce ions (Fig. 2(f)). The energy difference between the 4f ground state and 5d excited state decreases significantly, referred to as the centroid shift *D*(*A*) for the NSYP host, which is mainly governed by two factors: the centroid shift and the crystal ﬁeld splitting of the energy levels in the 5d state [33,34]. In contrast, the energy levels of the Ce ion in the 4f state are influenced primarily by the spin–orbit coupling (s/o), which splits the 4f state into two energy levels, 2F5/2 and 2F7/2. The s/o interaction results from the magnetic dipole of the 4f electron interacting with the spin and orbital motions of the electron. This coupling is largely unaffected by the host crystal field, as the 4f electron is well-shielded by the outer filled 5s and 5p electron shells. *μ*(*→*)s and *μ*(*→*)l represent the magnetic dipole due to the spinning electron S(→) and the relative orbital motion of the nucleus and electron L(→), respectively [35,36]. The excitation energy generated by the 4f → 5d transition decreases because of the vibrational relaxation caused by the Δ*S* [37]. The potential energy curves of Ce ion in its electronic ground and excited states are plotted against nuclear coordinates representing atomic motions localized at the luminescent center. When an electron is excited to the 5d parabola, the surrounding lattice ﬁrst relaxes the electron to the lowest vibrational level of the 5d parabola before returning to the 4f parabola. This process results in an inevitable energy loss, limiting the attainable luminescence efficiency. In this case, the small Δ*S* of 0.53 eV is beneficial for achieving as high luminescence efficiency as possible.

Under 325 and 256 nm excitation, the IQE values are 98.5% and 45.4%, respectively (Figs. 3(a) and 3(b)). As shown in Fig. 3(c), the IQE of NSYP:0.5%Ce is greater than those of Ce-activated multicolor phosphors reported with other host structures. The detailed data are summarized in Table S5 in the ESM. For Ce luminescence, small Δ*S* associated with high structural rigidity can reduce energy loss and improve the optical behavior [20]. The Debye temperature is a useful proxy for structural rigidity. Hence, the high *Θ*D (695 K) of NSYP is responsible for the high quantum efficiency of NSYP:0.5%Ce. Additionally, several double phosphate-based phosphors with high luminescence efficiency were found, further supporting the structural merits of the double-phosphate NSYP host (Table S6 in the ESM). The temperature-dependent spectra of NSYP:0.5%Ce are shown in Fig. 3(d). The emissive profile is unchanged except for the intensity. At high temperature (423 K), the integrated intensity can retain 78% (*λ*ex = 325 nm) and 71% (*λ*ex = 256 nm) of its room-temperature value (Fig. 3(e)). The difference in thermal quenching originates from different excitation energies. When Ce is excited by different wavelengths, the energy of the excited state affects the distribution of the electronic energy levels and interactions [38]. The shorter the excitation wavelength is, the more the resulting excited state is influenced by lattice thermal vibration, leading to an increase in non-radiative transitions [39]. In addition, the thermal activation energy (*E*a) is determined. Under 325 and 256 nm excitation, the *E*a parameters are as high as 0.31 and 0.26 eV, respectively. With increasing temperature, thermally activated electrons can overcome *E*a, undergo an exothermic process and return to the ground state via the nonradiative transition, giving rise to thermal quenching [40]. Therefore, a large *E*a can weaken the temperature quenching behavior. Short-wavelength excitation promotes the conversion of Ce electrons to higher-lying 5d states, resulting in stronger electron–phonon coupling with the host lattice. The enhanced lattice vibration interactions at elevated temperatures increase the non-radiative relaxation, thereby exacerbating the thermal quenching effect for the 256 nm excitation [41]. On the other hand, the high *Θ*D (695 K) of NSYP reveals the strong rigidity of the host lattice, effectively suppressing the phonon-assisted nonradiative transitions associated with temperature [20]. As a result, the NSYP:0.5%Ce nanophosphor demonstrates good thermostability at different excitation wavelengths compared with the nanophosphors recently shown in Fig. 3(f). The detailed data are listed in Table S7 in the ESM. The exceptional luminescent performance, particularly the high luminescence efficiency and thermostability, is pivotal for advancing optoelectronic applications.

The variable-temperature PLE (298−473 K) was employed to analyze the thermally induced variation in PLE intensity for NSYP:0.5%Ce. As shown in Fig. 3 and Fig. 4(a), the PLE intensities at the 256 nm (2F7/2→5d) and 325 nm (2F5/2→5d) peaks decrease with increasing temperature (Fig. 4(b)). As a result, the relationship between the PLE peak and temperature can be revealed via an excited-state absorption intensity ratio (EIR) technology. The temperature-dependent EIR values of NSYP:0.5%Ce is fitted well with the empirical exponential Eq. (S5) in the ESM. Subsequently, the relative sensitivity (*S*R) was determined using Eq. (S6) in the ESM to evaluate the temperature sensing properties. Excellent *S*R can effectively improve the temperature resolution of practical fluorescence thermometers [42,43]. As shown in Fig. 4(c), the maximal *S*R value (0.94% K−1) is achieved at room temperature, which outperforms reported phosphors based on the EIR model (Table S8 in the ESM). After 20 consecutive spectral tests at 298 K, the EIR value remains stable without significant fluctuations (Fig. 4(d)). The temperature uncertainty (δ*T*) was calculated for quantitative evaluation using Eq. (S7) in the ESM. The δ*T* parameter is as low as 0.25 K at 298 K, as plotted in Fig. 4(e), which greatly exceeds the requirements for actual applications. Moreover, the periodic response of the EIR for NSYP:0.5%Ce was measured (Fig. 4(f)). The plotted curve is fully reversible without obvious hysteresis. The corresponding EIR value remains constant after multiple heating and cooling cycles, demonstrating the extremely high repeatability and stability of the designed thermometer phosphor.

For solution-processed X-ray scintillator screens, employed phosphor should demonstrate outstanding chemical stability against polar solvents (e.g., ethanol, methylbenzene) to maintain good optical properties during screen fabrication and long-term operation under radiation exposure [44,45]. Therefore, investigating solvent resistance is crucial for the studied nanophosphor to function as a scintillator screen. The nanopowder particles were dispersed in ethanol to evaluate their stability and dispersibility over time. After 10 d of soaking in ethanol, the emissive intensity remains at 82% of the initial level, without any change in the emissive shape or position (Figs. 5(a) and 5(b)). The nanophosphor maintains colloidal stability in ethanol for more than 12 h under static ambient conditions (Fig. 5(c)). No detectable sedimentation was observed. In addition, the angular contact angle remains in the 20°–30° range, demonstrating excellent solubility. The NSYP:0.5%Ce nanophosphor can be excited by X-ray (Fig. 5(d)). Subsequently, the material was thoroughly dispersed in the polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) and ethyl alcohol matrix materials to prepare a flexible transparent film (Fig. 5(e)). Moreover, a homemade X-ray imaging device was used for practical application analysis on objects (Fig. 5(f)). As shown in Fig. 5(g), modulation transfer function (MTF) was used to confirm the spatial resolution. The NSYP:0.5%Ce-integrated film was employed as a scintillator screen, and the achieved spatial resolution is 11 line pairs per millimeter (lp/mm), which is comparable to the 10 lp/mm for the commercial scintillator CsI:Tl [46]. Moreover, compared with other scintillator materials reported in Table S9 in the ESM, the NSYP:0.5%Ce nanophosphor demonstrates satisfactory advantages. This is further supported through a grayscale illustration of the light and dark stripes. Finally, an imaging analysis of the internal chip was performed using the NSYP:1%Ce scintillator screen. As shown in Fig. 5(h), the detailed chips inside the circuit board are clearly visible at the three marked positions. These results indicate that the NSYP:Ce nanophosphor has significant potential for X-ray imaging applications.

## 4 Conclusions

In conclusion, an efficient and stable n-UV-emitting NSYP:Ce nanophosphor is constructed based on small Δ*S*. The theoretical calculations of the NSYP host confirm that the ultrahigh structural rigidity (*Θ*D = 695 K) contributes to the small Δ*S* (~4280 cm−1) of Ce. Structural rigidity engineering enables an unprecedented combination of n-UV luminescence efficiency (IQE = 98.5%) and thermal stability (78%@423 K). Moreover, this study develops an advanced temperature-detecting model and thin-film scintillator. The corresponding EIR value remains constant after multiple heating and cooling cycles, showing high repeatability and stability. The solution-processable NSYP:Ce scintillator with 11 lp/mm spatial resolution allows distinct X-ray imaging of internal objects. In this work, the strategy of high-rigidity structure-induced small Δ*S* is introduced for the development of efficient Ce-activated phosphors.
