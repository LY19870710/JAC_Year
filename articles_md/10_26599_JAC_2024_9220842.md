## 1 Introduction

Zirconium diboride-based materials are currently considered a class of promising materials for several applications, in particular in the aerospace sector. In the last decade, research has focused on the fabrication of dense composites possessing high strength (500−1000 MPa) even at temperature above 2000 ℃ and adequate fracture toughness (> 5 MPa·m1/2) [1−10]. Indeed, the low fracture toughness remains one of the major concerns for the application of these materials under severe environmental conditions.

Fibre reinforcement has demonstrated to offer the major potential for improving the damage tolerance of these ceramics, thanks to effective toughening mechanisms such as enhanced crack deflection and load-carrying capability. In addition, other toughening mechanisms, such as crack bridging and pull-out, are possible. Indeed, significant increases of fracture toughness were obtained through addition of SiC whiskers [11−14], chopped SiC fibres [15,16], continuous SiC fibres [17], and more recently milled/chopped/continuous carbon fibres [18−23].

On the other hand, bulk ZrB2−SiC materials have been often studied in combination with graphite, for both reinforcement and lightweight purposes. Many researchers have investigated the addition of discontinuous graphite flakes and reported significant increases of fracture toughness. Wang *et al.* [24] studied the addition of 25 vol% graphite flakes on mechanical properties and thermal shock resistance. Shahedi Asl *et al.* [25] studied the effect of addition of nano flakes of graphite on the mechanical properties, reporting significant improvements in fracture toughness up to 7.1 MPa·m1/2. Zhou *et al.* [26] studied the anisotropy in the flexural strength and fracture toughness of a hot pressed ZrB2–20 vol% SiC–15 vol% graphite composite (perpendicular vs. parallel to pressure applied: 480 MPa vs. 390 MPa and 6 MPa·m1/2 vs. 4 MPa·m1/2). Pourmohammadie Vafa *et al.* [27] observed the highest flexural strength of 366 MPa for composite containing 7.5 vol% graphite, while the maximum Vickers hardness has resulted for 2.5 vol% graphite, and its value was 20.8 GPa. Most of the authors agree that hot pressing induces the preferential alignment of graphite with the basal planes perpendicular to the pressing direction and phase analysis of hot-pressed samples revealed the formation of the ZrC and B4C phases besides the constituent ZrB2, SiC, and graphite phases. Addition of graphite to ZrB2–SiC composite improved the sintering process and the grain refinement, and ZrB2 grains showed a transgranular type of fracture.

The fracture toughness and work of fracture (WOF) were evaluated by Savari *et al.* [28] using the Single-Edge Notched Beam (SENB) technique. It was found that the fracture toughness and WOF were improved by the addition of graphite platelets and SiC to the monolithic ZrB2. The maximum fracture toughness of 4.8±0.1 MPa·m1/2 was obtained for the ZrB2−15 vol% graphite specimen. Another investigated additive is graphene [29]. The addition of approximately 5 vol% graphene nanosheets improved the fracture toughness of ZrB2–SiC up to 7.3 MPa·m1/2, and the strength was also raised to 1.0 GPa. However, the majority of the above mentioned materials were reinforced only with a very low content of carbon that acted mostly as particle reinforcement.

In this work, we proposed an alternative method to introduce carbon-based phases into a ZrB2–SiC matrix, using filter paper as a low-cost source of graphite. In particular, filter paper was chosen as it is water resistant and thus can be easily impregnated with aqueous slurries maintaining its structural integrity and ease of handling. Moreover, being composed of randomly oriented microfibres that undergo thermolysis without a melting stage [30,31], this type of paper is suitable for high temperature treatments, such as the sintering process. We hypothesized that the resulting strong fibrous carbonaceous residue can be used as toughening phase instead of conventional milled carbon fibres.

The aim of this work is to evaluate the efficacy of paper-derived graphite as potential toughener for ZrB2. For the sake of comparison, the microstructure and properties were compared to those of layered, short fibre-reinforced ZrB2–SiC composites, recently developed [32]. Furthermore, the performance of paper-toughened composites was compared to that of other toughened composites containing graphite flakes or graphene sheets.

### 2.1 Preparation of paper-derived graphite toughened ZrB<sub>2</sub> materials

Commercial starting materials were used for the production of materials: ZrB2 (powder, grade B, H.C. Starck, Germany; particle size range 0.5–6 μm, impurities (wt%): 0.2 C, 1.3 O, 0.19 N, 0.1 Fe, 1.4 Hf); α-SiC (powder, grade UF-25, H.C. Starck, Germany; D50: 0.45 μm); cellulose (filter paper, grade 600, VWR, USA; thickness: 150 µm, pore size: 25–60 µm, particle retention: 13 µm, grammage: 64 g/m2), see the morphology in Fig. 1(a).

Water-based slurries consisting of ZrB2–10 vol% SiC were prepared by wet ball-milling and drying by a rotary evaporator. The slurry concentration was changed in order to tailor the cellulose/powder ratio and consequently the paper-derived graphite/ceramic ratio after heat treatment. The slurries were used to infiltrate the filter paper that took on a grey colour, varying from light to dark depending on the slurry concentration (Fig. 1(b)). The impregnated layers were then piled-up and dried. A series of green samples with different powder−paper mixtures were sintered by hot pressing (HP) in vacuum using an induction heating graphite furnace (MP 20(II), SIATEM, Italy), at 1900 ℃ with applied pressure of 30−40 MPa and holding time of 15 min. During this high temperature treatment, cellulose underwent thermal decomposition leaving graphite residues amongst the grains. The whole process is sketched in Fig. 1(c).

Four samples were selected for further characterization, labelled as Samples A, B, C, and D. Specifically, Sample A was obtained through impregnation of dried paper sheets using a high solid loading (~60 wt%) aqueous ZrB2 slurry and sintering by applying a pressure of 40 MPa; for Sample B, sheets were wet with equal quantity of deionized water until paper absorbed it, before impregnation and consolidation in the same conditions of Sample A; Sample C was prepared similarly to Sample B but with a sintering pressure of 30 MPa; Sample D was prepared by impregnating paper sheets with a low solid loading (20 wt%) slurry to achieve a high paper-derived graphite/ceramic ratio followed by hot pressing at 40 MPa.

### 2.2 Microstructural characterization

The carbon content inside the composite material was determined as the difference between the total pellet weight after sintering and the ZrB2−SiC powder weight. Bulk density was determined by both Archimedes’ method and by weight-to-volume ratio.

Residual porosity, *p*, was determined by image analysis (commercial software package Image-Pro Plus Analyzer 7.0, Media Cybernetics, USA) on scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of polished sections. The relative density, *ρ*rel, was deduced as (1 − *p*).

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out from 10° to 80° (step 0.02, step time 0.5 s) using an apparatus (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation to determine crystalline phases, both in parallel and perpendicular orientations to the applied pressure during sintering.

Microstructures of pristine and dried impregnated paper sheets were analysed using an environmental scanning electron microscope (E-SEM Quanta 200, FEI Company, USA). Microstructures of sintered samples were analysed using a field emission gun-scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, ZEISS Sigma, Carl Zeiss Microscopy Gmbh, Germany) and an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS, INCA Energy 300, Oxford instruments, UK). FE-SEM analysis was carried out on polished cross section and polished surface of each sample, that were prepared polishing them down to a 0.25 μm finish with a semi-automatic polishing machine (Tegramin-25, Struers, Italy), as well as on fracture surface of specimens after bending tests. The mean grain size of SiC and ZrB2 phases were measured on polished surface SEM micrographs by image analysis using a commercial software (Image-Pro Plus Analyzer 7.0, Media Cybernetics, USA), following the standard ISO 13383-1, method A2. At least 100 grains were measured.

### 2.3 Mechanical characterization

Bending tests in 4-point fixture were carried out at room temperature (RT) on 2 mm × 2.5 mm × 25 mm (thickness by width by length) bars, lower and upper span of 20 and 10 mm respectively, using a screw-driven load frame (Z050, Zwick-Roell, Germany), with a cross-head rate of 1 mm/min. Bending tests at 1500 ℃, under argon flow (3.5 L/min), were performed using a screw-driven load frame (Instron mod. 1195, Instron, USA) equipped with a furnace (HTTF mod. 924, Severn Furnaces Limited, UK). Test bars were heated up in Ar flux with a rate of 10 ℃/min to 1500 ℃ and held for 15 min before the load application. Again, 2 mm × 2.5 mm × 25 mm (thickness by width by length) bars were tested using lower span of 20 mm, upper span of 10 mm, and a cross-head rate of 1 mm/min. For each group, at least three specimens were tested.

The fracture toughness (*K*IC) was evaluated at room and high temperature by 4-point bending on chevron-notched beams (CNB), following the standard of advanced technical ceramics EN 14425-3:2010. Test bars of 2.5 mm × 2 mm × 25 mm (thickness by width by length, respectively) were notched using a cutting machine (T34, Missimi-Berney, Switzerland) with a 0.1 mm-thick diamond saw; the chevron-notch tip depth and average side length were about 0.12 and 0.80 of the bar thickness, respectively. Tests were carried out at both RT and 1500 ℃ in Ar flux using the same facilities for flexural tests at a cross-head rate of 0.05 mm/min. For each group, at least three specimens were tested. The “slice model” equation of Munz *et al.* [33] was used to calculate *K*IC.

### 2.4 Short-term oxidation tests

Oxidation tests were carried out in a bottom loading furnace (FC18-0311281, Nannetti S.r.l., Italy) at 1500 ℃ in air for 1 min. Bars with dimensions of 2 mm × 2.5 mm × 12.5 mm (thickness by width by length, respectively) were machined from graphite-based material labelled as Sample C, cleaned with solvents, dried under IR lamp, and placed on an alumina sample holder. After the furnace reached the target temperature, the bars were introduced in the chamber. The temperature dropped ~50 ℃ with the furnace opening but in just one minute it was recovered, at that point a holding time of 1 min was taken. At the end of oxidation test, the specimens were quickly removed and cooled down naturally in air.

The bars were weighed (accuracy = ±0.01 mg) before (*w*in) and after (*w*fin) oxidation test and the weight difference was normalized over the initial surface area *S* (calculated from measurements made with calliper) by the equation:  ΔmS=wfin−winS. After coating with a thin layer of carbon using a turbo-pumped sputter coater (Q150 T ES, Quorum Technologies Ltd, UK), surface and polished cross-section of oxidised specimens were analysed similarly to non-oxidised samples by FE-SEM/EDS.

### 3.1 Pyrolysis and sintering

During the hot pressing experiments, the combination of fast heating ramp (25 ℃/min), vacuum environment (0.2 mbar), and simultaneous application of high mechanical pressure (up to 40 MPa) created atypical pyrolysis conditions. Between 350 and 400 ℃, e.g., at temperatures much lower than sintering of the ceramic matrix, the cellulose thermolysis (or pyrolysis) occurred. According to the literature, the reaction pathway is very complex, being composed of primary and secondary reactions, often interconnected. Two competitive pathways have been proposed in Ref. [34] for primary reactions:

1) Dehydration of cellulose due to cleavage of C–O bonds with evolution of CO2, H2O, and CO and formation of high yield solid carbonaceous residue;

2) Depolymerisation, via breaking of the 1,4 glycosidic linkage followed by intramolecular rearrangement of cellulosic units to levoglucosan.

In any case, the maximum theoretical carbon yield in carbonization of cellulose is 44.4 wt%, corresponding to the formal loss of five molecules of water per anhydro-glucose unit, but generally the total mass loss during pyrolysis can be up to 90 wt% [35].

In this work, all the sintered paper-derived UHTCs showed a carbonaceous residual solid content of about 10 wt%, which is in line with data reported in literature. For example, Soares *et al.* [34] found 6 and 12 wt% of solid residue for Whatman paper and Kraft paper after pyrolysis at 600 ℃, respectively. In their experiments, the char yield was unaffected by variation of heating rate (1, 5, 10, 40 ℃/min) and environments (nitrogen vs. vacuum), but composition ratio of resulting high boiling products and gases side-products were influenced by changing those parameters.

As far as we known, no data about mechanical properties of carbonized paper are reported; however, Kang *et al*. [36] reported that the material obtained from pyrolysis of thin paper sheets is strong and flexible enough to be folded repeatedly, without any quantification.

A temperature as high as 1900 ℃ was necessary for the samples’ densification, with the onset at around 1815 ℃. The microstructural evolution and the densification mechanisms during the thermal cycle are discussed in Section 3.3.

The XRD analysis (Fig. 2) confirmed the presence of the following crystalline phases: ZrB2 (PDF#34-0423), α-SiC (PDF#29-1131), and cellulose-derived graphite. The samples displayed different microstructural features depending on the process and sintering parameters as described below and were highly anisotropic.

**Samples A and D**: Sample A appeared as an almost fully dense layered material (Fig. 3(a)) where the layers were not homogeneously spaced between each other (the inset in Fig. 3(a)). This was probably due to the dry filter paper, which acted as a sponge absorbing a large part of the water content of the slurry, thus becoming saturated; consequently, high concentration of the slurry caused matrix accumulation onto the surface of the paper sheets during the infiltration step. The coarser fraction of ZrB2 particle was progressively eliminated; therefore, the subsequently infiltrated layers were more regularly spaced. Nevertheless, such a highly concentrated slurry made it difficult to control the spacing between the sheets. The opposite case, Sample D, was manufactured starting from a much more diluted slurry (i.e., 20 wt% powder loading) that resulted again in a very inhomogeneous sample with a carbon-rich core and higher concentrations of ZrB2 powder in the first stacked layers (the inset in Fig. 3(b)). The average graphite content in the latter sample was as high as 60 vol% (Fig. 3(b)). Amongst the carbon layers, inclusions of ZrB2 and SiC were detected (Figs. 3(c) and 3(d)) by EDS analysis (Figs. 3(e) and 3(f)).

### 3.3 Microstructural evolution during sintering

The microstructural evolution during the whole thermal cycle is sketched in Fig. 6, taking Sample C as an example, and was mainly deduced from microstructural observation after densification. Before sintering the samples had a stratified structure, with an alternation of paper layers impregnated with the smaller fraction of ZrB2 and SiC particles and matrix layers with the coarse powders, as depicted in the lower left sketch of Fig. 6. As previously mentioned, between 300 and 400 ℃ the thermolysis of the paper occurred, resulting in weight losses and graphite formation. The matrix phase was inert at this stage (see sketch “intermediate stage” in Fig. 6). At around 900 ℃, the mechanical pressure was applied (30 MPa for Sample C), causing a reduction of the distance between the layers, bending and rupture of the graphite filaments, and relative density increase from 33% to 74%. No appreciable shrinkage was then observed from 900 up to 1815 ℃, when the sintering onset was observed.

*
Fig. 6Densification curve of Sample C reported as an example for paper-derived graphite/ZrB2–SiC composites. On the right-hand side of the graph, sketches of the microstructure evolution from the green sample (initial stage), via formation of graphite layers due to filter paper thermolysis (intermediate stage), to the sintered composite (final stage).

At temperatures over 1500 ℃, graphite residues acted as sintering aids for ZrB2–SiC grains [40]. It is well known that carbon is an effective sintering additive for ZrB2, helping to remove the oxide phases from ZrB2 particle surface, e.g., B2O3 and ZrO2, that usually hinder the densification. Therefore, Reactions (1) and (2) became favourable:

(1) 5C(s)+ZrO2(s)+B2O3(l)→ZrB2(s)+5CO(g),ΔG0forT>1507∘C

(2) 8C(s)+SiO2(l)+B2O3(l)+ZrO2(s)→ZrB2(s)+SiC(s)+7CO(g),ΔG0forT>1500∘C

These reactions were useful to promote the bonding between ZrB2 phase and C filaments especially in areas where the paper was more abundant, but they did not seem to promote a visible shrinkage until 1800 ℃.

At T* > 1760 ℃, the formation of ZrC was also favourable (Reaction (3)) in agreement with microstructural observations (Fig. 4):

(3) 3C(s)+ZrO2(s)→ZrC(s)+2CO(g),ΔG0forT>1670∘C

At 1800 ℃, another reaction (Reaction (4)) became favourable, involving the sintering of matrix phases, ZrB2 and SiC:

(4) 2.5SiC(s)+B2O3(l)+ZrO2(s)→ZrB2(s)+2.5SiO(g)+2.5CO(g),ΔG0forT>1775∘C

The simultaneous occurrence of all Reactions (1)–(4) finally led to a measurable shrinkage that was slowed down only after 1900 ℃.

### 3.4 Mechanical properties

The flexural strength was tested for all the four samples (Table 1) and was found to be quite independent of the graphite distribution and matrix porosity, as Samples A, B, and C resulted in very close values, around 190 MPa. The lack of relevance of graphite spacing on the measured strength can be attributed to the macroscopic parallel alignment of the layers, where the graphitic flakes can be thought as planar cracks oriented in parallel with the stress direction. In contrast, Sample D with 60 vol% of carbon, had a strength of only 80 MPa, probably due to lack of structural phase (i.e., the ZrB2–SiC matrix) (Table 1, see load–displacement curves in Fig. 7(a)). All the materials showed a brittle failure (Fig. 7(a)) even if the crack path was quite tortuous (Fig. 7(b)).

As for the fracture toughness, addition of graphite layers brought a positive contribution increasing the fracture toughness from 3−4 MPa·m1/2 (for un-reinforced materials) to 5.1 and 5.5 MPa·m1/2 for Samples B and C, respectively (Table 1, see load–displacement curves in Fig. 7(c)).

During the analysis of the crack path propagating perpendicular to the layers, we observed crack front deflection (as shown in Fig. 7(b)) caused by the layered structure. At the microscopic level, we frequently observed crack deflection, branching, and blunting due to interactions with graphite flakes, as exemplified in Figs. 7(d) and 7(e). This interaction between the crack front and the graphite layers resulted also in irregular fracture surfaces, with detachment of large portions of matrix materials (up to 100 µm wide) that increased the crack tortuosity (Fig. 8). Therefore, we hypothesized that a partial contribution to toughening, for both samples, could occur through crack deflection, bifurcation and tip blunting [41]. For Sample B, it is plausible to speculate that the dense layers bore the external load, whereas delamination between the layers may have contributed to an increase in toughness. In the case of Sample C, porosity could have served as an additional toughening mechanism, as the pores might have acted as crack sinks.

The results of flexural strength and fracture toughness are in agreement with literature (e.g., see Ref. [24]): ZS10–graphite composites showed higher toughness and lower strength than unreinforced ZS10 ceramics [42]. The incorporation of carbon filaments several hundred micrometres long in a fine matrix (ZrB2 mean grain size: 3 μm) strongly changed the defects population. In fact, according to the Griffith equation (Eq. (5)) [43],

(5) c=KIC2πσ2

the critical flaw size (c*) increased from 5−9 μm of a ZrB2-based ceramics with 30 vol% of SiC to 170–360 μm [44], suggesting that large accumulations of graphite acted as critical flaws, as outlined by Refs. [9,24].

The combination of microstructural features, e.g., amount of C phase, and porosity was very favourable for Sample C. Therefore, this sample was selected for further characterization, including high temperature properties.

The flexural strength was tested at 1500 ℃ in Ar and confirmed a general trend found for ZrB2-based composites reinforced with carbon fibres, e.g., the increase of strength with temperature and the decrease of the moduli, as can be seen from the decrease in the slope in the load-displacement curve (Fig. 7(a)). As for continuous carbon fibre-reinforced composites [22,42], this improvement was mainly attributed to residual stress release at high temperatures. Since softening of amorphous phases is the most important reason for decrease of ZrB2–SiC strength at high temperatures [45], the observed opposite trend confirmed that the abundancy of carbon introduced through the filter paper helped in the elimination of residual non crystalline phases. The fracture toughness at 1500 ℃ maintained its RT value, i.e., 5.5 MPa·m1/2 (Table 1), but the area below the load–displacement curve was visibly larger (Fig. 7(c)), implying a higher work of fracture likely due to the increased ductility at 1500 ℃ coupled with the toughening effect of the graphite flakes. The overall mechanical behaviour was nonetheless consistent in the 25–1500 ℃ temperature range in inert atmosphere, showing similarities with fibre-reinforced ceramic composites.

### 3.5 Effect of the reinforcement type (chopped fibres, graphene nanosheets, and graphite flakes)

For the sake of comparison, layered short fibre-reinforced composites (fibre length of 6 mm) were produced using pliable ceramic sheets hot pressed at 1900 ℃ through a novel technique described in Ref. [32]. The fibre volumetric amount was between 35 and 50 vol% and the fibres were high modulus pitch-based carbon fibres (Nippon Graphite Fiber Granoc, XN-80C-06S). Figures 9(a)–9(f) compare the morphologies of such material and the graphite-toughened Sample B, in two directions. Even for the short fibre-reinforced composites, the structure was highly anisotropic, with a visibly layered structure on the cross section (Figs. 9(a) and 9(d)) and fibres randomly distributed prevalently in 2D on the surface perpendicular to the applied pressure (Figs. 9(b) and 9(e)). The mechanical properties of these sintered randomly oriented short fibre-reinforced composites are summarized in Table 2 (labelled as “short fibre”). Although carbon-based phases in those materials are technologically advanced fibres, strength and toughness of the paper-derived graphite toughened composites of this work seem to be very comparable or even better. Upon analysis of the microstructure at higher magnification (Figs. 9(c) and 9(f)), it is apparent that the graphite filaments released by the paper are more branched and thinner compared to straight carbon fibres, which could be an advantage especially for the fracture toughness.

### 3.6 Oxidation behaviour

Although specific tests in relevant conditions should be carried out to prove the resistance to harsh environment of these composites, the similarity with short carbon fibre-reinforced composites that were tested and validated in multiple plasma wind galleries [46] suggests that the paper-derived graphite toughened UHTCs possess promising high-temperature oxidation resistance [48,49]. Therefore, preliminary oxidation tests were conducted at 1500 ℃ in a bottom loading furnace and compared with a short carbon fibre-reinforced UHTC composite, which was tested in the same conditions. As said, specimens were introduced in the furnace at final temperature, and thus all competing oxidation phenomena occurred almost instantly.

Weight and dimensional variations of the specimens are reported in Table 3. All bars machined from Sample C exhibited weight gain around +1.1 mg/cm2. On the contrary, the specimens of short fibre-reinforced composite did not follow a specific trend as indicated by large data range (Table 3), registering both weight losses and weight increase up to 0.1 mg/cm2 after 1 min exposure, likely due to slight variations in the sample composition and fibre content close to the specimen surface. Indeed, mass variations are the result of negative terms (formation of volatile species due to carbon phase oxidation and matrix decomposition) and positive terms (formation of solid oxide scale). Moreover, for the latter material higher weight loss was expected because of the higher volumetric content of carbon phase (i.e., carbon fibre ≈ 40 vol% vs. graphite ≈ 30 vol%). Common to both samples was a slight volume increase, twice for sample with short fibres (Table 3).

Looking at the detail of the cross-section (Figs. 10(c) and 10(d)) of both samples, the oxide scale was constituted by two main regions: an outer thin silica layer, and an intermediate ZrO2/SiO2 layer. No porous ZrO2 layer was observed at the interface between the oxide and the unoxidised material (the insets in Figs. 10(c) and 10(d)), contrary to what is typically observed for ZrB2–SiC composites. As described in Refs. [5,50,51], the exposure of these latter to air from a temperature of 1500 ℃ results in the formation of a partially oxidised region depleted of SiC, because the outer SiO2 scale acts as a diffusion barrier lowering the oxygen activity and thus favouring the active oxidation of SiC (i.e., formation of volatile sub-oxide SiO(g)). Rezaie et al*. [52] studied the effect of graphite addition on the oxidation behaviour of ZrB2–SiC bulk ceramics. According to their studies on oxidation at 1500 ℃, the addition of at least 15 vol% graphite to ZrB2–SiC may reduce the formation of the SiC-depleted layer underneath the outer SiO2-rich scale. Indeed, graphite increases the activity of C compared to “nominally pure” SiC, which should have a corresponding increasing effect on the pressure of carbon monoxide beneath the silica layer. Consequently, the addition of a high amount (30–40 vol%) of highly graphitic phase as flakes or short fibres in the composites of this work may have inhibited the vaporization of SiC. Moreover, the discontinuous nature of the carbon reinforcement helped to avoid the formation of a continuous porosity; indeed, in long fibre-reinforced composites, the formation of an intermediate, porous ZrO2/SiO2 layer is often observed, but unlike for bulk ZrB2/SiC composites, the formation of this layer is mostly attributed to the oxidation of the fibres and less to the depletion of SiC. This effect is typically more visible in more highly oxidising conditions [48].

In the sample prepared using paper, the graphite underneath the surface was completely burnt (Reaction (6)); nevertheless, the inward O2 diffusion was limited thanks to preferential 2D orientation of the graphite layers (Fig. 10(c)). Indeed, following the burnout of the outer nano-layered graphite flakes, the underlying ZrB2 and SiC grains formed a borosilicate glass layer (Reactions (7)–(9)) which acted as a barrier against further oxygen diffusion, thus exhibiting a behaviour similar to that of bulk materials.

(6) C(s)+1/2O2(g)→CO(g)

(7) ZrB2(s)+5/2O2(g)→ZrO2(s)+B2O3(l,g)

(8) SiC(s)+3/2O2(g)→SiO2(s)+CO(g)

(9) SiO2(s)+B2O3(l)→SiO2–B2O3(l)

In the short fibre-reinforced specimen, some of the fibres pointing towards the surface were oxidised and acted as preferential channels for O2 diffusion, before getting filled by the silica melt (Fig. 10(d)).

From the surface analysis of the oxide scale (Figs. 10(e) and 10(f)), the outer layer was confirmed to be mainly constituted by zirconia grains embedded in a silica layer. This behaviour is typical of ZrB2/SiC-based composites and the presence of carbon flakes in the matrix does not seem to affect the composition of this layer, as the carbon layers lie below the ceramic matrix. On the other hand, the fibre-reinforced composite was characterised by the occasional presence of holes arising from the oxidation of the outer randomly distributed carbon fibres, that were later filled with the silica melt.

Finally, the oxidation resistance of Sample C was also investigated on the side surface (i.e., perpendicularly to the paper-derived graphite layers) in order to account for potential oxidation phenomena on all sides in the case of oxygen breakthrough (e.g., in the case of a crack formation). This side of the sample would normally not be exposed to oxidation under service conditions, but it is nonetheless important to assess the oxidation damage on all sides. The oxygen penetration depth on the side was in the range of 30–70 µm, with thicker oxide scale in correspondence of the graphite layers which behave similarly to the fibre-reinforced composite with the fibres pointing towards the surface. By comparing the cross-section of the exposed side surface of Sample C with that of the short fibre-reinforced composite (oxide thickness of 50–90 µm), the former still performed slightly better; this could be attributed to the thinner gaps left by the paper-derived graphite that were easier to fill with the borosilicate glass, thus hindering further oxidation more efficiently.

## 4 Conclusions

The addition of graphite through impregnation of filter paper has demonstrated to be a viable and sustainable method for introducing large amount of ordered graphite layers into bulk ZrB2–SiC ceramics, improving the fracture toughness of brittle ZrB2 ceramics to 5.5 MPa·m1/2 for compositions containing 30 vol% graphite. As far as thermo-mechanical properties are concerned, the bending strength and toughness of paper-derived graphite composites are comparable to that of short carbon fibre-reinforced composites. The oxidation behaviour is even slightly superior to short fibre-reinforced composites, with an oxide scale of only 44 μm after exposition at 1500 ℃ and without the presence of the porous, intermediate layer typical of long fibre-reinforced composites. These characteristics, in combination with other key properties as high thermal and electrical conductivities, superior oxidation resistance and fracture toughness make paper-derived graphite toughened UHTCs particularly attractive for more sustainable high temperature applications.

It must be recalled that carbon fibres, SiC fibres, and graphene nanosheets are expensive products and their sophisticated properties are somewhat degraded during the high temperature sintering treatments needed for densification of UHTCs. Thus, their use, compared to that of cheaper graphite-supplier should be carefully considered. The incorporation of paper offers several advantages: (1) achievement of a layered structure with oriented graphite using paper sheets as templates; (2) tuning of graphite content from 30 to 60 vol%. Furthermore, this study is a starting point for potential exploitation of secondary raw materials such as wastepaper, as toughening phases.
