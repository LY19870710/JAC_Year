## 1 Introduction

Thermal management materials serve as pivotal technologies driving advances in high-power electronics [1−3], aerospace systems [4,5], and energy conversion technologies [6−8]. Practical applications require these materials to meet conflicting requirements, such as high-temperature heat dissipation versus low-temperature insulation, structural integrity under mechanical stress, and stability across extreme thermal gradients [9]. An emerging trend in advanced thermal management materials is to integrate components with distinct thermal properties into composite systems, thereby addressing intelligent thermal management requirements [10]. Aluminum nitride (AlN) ceramics exhibit excellent room-temperature thermal conductivity, robust mechanical properties, and high stability, making them ideal thermal management materials [11]. However, the phonon-dominated heat transport in AlN has inherent limitations [12,13]. At elevated temperatures, the dominant scattering mechanism transitions to Umklapp processes (U-processes). In these specific phonon‒phonon scattering events, the resultant phonon wavevector exceeds the first Brillouin zone boundary, necessitating the involvement of a reciprocal lattice vector to conserve momentum. This process effectively reverses the direction of the phonon momentum, drastically reducing the mean free path and causing a characteristic decrease in the thermal conductivity with increasing temperature. The negative temperature dependence of the thermal conductivity, which is widespread in crystal ceramics, severely restricts the application of advanced ceramics in thermal management.

As is well established, pure metals such as Al and Cu exhibit decreasing thermal conductivity with increasing temperature [14,15], whereas alloys subjected to elemental doping may exhibit inverse behavior due to competing electron‒phonon transport mechanisms [16−18]. Similarly, does doping AlN with other metal nitrides result in alloy-type thermal transport behavior? Inspired by the pioneering concept of entropy stabilization in multicomponent systems, incorporating multiple metallic elements into base nitrides (e.g., AlN, TiN) to form entropy-stable multicomponent nitride solid solutions or high-entropy ceramics (HECs) has emerged as a promising approach for enhancing ceramic performance [19−22]. To date, extensive studies in HECs have shown that exceptional mechanical, thermal, and functional properties are attributable to severe lattice distortion, sluggish diffusion, and the cocktail effect [23,24]. Recent advances have demonstrated that chemical disorder in high-entropy ceramics intensifies phonon scattering, resulting in significantly lower thermal conductivity than that of constituent-based single compounds [25−29]. However, only a limited number of studies have reported thermal conductivity enhancement in high-entropy carbonitrides across extended temperature ranges, with achievable thermal conductivity (*κ*) increases remaining marginal and lacking in-depth discussion of the underlying thermal transport mechanisms [30]. In addition, research on multicomponent nitride ceramics has focused predominantly on thin films or powders due to sintering challenges [31−35], with few reports on bulk samples [36,37].

This study investigated the fabrication of dense multicomponent nitride bulk materials, eliminating the size limitations of thin films and powder materials, and the thermal transport properties of multicomponent nitride ceramics were studied at multiple scales. To explore strategies for regulating the temperature-dependent thermal conductivity of nitride ceramics, multiprincipal-element nitride systems with synergistic phonon and electron interactions were designed on the basis of highly conductive AlN. Transition metal elements (Ti, Zr, V, and Cr) were strategically incorporated according to their compatible nitride structures, diverse atomic radii, and complementary electronic properties, thereby enabling effective synergistic regulation of phononic and electronic contributions. A series of compositions with increasing complexity from binary (TiAlN) to ternary (TiZrAlN), quaternary (TiZrVAlN), and quaternary (TiZrVCrAlN) systems was synthesized to investigate the influence of high-entropy effects on the thermal transport behavior. For simplicity, these systems are referred to as M1AlN, M2AlN, M3AlN, and M4AlN, respectively, where “M” denotes transition metals and the numbers indicate the quantity of metal species. The introduction of multiple principal elements simultaneously suppresses phonon transport and enhances electronic thermal conduction through metallic nitride conductive networks. As a result of this phonon‒electron synergy, the thermal conductivity of multicomponent nitrides ultimately transitions from negative to positive temperature dependence.

### 2.1 Preparation

Commercial TiN, ZrN, V, CrN, and AlN powders (purity 99.9%, Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) were mixed in an equal molar ratio with respect to the metals and ground for 12 h at a rotational speed of 400 r·min−1 via a planetary ball mill. Si3N4 balls with a ball-to-material ratio of 20 : 1 were selected as the grinding media. Following thorough mixing, the powders were sintered under a nitrogen atmosphere by hot pressing at a heating rate of 20 °C·min−1, while pressure was applied from 1200 °C to 30 MPa at a rate of 5 MPa·min−1. After being held at 1800 °C and 30 MPa for 60 min, the samples were naturally cooled to room temperature. Finally, the graphite foil contamination on the surface of the prepared sample was removed completely by grinding.

### 2.2 Characterizations

After grinding and polishing, the phase composition of the nitride ceramics was characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Empyrean, Panalytical, the Netherlands) with a test range of 10°–90° and a scanning rate of 8 (°)·min−1. *In situ* XRD analysis was conducted via a high-resolution X-ray diffractometer (Empyrean, Panalytical, the Netherlands) across the temperature range of −60 to 600 °C within the same angular range, with a scanning rate of 5 (°)·min−1. The microstructure was observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; AMBER GMH, TESCAN Co., Ltd., China). Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) was subsequently employed to analyze the elemental distribution within the sample.

### 3.1 Composition and microstructural analysis of the synthesized nitrides

Employing a multiprincipal-element nitride compositional design strategy, a gradient system spanning binary (TiAlN) to pentary (TiZrVCrAlN) nitrides was fabricated to investigate the complex microstructural features and the associated evolution of the thermal conductivity temperature dependence in these ceramics. Equimolar mixtures of transition metal nitrides and AlN powder were consolidated through a standardized processing procedure involving ball milling and subsequent hot-press sintering, yielding fully dense bulk ceramics designated M1AlN (TiAlN), M2AlN, M3AlN, and M4AlN (TiZrVCrAlN). Phase evolution analyses conducted before and after sintering (Fig. 1 and Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) revealed that the structural transitions were governed by compositional complexity. While M1AlN is a dual-phase mixture of face-centered cubic (fcc, NaCl-type) and hexagonal close-packed (hcp) structures due to the limited mutual solubility in binary Ti–Al–N systems [38], the sequential introduction of Zr, V, and Cr progressively stabilizes fcc solid solutions (space group *Fm*3* m¯) in M2AlN, M3AlN, and M4AlN. In alignment with established principles of entropy-driven stabilization in multicomponent ceramics, increased elemental diversity enhances phase stability through configurational entropy maximization. The presence of five distinct (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) diffraction peaks in Fig. 1(a) further confirms the formation of fcc structures within M2AlN-M4AlN, despite their compositional deviation from the conventional high-entropy design criterion requiring five or more principal elements. Compared with those of M2AlN, the XRD peaks of M3AlN and M4AlN shift toward higher angles with the incorporation of V and Cr, which is attributed to lattice contraction induced by the addition of smaller-radius elements according to Bragg’s law. This was further confirmed by the lattice parameters of M2AlN, M3AlN, and M4AlN (Table S3 in the ESM) determined through Rietveld refinement of the XRD patterns. Furthermore, the segregation of V in M3AlN (Fig. S2(c) in the ESM) disrupts the long-range order of the crystal lattice, broadening the XRD peaks via diffuse scattering.

Solid solution formation among the metallic elements in the multicomponent nitrides was confirmed, as shown in Fig. 2 and Fig. S2 in the ESM. In contrast to the distinct secondary phases found in some high-entropy ceramics [39], although compositional fluctuations were detected in MAlN by EDS, spot analysis confirmed that all the measured phases contained all the constituent elements and varied only in their relative contents, as summarized in Fig. 3. Atomic radius disparities constitute a critical parameter governing solubility, as quantified in Table S1 in the ESM. In M1AlN, the minimal difference between the Ti (1.47 Å) and Al (1.43 Å) atomic radii permits significant Al diffusion during high-temperature sintering, thereby facilitating the nucleation of the thermodynamically stable hcp-AlN phase. The characteristic dual-phase microstructure comprising Ti-rich (α1) and Al-rich (β1) phases is distinctly revealed in the backscattered electron images (Fig. 2(a1), starred). The incorporation of larger Zr atoms (1.60 Å) partially counterbalances the lattice contraction tendencies, effectively expanding the solid-solution window to enable single-fcc-phase formation in M2AlN. However, substantial lattice constant mismatches persist among the constituent nitrides (ZrN (4.58 Å), TiN (4.24 Å), and fcc-AlN (4.04 Å)), ultimately preventing complete miscibility. Consequently, EDS mapping quantitatively verified tri-phase segregation in M2AlN (Fig. 3(b) and Fig. S2(b) in the ESM), with Ti-rich, Al-rich, and Zr-rich regions.

Vanadium doping induces transformative microstructural and compositional changes in M3AlN. First, pronounced grain coarsening occurs, with the average grain size increasing to approximately twice that of M2AlN (Fig. 3(a)). This kinetic phenomenon appears counterintuitive to the sluggish diffusion effect often expected in multicomponent systems [40]. The origin of this anomalous coarsening is attributed to the reduced atomic diffusion barriers during sintering and accelerated grain boundary migration, stemming from the distinct chemical bonding characteristics of the incorporated vanadium. First, the V‒N bond is more covalent and has a lower bond energy than the Ti‒N and Zr‒N bonds do, which is a consequence of the smaller electronegativity difference between V and N [41]. This reduced energy barrier facilitates easier atomic motion and grain boundary migration during the high-temperature sintering process, temporarily overriding the diffusion-slowing effect of configurational complexity. Second, EDS analysis reveals unique elemental partitioning: Within the α3 phase, the Ti and V concentrations approach equimolarity, whereas the Zr and Al contents remain minimal (3+: 0.64 Å, Ti4+: 0.61 Å) and identical NaCl-type crystal structures shared by VN and TiN (Table S1 in the ESM), promoting continuous solid-solution formation. The β3 phase has near-equimolar Ti/Zr/V ratios, suggesting that vanadium atoms function as structural “bridges” that may enhance the solubility between Al and transition metals. Consequently, grain growth in M3AlN is kinetically facilitated by V addition. The addition of Cr in M4AlN further elevates the configurational entropy (ΔS*conf = 1.61R vs. 1.39R in M3AlN), intensifying high-entropy stabilization effects. The increased compositional complexity reinforces the sluggish diffusion effect, which, combined with the similar and relatively small atomic radii of Cr and V that mitigate excessive lattice strain, effectively suppresses the excessive grain coarsening observed in M3AlN. These combined thermodynamic and steric effects drive fundamental phase reconstruction from the tri-phase architecture to the dual-phase architecture, leading to a more refined and stable microstructure in the quintinary system. EDS quantification confirmed this transition, revealing near-equimolar Ti/Zr/V/Cr distributions (atomic ratios of 1.39 : 1.15 : 1.11 : 1.21 refer to Table S2 in the ESM) within the dominant α4 phase (Figs. 2(e) and 3(b)), a signature characteristic of high-entropy solid-solution formation.

#### 3.2.1 Mechanical properties

Figures 4(a) and 4(b) present the bulk density and mechanical properties of the multicomponent nitride ceramics. All the systems achieved relative densities exceeding 98%, effectively eliminating significant porosity effects and making microstructure the dominant factor governing mechanical behavior. The bulk density of M4AlN reached 5.573 g·cm−3, closely approaching its theoretical density of 5.644 g·cm−3 (Table S3 in the ESM), demonstrating exceptionally high densification. In terms of mechanical properties, M1AlN, which features the finest grains (Fig. 3(a)) and an hcp–fcc dual-phase structure, exhibited the second-highest hardness of 25.39 GPa. However, potential incompatibilities at hcp–fcc interfaces may compromise long-term stability. Owing to the relatively fine-grain size and abundant grain boundaries introduced by the complex tri-phase structure, M2AlN exhibited the highest hardness of 26.03 GPa, demonstrating pronounced grain boundary strengthening. M3AlN exhibited the lowest hardness (24.59 GPa) among the systems, which is consistent with Hall‒Petch behavior due to extreme grain coarsening. Notably, M4AlN achieved a hardness (24.52 GPa) comparable to that of M3AlN despite similar grain dimensions, but through a fundamentally distinct mechanism, within its high-entropy α4 phase, severe lattice distortion from atomic size mismatches (Ti/Zr/V/Cr) generated potent solid-solution strengthening, which effectively counterbalanced coarsening-related hardness reduction, highlighting the high-entropy effect’s efficacy in preserving deformation resistance.

The elastic modulus values, reflecting the material stiffness, showed limited variation (413.08–426.92 GPa) among the prepared multicomponent nitrides (Fig. 4(b)). The maximum modulus M2AlN (426.92 GPa) likely originates from its dense triphase structure and high-modulus Zr incorporation. M4AlN exhibited a modulus of 420.69 GPa, which is comparable to that of M1AlN (418.69 GPa) and exceeds that of M3AlN (413.08 GPa), indicating robust atomic bonding in the high-entropy α4 phase and a positive lattice-distortion contribution to the stiffness. The synergistic interactions between multiple covalent bonds and elastic‒strain constraints within distorted lattices collectively sustain this elevated modulus. As a critical indicator of fracture resistance, the bending strength results demonstrate the unique superiority of M4AlN. M1AlN achieved a peak strength of 395.48 MPa, benefiting from crack initiation suppression by its fine-grained and dual-phase structure. Despite its superior hardness/modulus, M2AlN showed a markedly reduced strength of 266.42 MPa, revealing the vulnerability of its complex tri-phase architecture. Numerous phase boundaries may serve as preferential microcrack initiation sites, promoting premature failure under bending stress. Severe grain coarsening further diminished the bending strength of M3AlN to 210.96 MPa, underscoring the coarse grains’ universal weakening effect. Remarkably, M4AlN attained a flexural strength of 273.25 MPa, significantly surpassing that of M3AlN. This core advantage primarily stems from Cr addition and high-entropy α4 phase formation.

Building upon the prior characterization of the high-entropy α4 phase and Al-rich β4 phase in M4AlN (Fig. 3(b)), fracture surface analysis revealed critical toughening mechanisms. The SEM images in Figs. 4(c) and 4(d) clearly reveal a “rock-candy” fracture morphology corresponding to cleavage failure in the brittle Al-rich phase alongside “lamellar structures”, reflecting plastic deformation characteristics within the ductile high-entropy α4 phase. This morphological contrast reveals key toughening pathways: (i) Crack deflection: When propagating cracks in the brittle Al-rich phase encounter the ductile high-entropy domains, they undergo deflection or induce plastic deformation within the high-entropy α4 phase, forming energy-absorbing tear ridges (manifested as lamellar structures). This process substantially dissipates the fracture energy. (ii) Ligament bridging: The plastically deformable high-entropy α4 phase may form an incompletely fractured ligament behind crack tips, bridging crack surfaces and reducing the stress concentration at critical regions. (iii) Interfacial barrier effect: Well-defined interfaces between the α4 and β4 phases in M4AlN act as potent obstacles, effectively hindering linear crack propagation and extending crack paths and further enhancing energy dissipation.

M4AlN achieves synergistic performance optimization, where severe lattice distortion from multiple principal elements not only enhances hardness, as previously established, but also enables microstructural refinement. Compared with M3AlN, the Cr addition and high-entropy α4 phase formation partially suppressed excessive grain coarsening during sintering. The Al-rich/high-entropy dual-phase architecture simultaneously enhances toughening and crack resistance while maintaining a high elastic modulus. Compared with multiphase mixtures, the dual-phase structure significantly demonstrates superior interfacial features in M2AlN/M3AlN. Therefore, through innovative high-entropy design and optimized dual-phase architecture, M4AlN successfully maintains high hardness and stiffness while dramatically improving fracture resistance, demonstrating substantial potential as a novel high-performance structural ceramic.

#### 3.2.2 Electrical conductivity and thermal transport at room temperature

As mentioned before, the high-entropy α4 phase in M4AlN contributes to its superior mechanical properties. The room-temperature electrical and thermal conductivities of the synthesized nitrides were subsequently investigated, and the results are presented in Fig. 5, to elucidate the influence of high-entropy effects on the electrothermal transport properties. The electrical conductivity increases with increasing metallic element content: It increases from 0.40×105 S·m−1 in Ti-doped M1AlN to 0.95×105 S·m−1 in Ti/Zr-doped M2AlN, then dramatically increases to 2.85×105 S·m−1 in Ti/Zr/V-doped M3AlN, and ultimately reaches 4.69×105 S·m−1 in Ti/Zr/V/Cr-doped M4AlN, representing a nearly 12-fold increase over M1AlN. It can be inferred that the introduction of transition metal elements such as Ti, Zr, V, and Cr contributes a large number of free-electron carriers, and the formation of a high-entropy α4 phase in M4AlN further enhances the carrier mobility and achieves high metal-like conductivity properties, probably by reducing the scattering of electrons by lattice defects. Notably, vanadium incorporation delivers exceptional conductivity enhancement (M2AlN → M3AlN). The valence electron configuration of V (3d34s2) features partially filled 3d orbitals that enable efficient electron donation, while its multivalent nature facilitates flexible electron transfer. The unique electronic structure of V optimizes the conduction pathways within M3AlN and M4AlN.

Analysis of the thermal transport mechanisms confirms that this reduction is governed primarily by suppressed phonon thermal conductivity (Fig. 5(b)). where * Cv denotes the phonon heat capacity, ν* is the average phonon group velocity, *l* is the phonon mean free path, and *τ*C is the phonon relaxation time. Phonon thermal conduction is collectively modulated by intrinsic phonon‒phonon interactions along with extrinsic scattering mechanisms involving electrons, grain boundaries, and crystalline defects.

Among the gradient multicomponent nitride systems designed in this study, M4AlN has a significantly lower phonon thermal conductivity than all the other systems, despite containing only two phases and having a relatively large grain size (considerably larger than the phonon mean free path *l*). This conclusively demonstrates that chemical disorder within the high-entropy phase serves as the predominant factor that suppresses phonon transport, overwhelmingly surpassing traditional scattering mechanisms such as grain/phase boundaries. The extreme atomic mass contrast (e.g., light elements Al(27)/N(14) coexist with heavy Zr(91)), combined with severe atomic size mismatch in the highly disordered lattice, creates substantial mass fluctuations and lattice distortions that induce strong broadband phonon scattering. Furthermore, heterogeneous distributions of bond strengths and lengths among multiple metal‒nitrogen pairs perturb interatomic force constants, intensifying phonon‒point defect interactions. The unique combination of high electrical conductivity, such as metals, and low thermal conductivity, such as ceramics, exhibited by M4AlN reveals the regulation of lattice thermal transport through chemical disorder in high-entropy ceramics and opens new avenues for thermal management under extreme conditions, such as high-temperature thermoelectric materials and thermally conductive bonding layers for thermal barrier coatings. The transport properties of M4AlN at different temperatures are further discussed below.

### 3.3 Temperature-dependent thermal conductivity of multicomponent nitrides

Multicomponent nitrides exhibit thermal transport behavior distinct from that of monocomponent ceramics due to coupled electron‒phonon contributions, as revealed by room-temperature electrical and thermal transport characterization. To further investigate this distinct behavior, the evolution of thermal transport from TiAlN (M1AlN) to TiZrVCrAlN (M4AlN) was investigated across a wide temperature range (−60–900 °C). As shown in Fig. 6(b), the heat capacity of all the systems increases with temperature, which is consistent with the fundamental heat capacity behavior of solids. The lattice molar heat capacity (*C**v*), whose magnitude is directly governed by the phonon density of states (*g*(*ω*)) distribution across the entire frequency range (Eq. (7), where *k*B is the Boltzmann constant, *ħ *is the Planck constant, and *ω* is the phonon angular frequency), reflects the vibrational energy storage capacity of the lattice.

*
Fig. 6Elevated-temperature (a) thermal conductivity and (b) heat capacity of prepared multicomponent nitrides.

(7) Cv=kB∫[(ℏωkBT)2⋅exp⁡(ℏωkBT)/(exp⁡(ℏωkBT)−1)2]⋅g(ω)dω

According to the Einstein model and Debye model, low-frequency acoustic phonons dominate thermal excitation at cryogenic temperatures, storing energy and inducing the characteristic Debye T3 dependence of the lattice heat capacity. As the temperature increases toward the Dulong–Petit regime, near-universal phonon mode activation drives the heat capacity toward the classical 3R limit. The c**p*−*T* relationships in multicomponent nitrides deviate from these fundamental models due to structural complexity. M1AlN and M3AlN exhibit pronounced d*c**p*/d*T* increases near 300 °C (Fig. 6(b)), suggesting enhanced energy storage capability. For M1AlN, atomic vibrations along the *c*-axis in the hcp phase are confined at low temperature, resulting in the “freezing” of low-frequency optical and acoustic phonon branches associated with *c*-axis vibrations in the phonon spectrum [42]. As the temperature increases, the energy barrier for *c*-axis vibrations is overcome. The corresponding phonon frequencies (*ω*) soften due to anharmonic effects, redistributing the phonon density of states *g*(*ω*) and causing anomalous heat capacity. In M3AlN, V doping induces severe lattice shrinkage and distortion. Bond length/strength fluctuations exacerbate phonon softening, whereas compositional heterogeneity in its three-phase segregated structure amplifies anharmonicity, collectively accelerating heat capacity growth. Conversely, M2AlN and M4AlN demonstrate attenuated *c**p*–*T* increases. The superior hardness and modulus (Fig. 4(b)) of M2AlN indicate stronger average bonding, higher characteristic phonon frequencies, and fewer thermally accessible modes. Moreover, the biphasic structure of M4AlN promotes ordered phonon transport, with the high-entropy α4 phase suppressing local distortion through configurational entropy stabilization. Furthermore, Cr addition further counteracts V-induced bond weakening, constraining high-temperature *c**p*–*T* elevation.

Unlike heat capacity, thermal conductivity undergoes a transformative shift from “negative temperature dependence” to “positive temperature dependence” as metallic element diversity increases and a high-entropy phase forms (Fig. 6(a)). This results in three characteristic thermal conductivity‒temperature patterns across the four systems. M1AlN follows classical phonon thermal transport behavior, exhibiting a linear decline in thermal conductivity with increasing temperature from 48.90 W·m−1·K−1 at −60 °C to 39.13 W·m−1·K−1 at 900 °C (*κ*total = 48.49 − 0.01*T*). The high initial thermal conductivity originates from effective phonon transport channels enabled by their dual-phase structure (hcp + fcc) and fine-grain morphology. However, enhanced Umklapp scattering at elevated temperatures reduces the number of phonon mean free paths, consequently diminishing the thermal conductivity. Conversely, M2AlN and M4AlN demonstrate anomalous positive temperature dependences. Their thermal conductivities increase from 18.80 to 28.17 W·m−1·K−1 (*κ*total = 19.94 + 0.01*T*) and from 8.65 to 18.34 W·m−1·K−1 (*κ*total = 9.72 + 0.01*T*), respectively. Combined with prior analyses of electron/phonon contributions, these findings indicate that metallic nitrides (such as ZrN) form conductive networks where elevated carrier concentrations at high temperatures increase the electronic thermal conductivity component, partially compensating for phonon scattering losses. Notably, M4AlN achieves the lowest thermal conductivity due to severe lattice distortion within its high-entropy α4 phase. M3AlN exhibits distinctive nonlinear behavior characterized by an overall increasing thermal conductivity trend but features an abrupt increase to 33.85 W·m−1·K−1 at 900 °C, with a single-point increase of up to 55%, which is far beyond the linear extrapolation prediction. Considering the localized segregation of elements within its tri-phase structure and the maximum grain size, it is speculated that grain boundary migration or localized ordering transition may have occurred near 900 °C, which reduces the intensity of phonon scattering and leads to a jump in thermal conductivity.

In view of the outstanding comprehensive performance of M4AlN, the temperature dependence of its electrical conductivity (*σ*) was investigated. The results indicate that the electrical conductivity of M4AlN decreases from 4.89×105 to 4.03×105 S·m−1 in the range from −60 to −300 °C. The high *σ* value and negative d*σ*/d*T* are consistent with metallic conductor behavior, suggesting that the transport of charge and heat is dominated primarily by the interaction of degenerate electrons with phonons and defects. Therefore, the electronic and phononic thermal conductivity contributions at various temperatures were separated preliminarily via the WF law (Eq. (5)), employing the Sommerfeld value of the Lorenz number (*L*0 = 2.44×10−8 W·Ω·K−2). As shown in Fig. 7(a), *κ*total increased from 8.65 W·m−1·K−1 at −60 °C to 12.68 W·m−1·K−1 at 300 °C. Specifically, *κ*e rose linearly from 2.55 to 5.66 W·m−1·K−1, whereas *κ**p* exhibited a modest increase from 6.09 to 7.02 W·m−1·K−1. These results challenge the conventional understanding of phonon-dominated thermal transport in typical ceramics (e.g., single-phase AlN), demonstrating that heat conduction in M4AlN is governed by both phonon and electron contributions. The linear growth in *κ*e originates from the coupling between the temperature dependence of the electrical conductivity and the WF law. Particular attention is given here to the anomalous elevation in *κ*p at high temperatures. The temperature dependence of the phononic thermal conductivity is governed by the heat capacity and phonon relaxation time (*τ*C) according to Eq. (8). Considering scattering mechanisms involving phonon‒phonon collisions and defect‒grain boundary interactions, the total relaxation time represents a harmonic average over all scattering processes, expressed as Eq. (8):

(8) τC−1=τU−1+τB−1+τD−1+τR−1

The last four terms represent relaxation times for Umklapp scattering (U), grain boundary scattering (B), point defect scattering (D), and resonant atomic scattering (R), respectively.

Since phonon‒phonon scattering adheres to the conservation of both energy and quasimomentum, when postcollision wavevectors exceed the first Brillouin zone boundary, Umklapp scattering involving reciprocal lattice vector compensation occurs. The U-process alters the phonon group velocity distribution and disrupts the net momentum transport along the heat flow direction, thereby becoming the primary factor reducing the lattice thermal conductivity. At high temperatures, increased high-momentum phonon populations increase the Umklapp probability, explaining the typical decrease in the thermal conductivity of phonon-dominated ceramics such as M1AlN. In M4AlN, the profound lattice distortion and mass fluctuation scatter phonons so effectively that the mean free path for a large portion of the phonon spectrum is reduced to a minimum, which is governed primarily by intense compositional disorder scattering. This effect saturates the scattering strength for these phonons, making their mean free path remarkably temperature insensitive. Consequently, the additional scattering rate from Umklapp processes, which normally increases sharply with temperature and causes *κp* to decrease, is significantly diminished. First-principles phonon spectrum calculations provide further microscopic insight into the proposed saturation of Umklapp scattering and will be carried out in future theoretical work. In this study, the experimentally observed increase in the lattice heat capacity with temperature becomes the dominant factor controlling the temperature dependence of *κp*, a behavior analogous to that observed in amorphous materials. To further investigate this phenomenon, *in situ* XRD was performed on M4AlN from −60 to 600 °C (Fig. 7(b)). The results confirmed that M4AlN maintained the fcc structure without transitions throughout this temperature range, ruling out abrupt changes in scattering mechanisms due to structural transformations. Additionally, a high relative density eliminated porosity effects, whereas a high hardness and modulus indicated a strongly bonded structure, ensuring stable thermal transport.

The synthesized M2AlN, M3AlN, and M4AlN systems exhibit relative thermal conductivity increases (Δ*κ*/*κ*low, where Δ*κ* is the absolute increment, and *κ*low denotes the baseline conductivity at low temperature) of 50%, 140% and 112%, respectively, across a temperature gradient (Δ*T*) of nearly 1000 °C. The thermal transport properties of MAlN present a compelling alternative to conventional smart thermal materials such as phase-change materials (PCMs). As illustrated in Fig. 8, these multicomponent nitrides demonstrate emerging application potential compared with reported PCMs, high-entropy materials, and some MAX phases. PCMs such as Ni–Mn–In compounds (martensitic transformation) and VO2 (metal-insulator transition (MIT)) exhibit abrupt changes in thermal conductivity [43−45]. While the conductivity of Ag/NiFeS increases by more than 150% through the MIT, its narrow transition window (46]. High-entropy materials (e.g., AlVNb alloys and high-entropy carbides/nitrides) achieve gradual conductivity increases of ~50% (Δ*T* = 400–500 °C), yet their limited enhancement magnitude and high-temperature softening remain challenges [18, 30]. MAX phases show minimal temperature sensitivity; for example, the optimized high-entropy MAX phase (TiVNb)2AlC only slightly increases from 12.04 to 17.53 W·m−1·K−1 between room temperature and 1200 °C [47]. Notably, amorphous SiO2 exhibits near-linear thermal conductivity growth above room temperature due to its disordered atomic structure, which minimizes the phonon mean free path and renders heat capacity the dominant factor [48,49]. In stark contrast, M4AlN achieves a large and linear 112% increase in *κ* over a nearly 1000 °C range. This “gradient” adaptive behavior is devoid of abrupt transitions and offers predictable and stable performance, which is crucial for applications in environments with wide and continuous temperature fluctuations.

In this study, the progression from M1AlN to M4AlN transforms the thermal conductivity behavior from a linear reduction to a linear increase. In M4AlN, high-entropy-induced lattice distortion suppresses phonon thermal transport, whereas the metallic nitride network facilitates electron conduction that compensates for phonon scattering losses, enabling stable linear conductivity growth with temperature. Moreover, M4AlN achieves a 112% increase in thermal conductivity approaching the PCM level but avoids the abrupt transition risks inherent in PCMs while spanning a 1000 °C range that exceeds that of high-entropy materials reported previously. As a result, it retains the progressive thermal conductivity mechanism characteristic of amorphous solids while maintaining robust mechanical strength, achieving “amorphous-like” thermal transport characteristics within a crystalline framework. The adaptive thermal conductivity offers a novel thermal management strategy, exemplified by its function as a thermal buffering layer in power devices where rising chip temperatures autonomously trigger conductivity enhancement to mitigate localized hotspots, or an extreme-environment component that resolves the “low-temperature thermal insulation vs. high-temperature heat dissipation” paradox. Long-term development includes integrating M4AlN powder into thermal grease or constructing M4AlN thin-film composites with high-thermal-conductivity materials (e.g., AlN) as structural amplifiers to increase the overall thermal management efficiency. By strategically tailoring the high-entropy phase composition to suppress phonon scattering, multicomponent nitrides enhance the temperature adaptability, establishing a new paradigm for designing high-temperature-adaptive thermal management materials.

## 4 Conclusions

This work reports a regulatory approach for regulating the temperature dependence of the thermal conductivity of ceramics. Through the design and fabrication of multicomponent nitride MAlN (M = Ti, Zr, V, and Cr) systems with gradient compositions, an anomalous positive temperature dependence of the thermal conductivity is achieved. Progressive compositional evolution from binary M1AlN (TiAlN) to quinary M4AlN (TiZrVCrAlN) drives microstructural variations from dual-phase to tri-phase and back to dual-phase configurations, and the thermal conductivity transitions from a conventional negative temperature dependence to a positive temperature dependence. Remarkably, M4AlN demonstrates a linear increase in *κ* from 8.65 W·m−1·K−1 at −60 °C to 18.34 W·m−1·K−1 at 900 °C, which is fundamentally distinct from monocomponent crystalline ceramics such as AlN. More significantly, the linearly increasing *κ* over a wide temperature range provides a key advantage for intelligent thermal management over phase-change materials since their sharp thermal conductivity transitions can induce thermal stress damage. The anomalous behavior originates from dual transport mechanisms: Severe lattice distortion within the high-entropy phase substantially suppresses phonon conduction through disordered scattering, while concurrently formed metallic nitride networks increase the electronic thermal conductivity to compensate for phonon transport losses. The resulting electron‒phonon synergistic heat transport effectively inverts the conventional negative temperature dependence observed in binary M1AlN. Critically, M4AlN maintains a robust mechanical integrity of 24.5 GPa hardness, a 420 GPa elastic modulus, and 273 MPa bending strength through its engineered Al-rich/high-entropy dual-phase microstructure. By strategically leveraging entropy-mediated lattice distortion alongside electron‒phonon synergy, multicomponent nitrides establish autonomous thermal conductivity enhancement triggered by increasing the operational temperature while preserving structural stability, providing a new approach for intelligent thermal management in extreme temperature variation environments.
