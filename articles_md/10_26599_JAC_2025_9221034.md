## 1 Introduction

Piezoelectric perovskite materials exhibit an electrical response to mechanical inputs and mechanical deformation when subjected to an external electric field. These materials can be easily polarized under an electric field, resulting in a significant electromechanical response, making them highly valuable in various applications, such as capacitors, actuators, sensors, micro-electro−mechanical system (MEMS) devices, and other electronics [1−8]. The electric-field-induced strain is crucial for the realization of piezoelectric actuators, particularly in lead-based morphotropic phase boundary (MPB) ceramics and single-crystal relaxor perovskites [7−11]. The origin of field-induced strain in oxides can be intrinsic, such as the piezoelectric shift of ions within the crystal lattice, or extrinsic, resulting from ferroelectric domain switching, structural phase transitions, or the influence of point defects [8−12]. Typically, the principal elements responsible for field-induced strain in ferroelectrics are field-driven phase transitions, domain switching, and the presence of point defects. For example, a large electric field-induced strain of 1.3% was achieved in a lead-containing BiFeO3–PbTiO3–LaFeO3 mixture under an electric field of 80 kV/cm [8]. However, the toxicity of lead has raised significant health and environmental concerns, leading to strict regulations [7−10]. Consequently, developing competitive lead-free alternatives is essential for potential device applications [13−18].

Bi1/2Na1/2TiO3 (BNT) has been extensively studied as a lead-free substitute because of its structural flexibility and promising piezoelectric and ferroelectric properties [12,14,17−20]. However, BNT materials often exhibit lower mechanical-to-electrical energy conversion efficiency and require a large applied electric field because of their lower electric-field-induced strain [18,19]. To overcome these limitations, various approaches have been explored, including the preparation of solid solutions and composites with other oxides to enhance the piezoresponse and understand the underlying mechanisms [12,14,20−23]. For example, large electrostrains have been achieved in BNT-based perovskites due to stresses at grain boundaries, structural phase transitions between ergodic relaxor and ferroelectric phases, and polar nanoregions, in addition to point defects [14,17,20−23].

In addition to bulk ceramics, BNT-based thin films are also fabricated for faster conversion of electrical to mechanical energy at low applied fields, making them favorable for integration. The inverse piezoelectric coefficient (* d33,f∗) of BNT-based polycrystalline films has been significantly enhanced, ranging from 56 to 120 pm/V, due to structural phase transitions from ferroelectric to relaxor phases [24−27]. For example, 1 mol% BiMnO3-modified BNT thin films exhibited significant enhancements in piezoelectric (maximum strain/maximum applied electric field (S*max/*E*max) = 100.4 pm/V) and ferroelectric (*P*max = 48.0 μC/cm2) responses [26]. Recently, a (1−*x*)(0.94Bi1/2Na1/2TiO3–0.06BaTiO3)–*x*(Sr0.7Bi0.2)TiO3 (BNT–BT–*x*SBT) thin film with a preferred (110) orientation demonstrated a substantially increased inverse piezoelectric coefficient (* d33,f∗) of 170 pm/V, indicating a significant improvement over that of nonoriented films [27]. However, these lead-free alternatives still fall short compared with lead-based ceramics and thin films in terms of piezoelectric coefficients, limiting their use as piezoelectric actuators.

BaAlO3 (BA) perovskite, with rhombohedral symmetry, exhibits a large spontaneous polarization (~76 µC/cm2) and a high Curie temperature (~527 °C), as revealed by theoretical calculations [28]. However, its poor thermal stability and extreme fabrication conditions hinder its practical use in industrial applications [28]. To address this, BA is stabilized with BNT to form (1−x*)BNT–*x*BA ceramics, where the strain response is significantly enhanced because of a phase transition from rhombohedral to pseudocubic symmetry near the phase boundary composition 0.95BNT–0.05BA [29]. Nevertheless, the origin of the high strain in BNT–BA ceramics remains unclear, and electric-field-induced strain in polycrystalline BNT–BA oxides has rarely been explored, with further improvements in piezoelectric coefficients being highly desirable for actuator design [29].

In this study, we demonstrated a recoverable electric-field-induced high electromechanical response in perovskite BNT–BA oxides. The fabrication and characterization processes were divided into two steps to investigate the possible origin of the high electric-field-induced strain response in the BNT–BA samples. First, we prepared ~500-nm-thick pure BNT and 0.95BNT–0.05BA (BNT–BA) polycrystalline films on Pt(111)/TiO2/SiO2/Si(100) substrates via the sol‒gel technique. The structural, microstructural, ferroelectric, and piezoelectric properties of both the BNT and 0.95BNT–0.05BA thin films were analyzed to assess the effects of BA substitution on the BNT thin films. Intriguingly, local and macroscopic piezoelectric results revealed a recoverable high electric-field-induced strain. The BNT–BA films exhibited a high electric field-induced strain (*S*max/*E*max) of 203 pm/V at a driving electric field of 200 kV/cm, which was greater than that of BNT (*S*max/*E*max = 72 pm/V). To gain further insight into the high strain in BNT–BA oxides, we prepared 0.95BNT–0.05BA (BNT–BA) ceramic samples and conducted electric field-dependent X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements [29]. The* in situ* XRD results indicated a recoverable electric-field-induced phase transition, attributed to the structural transformation from a mixed cubic and rhombohedral phase at *E* = 0 to a dominant rhombohedral structure at a high electric field (*E* = 45 kV/cm) and then back to the mixed phase at zero electric fields. As electrostrain is an effective parameter for MEMS devices in piezoelectric thin films, this report focuses on the strain response of synthesized BNT–BA thin films.

## 2 Experimental

To prepare the BNT–BA solutions via the sol‒gel technique, a precursor solution was synthesized by first dissolving an appropriate amount of bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (Bi(NO3)3·5H2O; 99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), sodium acetate (CH3COONa; 99%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and aluminum-nitrate nonahydrate (AlN3O9·9H2O; 98%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in a mixture of acetic acid and 2-methoxyethanol at 40 °C in a nitrogen atmosphere. Acetylacetone was added to titanium butoxide (Ti(OC4H9)4; 97%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to form a stable solution. A stoichiometric amount of the titanium butoxide solution was subsequently added to the nitrate and acetate solutions at room temperature. The mixture was stirred constantly until a transparent and stable yellow precursor solution was achieved with a molar concentration of 0.4 M. To fabricate BNT-based films, the precursor solutions were spin-coated onto Pt(111)/TiO2/SiO2/Si(100) substrates at a spin-coater (SPIN-1200D, MIDAS system, Republic of Korea) speed of 3000 r/min for 20 s. After each coating step, the wet film layer was dried in air at 150 °C for 5 min on a hotplate (HP-20D, DAIHAN, Republic of Korea). The dried gel film was subsequently calcined at 400 °C for 5 min and then heated at 550 °C for 5 min in an oxygen atmosphere in a tube furnace (LABSYS RTP-1200, NEXTRON, Republic of Korea). These steps were repeated several times to obtain the desired thickness (500 nm) of the film. Finally, the film was crystallized at an annealing temperature of 700 °C for 30 min in ambient oxygen.

The solid-state reaction method was used to prepare the BNT–BA ceramic pellets. The starting precursor materials were Bi2O3, Na2CO3, TiO2, and Al2O3 (99.9%, High Purity Chemicals, Japan), which were mixed according to the experimental steps reported in our previous work [29]. This mixture was ball-milled with zirconia balls or ethanol at a volume ratio of 1 : 2 : 3. The successive blends/mixtures were subjected to continuous ball milling at 150 revolutions per minute for 24 h. Following this milling step, the powders were dried at 100 °C in an oven (J-300M, JISICO, Republic of Korea) for 24 h and then briefly ground for 10 min. The crushed powders were subsequently subjected to calcination at 800 °C for 2 h. After calcination, the powders were again ball-milled and dried under the same conditions. We added a 5% solution of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) as a binder, mixed it with the powders, and carried out additional grinding. The resulting PVA-added powders were pelletized into ceramic disks at a pressure of 50 MPa. These targets were subsequently sintered at 1170 °C for 2 h and further utilized for electric-field-induced structural measurements.

The structural properties of the pure and BNT–BA films were investigated via an XRD (X’Pert-PRO MRD, Philips/Panalytical, the Netherlands; Busan Center, Korea Basic Science Institute (KBSI)). The microstructures of the films were investigated via a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; JSM-6500F, JEOL, Japan). For the measurement of electrical properties, platinum top electrodes were deposited on the surface of the films via DC sputtering with an area of 2.5×10−4 cm2 via a metal mask. The ferroelectric properties of the films were measured via a ferroelectric tester (PRECISON LC, RADIANT Technologies, USA). The ferroelectric hysteresis loops of both the pure BNT and BNT–BA films were obtained at a measurement frequency of 1 kHz. Large-signal piezoelectric properties were evaluated from the electric-field-induced displacement of the films via a single-beam laser doppler vibrometer (AT500-05, Graphtec, Japan) at room temperature. The spot size, measurement frequency, and wavelength of the laser beam used were 20 μm, 100 Hz, and 623.8 nm, respectively. A bipolar or unipolar sine wave with a 10 ms period drove the piezoelectric capacitor, and the displacement above the top electrode was recorded by using an oscilloscope (TDS3032B, Tektronix, USA). An *in situ* electric-field-dependent structural transition was characterized via a powder X-ray diffractometer (X’Pert-PRO MRD, Philips/Panalytical, the Netherlands; Busan Center, Korea Basic Science Institute (KBSI)) with a high-voltage amplifier (TREK 610E, Advanced Energy, USA) [30,31].

## 3 Results and discussion

To determine the crystal structure of the pure BNT and 0.95BNT–0.05BA thin films, XRD analysis was conducted in the 2*θ* range of 20° to 80°. XRD *θ*–2*θ* scans of both polycrystalline thin films were performed at a grazing incidence angle of 0.45° to suppress the intense XRD signal from the Pt(111)/TiO2/SiO2/Si(100) substrate. As shown in Fig. 1(a), XRD patterns of both films exhibited diffraction peaks characteristic of a single perovskite phase. The absence of secondary peaks in the BNT–BA film indicated the complete incorporation of BA into the BNT lattice. A slight shift of the (110) Bragg peak to a higher angle from 32.60° to 32.65° in the BNT–BA film suggested a reduction in the lattice parameters, which was likely attributed to the smaller ionic radius of Al3+ (54 pm) than that of Ti4+ (60.5 pm) [32].

The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the as-grown BNT and BNT–BA films on the Pt(111)/TiO2/SiO2/Si(100) substrates were examined via FE-SEM (Figs. 1(b) and 1(c)). Both films exhibited similar thicknesses of approximately 500 nm. In the SEM image of the pure BNT film, the surface displayed noticeable cracks, which was further corroborated by the cross-sectional image shown in Fig. 1(c). Additionally, we acquired a more detailed high-resolution surface SEM image of the BNT, revealing microlevel cracks without clearly defined grains or grain boundaries, as illustrated in Fig. S1(a) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). In contrast, the BA-substituted BNT film exhibited a more uniform and crack-free surface morphology (Fig. 1(b)). Cross-sectional images of BNT–BA revealed dense, crack-free structures, underscoring the successful fabrication of the BNT–BA film. Enlarged SEM topographical images revealed well-defined grains and grain boundaries on the surface of the BNT–BA film, with an average grain size of approximately 100–120 nm, as represented in Fig. S1(b) in the ESM. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) topographical images confirmed a smoother surface and larger grain size of ~100–120 nm for the BNT–BA film than for the BNT film, which was ~80–90 nm in size, as shown in Fig. S1 in the ESM.

To investigate the impact of BA substitution on ferroelectric polarization, polarization–electric field (P*–*E*) hysteresis loops were measured under an applied field of 500 kV/cm (Fig. 2(a)). The BNT film exhibited remnant (*P*r) and maximum (*P*max) polarizations of 11.8 and 35.1 μC/cm2, respectively, which are consistent with previous reports [33]. Notably, the BNT–BA film displayed a significant increase in *P*r of 15.8 μC/cm2 and *P*max of 49.5 μC/cm2. Furthermore, the difference between *P*max and *P*r (*P*max − *P*r) was greater for the BNT–BA film than for the BNT film. This difference represents the disparity in ferroelectric polarization states between the absence and presence of an applied electric field. Consequently, the larger *P*max − *P*r value for the BNT–BA sample indicates a more pronounced change in polarization states under applied and nonapplied electric field conditions than that observed in the pure BNT sample. Both films showed comparable coercive fields (*E*c ≈ 100 kV/cm). The *P*–*E* hysteresis loops of both the BNT and BNT–BA films at various applied fields were obtained, as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. *P*r of the BNT and BNT–BA films are plotted as a function of the applied electric field in Fig. 2(b). As the applied field increases, the remnant polarization of the corresponding films increases. Interestingly, the BNT–BA film endured up to 1200 kV/cm, in contrast to the BNT film, which experienced an electrical breakdown above 700 kV/cm, indicating its high dielectric strength. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM, both films exhibited a steady increase in *P*max as the field increased. In contrast, the BNT–BA film exhibited polarization hysteresis loops even after the breakdown field strength of the BNT film increased, suggesting the high breakdown strength of the BNT–BA film even at high applied electric fields. The increase in breakdown strength observed in the BNT–BA film can be attributed to a reduction in surface cracks. Typically, BNT films demonstrate a low dielectric breakdown strength due to the high conductivity resulting from the volatility of Bi and alkali-based elements during high-temperature crystallization [34−36], which leads to crack formation. However, with the substitution of BA for BNT, the majority of these cracks are significantly reduced, resulting in well-defined grains and grain boundaries on the BNT–BA film surface (Figs. S1(b) and S1(d) in the ESM). In other words, the overheating and thermal stress associated with the presence of cracks in the BNT film at elevated electric fields were mitigated through BA substitution. This ultimately contributes to a higher electrical breakdown strength than that of the pure BNT film.

On the basis of the electric field-dependent *P*–*E* hysteresis loops, recoverable energy storage density (*W*rec) values were obtained for both films, as shown in Fig. 2(c) [37]. Both films showed comparable recoverable energy storage densities up to an applied field of 300 kV/cm. However, beyond this threshold, the BNT–BA film showed moderately higher recoverable energy storage densities than pure BNT. The increase in the recoverable energy storage density values of the BNT–BA films emerged from the large difference in *P*max − *P*r values compared with those of pure BNT and could be attributed to the more distinct evolution in polarization states under applied and nonapplied electric field conditions. The recoverable energy storage densities of pure BNT were constrained by electrical breakdown, which occurred at an applied electric field of 700 kV/cm. This limitation may be associated with the presence of cracks identified via SEM analysis. Interestingly, *W*rec of BNT–BA film substantially increases beyond the breakdown field strength of BNT (700 kV/cm), persisting up to the maximum electric field of 1200 kV/cm. This enhancement can be attributed to the crack-free surface morphology of the BNT–BA film. Consequently, the substitution of BA in the BNT film extended the electric field endurance range from 700 to 1200 kV/cm, allowing for greater recoverable energy storage density at higher electric fields.

To examine the electric-field-induced piezoresponse and evaluate its potential for actuator applications, bipolar and unipolar strain measurements were performed on BNT and BNT–BA films via a modified laser vibrometer system, as illustrated in the schematic in Fig. 3(a). An electric field was applied between the top and bottom Pt electrodes while the laser light was focused on the top Pt electrode. The resulting strain-induced vibrations were detected via a laser vibrometer, as shown in Fig. 3(a). The captured vibrational signals of the BNT and BNT–BA films were converted into bipolar and unipolar strain hysteresis loops, represented by blue squares and yellow circles in Figs. 3(b) and 3(c), respectively.

The BNT and BNT–BA films exhibited bipolar S‒E* loops with butterfly-shaped hysteresis curves, as illustrated in Fig. 3(b). The negative strain observed in the bipolar hysteresis loops of both films indicated the characteristics of the perovskite ferroelectric materials [29]. Intriguingly, the BNT–BA film displayed a maximum bipolar strain amplitude three times greater than that of the BNT film. To validate the strain response obtained under bipolar strain, we subjected both films to unipolar loading under the same applied electric field. The BNT film produced a slim unipolar hysteresis loop, yielding a maximum strain of approximately 0.25% with a corresponding normalized strain of *S*max/*E*max = 72 pm/V (Fig. 3(c)). Conversely, the *S‒E* curve of the BNT–BA film exhibited a distinct two-stage response when subjected to an increasing electric field. Initially, the film responded similarly to the BNT film at low fields (≤ 75 kV/cm). However, in the second stage, when the applied field surpassed the poling field (≥ 75 kV/cm), the strain response linearly increased, ultimately reaching a significant increase of approximately 0.35%. Thus, it can be inferred that the unipolar strain curve of the BNT–BA film shows three different slopes during the loading and unloading of the applied field marked by the black dashed lines. The strain level of the BNT–BA film consistently increased to 75 kV/cm, which was identical to that of the BNT and represented by a line (slope-1). Beyond this field (*E* = 75 kV/cm), the strain response of the BNT–BA film abruptly increased to a maximum level, surpassing the strain in the BNT film, represented by slope-2 (*E* = 200 kV/cm), and then returned to the initial position with a slight alteration in the corresponding loop of the film before reaching the initial point (*E* = 0 kV/cm), represented by slope-3. In contrast, no significant changes in the strain hysteresis were observed in the BNT film upon loading and unloading the applied field, as shown in Fig. 3(c). Similar behavior was observed in BiFeO3 thin films [38]. The normalized strain (*S*max/*E*max) response significantly increased from 72 pm/V in pure BNT to 203 pm/V in the BNT–BA film, accompanied by an evident increase in hysteresis. The *S*max/*E*max value of the BNT–BA film was almost three times greater than that of the BNT film.

The  e31,f∗ was evaluated by analyzing the curvature of a unimorph cantilever resulting from the actuation of a piezoelectric oxide film with a sinusoidal strain while being fixed at the opposite end. The BNT film on the Pt/TiO2/SiO2/Si substrate revealed minimal/slim hysteresis, with a maximum displacement amplitude of *δ* = −93 nm at an applied electric field of 200 kV/cm, resulting in * e31,f∗ = 1.09 C/m2 (Fig. 4(b)). In the BNT films, no significant differences in the effective transverse piezoelectric curves were observed upon loading and unloading the applied field, as shown in Fig. 4(b). In contrast, compared with the pure BNT film, the BNT–BA film presented a hysteretic unipolar loop. Initially, the BNT–BA films revealed the same transverse piezoelectric response as the BNT film up to low electric fields of ≤ 75 kV/cm (i.e., slope 1 marked by a dotted black line), as depicted in Fig. 4(b). Interestingly, in the second stage, when the electric field was greater than the poling field of ≥ 75 kV/cm (i.e., slope 2, marked by a dotted black line), the transverse piezoelectric response of the BNT–BA films abruptly increased linearly to a maximum level, with a significant increase of approximately 2.3 times greater than that of the pure BNT films (Fig. 4(b)). The BNT–BA film displayed a displacement amplitude of ~212 nm, leading to  e31,f∗ = 2.48 C/m2, which was more than twice the amplitude of the pure BNT film under the same applied oscillating voltage. The  e31,f∗ values of BNT and BNT–BA films were compared with other lead-based and lead-free materials in Table 1. After the removal of the electric field (E* = 0 kV/cm), the loops returned to the initial position, with little variation in the corresponding loops of the BNT–BA film before reaching the initial point. Thus, the transverse piezoelectric behavior of the BNT–BA film also showed three different slopes, similar to the strain loops in Fig. 3, during the loading and unloading of the applied field marked by the black dashed lines in Fig. 4(b). Therefore, the improvement in the piezoelectric response, facilitated by the electric-field-induced phase transition in the BNT–BA film, holds promise for practical applications, including the use of these materials in actuators.

To explain the mechanism of the high electric field-induced strain in the BNT–BA films, we attempted to perform electric field-dependent in situ* XRD of both the BNT and BNT–BA films. In this case, we coated the Pt electrode on the whole surface of the BNT–BA/Pt/TiO2/SiO2/Si films, forming the Pt/BNT–BA/Pt/TiO2/SiO2/Si geometry [30]. However, successful execution of *in situ* XRD of BNT-based films is extremely challenging because of the defects/vacancies present in the as-prepared BNT-based films [34]. Note that the surface of the whole film was coated with the conducting Pt electrode, and the presence of few vacancies in the low-dimensional (~500 nm) films could easily be mobilized by the substantial electric field applied during the *in situ* XRD measurement, potentially leading to electrical breakdown.

To overcome these challenges, we alternatively conducted *in situ* XRD of both the BNT and BNT–BA ceramics with the same composition despite the unavoidable clamping and constraint effects of the substrates in the thin films. Since both the films and ceramics were intrinsically polycrystalline, the physical properties of both the BNT–BA films and ceramics could be the same except for the large electric field required in the case of thin films because of the clamping effect of the substrates [29,33]. Moreover, the thickness of the BNT–BA films was ~500 nm, where strain relaxation of the films could have occurred, as reported in previous work [35,36,47].

To understand the possible origin of the recoverable electric-field-induced strain response in the BNT–BA sample, we performed *in situ* XRD measurements on both the BNT and BNT–BA ceramic samples before and after exposure to high electric fields (Fig. S3 in the ESM shows the SEM image of the sample preparation and an illustration of the XRD measurements). Section 2 provides a detailed description of the fabrication process for both the BNT and BNT–BA ceramics synthesized via the conventional solid-state-reaction technique. For easy understanding and systematic analysis, we divided the *in situ* XRD results into three distinct scan steps: (i) before the application of an electric field (at *E* = 0 kV/cm), (ii) under a maximum applied electric field (at *E* = 45 kV/cm), and (iii) after the removal of the applied (poling) electric field (at *E* = 0 kV/cm). Note that the 1st cycle represented the poling process of both BNT and BNT–BA samples under an applied electric field of *E* = 45 kV/cm. Furthermore, to validate the recoverable electric-field-induced structural transformation, we replicated measurements on the same sample during the same distinct scan steps: (iv) again subjected to an applied electric field intensity (*E* = 45 kV/cm) and (v) measured again under zero electric field conditions (*E* = 0 kV/cm), as depicted in Fig. 5(a).

To understand the phase evolution comprehensively, we implemented Rietveld refinements of the *in situ* XRD results for both the BNT and BNT–BA samples by following the prescribed steps outlined in Figs. S4–S6 in the ESM [48−50]. These refinements enabled us to accurately identify the structural symmetry and analyze the phase fractions of the crystal symmetry in the oxide materials via the Profex fitting program [20,48−50]. To determine the crystal symmetry and corresponding phase fraction of each phase, we utilized reference XRD files of the rhombohedral BNT (*R*3*c*), cubic BNT (*Pm** 3¯m*), tetragonal BNT (*P*4*mm*), and gold (Au used as an electrode) phases to the FullProf Profex program, as shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM [51−53]. The phase fraction and lattice parameters of each structural phase symmetry at various stages are presented in Fig. 5(b) and Table S1 in the ESM, respectively.

According to the Rietveld fits of the XRD data at *E* = 0 kV/cm of pure BNT, the sample exhibited a mixed phase comprising cubic (28%) and rhombohedral (71%) structures, with a minor presence of the tetragonal phase (1%), representing rhombohedral phase dominance in the as-prepared state (Fig. 5(b)). Interestingly, under the influence of an electric field (state (ii), *E* = 45 kV/cm), the cubic phase fraction decreased significantly to only 1%. In contrast, the rhombohedral phase fraction further increased to approximately 96%, accompanied by a slight increase in the proportion of the tetragonal phase (3%) (Fig. 5(b)). These findings suggest a transition toward a dominant rhombohedral phase in BNT under high electric fields. Upon returning the electric field to zero (state (iii)), the ruling rhombohedral phase did not revert, and the rhombohedral phase remained the same after the poling process, as depicted in Fig. 5(b). This implies that the cubic phase (~28%) in the BNT sample in the sintered state was a nonergodic relaxor and evolved to the rhombohedral phase at a high electric field. Accordingly, the nonergodic relaxor states of the cubic phase in the initial state were completely transformed into a rhombohedral structure upon the poling process in the 1st cycle and remained the same in the second cycle at *E* = 0 and 45 kV/cm (Fig. 5(b)).

On the basis of the Rietveld fits of the BNT–BA XRD data/results at *E* = 0 kV/cm (Fig. 5(c) and Table S1 in the ESM), the BNT–BA sample exhibited a mixed phase comprising cubic and rhombohedral structures, with a minor presence of the tetragonal phase. Initially, state (i), the cubic phase dominated, constituting approximately 59% of the composite, surpassing the rhombohedral (33%) and tetragonal (8%) phases (Fig. 5(c)). Interestingly, under the influence of an electric field (*E* = 45 kV/cm, state (ii)), the cubic phase fraction decreased significantly to only 4%. In contrast, the rhombohedral phase fraction increased to approximately 79%, accompanied by a slight increase in the proportion of the tetragonal phase (17%). These findings suggest a transition toward a dominant rhombohedral phase in BNT–BA under high electric fields. Upon returning the electric field to zero (state (iii)), the dominant rhombohedral phase reverted to a mixture of cubic (44%) and rhombohedral (47%) phases, mirroring the phase fraction ratio observed at zero electric fields, as depicted in Fig. 5(c). Compared with state (i) (i.e., representing the initial or as-sintered state in the 1st cycle), state (iii) showed a decrease of 15 percentage points for the cubic phase, which was attributed to the presence of the 15 percentage point nonergodic relaxor state in the cubic phase. Thus, most nonergodic relaxor states corresponded to 15 percentage points of the cubic phase and were phase-transformed into a rhombohedral structure upon the poling process in the 1st cycle. After the poling process, subsequent measurements involved applying a high electric field (*E* = 45 kV/cm) to the sample and then reducing it to zero during the second cycle, as shown in states (iv) and (v) of Fig. 5. At a high electric field of 45 kV/cm in the second cycle (state (iv)), the BNT–BA sample again exhibited a dominant rhombohedral phase. In contrast to the 1st cycle, the phase fractions of the cubic, rhombohedral, and tetragonal phases in state (v) almost completely reverted to the values observed in state (iii) after the removal of the electric field. Thus, the approximately 44% cubic phase remaining after the poling process (in state (iii)) represents an ergodic relaxor phase, which undergoes an electric-field-induced structural transition primarily into the rhombohedral and tetragonal phases (mainly the rhombohedral structure) under high electric fields (in state (iv)) and then returns to its original state when the electric field is removed (in state (v)). The observed changes in the structural phases during the second cycle were attributed to an electric-field-induced structural transition in the BNT–BA sample, which leads to significant electric-field-induced strain in the BNT-based ergodic relaxor [31,54].

The possible origin of the recoverable electric-field-induced strain in our BNT–BA sample was elucidated through a systematic framework, as depicted in the schematic in Fig. 6. In the initial as-synthesized state (*E* = 0), the nonergodic relaxor BNT manifested a rhombohedral phase, denoted by step 1 in Fig. 6(a). Conversely, the BNT–BA sample exhibited a mixture of cubic and rhombohedral phases, with a predominance of the cubic phase, represented by step 3 in Fig. 6(b). Distinct responses were observed in both oxide samples when they were exposed to an electric field. The pure BNT film demonstrated a linear strain response attributable to the intrinsic behavior of the BNT maintaining rhombohedral symmetry even under a high electric field, as marked by step 2 in red and not returning to the original state after polling (Figs. 6(a) and 6(c)). In contrast, the predominant cubic phase of BNT–BA transformed into a tetragonal phase with a dominant rhombohedral symmetry at elevated electric field strengths, as depicted by step 4 in the schematic in Figs. 6(b) and 6(d). This means that BNT–BA exhibited a relaxor ferroelectric (i.e., cubic plus rhombohedral) phase at *E* = 0 kV/cm and began to transform into a ferroelectric phase (i.e., dominant rhombohedral symmetry) at a high electric field (Fig. 6(d)). Such transformation features of an electric-field-induced structural phase transition within the BNT–BA system would likely lead to enhanced polarization and, consequently, a large strain response at a high electric field [29]. Remarkably, when the electric field strength was reduced to zero, the electric-field-induced strain in BNT–BA did not entirely revert to the original zero state but rather transitioned to a mixed phase characterized by both cubic and rhombohedral symmetries, with nearly equivalent phase fractions instead of the previously dominant cubic phase, as evidenced by step 3 in the schematic in Fig. 6(b). It was highly plausible that some of the cubic phases (15 percentage points in states (i) to (iii) of Fig. 5(b)) were nonergodic relaxor states and transformed into tetragonal and rhombohedral phases upon application of an electric field. Consequently, in the second cycle, BNT–BA followed a similar trajectory of strain states observed under a high electric field, with a dominant rhombohedral symmetry within a mixed rhombohedral and tetragonal phase ensemble. Ultimately, at *E* = 0, the materials were restored to a mixed cubic and rhombohedral phase configuration, as outlined by step 3 in the schematic in Figs. 6(c) and 6(d). Notably, the remaining cubic phase in state (iii) of Fig. 5(b) was the ergodic relaxor phase in the BNT–BA sample, which transformed to a rhombohedral phase only when exposed to the external electric field and then was restored to the initial cubic phase after removal/reduction of the electric field. Therefore, the observed enhancement in ferroelectric polarization and high strain response in the BNT–BA film was highly likely due to the emergence of a dominant rhombohedral crystallographic phase induced by the applied field, as detected in the *in situ* *θ*–2*θ* XRD scans of our BA-substituted BNT films. Specifically, the enhancement could be traced back to an electric-field-triggered structural phase transition from cubic symmetry to a dominant rhombohedral phase.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, we successfully synthesized BNT and BNT–BA films with a uniform thickness of 500 nm on a Pt(111)/TiO2/SiO2/Si(100) substrate via the sol‒gel technique. A comparative analysis revealed distinct structural and electromechanical properties in both films. Compared with BNT, the BNT–BA film exhibited a saturated polarization hysteresis loop with higher remnant polarization and sustained high field strength. Additionally, the BNT–BA film demonstrated a remarkable normalized strain (*S*max/*E*max = 203 pm/V) and an effective piezoelectric coefficient (* e31,f∗ = 2.48 C/m2).

The high electric-field-induced strain in the BNT–BA film was attributed to the phase transformation from a cubic structure at zero electric fields to rhombohedral and tetragonal phases (predominantly rhombohedral) under high electric fields, as revealed by field-dependent Rietveld analysis of the in situ* XRD results. Since the cubic phase, accounting for 44% of the phase fractions, was an ergodic relaxor phase, the phase transition between the cubic/rhombohedral mixed phase and the rhombohedral/tetragonal mixed phase was reversible upon application and removal of the electric field. This recoverable phase transition enables the BNT–BA film to exhibit high electric-field-induced strain. Our findings on BNT–BA films provide valuable insights for designing piezoelectric materials for MEMS devices.
