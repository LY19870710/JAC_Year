## 1 Introduction

As a typical garnet structure material with a cell formula of A3B2C3O12 [1−3], Y3Al5O12 (YAG) can be grown either in the form of a single crystal or polycrystalline material, wherein Y3+ in the A site and Al3+ in the B site can be easily substituted for rare-earth ions or transition metal ions with similar ionic radii [4−7]. In addition, YAG has excellent optical properties and thermal, chemical, and mechanical stability, which makes it an ideal host for doping activated ions, especially lanthanide ions, which have abundant energy to utilize spectral characteristics such as fluorescence and lasers [8−15]. In 1995, Ikesue *et al*. [16] first synthesized 1.1 at% Nd3+:YAG polycrystalline transparent ceramics by a solid-state reaction method using Y2O3 and Al2O3 high-purity powders and achieved an oscillation threshold of 309 mW and a slope efficiency of 28%, respectively, which made the polycrystalline transparent ceramics a potential material for use as a gain medium for lasers.

Nevertheless, obtaining perfect single crystals requires extremely strict growth conditions with a slower growth efficiency, which increases the preparation costs [17−19]. Similarly, it is also extremely difficult to obtain sufficiently transparent polycrystalline ceramics as laser gain medium materials because of the two essential requirements of ultrapure raw powders and high temperature in vacuum, as in Refs. [20−22]. Furthermore, the average grain size of polycrystalline transparent ceramics prepared by the vacuum sintering method is usually greater than 50 μm, and the sizes of internal defects such as pores and grain boundaries are close to the wavelength of light, which is the principle reason why an opaque matrix causes diffraction and absorption of light at the interface of defects [23−25]. As a result, only by minimizing the internal pores and grain boundaries as much as possible can traditional polycrystalline ceramics become transparent. Kosyanov *et* *al*. [26] revealed the regularity of the substitution of Nd3+ for Y3+ in the garnet structure of Nd3+:YAG via a reactive sintering method in 2016. In 2019, Yavetskiy *et* *al*. [27] studied the influence of green body annealing on the structure of Nd3+:YAG transparent ceramics. Vorona *et* *al*. [28] concluded that multilayer YAG/Nd3+:YAG/YAG composite laser ceramics have a slope efficiency almost twice that of single-layer ceramics with the same Nd3+ concentration (1–4 at%) (22% and 12.5%, respectively) obtained by reactive sintering in vacuum in 2017. These samples were prepared by solid reaction sintering methods to obtain ceramics with microscale grains, which are much larger than nanoscale grains, without considering concentration quenching. In 2018, Prof. Li’s research group [29] successfully prepared YAG–Al2O3 multiphase transparent ceramics with nanocrystalline material using a pressureless amorphous crystallization method, which demonstrated that nanocrystalline transparent ceramics of YAG–Al2O3 could be successfully synthesized when the stoichiometric ratio of Y2O3 : Al2O3 was 26–28 mol% : 74–72 mol% with greater hardness. The transparency mechanism of YAG–Al2O3 multiphase nanocrystalline transparent ceramics is quite different from that of YAG transparent ceramics with a grain size of 50 μm. The average grain size of the nanocrystallites is not greater than 50 nm, and defects such as grains and grain boundaries are also far smaller than the wavelength of light, which makes them too dissimilar to diffract or scatter according to Rayleigh scattering theory. Therefore, transparent ceramics with nanocrystalline materials can be expected to meet basic requirements as laser gain medium matrix materials despite being multiphase. For the laser gain matrix, the doping concentration of Nd3+ has an important effect on the light absorption, excitation, and florescence lifetime [30−32]. However, it is almost impossible to improve the doping concentration of Nd3+ homogeneously to over 0.9 at% due to the lower effective segregation coefficient of Nd3+ in YAG monocrystals [33,34]. Moreover, the concentration quenching effects are strongly aggravated when the doping concentration of Nd3+ exceeds 1.0 at%, and the fluorescence lifetime sharply decays with increasing doping concentration of Nd3+. For example, the florescence lifetime decreases to 184.2 μs in 2.0 at% Nd3+:YAG ceramics [35]. Therefore, improving the Nd3+ doping concentration in YAG-based nanocrystalline transparent ceramics without losing fluorescence and laser properties is extremely challenging for developing future fluorescent and laser gain medium materials.

In this work, the feasibility of doping a YAG–Al2O3 matrix with a high Nd3+ concentration via complete crystallization of 74 mol% Al2O3–26 mol% Y2O3 (hereinafter referred to as AY26) parent bulk glass was explored, and the effects of different doping concentrations of Nd3+ and crystallization temperatures on the structure and optical properties were investigated.

### 2.1 Experimental

Nanometer (60–100 nm) *α*-Al2O3 powders (purity > 99.99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), Y2O3 powders (50–70 nm) (purity > 99.999%, Beijing Haoke Technology Co., Ltd., China), and Nd2O3 powders (10–40 nm) (purity > 99.999%, Beijing Haoke Technology Co., Ltd., China) were used as the raw materials. Powders with a molar ratio of Y2O3 : Nd2O3 : Al2O3 = (0.26 − *x*) : *x* : 0.74 (*x* = 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, and 5.0 at%) were mixed, ball-milled in ethanol, dried, pressed to form pellets and then broken into small blocks of 70–120 mg. The blocks were aerodynamically levitated in O2 current and heated to melt by a CO2 laser to form vitreous molten-state beads [36−38]. The rated power of the CO2 laser is 75 W, and the working power is 0%–100% of the rated power. Next, the laser was turned off suddenly to reduce rapid cooling (~400–500 °C·s−1), and vitrified beads with diameters of 3–5 mm were obtained [39−43]. After that, the beads were directly crystallized at different temperatures from 1000 to 1200 °C for 2 h or subsequently ground into small discs. Finally, the crystallization samples were ground and polished into small circular discs with smooth surfaces, diameters of 2–4 mm, and thicknesses of 0.5–1.0 mm.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The Archimedes method was used to measure the density of the glass beads. Thermal analysis of the glass beads was conducted by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC; STA 449F3 Jupiter, Netzsch, Germany) in the temperature range of 30–1300 °C at a constant heating rate of 10 °C·min−1 in an air atmosphere, and TA universal analysis 2000 was used to analyze the DSC data. The magnetic angle rotation solid nuclear magnetic resonance coordination number was calibrated using magic angle rotation solid nuclear magnetic resonation spectroscope (MAS NMR; JEOL JNM-ECZ600R, 27Al NMR spectroscopy at 14 T, JEOL, Japan). Room temperature X-ray powder diffraction (RT-XRD, Rigaku, Japan) was used to determine the optimal crystallization temperature. *In* *situ* high-temperature X-ray powder diffraction (*in* *situ* HT-XRD; Rigaku SmartLab 9 kW with an HTK Anton Paar chamber, Cu-Kα radiation, 1.5406 Å, Rigaku, Japan) was used to follow the changes in phases during heat treatment of glass beads or ceramic pebbles from room temperature up to 1200 °C between 10° and 80° and refine the results based on the Rietveld method via GSAS II [44] to determine the phase composition, crystallinity, crystal size, lattice constant, cell volume, density, and measurement error. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEOL JEM-2010, JEOL, Japan) and high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM; JEOL JEM-2010, JEOL, Japan) with accelerating voltage of 200 kV were used to observe the nanostructure of the ceramic pebble sample. The transmittance and absorption spectra were obtained with an ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared (UV‒Vis‒NIR) spectrophotometer (Cary 7000, Agilent Technologies, Inc., USA). A steady-state transient fluorescence spectrometer (FLS1000, Edinburgh Instruments, UK) was used to investigate the emission spectrum and fluorescence lifetime.

### 3.1 Investigation of the doping concentration of Nd<sup>3+</sup>

First, it is necessary to discuss the effects of different doping concentrations of Nd3+ on the samples during the amorphous formation and crystallization stages.

The primary issue is ensuring the formation of an amorphous structure during the process of crystallization from an amorphous state. Although the mol ratio for obtaining YAG–Al2O3 nanocrystalline transparent ceramics was determined to be 26‒28 mol% : 74‒72 mol%, there is no obvious evidence showing how doped Nd3+ influences the amorphous phase, so the effects were investigated first. Figure 1 shows the variations in temperature during the melting and quenching processes. The XRD patterns and the changes in density of the Nd3+:26 mol% Y2O3‒74 mol% Al2O3 vitrify samples.

Fig. 1(a) Heating curves during melting process, (b) cooling curves during quenching process, (c) XRD patterns, and (d) density of 0–5.0 at% Nd3+:AY26 glass.

As shown in Fig. 1(a), when the vitreous state of AY26 is prepared using the containerless solidification method with different doping proportions of Nd3+, the different doping concentrations have different effects on the heating rate of the green blocks. The melting rate of the green body accelerated with increasing Nd3+-doping concentration. Under the same laser power, green blocks with higher doping concentrations can reach higher temperatures at the same time and thereby reach the melting point faster. When cooling, as shown in Fig. 1(b), after the laser was turned off, the cooling rate rapidly decreased from ~500 °C·s−1 without the recalescence phenomenon, which means that crystallization did not occur. At 5 s, the temperature of the samples with a doping concentration of 0 decreased to ~1500 °C. Nevertheless, the temperature was 50–400 °C lower than the 1500 °C temperature at which 0.5–5.0 at% Nd3+ was doped, which indicates that Nd3+ doping is more conducive to the formation of amorphous whether in the melting and cooling stages. To further demonstrate that the vitreous beads were amorphous, the beads were ground into powders for XRD analysis. Figure 1(c) shows that broadened amorphous peaks are obtained at different doping concentrations, which suggests that the Nd3+ ions are better merged into the amorphous phase. The density of the amorphous phase is basically stable between 3.694 and 3.840 g·cm−3, as shown in Fig. 1(d).

Then, the effect of the Nd3+ doping concentration on the crystallization temperature of the Nd3+:AY26 glass was analyzed via DSC, and the temperature at which the Nd3+:AY26 glass could be fully crystallized was determined via XRD. Based on the determined complete crystallization temperature, the transmittance and emission spectra of samples with different doping concentrations were investigated to determine the proper Nd3+ doping concentration that can achieve the best fluorescence performance. As shown in Fig. 2, DSC tests were conducted on glass samples with Nd3+ doping concentrations of 0.0, 1.5, and 3.0 at%. There was obvious separation of the exothermic peaks, and the separation became increasingly pronounced from 4.59 to 16.76 °C with increasing Nd3+ concentration. According to the principle of DSC measurement, the exothermic peak characterizes the heat capacity change of the sample during crystallization. Two peaks appear apart, indicating that a phase transition has occurred during or after crystallization. When Nd3+ is not doped, the first phase transforms into the second phase immediately and forms due to the shorter separation distance. However, when the doping concentration of Nd3+ increases, the first phase can form more easily and transform to the second phase later, which suggests that the doping concentration of Nd3+ can influence the phase transition during crystallization. Moreover, Fig. 2(b) shows that Nd3+ doping affects the glass transition temperature (T*g), crystallization start temperature (*T**x*), and crystallization end temperature. The size of the undercooled liquid phase zone Δ*T* expands slightly from 50.48 to 52.09 °C, which indicates that the doping of Nd3+ can help the crystallization process from an amorphous state to a crystalline state. In addition, crystallization starts at ~930 °C and ends at ~980 °C. Thus, the selected crystallization temperature should be greater than 980 °C and be held for a sufficient time to complete the phase transition process. Therefore, in this study, samples with 0–5 at% doping concentrations were crystallized at four temperature points: 1000, 1050, 1100, and 1150 °C. Room-temperature XRD was performed to determine the proper crystallization temperature, as shown in Fig. 3.

In addition, the emission width, i.e., the full width at half maximum (FWHM), of the strongest peak at 1064 nm, as shown in Fig. 4(b), has the same trend but different inflexion points compared with the FWHM of Nd3+:YAG, as shown in Fig. 4(c). For the emission peak at 1064 nm, the intensity increases, and the FWHM remains basically unchanged as the concentration of luminescent ions increases when the doping concentration of Nd3+ is between 0.5 and 1.5 at%. Nevertheless, the intensity decreases as the doping concentration exceeds 1.5 at%, and the FWHM increases due to concentration quenching effects. Concentration quenching not only leads to a decrease in luminescence efficiency but also causes spectral broadening, thereby affecting the monochromaticity of the laser. When the concentration of activated ions exceeds a certain threshold, concentration quenching becomes apparent. Therefore, although there is no limit to the doping concentration from the perspective of solid solutions in YAG–Al2O3 nanocrystalline ceramics and YAG ceramic samples, excessive Nd3+ doping can lead to concentration quenching, which can cause spectral broadening and a decrease in the fluorescence lifetime. For Nd3+ in a YAG polycrystalline ceramic matrix, Lu [35] showed that the threshold is approximately 1.0 at%, as shown in Fig. 4(c), but YAG–Al2O3 nanocrystalline ceramics exhibit a higher inflexion concentration quenching of Nd3+ in Fig. 4(b), that is, 1.5 at%, through a two-phase composite. Analysis of the emission spectra and spectral broadening, as shown in Figs. 4(a)–4(c), indicates that the doping concentration causing concentration quenching is 1.5 at% Nd3+ in the YAG–Al2O3 nanocrystalline ceramics, which is significantly greater than the 1.0 at% observed in the YAG ceramics. This may be attributed to the encapsulation of nascent nano-Al2O3 grains within the grain boundaries of the YAG nanoscale grains, thereby preventing energy level cross-relaxation to some extent.

As shown in Fig. 4(d), the Nd3+-doped concentration has different influences on the absorption bands and nonabsorption bands. For absorption bands, the concentration has a significant impact on the internal transmittance of the absorption bands. The internal transmittance decreases gradually with increasing Nd3+-doping concentration, which corresponds to an increase in the absorption intensity. From the perspective of solid solution theory, the doping concentration of Nd3+ in YAG single crystals is generally not more than 0.9 at% due to the limitation of the segregation coefficient (only ~0.2). However, YAG–Al2O3 nanocrystalline ceramics, similar to YAG ceramics, have no limitation of the segregation coefficient, which would be beneficial for light absorption if the effect of concentration quenching due to high values is not considerable. Moreover, the doping concentration of Nd3+ has little effect on the transmittance of the nonabsorption bands, and the transmittance can substantially reach over 88% at 1064 nm, especially in the infrared bands; that is, the transmittance is 89.65% at a doping concentration of 1.5 at% Nd3+, which is very favorable for lasers.

From the perspective of laser gain materials, the gain effect is related to the quenching concentration. In the case of a small signal gain, the gain coefficient (g*0) is a constant, which is proportional to the difference in population per unit volume *n*2(*z*) − *n*1(*z*). When the concentration is less than the quenching concentration, the difference in population per unit volume is directly proportional to the concentration of the activating substance, and the quenching concentration corresponds to the maximum value of *g*0. Therefore, increasing the quenching concentration is equivalent to increasing the effective doping concentration of the active substance, thereby increasing the threshold of *g*0. Furthermore, from the perspective of the laser as a whole, the condition for achieving self-excited oscillation is *g*0 ≥ *α* (loss coefficient). Under the premise of a certain optical cavity loss coefficient *α*, increasing the *g*0 makes it easier to achieve self-excited oscillation. Moreover, the maximum light intensity (*I*m) of the laser is proportional to (*g*0 – *α*). When *g*0 increases, *I*m increases, which means that the material has good gain characteristics.

In subsequent experiments and tests, a doping concentration of 1.5 at% Nd3+ was selected to analyze the amorphous state, crystallization process, microstructure, and optical properties of the samples.

Three broadened line shapes are exhibited in both curves before and after Nd3+ doping, which further indicates that the samples are amorphous, and the asterisks represent the curves from the distribution of quadrupolar parameters.

Al[6] is generally a type of aluminum ion in crystals [47−49]. The increase in the percentage of Al[4] clarifies the improvements in the amorphous formation ability of the Y2O3–Al2O3 system.

### 3.3 Crystallization process of Nd<sup>3+</sup>:YAG–A2O3 transparent ceramics

High-temperature *in* *situ* XRD was conducted on 1.5 at% Nd3+:AY26 glass beads from room temperature to 800 °C, and then the data were collected at a rate of 4 °C·min−1 at set temperature intervals, as shown in Fig. 6.

*
Fig. 6(a) HT-XRD patterns of 1.5 at% Nd3+:AY26 glass powders, (b) variation in coordination of Y3+ and Al3+ during crystallization, (c) magnified image of lower-angle region in HT-XRD patterns, and (d) magnified image of higher-angle region in HT-XRD patterns.

As shown in Fig. 6(a), hexagonal YAlO3 (YAH) appears at 940 °C, accompanied by the formation of YAG. This is very different from the solid reaction wherein Y4Al2O9 (YAM) is formed first. The difference may be due to the different initial states: One is a mixture of two kinds of crystals containing Y2O3 and Al2O3, and the other is an amorphous glassy network structure that has a lower formation energy, resulting in crystallization without YAM. As shown in Fig. 6(a), δ*-Al2O3 starts to emerge, accompanied by the formation of YAH and YAG at 940 °C. At this point, *δ*-Al2O3 is highly likely transformed from γ-Al2O3 formed at low temperature. When the temperature reaches 950 °C, YAH disappears, and YAG becomes the main phase accompanied by the growth of *δ*-Al2O3, which is simultaneously transformed into *θ*-Al2O3, which is consistent with the DSC results. Thus, the crystallization of amorphous Nd3+:AY26 involves two subprocesses, as shown in Fig. 6(b). For stoichiometric Y2O3 and Al2O3, YAH with three kinds of Y3+ coordination modes resulting in a lower-order structure is first generated at lower temperatures [2,50,51]. Then, Y[6] from YAH is converted into Y[8] with a higher degree of order, while YAH is transformed into YAG; finally, YAH completely turns into YAG after 950 °C, and YAG grows. For the other part of the excess pure Al2O3, *δ*-Al2O3 first forms between 900 and 940 °C, *δ*-Al2O3 starts to gradually convert into *θ*-Al2O3 after ~950 °C, and finally, both phases accompany YAG, which agrees with the opinion of Refs. [50,52−54], which suggested that *δ*-Al2O3 and *θ*-Al2O3 are associated with each other.

It is worth mentioning that during the process of temperature increase, the YAG characteristic peaks at lower angles shift slightly toward higher angles compared to the peaks at lower angles of the standard spectral peak of YAG, while the peaks at higher angles shift slightly toward lower angles compared to the peaks at higher angles of the standard spectral peak of YAG, as shown in Figs. 6(c) and 6(d), respectively. In contrast, for the movement of the lower angle toward a higher angle, the peaks at lower angles corresponding to larger interplanar spacings (*d*) move toward higher angles, which means that the observed value is less than the theoretical value, indicating that there is lattice deficiency in the YAG crystal, likely due to the deficiency of Y. For example, in Fig. 6(c), the theoretical *d* between the (211) planes is 4.9055, whereas the value observed is 4.9233 after displacement. However, the shift of the peaks from higher to lower angles, as shown in Fig. 3(d), may be attributed to both lattice expansion caused by thermal expansion and lattice distortion caused by the doping of Nd3+ at the Y3+ position. In addition, the zero drift due to the effect of thermal expansion during the heating process of *in* *situ* XRD should cause a synclastic displacement of the peaks. Nevertheless, the difference between lower angles and higher angles indicates that zero drift is not the main reason for the shift in the peaks.

### 3.4 Microstructure of the Nd<sup>3+</sup>:YAG–Al2O3 transparent ceramics

Based on the above analysis results, TEM, EDS, and HR-TEM were used to analyze the microstructure of the 1.5 at% Nd3+:YAG–Al2O3 ceramics crystallized at 1050 °C, as shown in Fig. 9.

*

(e, f) Fourier transform diffraction patterns from HR-TEM accompanied by YAG and θ-*Al2O3 standard diffraction patterns.

As shown in Fig. 9(a), the morphology containing the nanometer YAG grains and the lighter intergranular Al2O3 can be clearly observed in the TEM surface image, and the atomic percentages of different elements in the sample can be obtained through the EDS image. C is the element introduced to conduct electricity during the testing process, and Nd accounts for ~1.5 at% of Y, which is consistent with the ratio of raw materials. The distribution of grain sizes in Fig. 9(b) was measured by NanoMeasure 1.2 based on the image in Fig. 9(a). Figure 9(b) shows that the grain size range is 12.50–40.48 nm, 90% of the grains are less than 29.66 nm, and the median grain size and integrated average size are 22.56 and 23.88 nm, respectively, which are consistent with the XRD analysis results. This suggests that the grains belong to the nanocrystalline category, which is much smaller than the wavelengths of visible and infrared light, so it displays perfect transparency and is consistent with the transmittance results. The selective area electron diffraction pattern is presented in the left image of Fig. 9(c), in which the spots located on the diffraction rings can be clearly observed. The diffraction patterns of two regions e and f after Fourier transform are shown in Figs. 9(e) and 9(f). The diffraction patterns of these two regions are consistent with the standard diffraction patterns of YAG and *θ-*Al2O3, which corresponds to the results of the XRD refinement.

It is precisely because of its relatively uniform grain size distribution and phase distribution that, according to Rayleigh scattering theory, light does not diffract when passing through far smaller grains, and samples are able to achieve high transmittance, reaching 89.65%, which can meet the basic requirements of being laser ceramics.

### 3.5 Optical properties of the Nd<sup>3+</sup>:YAG–Al2O3 transparent ceramics

To investigate the optical properties of the Nd3+:YAG–Al2O3 ceramics, samples with Y2O3 : Nd2O3 : Al2O3 = (0.26 − *x*) : *x* : 0.74 (*x* = 1.5 at%) crystallized at 1050 °C were analyzed from the perspective of internal transmittance and fluorescence lifetime (Fig. 10).

*
Fig. 10(a) Transmittance spectra of 1.5 at% Nd3+:YAG–Al2O3 ceramics with a thickness of 1 mm crystallized at 1000–1200 °C. (b) Fluorescence lifetime of 1.5 at% Nd3+:YAG–Al2O3 ceramics crystallized at 1050 °C.

As shown in Fig. 10(a), the crystallization temperature, which is the key factor for obtaining the corresponding grain size, has a significant impact on the internal transmittance of the absorption bands. When the crystallization temperature is 1000 °C, the internal transmittance is lower than that at 1050 °C. At this point, crystallization is not yet complete, and the material is a mixture of nanocrystals and an amorphous state. Although either amorphous or nanocrystalline states can achieve high transmittance individually, the mixture of the two substances may have certain interfaces between phases, which to some extent reduces the transmittance. When the crystallization temperature is increased to 1050 °C, the crystallization is essentially complete, the grains are uniform and fine with an average size of 23.88 nm, and the internal transmittance reaches its maximum. When the temperature is increased to 1100 °C, the grain size of ~25 nm increases slightly, and the internal transmittance slightly decreases, but the change is not significant, wherein the near-infrared transmittance remains consistent, reaching 89.65% at 1064 nm, which is very favorable for lasers. When the temperature exceeds 1100 to 1150 °C or higher, the internal transmittance drops sharply. From the physical images in Fig. 10(a), it can be visually observed that at 1200 °C, the sample exhibits a certain milky white color and has a low transmittance in the visible light region.

For the fluorescence lifetime, the doping concentration of Nd3+ should be taken into account. Therefore, although there is no limit to the doping concentration from the perspective of solid solutions in YAG–Al2O3 nanocrystalline ceramics and YAG ceramic samples, excessive Nd3+ doping can lead to concentration quenching and a decrease in the fluorescence lifetime. Lu et al*. [35] compared the fluorescence lifetime of YAG single crystals and YAG ceramics doped with different concentrations of Nd3+ and reported that the fluorescence lifetime was ~233 μs at a concentration of 1.0 at%, which significantly decreased when the doping concentration exceeded 1.0 at%. In this article, the decay time of the Nd3+ emission peak at 1064 nm excited at 808 nm was collected, and the decay curve was fitted by the first-order exponential decay formula, as shown in the equation: * I(t)=I0exp⁡(−t/τ) The fluorescence lifetime (τ*) of the sample with 1.5 at% Nd3+ crystallized at 1050 °C reaches 231 μs, as shown in Fig. 10(b).

## 4 Conclusions

The 0–5.0 at% Nd3+:AY26 purified beads were obtained via a laser-heated aerodynamic levitation method. In the process of forming amorphous Nd3+:AY26 glass, Nd3+ could promote the transition of Al[5] and Al[6] to Al[4] to improve the ability of the material to form amorphous materials. The Nd3+:AY26 glass was successfully transformed into Nd3+:YAG–Al2O3 nanocrystalline ceramics via amorphous crystallization. The results show that 1.5 at% Nd3+:YAG–Al2O3 nanocrystalline ceramics could be crystallized at 1050 °C, composed of a matrix of YAG and concomitant *δ*-Al2O3 and *θ*-Al2O3 and the active ions of Nd3+. The nanoceramics show an internal transmittance of 89.56% at 1064 nm, and strong emission peaks from the energy level transition of 4F3/2 to 4I11/2 of Nd3+ with a fluorescence lifetime of 231 μs are generated by an 808 nm laser. The doping concentration causing concentration quenching is 1.5 at% Nd3+, which is significantly greater than the 1.0 at% observed in YAG ceramics, which means that employing YAG–Al2O3 nanocrystalline transparent ceramics as laser gain media can increase the quenching concentration inflexion point of activated Nd3+ ions. These results suggest that 1.5 at% Nd3+:YAG–Al2O3 nanocrystalline ceramics have great potential as laser gain media, offering a novel approach for optimizing the performance of laser transparent ceramics.
