## 1 Introduction

As specialized functional materials, X-ray scintillators are capable of intercepting a portion of incident X-rays and converting them into ultraviolet or visible light, which can then be received by photodetectors and transformed into visual information [1]. Owing to their high sensitivity, rapid response, high resolution, and other advantages, X-ray scintillators have been widely applied in X-ray detectors, space exploration, medical imaging, and radiation exposure monitoring [2−9].

Ideal scintillators normally exhibit excellent absorption of X-ray photons, high light yield (LY), enduring physical/chemical stability, and robust radiation resistance. Inorganic scintillators boast a high density and a high atomic number, coupled with superior chemical stability and mechanical strength. These properties collectively contribute to higher detection efficiency and greater suitability for applications in harsh environmental conditions. Currently, commercial inorganic scintillators primarily include single-crystal scintillators (e.g., Bi4Ge3O12), ceramic scintillators (e.g., Gd2O2S:Tb3+ and Gd3Ga5O12:Ce3+), and glass scintillators (e.g., Tb3+-doped gadolinium silicate glass) [10−12]. Among them, glass scintillators are renowned for their transparency, stability, and machinability, making them easy to process into any shape and size, including optical fibers, to meet diverse device requirements [13]. However, there is still an urgent need to address issues such as the persistence of low LY.

Glass-ceramic (GC) scintillators have attracted increasing attention because they combine all the aforementioned advantages of glass with the excellent LY of crystals. In recent years, various GC scintillators, such as K(Y,Gd)F4:Ce3+ [14] and Sr2GdF7:Tb3+ [15], have been developed. Compared with glass scintillators, these materials exhibit a significantly improved LY. However, it is still challenging to achieve GCs with both high crystallinity and high transmittance through *in situ* crystallization of precursor glass. According to Rayleigh’s scattering theory, when light is generated within a GC scintillator upon excitation by X-rays and subsequently escapes from the interior to the exterior, it inevitably undergoes scattering by crystalline particles. On the other hand, in GCs, the majority of rare earth ions preferentially enter the crystalline phase, which has a lower defect density and consequently acts as the primary functional unit responsible for scintillation performance [11]. While the increased crystalline phase content helps to enhance LY, it also increases the Rayleigh scattering, which is detrimental to transparency, arousing a contradiction between achieving high crystallinity and high transparency. Therefore, it is crucial to make ongoing efforts to propose material design strategies to address this contradiction.

To address the aforementioned issues, this work proposes a comprehensive regulation strategy involving regulating the heat-treatment temperature, modulating the raw material amount for precipitated nanocrystals (NCs), and adjusting the glass network structure. For example, for NaLuF4:Tb3+-based GC emitting at 542 nm (corresponding to the Tb3+ 5D4→7F5 transition and compatible with CCD detection sensitivity [16,17]), the results indicate that when the heat treatment temperature is set at 700 °C, with a total molar percentage of 31.33% for NaF, LuF3, and TbF3 combined and the Si/Al ratio is adjusted to 5.09, the crystallinity and transmittance of the GC reach 58% and approximately 90%@ 542 nm, respectively. Consequently, the LY, detection limit, and image resolution of the synthesized composite were determined to be 10,200 photons·MeV−1, 1.26 nGy·s−1, and 25.3 lp·mm−1, respectively. These results indicate that the comprehensive regulatory approach provides valuable insights into the design and development of high-crystallinity, high-transmittance GC scintillators.

### 2.1 Materials

SiO2 (Bodi, analytical grade), Al2O3 (Macklin, 99%), Na2CO3 (Macklin, 98%, Na2O precursor), NaF (Macklin, 98%), LuF3 (Innochem Co., 99.9%), and TbF3 (Innochem Co., 99.9%) were used as raw materials.

### 2.2 Sample preparation

The precursor glasses (PGs) were prepared via a melt-quenching method. For each batch, ~8 g of raw material was thoroughly mixed and melted in an alumina crucible at 1550 °C in air to form a homogeneous melt. The melting time is fixed at 27 min, as this duration yields glass-ceramics (GCs) with high crystallinity and transparency. The melt was then poured into a brass mold cooled to room temperature. The obtained PGs were heat-treated at a specific temperature for 2 h to obtain the corresponding GCs. To achieve optimal crystallinity and transparency in GCs, the thermal treatment temperature of PGs was varied between 500 and 800 °C, the raw material concentration for precipitated NCs (the total molar percentage of NaF, LuF3, and TbF3) was adjusted within the range of 27.56%–33.08%, and the Si/Al ratio in the precursor mixture (the molar ratio of silicon to aluminum) was altered within the range of 4.33–5.34. Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) summarizes the detailed components of PGs and heat-treatment parameters of prepared GCs.

### 2.3 Characterizations

To characterize the crystalline phase, powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained via an X-ray diffractometer (SmartLab Rigaku) with Cu Kα1 radiation (*λ* = 0.154 nm). A transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-2010) was used to examine the particle size and morphology of the samples. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images were recorded to observe the atomic planes inside the crystalline lattice. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) in high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) mode, which is sensitive to the atomic number (*Z*) of the sample, was performed on a transmission electron microscope (FEI aberration-corrected Titan Cubed S-Twin) operated at 200 kV. The samples were ground into a very fine powder that was placed onto a carbon-coated copper grid and introduced into the microscope. The transmittance spectra of PG and GCs (10 mm × 10 mm × 1 mm) were recorded on a spectrophotometer (Lambda 950) in the range of 400–800 nm. 29Si and 27Al MAS-NMR spectra were obtained via a Bruker ADVANCE III HD 400 instrument. Raman spectra of powder samples with various Si/Al ratios were acquired via an Edinburgh Instruments RMS1000 instrument equipped with a 785 nm laser. Differential scanning calorimeter (DSC; Netzsch STA449F3) was carried out by heating ~20 mg of PG powder in an air atmosphere (α-Al2O3 crucible) at a heating rate of 10 °C·min−1. PLE spectra (powder samples), PL spectra (powder samples), PL lifetimes (powder samples), XEL spectra (powder samples), X-ray radiation stability tests (sample size: 5.0 mm × 5.0 mm × 1.0 mm), and LY (sample size: 5.0 mm × 5.0 mm × 1.0 mm) were measured via a fluorescence spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments FLS1000) equipped with a 450 W continuous xenon lamp and a 12 W X-ray light source. XEL lifetime (powder sample), X-ray detection (powder sample) and imaging (sample size: 25.0 mm × 25.0 mm × 1.0 mm) were performed by an analysis system (Zolix) equipped with an X-imaging module, a steady-state X-ray source, a nanosecond pulse X-ray source, and a photon counter. To calculate the modulation transfer function (MTF) via the slanted-edge method, we captured X-ray images of a sharp edge from a piece of aluminum (thickness: ~1.0 mm) [18]. The edge spread function (ESF) was derived from the edge profile, and from this, we deduced the line spread function (LSF) by calculating its derivative. Finally, the Fourier transform of the LSF defines the MTF. This means that the MTF curve can be calculated via Eq. (1):

(1) MTF(ν)=F(LSF(x))=FdESF(x)dx

where *ν* represents the spatial frequency, and *x* represents the position of the pixels, which is defined by Eq. (2):

(2) x=N⋅dβ

where *N* is the ordinal number of pixels in the X-ray edge image, *d* is the pixel size, and *β* is the optical magnification.

The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of the NaLuF4:Tb3+-based GC reveals significantly embedded NCs within the glass. The NCs have an average diameter of ~30 nm and a narrow size distribution (Fig. 1(b)). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) analysis further revealed well-resolved lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of 1.361 Å, corresponding to the (400) crystal plane. This value lies between those of cubic NaLuF4 (*d**(*400) = 1.358 Å) and cubic NaTbF4 (*d*(400) = 1.365 Å), confirming the successful incorporation of Tb3+ ions into the NaLuF4 lattice sites (Fig. 1(c)). Additionally, high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) imaging and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Fig. 1(d)) provide further evidence of the presence of NaLuF4:Tb3+ NCs within the glass matrix. This is supported by the distinct *Z*-contrast between the NCs (bright) and the glass matrix (dark), as well as the localized enrichment of Lu and Tb within the particle region. Notably, the designed GCs exhibit exceptional crystallinity and maintain favorable transparency (Table 1) [14,15,19−22].

In this context, the proposed comprehensive regulation strategy involves regulating the heat-treatment temperature, modulating the percentage of all fluorides, and adjusting the glass network structure (i.e., the Si/Al ratio):

(1) As demonstrated in Fig. 2(a) and Figs. S1 and S2 in the ESM, increasing the heat treatment temperature from 500 to 700 °C results in a substantial increase in crystallinity, which increases from 7% to 49%. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis revealed that the glass transition temperature (*T*g) of our designed glass component is approximately 498 °C (Fig. S3 in the ESM). When this heat treatment temperature exceeds *T*g, thermal energy can facilitate orderly movement/diffusion of amorphous structural building blocks and atomic rearrangements, which in turn induce the nucleation and growth of NCs [23]. However, further increasing the heat treatment temperature to 800 °C results in a significant reduction in the transmittance to 43% and an unusually low crystallinity of 37%, which is likely attributed to Ostwald ripening (Fig. 2(a) and Figs. S1–S3 in the ESM). Therefore, the optimal heat treatment temperature was determined to be 700 °C.

This suggests that adding an appropriate amount of raw materials promotes grain formation, whereas an excess may inhibit NC nucleation and growth by altering the glass structure.

(3) We conducted an optimization experiment involving the Si/Al ratio in the raw materials. As demonstrated in Fig. 2(c) and Figs. S6, and S7 in the ESM, the Si/Al ratio has a significant influence on the crystallinity. When the Si/Al ratio is increased from 4.34 to 5.09, a sharp increase in crystallinity can be seen from 41% to 58%. However, a further increase in the Si/Al ratio to 5.34 led to a decrease in crystallinity.

To gain deeper insights into how the evolution of the glass network influences the nucleation and growth of nanocrystals, amorphous structure analyses were conducted on samples with three representative Si/Al ratios. As indicated by the Raman spectra (Figs. 2(d)–2(f)), the band located between 834 and 1250 cm−1 is ascribed to the stretching of tetrahedral structural units Qn* (where Q*n* refers to [SiO4] with a number of bridging oxygen atoms per unit, with *n* taking values of 1, 2, 3, or 4) [24]. An increase in the Si/Al ratio results in a greater number of bridging oxygens in the [SiO4] tetrahedra, indicating a more compact network structure of the glass. Furthermore, the NMR spectra of 29Si (Fig. S8 in the ESM) and 27Al (Figs 2(g)–2(i)) corroborate that an elevated Si/Al ratio results in an increase in the number of bridging oxygens of [SiO4] and a greater proportion of [AlO4] structures [25−27]. This implies that more [AlO4] and nonisolated [SiO4] network formers are incorporated into the glass structure, contributing to a more complete and dense glass structure. To achieve high crystallinity, the Si/Al ratio must be fine-tuned to an optimal level (Fig. S9 in the ESM).

By applying the proposed composite regulation strategy, the crystallinity of the NaLuF4:Tb3+-based GC was successfully increased from 7% to 58%, while a transmittance of 90% @ 542 nm was preserved (Table 1).

The X-ray excited luminescence (XEL) behavior was subsequently examined. As depicted in Fig. 3(a), owing to the localized enrichment of activators (Tb3+) within the NC region of GC, the XEL process can be simplified into three sequential stages: (1) energy absorption and conversion, (2) carrier migration and transport, and (3) radiative transition of Tb3+ [28−32]. On the basis of maintaining transmittance, the emission intensities of the 5D3→7F*J* (*J* = 5, 4, 3) and 5D4→7F*J* (*J* = 6, 5, 4, 3) transitions for Tb3+ ions under X-ray excitation strongly depend on the crystallinity; specifically, a higher crystallinity is correlated with a stronger XEL intensity (Figs. S10–S12 in the ESM). Consequently, subsequent investigations focused solely on the XEL behavior of GCs with optimal crystallinity and transparency over varying concentrations of Tb3+ (Fig. 3(b)). By controlling the substitution of Lu3+ with Tb3+ (5–40 mol%), the efficiency of the process (2) is enhanced, resulting in a monotonic increase in the XEL intensity (Fig. 3(c)). However, a further increase in the Tb3+ concentration leads to XEL quenching due to cross-relaxation between Tb3+ ions [33], which was also observed in the photoluminescence (PL) behavior (Fig. 3(c) and Figs. S13–S15 in the ESM). The 40 mol% Tb3+ sample displays a luminescence lifetime of 444 μs upon X-ray irradiation (Fig. 3(d)), suggesting its potential for detecting slow events [31]. Notably, the XEL lifetime is significantly shorter than the PL lifetime, possibly owing to the differences in their excitation mechanisms: PL involves nonsaturated excitation, whereas XEL operates under saturated high-energy excitation conditions. Additionally, as revealed from an X-ray radiation stability test performed over three cycles (*T* = 60 min), the prepared GC exhibited only a slight intensity reduction (~2.5%) during the first initial cycle, followed by exceptional XEL stability in the subsequent cycles (Figs. 3(e)–3(g)).

Furthermore, the potential application of the proposed GC in X-ray imaging was assessed. As depicted in Fig. 4(c), the sharp image of the chip’s structure underscores the superior scintillation properties. An observation limit exceeding 20 lp·mm−1 was achieved (Figs. 4(d) and 4(e)). To quantitatively evaluate the quality of X-ray imaging, the slanted-edge method was employed to obtain the modulation transfer function (MTF) curve [42]. Considering that a spatial frequency of MTF = 0.2 is conventionally defined as the spatial resolution, the image resolution is approximately 25.3 lp·mm−1 (Fig. 4(f)). The spatial resolution of X-ray imaging actually hinges on both the LY and transmittance of scintillators. For example, Na5Lu9F32:Tb3+ GC has an impressive XEL intensity of approximately 219.9% of that of BGO; however, its imaging resolution is constrained to 18 lp·mm−1 because of its relatively low transmittance of ~60% at 542 nm [43]. Conversely, Tb3+-activated oxyfluoride glass, despite having an XEL that is approximately 26% lower, achieves a resolution of 20 lp·mm−1 owing to its higher transmittance of ~80% at 542 nm [10]. Our GC currently has a record transmittance of 90% at 542 nm. This high transmittance effectively compensates for its slightly lower LY, leading to superior imaging resolution compared with those of most existing fluoride glass- and GC-based scintillators (Table 2) [10,14,43−48].

## 4 Conclusions

By employing a comprehensive regulation strategy that involves regulating the heat-treatment temperature, modulating the raw material amount for the NCs, and adjusting the glass network structure, we achieved high levels of both crystallinity and transparency in oxyfluoride glass-ceramics (GCs). Notably, the resulting GCs embedded with NaLuF4:Tb3+ nanocrystals demonstrated exceptional properties, including a transmittance of 90% at 542 nm, a crystallinity of 58%, a light yield of 10,200 photons·MeV−1, a detection limit of 1.26 nGy·s−1, and an image resolution of 25.3 lp·mm−1. These findings establish the developed GCs as promising candidates for high-resolution X-ray imaging and high-sensitivity X-ray detection applications, while the proposed method can provide valuable insights into the design and development of high-performance scintillating GCs.
