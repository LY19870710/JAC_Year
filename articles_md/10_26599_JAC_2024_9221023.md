## 1 Introduction

Currently, electrochemical energy storage systems have attracted significant attention because of their high energy density and conversion efficiency, which aligns with the growing demands of social development [1−3]. Sodium-ion batteries (SIBs), which possess the advantages of ample raw materials and stable performance, have emerged as economically viable options for large-scale energy storage [4−6]. However, most SIBs rely on organic liquid electrolytes, with potential risks of leakage and flammability during battery operation [7−9]. In response to potential safety hazards, sodium-ion solid electrolytes (SSEs) are actively being developed, heralding their broader implementation in large-scale energy storage applications [10].

Various SSEs are under investigation, including polymer solid electrolytes [11], sulfide solid electrolytes [12], and oxide solid electrolytes [13]. Among them, Na super ionic conductour (NASICON)-type Na3Zr2Si*2*PO12 (denoted as NZSP) oxide solid-state electrolyte has received extensive attention recently because of its good stability and wide electrochemical window [14−16]. The NASICON structure features a three-dimensional framework, with SiO4/PO4 tetrahedra connected to the top corners of the ZrO6 octahedra [17]. This framework facilitates the synergistic diffusion of Na ions. However, the maximum ionic conductivity of conventional NASICON electrolytes is only 0.67 mS·cm−1, which limits their practical application [18−20].

To increase the ionic conductivity of NZSP electrolytes, numerous studies have focused predominantly on strategies involving the incorporation of larger-radium ions [21−25]. For example, Wang *et al*. [26] demonstrated that replacing Zr4+ with trivalent Sc3+ in Na3.4Zr1.6Sc0.4Si2PO12 samples increased the ionic conductivity to 1.77 mS·cm−1 at room temperature. The improvement resulted from Sc3+ doping, which not only increased the proportion of the rhombohedral structure and cell volume but also altered the Na‒O bond length and the area of the triangular bottleneck. Similarly, Wang *et al*. [27] reported the substitution of Zr4+ (72 pm) by Lu3+ (86.1 pm), Eu3+ (94.7 pm), and Pr3+ (99 pm). These ions with larger ionic radium facilitated broadening of the sodium ion transport channel, resulting in improved ionic conductivity. In addition, Park *et al*. [28] reported that lattice expansion resulting from the substitution of Ge4+ at the Zr4+ site in Na3Zr2Si2PO12 improved ionic conductivity. Owing to the larger ionic radius, high-temperature reducibility, and lower electronegativity of Ce, it is anticipated that Ce-doped NZSP would exhibit superior electrochemical properties. However, the improvement in the ionic conductivity of Ce-doped NZSP was limited in previous research [29]. Moreover, the mechanism behind the increase in ionic conductivity and the improvement in battery performance need to be further investigated.

In this work, Ce3+ was introduced for doping NZSP at the Zr4+ site. The effects of Ce doping on the phase transition, bottleneck size, valence state, and sintering behavior were examined through material characterization and theoretical calculations. The Ce-doped NZSP achieved an excellent room-temperature conductivity of 2.08 mS·cm−1 with a low electronic conductivity for Na3.2Zr1.8Ce0.2Si2PO12 at the optimized Ce content. The Na/Na3V2(PO4)3 cells equipped with the Na3.2Zr1.8Ce0.2Si2PO12 electrolyte exhibited high reversible capacity and good cycling stability, maintaining 111.18 mAh·g−1 after 200 cycles with a high retention of 98.06% at 0.5 C and 25 °C. The excellent electrochemical performance was attributed to the increased ionic conductivity, improved Na/electrolyte interfacial contact, and increased ability to inhibit dendrite growth. Therefore, Ce-doped NZSP provides favorable references for constructing NASICON-based solid-state sodium batteries (SSSBs) with excellent electrochemical performance.

### 2.1 Synthesis of solid-state electrolytes

The Na3+*x*Zr2−*x*Ce*x*Si2PO12 samples were synthesized via a conventional solid-state reaction method. The raw materials, including Na2CO3 (99.5%, Aladdin, China), ZrO2 (99.99%, Aladdin, China), SiO2 (99.99%, Aladdin, China), NH4H2PO4 (99%, Macklin, China), and CeO2 (99.95%, Aladdin, China) were measured according to the stoichiometric proportions. Owing to the volatility of Na2CO3 and NH4H2PO4 at high temperatures, 10% more raw materials were added than theoretically believed. The powders were mixed in anhydrous ethanol via a planetary ball mill (QM-QX, Nanda Instrument, China) for 12 h. The mixed slurry was dried at 80 °C for 24 h and then preheated at 800 °C for 10 h. The obtained precursor was dispersed in anhydrous ethanol for a second ball milling at 900 r·min−1 for 12 h. After drying, the powders were pressed into pellets. Finally, the pellets were covered with the same pristine powders and sintered at 1150 °C for 20 h in a tube furnace (OTF-1200X, HF-Kejing, China), followed by polishing to obtain the final solid electrolytes. The final thickness and diameter of the obtained electrolyte pellets were 1.55–1.60 and 12.8–13.2 mm, respectively.

### 2.2 Material characterizations

The crystal structures of Na3+*x*Zr2−*x*Ce*x*Si2PO12 (*x* = 0–0.25) were analyzed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany), with a 2*θ* range from 10° to 70°. The binding energy and bonding environment were analyzed via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; K-Alpha, FEI, USA). The thermal properties were evaluated via a differential scanning calorimetry (DSC; DSC 2500, TA, USA) in the temperature range of 25–200 °C. The microstructure and elemental distribution were investigated through a scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU-8010, Hitachi, Japan) combined with an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN, FEI, USA). The density was measured via Archimedes’ method. The mechanical properties were determined with a Nano indenter (G200, Agilent, USA). The indenter was a standard Berkovich indenter, and the test process adopted the static load mode, with 20 mN loading.

### 2.3 Density functional theory (DFT) computation

DFT calculations were performed with the Vienna *Ab initio* Simulation Package (VASP 6.4.2). The exchange‒correlation function used was the generalized gradient approximation (GGA). The interaction between valence electrons and ion cores was represented via the projected augmented wave (PAW) method. Grimme’s DFT-D3 correction was integrated to address the weak van der Waals forces. The convergence thresholds for the electronic self-consistent loop and ionic relaxation were set at 10−6 eV and 0.02 eV·Å−1, respectively. Integration over the Brillouin zone was conducted via a Monkhorst-Pack *k*-point grid with a resolution of 2π × 0.04 Å−1 across all the structures.

### 2.4 Conductivity characterizations

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed on an electrochemical workstation (CHI760E, Chinstrument, China) across a frequency spectrum from 1 Hz to 1 MHz. Prior to measurement, Au was sputtered onto both sides of the pellets to act as ion-blocking electrodes. The electronic conductivity of the SSEs was tested via direct-current polarization, where a constant voltage of 0.1 V was maintained for 2100 s.

### 2.5 Electrochemical measurements

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was used to evaluate the electrochemical window of the electrolytes in an asymmetric battery configuration consisting of a Na/Na3.2Zr1.8Ce0.2Si2PO12/stainless steel cell. Measurements were conducted within a voltage range from −0.5 to 6.5 V at a scan rate of 1 mV·s−1. Symmetric batteries, including Na/Na3Zr2Si2PO12/Na and Na/Na3.2Zr1.8Ce0.2Si2PO12/Na, were assembled to test the compatibility of the electrolytes. SSSBs were assembled in a glove box with a 2025 cell configuration, featuring Na3V2(PO4)3(NVP) as the cathode, Na3Zr2Si2PO12/Na3.2Zr1.8Ce0.2Si2PO12 as the solid electrolyte, and sodium metal as the anode. Details of NVP fabrication are displayed in Section S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Five microlitres of liquid electrolyte (1 M NaClO4 in ethylene carbonate (EC) : diethyl carbonate (DEC) = 1 : 1 vol% with 5.0% fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC)) was added between the electrolyte and the NVP to wet the interface. Electrochemical testing was carried out with a battery test system (CT-4008T-5V6A-S1, Neware, China) within a voltage window from 2.2 to 3.8 V.

### 3.1 Structural characterizations

Generally, the monoclinic phase and rhombohedral phase are the typical crystal structures of NZSP with space groups *C*2/*c* and *R*3*c*, respectively [30]. Owing to its favorable symmetry and larger diffusion channels for Na+ conduction [31], the rhombohedral phase is supposed to possess higher sodium ion conductivity than the monoclinic phase. To increase the overall ionic conductivity, increasing the content of the rhombohedral phase in the crystal structure of the electrolytes is crucial.

The phase structure of the Na3+*x*Zr2−*x*Ce*x*Si2PO12 (denoted as NZSP-*x*Ce, where *x* = 0–0.25) samples was investigated through XRD analysis (Fig. 1(a)). Compared with those of the standard crystal structure (PDF#84-1200), the dominant diffraction peaks of NZSP-*x*Ce corresponded to the peaks of NZSP. However, as shown in Fig. 1(b), the characteristic peaks generally shifted toward lower diffraction angles after Ce doping. This alteration could be attributed to the larger ionic radius of Ce3+ (1.02 Å) than that of Zr4+ (0.72 Å). The subsequent doping of Ce led to an increase in the lattice cell parameters, indicating the incorporation of Ce in the crystal structure. Typically, three characteristic peaks at 19°, 27°, and 34° could be employed to identify the monoclinic and rhombohedral structures in NZSP [32]. As shown in Fig. 1(b), the undoped sample displayed distinct double peaks at 19°, 27°, and 34°, representing the predominantly monoclinic typical structure of NZSP. Furthermore, as the concentration of Ce dopant increased, these double peaks gradually coalesced into single peaks. This transition indicated that the transformation of the monoclinic structure to the rhombohedral structure was driven by the substitution of Ce ions. In addition, the diffraction peak corresponding to Na3Ce(PO4)2 (PDF#48-0533) was observed in the NZSP-*x*Ce system when *x* ≥ 0.1, suggesting that a new phosphate phase was formed due to the limited solubility of Ce [33].

To gain in-depth insight into the relative phase compositions and lattice parameters of the NZSP-*x*Ce electrolytes, Rietveld refinement was performed via the GSAS program. The relative contents of the phases and the Rietveld refinements are illustrated in Figs. 1(c)–1(e). The undoped NZSP materials comprise two phases: 84.7% monoclinic structure (*a* = 15.641 Å, *b* = 9.043 Å, and *c* = 9.198 Å) and 15.3% rhombohedral structure (*a* = *b* = 9.096 Å, *c* = 22.987 Å). Upon Ce ion doping, the content of the rhombohedral phase gradually increased, in accordance with the analysis shown in Fig. 1(a). Specifically, NZSP-0.2Ce consisted of three phases: 38.2% monoclinic structure (*a* = 15.673 Å, *b* = 9.069 Å, and *c* = 9.190 Å), 41.5% rhombohedral structure (*a* = *b* = 9.129 Å, *c* = 22.886 Å) and 20.3% Na3Ce(PO4)2. The other refinement data are shown in Fig. S1 in the ESM. The cell volumes of both the monoclinic and rhombohedral structures expanded with increasing Ce doping concentration (Tables S1 and S2 in the ESM) [34].

It has been reported that two crystal structures can be transformed at a certain temperature [35]. Typically, the rhombohedral phase tends to stabilize at higher temperatures. It is essential to maintain the stability of the phase at lower temperatures for practical applications. To further examine the impact of Ce doping on the two-phase transition, DSC was performed. As depicted in Fig. 1(f), an endothermic peak at temperatures ranging from 150 to 200 °C can be observed, indicating the transition of the phase from the monoclinic phase to the rhombohedral phase in the electrolytes during the process. DSC revealed that the phase transition temperature (*T*p) of NZSP-*x*Ce (*x* = 0, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, and 0.25) samples were 164.73, 159.24, 156.72, 154.5, and 150.26 °C, respectively. The endothermic peaks that shifted from sharp to diffuse with a shift to lower temperatures were observed as the content of Ce doping increased. The substitution of Zr4+ with Ce3+ disrupted the original crystal symmetry and reduced the lattice stability, leading to a lower energy barrier for the phase transition. Thus, Ce doping could facilitate the transformation from monoclinic structure to rhombohedral structure at lower temperatures in the structure of NZSP.

To analyze the valence state and bonding environment of NZSP-*x*Ce, XPS was conducted. According to the Zr 3d scanning spectra, the binding energy of Zr4+ between NZSP and NZSP-0.2Ce remained essentially unchanged, demonstrating that the main structure remained unaffected (Fig. 2(a)). Figures 2(b) and 2(c) show shifts in the peak positions of P 2p and Si 2p spectra toward higher binding energies, indicating alterations in the electron density around the P and Si ions in NZSP-0.2Ce, which confirmed successful Ce doping within the NZSP matrix. Importantly, the peaks at 881.38 and 885.38 eV corresponded to Ce 3d5/2 of Ce3+, whereas the peaks at 899.98 and 904.08 eV corresponded to the Ce 3d3/2 peak of Ce3+ in NZSP-0.2Ce (Fig. 2(d)) [36]. However, the Ce element in the raw materials was in accordance with Ce4+ through XPS analysis, suggesting that all the Ce4+ was reduced to Ce3+ during prolonged high-temperature sintering. When Ce3+ entered the lattice to replace Zr4+, it could lead to an increased Na+ concentration to compensate for the decrease in positive charge. NZSP-0.1Ce, NZSP-0.15Ce, and NZSP-0.25Ce presented similar results, as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM.

Furthermore, the effect of Ce3+ doping on the Na+ transport channel was investigated through geometry-optimized structures obtained from DFT computations. The calculated results were consistent with the experimental results for the unit cell volume, confirming the reasonableness of the optimized structures (Table S3 in the ESM). In the monoclinic and rhombohedral structures, Na+ can traverse the triangular “bottleneck” region, which comprises three oxygen atoms from the ZrO6 octahedron and the SiO4/PO4 tetrahedron [37−40]. Larger bottleneck regions expedite Na-ion diffusion within the conduction channels, ultimately contributing to the lower energy barrier [41]. The standard rhombohedral structure features two inequivalent Na sites, Na1 and Na2, resulting in the production of two triangular bottleneck regions (T1, T2) [37,42]. With increasing Ce3+ concentration, the Zr–O bonds were partly replaced by Ce–O bonds. The ionic radius of the octahedral center and the distance between the oxygen ions within the PO4/SiO4 tetrahedra increased, resulting in expanded bottleneck areas. A comparison of the areas calculated via DFT computations in NZSP and NZSP-0.2Ce (Fig. 2(e)) revealed that NZSP-0.2Ce exhibited larger bottleneck areas than the undoped sample (Table 1). Additionally, the monoclinic structure of NZSP-0.2Ce also displayed increased bottleneck sizes compared with those of the undoped sample (as shown in Fig. S3 and Table S4 in the ESM), which could reduce the energy barrier for Na+ migration. Therefore, Ce doping, with its relatively large ionic radius, contributes to multiple effects on the crystal structure, including increasing the content of the rhombohedral structure and Na+ concentration, reducing the two-phase transition temperature, and expanding the size of the bottleneck areas for Na+ transport.

To further analyze the doping-induced microscopic morphology changes and mechanical responses of Ce-doped NZSP, SEM and nanoindentation measurements were conducted. SEM images of the cross-sections of NZSP-*x*Ce samples are shown in Figs. 3(a)–3(e). Notably, discernible microcracks resulting from the cooling process during sintering emerged along the grain boundaries in the undoped sample [43]. Upon Ce ion doping, the microcracks vanished, and the relative density obtained via Archimedes’ method markedly increased from 86.9% at *x* = 0 to 95.6% at *x* = 0.20 (Fig. S4 in the ESM) [44]. Furthermore, the grains in the undoped samples exhibited clear, faceted structures, whereas no obvious grains formed, and the grain boundaries became blurred in NZSP-*x*Ce (*x* = 0.10–0.25). This represented a shift in the fracture mode from the intergranular mode to the intracrystalline mode [19,45,46]. Typically, intergranular fractures reflect weak grain boundary strength, whereas intracrystalline fractures indicate that the strength of the grain boundary exceeds that of the grain [24,47]. The Ce doping promoted the grain boundary strength, which could reduce the grain boundary resistance of the samples. Consequently, Ce doping profoundly decreased the number of discernible microcracks and influenced the sintering behavior of the electrolyte, leading to a denser microscopic morphology. To clarify the elemental distribution of the electrolyte, EDS mappings of NZSP-0.2Ce are presented in Fig. 3(f). The results demonstrated that the distributions of Na, P, Si, and Zr were uniform in the sample, with Ce exhibiting partial clustering. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) was used to further analyze the partial aggregation with the assistance of EDS. The results are shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM. The accumulation of Na, Ce, and P and the absence of Zr and Si in distinct regions could be clearly observed, confirming the existence of Na3Ce(PO4)2, which was consistent with the results of the XRD analysis.

Existing ceramic defects are prone to be cracked if Na ions are inserted into the defects, which leads to dendrite nucleation [24,48,49,50]. Thus, the mechanical responses of electrolytes significantly influence their practical application. To analyze the mechanical properties of NZSP and NZSP-0.2Ce, nanoindentation measurements were conducted with five indentations per pellet. As displayed in Figs. 3(g) and 3(h), the mechanical strength distribution across the surface of NZSP-0.2Ce was more uniform than that across the surface of NZSP. Both the Young’s modulus (*E*) and hardness (*H*) significantly improved in NZSP-0.2Ce: The values of NZSP were 78.43±14.77 for *E* and 1.98±0.64 GPa for *H*, whereas those of NZSP-0.2Ce were 85.82±8.74 and 6.40±0.56 GPa, respectively (Fig. 3(i)). In general, hardness increases with decreasing porosity and grain size, suggesting that the enhancement in the mechanical properties of NZSP-0.2Ce is attributed to its denser microscopic morphology and closer grain interconnections.

First, introducing a larger radius of Ce3+ into NZSP stabilized the rhombohedral phase and broadened the bottleneck areas for Na+**,** which facilitated Na+ transport within the conduction channels (Figs. 1(c) and 2(e)). The lower electronegativity of Ce (1.12) in comparison with that of Zr (1.33) resulted in more ionic bonding properties of Ce–O bonds than of Zr–O bonds, resulting in a reduction in the proportion of shared electrons. This led to an increase in the migration energy of electrons and a decrease in electron conductivity [27,54]. An asymmetric cell comprising stainless steel/NZSP-0.2Ce/Na was tested (Fig. 4(d)) to determine the electrochemical window of NZSP-0.2Ce. Remarkably, there was no obvious oxidation current below 6.5 V. This result indicates that NZSP-0.2Ce exhibits a wide electrochemical window, which is favorable for enhancing the energy density of full cells.

To evaluate the compatibility of Ce-doped NZSP with sodium, symmetric cells based on NZSP and NZSP-0.2Ce were assembled. Figure 5(a) shows that the molten sodium formed an apparent contact angle of 125° on the surface of NZSP, whereas the angle decreased to 68° on the surface of NZSP-0.2Ce, indicating improved wettability of NZSP-0.2Ce to sodium. The dense microscopic morphology improved the interfacial contact between the metal Na and NZSP-0.2Ce. The Nyquist plots of the symmetric cells in Fig. 5(b) show that the resistance of Na/NZSP-0.2Ce/Na was significantly lower than that of Na/NZSP/Na because of the improved interfacial contact properties of NZSP-0.2Ce. In addition, the interfacial performance between Na metal and SSEs can be determined through time-resolved EIS [55]. As shown in Figs. 5(c) and 5(d), the impedance of Na/NZSP-0.2Ce/Na varied little during this period, in contrast to the more obvious impedance changes in the symmetric battery of NZSP. These results suggested that a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) quickly formed in the assembled NZSP-0.2Ce cell. On the basis of the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) method, time-resolved EIS was systematically analyzed to investigate the evolution of the SEI resistance. As shown in Figs. 5(f) and 5(g), the characteristic response at 10−6 s corresponded to the grain boundaries of the electrolytes and remained nearly unchanged over time. The peaks located at 10−5−10−4 s were attributed to the formation of the SEI, represented as *R*int, whereas the relaxation processes at 10−4–0.1 and 0.1–1 s represented the charge transfer process and diffusion process, respectively [56,57]. In the Na/NZSP/Na system, *R*int displayed pronounced signal variation, implying complex interfacial evolution. The instability hindered the formation of a stable interface between NZSP and Na metal, ultimately degrading the charge transfer and diffusion processes. More importantly, the Na/NZSP-0.2Ce/Na cell exhibited negligible changes in *R*int, with the signal intensity stabilizing after three days. Owing to the rapid formation of the SEI, parasitic reactions at the interface were further avoided, resulting in low interfacial resistance and excellent Na+ transport capability [58,59]. Additionally, the long-term cycling stability of symmetric cells was evaluated via Na plating/stripping experiments. When cycled at 0.1 mA·cm−2, the polarization voltage of the Na/NZSP/Na symmetric cell increased gradually after 180 h, followed by a sudden drop at 220 h due to short-circuiting caused by sodium dendrite penetration into the electrolyte sheet, at which time the polarization voltage was 532 mV (Fig. 5(e)). In contrast, the Na/NZSP-0.2Ce/Na cell exhibited a stable voltage response, with an initial polarization voltage of 79.9 mV. It remained stable after 500 h at room temperature, with the polarization voltage increasing to 93.3 mV (the average voltage increase was as low as 0.027 mV·h−1). Even at a higher current density of 0.2 mA·cm−2, Na/NZSP-0.2Ce/Na cell could still exhibit stable cycling performance for up to 200 h (Fig. S8 in the ESM). The excellent cycling performance verifies the long-term stability of theinterface and the effective inhibition of dendrite growth in NZSP-0.2Ce due to its improved Na/electrolyte interfacial contact,better mechanical properties, and lower electronic conductivity. These results demonstrate that Ce doping significantly enhances the electrochemical compatibility of solid electrolytes with sodium metal.

For further practical application, solid-state batteries were assembled and measured. As shown in Fig. 6(a), the CV curves of the NVP/NZSP-0.2Ce/Na cell overlapped well with one pair of strong redox peaks, indicating excellent reversibility of the battery in the process of extraction/insertion. Figure 6(b) presents the impedance spectra of the NVP/NZSP/Na and NVP/NZSP-0.2Ce/Na cells analyzed through simulated circuit fitting [60,61]. Two distinct semicircles were observed, which represented the electrolyte resistance and the impedance of the electrode/SSE interface. Compared with the NVP/NZSP/Na cell (3038 Ω), the NVP/NZSP-0.2Ce/Na cell displayed a lower interface resistance (853 Ω), reflecting the improved battery performance of the Ce-doped NZSP. The performance of the cells tested at different rates is shown in Fig. 6(c). The NVP/NZSP-0.2Ce/Na cell maintained capacities of 116.93, 116.37, 115.72, and 114.42 mAh·g−1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.5 C, respectively, which were significantly greater than those of the NVP/NZSP/Na cell. Although a slight increase in voltage polarization was observed with increasing charge/discharge rates, the NVP/NZSP-0.2Ce/Na cell still exhibited a more stable and flat charge/discharge plateau than did NVP/NZSP/Na (Figs. S9 and S10 in the ESM). Figure 6(d) shows the charge‒discharge curves at the rate of 0.2 C, and the cell with NZSP-0.2Ce exhibits lower charge‒discharge polarization and a higher initial discharge capacity of 110.01 mAh·g−1 than the 85.7 mAh·g−1 in the NZSP cell. The appropriate long-term cycling performance revealed that the discharge capacity of the NVP/NZSP-0.2Ce/Na cell was maintained at 108.91 mAh·g−1 after 100 cycles, with a capacity retention of 99% at 0.2 C (Fig. 6(e)). Even at a higher charge/discharge rate of 0.5 C (Fig. 6(f)), NZSP-0.2Ce demonstrated excellent battery cycling performance, sustaining a capacity of 111.18 mAh·g−1 after 200 cycles (98.06% capacity retention). Figure S11 in the ESM illustrates the charge/discharge curves of the cell across various cycles. The stable flat charge/discharge plateaus, which overlapped well at different cycles, corresponded to a reversible oxidation process from V3+ to V4+. Furthermore, a comparison of the EIS plots of the NVP/NZSP-0.2Ce/Na cell before and after 150 cycles at 0.5 C, as shown in Fig. S12 in the ESM revealed that the change in the interface resistance was only 6.3%, indicating good interfacial stability. This result could explain why there was almost no degradation in capacity during cycling. The great improvement in the performance of the NZSP-0.2Ce cell is due to two main aspects. On the one hand, the higher ionic conductivity reduces the ohmic polarization and facilitates the ion transfer kinetics. On the other hand, the improvement in the interfacial contact properties enhances the interfacial Na+ transfer. The synergistic improvement results in the lower polarization voltage and enhanced electrochemical properties. A comparison of published NZSP-based doped SSEs (Table S5 in the ESM) reveals that NZSP-0.2Ce has a notable advantage in terms of electrochemical performance. These results underscore that NZSP-0.2Ce can achieve excellent performance in SSSBs.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, we propose a novel method for fabricating excellent NZSP electrolytes with enhanced electrochemical properties via Ce doping. Through structural analysis and DFT calculations, it is inferred that Ce doping promotes the generation of the rhombohedral phase and increases the size of the bottleneck through which Na+ migrates. Ce4+ is reduced to Ce3+ during the high-temperature reaction process, which further increases the Na+ concentration. Additionally, the Ce-doped NZSP has a denser microscopic morphology and better mechanical properties. Therefore, an excellent room-temperature conductivity of 2.08 mS·cm−1 with a low electronic conductivity is achieved for NZSP-0.2Ce. The assembled Na3V2(PO4)3/Na3.2Zr1.8Ce0.2Si2PO12/Na cell presents excellent electrochemical performance, which is attributed to enhanced ionic transport and improved Na/electrolyte interfacial contact. The discharge capacity remains at 111.18 mAh·g−1 after 200 cycles, with a high capacity retention of 98.06% at 0.5 C. This work has shed light on the ionic transport optimization of NASICON-type electrolytes from the perspective of element doping, further facilitating the development of high-performance NASICON-based SSSBs.
