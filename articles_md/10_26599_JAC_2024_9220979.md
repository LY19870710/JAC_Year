## 1 Introduction

The monitoring and control of gas turbine engines involve high temperatures above 600 °C [1−6]. In the current automotive industry, a 150 °C environment is regarded as the standard for automobiles; however, in the near future, this high-temperature testing standard will be pushed to a new level, up to 500 °C. This achievement will not only increase the longevity of automobiles but also impose more stringent standards on the production process of automobiles and numerous components, including high-temperature sensors [7,8]. In the context of new energy research and exploitation, the pressure vessel outlet temperature of helium reactors can exceed 1000 °C, and new generation power plants, which are currently used at temperatures of 500–800 °C, will also be used above 1000 °C in the future [9,10]. Venus also poses a great challenge to conventional sensors because of its extremely high temperature (460 °C), high pressure (9 MPa), and strong corrosion [11]. High-temperature environments can lead to accelerated material aging and failure, destroying the structural integrity of the device [12−14]. High temperatures can also cause thermal expansion and contraction of components, placing additional stress on the mechanical structure. Therefore, application scenarios at extremely high temperatures are dependent on the development of high-temperature sensors to ensure that the cost of maintenance and energy can be reduced while increasing lifetime and reliability. Acceleration sensors can be used for quality detection, fault diagnosis, and vibration testing of rotating machinery such as motors, pumps, and turbines [15,16]. Continuous inspection can detect problems and potential failures as early as possible to prevent downtime and production disruptions during manufacturing. High-temperature acceleration sensors can withstand high temperatures without compromising their functionality or measurement accuracy, which is difficult to achieve with traditional sensors [17,18]. Therefore, the study of high-temperature acceleration sensors can ensure monitoring and control systems in critical areas such as aerospace, energy production, and automotive engineering, providing reliable base components for structural health monitoring, predictive maintenance, and overall system optimization.

A piezoelectric acceleration sensor is a type of acceleration sensor that is commonly used at high temperatures and has the advantages of high resonant frequency, a wide frequency response range, a small size, and a wide operating temperature range [8,19,20]. The operating temperature of a piezoelectric acceleration sensor mainly depends on the high-temperature resistance of piezoelectric materials of its core component. Currently, many studies have produced high-temperature piezoelectric sensors based on high-temperature piezoelectric materials that can be used at high temperatures. Compared with other types of sensors, their simple structure and stable performance make them more advantageous for use [21]. Jiang *et al*. [22] designed and produced a compression acceleration sensor based on Ba2TiSi2O8 (BTS) piezoelectric crystals. This sensor has a sensitivity of approximately 2.6–2.7 pC·g−1 and can work stably for more than 200 h at 600 °C and maintain ultrastable sensitivity at 650 °C for 1 h. The high resistivity and strong piezoelectric effect of the BTS crystal make it extremely advantageous in the field of high-temperature sensing, but its phase transition also leads to its applicable temperature range being extremely narrow. Kim’s group [23] produced a shear mode piezoelectric acceleration sensor based on a yttrium calcium borate (YCa4O(BO3)3, YCOB) single crystal in 2011, which can be used for high-temperature vibration sensing. The sensor was able to maintain sensitivity at 5.7 pC·g−1 in the frequency range of 80 Hz–1 kHz at an operating temperature of 1000 °C, indicating high stability and reliability. In 2021, the same group [24] developed a shear-type single-crystal aluminum nitride (AlN) accelerometer that can be applied to nuclear power plants under high-temperature conditions. The test results show that the sensor works normally under high-temperature conditions at 1000 °C, and the available frequency range is as high as 600 Hz; the sensitivity is 9.2 pC·g−1, and its performance does not decrease significantly after 10 h of high-temperature work. The key to designing and fabricating high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensors lies in the development of piezoelectric materials with high Curie temperatures and high stability [25,26]. Common high-temperature piezoelectric materials can be divided into piezoelectric single crystals and piezoelectric ceramics. Piezoelectric single crystals such as lithium niobate (LiNbO3) and YCOB are unsuitable for large-scale production because of their complex production processes and high production costs, although they have higher Curie temperatures. The piezoelectric properties of barium titanate and several high-temperature piezoelectric ceramics with tungsten bronze structures are poor, and the sensitivity of the acceleration sensor produced is very low. Therefore, it is necessary to further explore and study piezoelectric materials with high Curie temperatures, high sensitivity, good piezoelectric properties, and simple technology to prepare piezoelectric acceleration sensors [27−30].

As early as the 1950s, a bismuth-layered structure of ferroelectrics (BLSF) was discovered, and the structural peculiarity of this material is that it consists of bismuth oxide layers (Bi2O2)2+ and chalcocite-like layers (A*m*−1B*m*O3*m*+1)2− stacked alternately with each other along the *c*-axis, which has outstanding piezoelectric properties, dielectric properties, and high Curie temperatures [31]. As a bismuth-layered piezoelectric ceramic with the simplest structure, Bi2WO6 is also characterized by a low preparation cost and low synthesis temperature and has broad application prospects in the field of high-temperature sensing. However, the layered structure makes it difficult to fully polarize and flip, resulting in a low coercive field and poor piezoelectric performance. In this context, doping modification can be selected to enhance the material performance from a microscopic point of view. Among them, B-site doping with high-valent cations, which can reduce the concentration of oxygen vacancies, achieve a higher Curie temperature, and increase the piezoelectric properties, has been the first choice of many researchers to realize microscopic modification [32,33].

This work aims to address the current shortage of piezoelectric materials in the field of high-temperature sensing by developing a new system of piezoelectric ceramics with high-Curie temperature—bismuth tungstate (Bi2WO6, BW). Next, a high-temperature acceleration sensor was designed and fabricated using the prepared piezoelectric sensitive elements and placed in a prefabricated test platform for high-temperature sensing evaluation, providing ideas and references for the subsequent design and fabrication of high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensors.

## 2 Preparation and testing of BW piezoelectric ceramics

BW piezoelectric ceramics were prepared via solid-phase sintering. First, the raw material powders Bi2O3 (99.0 wt%, Zhongtian Fine Chemical Co., Ltd., China), WO3 (99.0 wt%, Zhongtian Fine Chemical Co., Ltd., China), and MoO3 (99.0 wt%, Zhongtian Fine Chemical Co., Ltd., China) were weighed according to a certain stoichiometric ratio via an analytical balance (ME204, Mettler Toledo Instruments Co., Ltd., China) and placed in a ball milling jar. An appropriate amount of deionized water was added to the ball milling jar, and then the ball milling jar was put into a planetary ball mill (KEQ-2L, Changsha Deko Instruments & Equipment Co., Ltd., China). The ball milling conditions were set at 250 r·min−1 for 12 h to ensure that the raw materials were mixed uniformly and that the particle sizes were consistent. After ball milling was complete, the evenly mixed slurry was dried at 90 °C for 12 h in an electric blast drying oven (XGQ-2000, Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China). The dried raw materials were sieved via an 80-mesh sieve and then pressed to obtain the ceramic billet. Subsequently, the press-formed billet was put into a muffle furnace (KF1100, Boyuntong Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., China) for ceramic sintering, and the sintering conditions were set at 820, 860, and 900 °C, with temperature increase time of 6 h and holding time of 3 h. After sintering was complete, silver electrodes were plated on the surface, and the sintered ceramic plate was immersed in 180 °C silicone oil and heated to carry out thermal polarization for 30 min. The polarization voltage was 7–9 kV so that a sample of BW piezoelectric ceramics could be obtained.

For BW piezoelectric ceramic samples, a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Gemini 300, Zeiss, Germany) and an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Bruker D8, Bruker, Germany) were used for morphology and physical phase analysis, respectively; an *in situ* confocal Raman spectroscope (Raman; LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba Scientific, Japan) was used for Raman spectroscopic tests; an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Thermo escalab 250Xi, Thermo Fisher, USA) was used for elemental analysis of samples; a digital multimeter DMM7510 from Keithley, USA, was used to measure insulation resistance; a ZJ-6A quasistatic *d*33*/d*31 meter (Institute of Acoustics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China) was used to measure the piezoelectric properties of the samples; a 4294A precision impedance analyzer from Agilent, USA, to analyze the ferroelectric properties of the samples; an RTI P-PMF2 ferroelectric test system from Radiant, USA, was used to test the hysteresis return lines of samples; an LF 427 thermal conductivity meter from NETZSCH, Germany, was used to test the coefficient of thermal expansion.

## 3 Performance analysis of BW piezoelectric ceramics

Samples BW-1, BW-2, BW-3, and BW-4 were obtained after doping Bi2Mo*x*W1−*x*O6 powder with Mo at four ratios of *x* = 0.0005, 0.001, 0.002, and 0.004, and they were sintered into porcelains at temperatures of 820, 860, and 900 °C, respectively. A comparison of the piezoelectric coefficient and microscopic morphology (Figs. S1 and S2 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) revealed that 900 °C was the optimal sintering temperature. The microscopic morphologies of the four samples after sintering at 900 °C are shown in Figs. 1(a)–1(d). The samples were all well-grown and had uniformly distributed grains, with obvious pores present at lower doping ratios, which gradually disappeared as the doping ratio increased, and the grains were tightly bound to each other with obvious grain boundaries. According to normal distribution statistics, the average grain sizes of BW-1, BW-2, BW-3, and BW-4 samples were 5.63, 2.27, 3.68, and 7.34 μm, respectively, indicating that the grain size of BW piezoelectric ceramics tends to decrease and then increase as the doping ratio of Mo increases. These changes in grain size during doping occur because Mo doping increases the migration and activation energy of grain boundaries, resulting in a slower grain growth rate and rapid size reduction. With increasing doping amount, more crystal defects and dislocations are formed, triggering the rearrangement and growth of grains, and the grain size begins to increase again [34]. Second, doped particles can enter the lattice and compete with the original tungsten and bismuth atoms, thus affecting the growth direction and speed of the crystal. When the doping content is low, lattice distortion will occur, and grain boundaries will form, which will lead to a reduction in the grain size. When the doping amount increases to a certain extent, too many doped atoms occupy the lattice position, resulting in an increase in the lattice size [35,36]. Figure 1(e) shows the XRD patterns of samples BW-1, BW-2, BW-3, and BW-4. The diffraction peaks of prepared BW ceramic samples are consistent with the standard card of Bi2WO6 (JCPDS No. 79-2381). It can be seen from the figure that the peak positions of the X-ray diffraction patterns do not change under different ratios, demonstrating that the materials with different ratios have the same structure; that is, the addition of Mo does not change the crystal structure type of BW ceramics, and it is still a single bismuth-oxygen layer alternating with a single [WO6]6– octahedral layer in the *Y*-axis direction. The diffraction peaks of the four groups of ceramics are similar; all form a single solid solution structure, and no obvious second phase is generated, indicating that the doped ions replace W6+ and diffuse into the lattice of BW ceramics. The main peaks of samples between 28° and 29° somewhat shift with the doping of Mo. This phenomenon may be due to the difference between the ionic radii of Mo6+ (*r*Mo6+ ≈ 0.59 Å) and W6+ (*r*W6+ ≈ 0.68 Å), as well as the certain distortion of the crystal after the substitution of doped ions, the decrease in the lattice constant, and the decrease in the lattice spacing in the crystal. The cell parameters of BW samples with different doping ratios can be calculated by fitting the XRD data via Jade software, as shown in Table 1. The error bars for the lattice parameters are presented in Fig. S3 in the ESM. With increasing Mo content, the volume of the crystal cell clearly decreases; when the doping ratio *x* = 0.0005, the volume of the crystal cell is 487.28 Å3. When the doping ratio increases to *x* = 0.004, the volume of the crystal cell decreases to 483.11 Å3. The lengths of the error bars are within reasonable limits, indicating that the errors in the lattice constants are small and that the stability of the lattice is good. Figure 1(f) shows the XRD patterns of BW piezoelectric ceramics at room temperature and 800 °C. The comparison reveals that the patterns at 800 °C are highly consistent with those at room temperature, with no spurious peaks generated and only small changes in peak intensity and peak position, suggesting that the ceramics have exceptional high-temperature stability. Figure 1(g) shows the Raman spectra of the four samples, which were fitted with peak splitting using Origin software to obtain 10 characteristic peaks located at 143, 221, 259, 281, 301, 327, 698, 707, 788, and 828 cm−1. The peaks below 200 cm−1 are attributed to the bending vibration of Bi–O and the simultaneous movement of Bi3+ and WO66−. As the content of Mo6+ gradually increases, the number of W‒O bonds decreases, which may be the reason for some decrease in the peak intensities at 698 and 707 cm−1. The strongest peak at 788 cm−1 and the nearby peak at 828 cm−1 may be attributed to the antisymmetric and symmetric stretching modes of tungstate [37]. Raman analysis revealed that the samples have good crystallinity and orthorhombic structure, which is in agreement with previous analyses.

Figure 2(a) shows the hysteresis loops of BW-1, BW-2, BW-3, and BW-4 ceramics. With increasing doping content, the hysteresis loops gradually narrow from the saturated circles, and the residual polarization intensity first decreases and then increases, which is related mainly to the change in the oxygen vacancy concentration. The O 1s XPS spectra of the four BW piezoelectric ceramic samples are shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM. The O 1s peaks are fitted to two different peaks, and the percentage areas of lattice oxygen and oxygen vacancies are shown below each peak. The peak with binding energy of approximately 529 eV corresponds to the lattice oxygen ion (O2−) in the oxygen octahedron, and the peak with binding energy of approximately 530 eV corresponds to the O2−/O− ion due to oxygen vacancies [39]. The variation in the oxygen vacancy concentration is inversely proportional to the amount of doped Mo. This is explained by the fact that, first, Bi3+ ions are easily volatilized during high-temperature sintering, forming bismuth vacancies and oxygen vacancies. Oxygen vacancies migrate and aggregate under the action of an applied electric field, generating the so-called pinning effect. This effect hinders the migration and flipping of the ferroelectric domains, and since the motion of the domain walls contributes significantly to the overall piezoelectric effect, the enhanced pinning effect leads to a decrease in the piezoelectric coefficient. The effect also reduces the residual polarization of the material, which is due to the presence of oxygen vacancies that reduce the electrical conductivity of the material and thus the effective storage capacity of electrical energy [40]. Second, Mo6+ and W6+ have different ionic radii and electronic structures. When the high-valence cation Mo6+ replaces part of W6+, it changes the space charge distribution in the material to a certain extent, resulting in local structural disorder. This structural change generates a localized electric field, which causes the surrounding dipoles to move directionally. As a result, the migration of defects such as oxygen vacancies in the crystal structure is hindered, and the concentration of oxygen vacancies decreases, thus weakening the pinning effect. Consequently, the residual polarization strength increases with time [41,42].

The trends of the dielectric constant and dielectric loss with temperature for the doped BW ceramic samples at 100 Hz, 1 kHz, and 1 MHz are shown in Figs. 2(b)–2(e). These figures indicate that the dielectric constants of the samples generally tend to reach the peak value first and then decrease sharply with increasing temperature. The dielectric constants of the four samples decrease sharply at 975, 966, 961, and 967 °C, and these transition temperatures are the Curie temperatures *T*C of the materials. At *T*C, the BW piezoelectric ceramic transitions from the ferroelectric phase of the tetragonal orthogonal structure to the paraelectric phase of the tetragonal structure, at which point the space dot matrix is *A*2/*m* [43]. Significant dielectric loss peaks appear at 723, 729, 796, and 738 °C at the 1 MHz scanning frequency, and the rapid change in dielectric loss here can be attributed to the quadratic phase transition that occurs in the bismuth tungstate structure at approximately 700 °C [44,45]. In association with the high-temperature XRD pattern of BW piezoelectric ceramics (i.e., Fig. S6 in the ESM), there is no obvious generation of spurious peaks at this time, accompanied only by a change in the peak intensity and a shift in the position of some peaks. The phase transition, although it does not change the orthorhombic structure of the material, may also lead to a drastic change in the electrical properties. The dielectric losses of the four BW ceramic samples consistently increase and then decrease with temperature, which is due to the change in their relaxation properties. At low temperatures, the defective dipoles in BW ceramic samples are subjected to less thermal energy, resulting in longer relaxation time, which cannot respond to changes in the applied electric field in time, and the materials exhibit transient polarization, leading to smaller dielectric constants and dielectric losses. As the temperature increases, the thermal energy of the defective dipole increases, and the relaxation time decreases. When the relaxation time of the defective dipole is comparable to the period of the external electric field change, the material exhibits relaxation polarization, with the result that the dielectric constant and dielectric loss increase. With a further increase in temperature, the relaxation time decreases further, and the relaxation process can keep up with the change in the external electric field, thus decreasing the dielectric loss to a low level [46].

The piezoelectric constants of the samples were tested after annealing the polarized samples at different temperatures, from which the depolarization properties of the samples were obtained, and the test results are shown in Fig. 2(f). The *d*33 values of all the samples may decrease slightly below the *T*C value, whereas it decreases rapidly when the annealing temperature is higher than *T*C. *d*33 at 800 °C remains above 90% of that at room temperature, suggesting that its properties are stable [47]. Its small decrease can be attributed to the interaction of defect dipoles and domain walls in BW piezoelectric ceramics, which inhibits their motion at lower temperatures, whereas the defects gradually randomize and *d*33 becomes more stable when the temperature is higher and even progressively approaches *T*C [48]. The annealing temperatures of Bi2Mo*x*W1−*x*O6 (*x* = 0.0005, 0.001, 0.002, and 0.004) were 975, 975, 950, and 950 °C, respectively. When *x* = 0.001, the high-temperature insulation resistances of the sample at 300, 500, and 800 °C are 206.57, 70.805, and 36.35 MΩ, respectively, and the corresponding high-temperature resistivities are 1.558×109, 5.338×108, and 2.741×108 Ω·cm, respectively.

To investigate the ferroelectric domain flip behavior of BW piezoelectric ceramics under an applied electric field, PFM tests were performed over a 10 μm × 10 μm area on the piezoelectric ceramic surface using a direct current (DC) voltage from −10 to +10 V. The test results are shown in Figs. 2(g)–2(l). Figures 2(g) and 2(h) show the in-surface magnitude and phase plots respectively; Figs. 2(i) and 2(j) show the out-of-surface magnitude and phase plots respectively; Figs. 2(k) and 2(l) show the hysteresis return lines of the magnitude and phase, respectively. In the PFM magnitude and phase plots, the shades of the color represent the strength of piezoelectric response and flip–flop of ferroelectric domains. The color of the PFM amplitude plots is consistent with little variation, which represents the stable and uniform distribution of piezoelectric properties within each region of BW piezoelectric ceramics. The amplitude response is a typical butterfly curve, indicating that the test region has ferroelectricity. The PFM phase diagram has a strong contrast between light and dark, showing a clear ferroelectric domain wall, indicating that the BW piezoelectric ceramics have good ferroelectricity and a ferroelectric domain inversion phenomenon, which is also proven again by the nearly 180° flip of the phase response.

After a systematic and in-depth study of BW piezoelectric ceramic materials, the optimal doping ratio for subsequent high-temperature testing can be accurately determined by analyzing its many key performance indicators, which also provides preliminary optimization for the performance of piezoelectric acceleration sensors. When *x* = 0.0005, the piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33) is the smallest, and the relative density is the lowest. When *x* = 0.002, the residual polarization intensity is small, and the high-temperature annealing temperature is also low. When *x* = 0.004, *d*33 decreases as the high-temperature annealing temperature and the Curie temperature decrease. In contrast, when *x* = 0.001, *d*33 reaches its maximum, which is the most important indicator for determining the performance of high-temperature sensing. On the other hand, the Curie temperature and high-temperature annealing temperature of piezoelectric ceramics at this doping ratio are very high, which indicates that piezoelectric ceramics can withstand higher working temperatures and have better high-temperature performance. This doping ratio not only ensures that the sensor can still operate stably under extreme temperature conditions but is also expected to improve the sensitivity and response speed of the sensor to adapt better to various practical application scenarios. Therefore, *x* = 0.001 was selected as the optimal doping ratio for the subsequent high-temperature test of sensors. At this time, the quality factor (*Q*m), the dielectric constant (*ε*), and the dielectric loss (tan*δ*) are 1642, 307 (1 kHz), and 0.33 (1 kHz). A comparison of the properties of BW-2 piezoceramics with those of other bismuth-layered piezoelectric materials that can be stabilized at high temperatures is shown in Table S1 in the ESM. This further demonstrates that the material has great potential for applications in high-temperature sensing [49−60].

### 4.1 Modeling of high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor

Dynamic modeling of sensors enables the evaluation of their performance at a theoretical level and the analysis of various factors affecting their performance. This approach also helps in selecting the best structure and part sizes, increases the efficiency of design optimization, and improves the performance of the sensor while avoiding extensive validation experiments. In this work, on the basis of the actual structure of a piezoelectric compression acceleration sensor, an improved central compression structure is proposed, and a dynamic model of the sensor is established step by step. On this basis, various factors affecting the inherent frequency of the sensor are further discussed, and the calculation accuracy of inherent frequency is verified.

The sensor consists of a mass block, a housing, piezoelectric ceramic plates, a base, bolts, and insulating spacers, all of which are connected together by the preload force exerted by the preload nuts. To further study the structure and vibration characteristics of the sensor, the piezoelectric acceleration sensor is converted into a multidegree-of-freedom vibration model here, and mechanical modeling is carried out, as shown in Fig. 3(a).

#### 4.2.1 Calculation of inherent frequency

#### 4.2.2 Simulation verification of the intrinsic frequency

The sensor was modeled via SOLIDWORKS, and the model drawn in SOLIDWORKS was imported into COMSOL Multiphysics finite element simulation software to construct solid objects. First, we need to simplify the structure of the sensor. There are several very small parts in the sensor, such as the conductive layer and wire fixing holes, which will not affect the final results if they are ignored when analyzing the problem, so these small parts can be omitted from the analysis of the sensor. In the end, only four key parts of the sensor are retained: the base, the mass block, the nut, and the piezoelectric sensitive element. Second, the materials were constructed using the predefined material parameters from the material library in COMSOL software. 0Cr19Ni9 austenitic stainless steel was used for the base and mass block, and alumina ceramics were used for the bolts, insulating sheets, and nuts. Mo-doped BW piezoelectric ceramics are customized materials, so the material construction was completed by inputting the material’s elasticity matrix, coupling matrix, and dielectric matrix, among other parameters.

Mesh hyperfine division of the sensor model was performed, and the total complete mesh contains 91,497 domain cells, 11,176 boundary cells, and 1093 edge cells. The generated network division model is shown in Fig. 3(b). In such a setup, the sensor will generate a sinusoidal displacement signal, and the piezoelectric element will generate an output signal accordingly. Calculations were performed to solve for the intrinsic frequency value and vibration pattern, which is shown in Fig. 3(c), and the intrinsic frequency value is 24.62 kHz, which is close to the previous calculations.

### 4.3 Assembly of the high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor

The overall size of the designed compressive acceleration sensor is 44 mm × 50 mm × 35 mm. The modeling of each component of the sensor is processed, and each component obtained from the processing as well as the assembled object is shown in Figs. 3(d) and 3(e). Four pieces of Mo-doped BW piezoelectric ceramic plates of the same size and polished as sensitive elements were placed into the grooves on the bottom insulating sheet to improve the stiffness coefficient and thus increase the intrinsic frequency of the sensor, which constrains the displacement of the sensitive element in the horizontal direction, avoiding the piezoelectric signals generated by shear force, reducing the lateral sensitivity and ensuring the accuracy of measurement results [62,63]. This approach also avoids the use of traditional ceramic perforation design, which is a complicated process and reduces the contact area of the sensitive element. In addition, to ensure the stable performance of the sensor at high temperatures, the temperature limits of each material are taken into account in the design of the assembly, and the upper operating temperature limit of all materials used in the sensor assembly is higher than the test temperature (~800 °C).

### 5.1 Room-temperature performance of high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor

To validate the room-temperature performance of the high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor, sinusoidal signals with a frequency range of 100–2500 Hz were generated via a signal generator. Moreover, the excitation system was set to operate at gravitational accelerations of 0.5*g*, 1*g*, 1.5*g*, 2*g*, 2.5*g*, and 3*g*. The sensor signals were obtained after sampling and denoising. As shown in Fig. 4(a), the output waveforms of the standard acceleration sensor, the BW high-temperature acceleration sensor, and the driving voltage are compared at 160 Hz and 1*g*. The charge sensitivity of the standard sensor at 160 Hz is 10 pC·g−1. This coincides with the calibration value in the manual, which indicates that the test results are accurate. At this time, the charge sensitivity of the BW high-temperature acceleration sensor reaches 2.34 pC·g−1. The local amplification of the output waveform is shown in Fig. 4(b), in which the rise time of the BW high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor is 1.799 ms, and the fall time is 1.769 ms. Undoubtedly, the rise time and fall time are very short, and the whole rise and fall process is completed quickly and smoothly without obvious delay or jitter, which reflects the stability and accuracy of the vibration measurement system and ensures the continuity and reliability of the output signal. Figure 4(c) shows the output of the BW high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor at 200 Hz under different acceleration excitations. As the acceleration increases, the amplitude of the sensor’s voltage output also increases, which is proportional to the acceleration. This proportional relationship is presented in Fig. 4(d), and the results show that the voltage output of the sensor is linearly correlated with the acceleration with high linearity. The results were fitted via the end-point method, the linear function obtained is * y=32.05x−1.225, and the deviation of the sensor input‒output curve from the fitted straight line is ±4.34%. When the frequency and gravitational acceleration are kept constant and the driving voltage is varied in the range of 0.5–3 V, the resulting relationship between the output and the driving voltage is shown in Fig. 4(e). The output voltage increases with increasing driving voltage and the phase is consistent. The frequency also has an important effect on the sensitivity of the acceleration sensor. Figure 4(f) shows the change curve of the sensitivity of the BW high-temperature acceleration sensor with frequency, i.e., the frequency response curve. In the frequency range of 0.1–1 kHz, the sensitivity of the device remains stable; above 1 kHz, the sensitivity increases steeply and reaches a peak value. Using the peak function to fit the curve, the resonant frequency of the BW high-temperature acceleration sensor is calculated to be 1290 Hz at room temperature. The frequency response curve indicates that the BW high-temperature acceleration sensor can be used steadily up to 1 kHz at room temperature, with an average sensitivity of 3.259 pC·g−1. Figures 4(g)–4(i) show the output of the BW high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor under different accelerations at 100, 300, and 500 Hz. For the signal output at vibration frequencies of 300 and 500 Hz, the change rule is the same as that at 200 Hz, which is a certain proportional relationship, whereas the output at 100 Hz is not proportional; this is because at 100 Hz, the vibration frequency is low, resulting in greater noise interference with the output signal.

### 5.2 High-temperature performance of high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor

The BW high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor was placed in a muffle furnace, and the muffle furnace was gradually heated from room temperature to 800 °C; the output of the device was tested at different accelerations and frequencies at 100 °C intervals to study the high-temperature performance of the BW high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor. The output waveforms of the BW high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor at temperatures of 100, 500, and 800 °C with a gravitational acceleration of 1g and a frequency of 160 Hz are compared with those of the standard sensor, as shown in Figs. 5(a)–5(c). As the furnace temperature increases, the output voltage of the sensor changes continuously and reaches 39.65 mV at 500 °C. When the furnace temperature reaches 800 °C, the output signal of the sensor increases to 147.88 mV. The increase in the voltage signal is caused by the quadratic phase transition of the BW piezoceramics between 600 and 700 °C. Figure 5(d) shows the relationship between the output of the BW high-temperature sensor and the gravitational acceleration at 200 Hz at different temperatures. When the gravitational acceleration is held constant, the output voltages at different temperatures do not fluctuate much, but they generally increase and then decrease with increasing temperature. There are two main reasons for this phenomenon. First, it is related to the thermal expansion coefficient of BW piezoelectric ceramics, as shown in Fig. S7 in the ESM. BW piezoelectric ceramics exhibit nonlinearly enhanced negative thermal expansion, and at 100–300 °C, the temperature increase causes the thermal expansion coefficient of BW piezoelectric ceramics to increase steeply, making it difficult for the sensor to respond quickly and affecting the mechanical properties. At 300–500 °C, the slow increase in the coefficient of thermal expansion enables the device to respond quickly to various input changes, promoting a temporary increase in output. When the temperature increases to a certain threshold, the increase in this coefficient tends to saturate, and the mechanical properties are gradually reduced due to high-temperature damage, which ultimately leads to a decrease in output [64,65]. Second, the increase in temperature changes the size of the sensor components, which affects the transmission of current and voltage inside the sensor as well as the sensor’s ability to respond to changes in current and voltage. Initially, the sensor size increases slightly because of the high temperature, and the sensor is able to respond more quickly and accurately to changes in the internal electrical signals, with the sensitivity increasing during the process. As the temperature increases further, the change in sensor size reduces the transmission efficiency of the internal circuitry and its ability to capture external signals, so the overall sensor sensitivity also decreases [66,67]. Figure 5(d) also reveals that the output voltage of the sensor at the same temperature and frequency is linearly related to the gravitational acceleration, which is highly consistent with the performance at room temperature. Through linear fitting, the linearity at different temperatures can be obtained, as shown in Fig. 5(e), and the linearity of the output voltage reaches more than 0.99 and as high as 0.9997 at 100 °C, which clearly shows that the sensor exhibits excellent linear performance. It still maintains high reliability and very high accuracy under extremely high-temperature environments. Figure 5(f) shows the frequency response of the BW high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor at room temperature and 800 °C. The device can be used stably in the range of 0.1–1.1 kHz at high temperatures. Moreover, there is no major fluctuation in the frequency response in this range. To verify the stability of the BW high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor, a sensitivity deviation was introduced to determine the temperature drift due to temperature change, which is defined as the percentage of the difference between the test temperature sensitivity and the room temperature sensitivity relative to the room temperature sensitivity. The average sensitivity at 800 °C is 3.305 pC·g−1, with a deviation from room temperature of 1.4%. Figure 5(g) shows the three-dimensional comparison of the output voltage at 300 Hz with different temperatures and accelerations; according to this figure, the charge sensitivity at different temperatures can be obtained, as shown in Fig. 5(h). Figure 5(i) shows the deviation of the BW high-temperature piezoelectric acceleration sensor at 300 Hz, and the analysis reveals that the maximum temperature drift does not exceed 14%, once again verifying the ultrahigh stability of the device.

## 6 Conclusions

In this work, Mo-doped bismuth layer piezoelectric ceramics Bi2Mo*x*W1−*x*O6 were created via a solid-phase sintering process and employed as the core piezoelectric element to fabricate a high-temperature compression acceleration sensor. The high-temperature characteristics of BW piezoceramics are discussed in detail. When *x* = 0.0005, 0.001, 0.002, and 0.004, the Curie temperatures are 975, 966, 961, and 967 °C, respectively, and the annealing temperatures are 975, 975, 950, and 950 °C, respectively. The best performance was obtained at *x* = 0.001: The relative density was 92% and *d*33 = 15 pC·N−1, *T*C = 966 °C, *Q*m = 1642, *ε* = 307 (1 kHz), and tan*δ* = 0.33 (1 kHz). The compressive acceleration sensor was subjected to tightly controlled vibration and temperature environments to validate its high-temperature sensing properties. At room temperature, the BW piezoelectric acceleration sensor can be used stably within 1 kHz with an average sensitivity of 3.259 pC·g−1. At 800 °C, the device can be used in the frequency range of 0.1–1.1 kHz, and the average sensitivity is 3.305 pC·g−1; the linearity is greater than 0.99, and the sensitivity deviation is 1.4%. The vibration test from room temperature to 800 °C fully proves that the BW acceleration sensor has excellent linearity, sensitivity, and stability and shows the application potential of BW piezoelectric ceramic materials in the field of high-temperature sensing.
