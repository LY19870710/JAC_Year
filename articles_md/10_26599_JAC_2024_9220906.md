## 1 Introduction

Alumina is an excellent engineered ceramic with a high melting point and corrosion resistance and has been widely used in the aviation, aerospace, navigation, energy, and other defense industries [1]. However, due to the brittleness, weak ductility, and processing difficulties of alumina, workpieces with large and complex shapes are difficult to form directly in one step. To reduce production costs, most complex engineering workpieces are connected by simple components, making the related connection process highly valued [2,3].

Currently, the main connection methods for ceramics include mechanical connecting [4−6], slurry joining [7−9], metal brazing [10−13], liquid phase connecting [14−16], and adhesive bonding [17,18]. Mechanical connecting is a method of joining ceramics using mechanical structures such as bolting and riveting. Due to the need for drilling, this method is expensive and often introduces severe stress concentration in machined components, which is not conducive to service life in extreme environments. In the slurry joining method, a ceramic slurry is applied to the surface of a ceramic billet, and the components are connected through sintering. Kim *et al*. [8] used Al/Al2O3 powder as an intermediate layer to connect alumina green bodies, and high-strength joints with a tensile strength of 495±40 MPa were obtained through the oxidation of Al after sintering at 1650 °C for 2 h under an air atmosphere. Han [9] used a mixture slurry of alumina sol and suspension to connect partially sintered green alumina compacts, and the average bonding strength of the joined rods reached 248.3±24 MPa after sintering at 1650 °C for 2 h. Despite the high connection strength, the slurry joining method is only applicable to the preparation of complex workpieces with small sizes, and it is not possible to reconnect the sintered alumina; thus, it is less versatile and cannot meet the needs of field installation or fixation. Brazing is a method of joining by changing the activity of the joining surface using a metal braze, which causes a chemical reaction between the braze and the surface. Zhang *et al*. [10] used an active Ni50Ti50 alloy as a braze and brazed alumina ceramic under vacuum conditions, and the joint was held at 1320 °C for 45 min and reached a strength of 109 MPa. Lu *et al*. [11] used a Ni–45Ti alloy for vacuum brazing alumina workpieces. The maximum shear strength of the joint was 125.63±4.87 MPa when brazed for 30 min at 1350 °C. Although the brazing method provides high connection strength, it usually requires a vacuum environment provided by expensive brazing equipment. The connected workpieces are usually small due to the equipment specifications, and the process cannot be applied to the field connections of large components or the connection of hard-to-reach parts such as the inner walls of pipes. Liquid bonding is a method of achieving a refractory joint by filling an intermediate layer with a lower melting point between the bonding substrates, forming a liquid phase to wet the surface of the substrate at high temperature, and reacting to generate a connecting layer with a higher melting point [14]. Zhu *et al*. [16] screen-printed B2O3 as the joining material on an alumina surface and applied a pressure of 30 MPa to the joint for 10 min at 1000 °C with plasma system sintering. The average shear strength of the joints also reached 149±4.1 MPa, which is impressive for structural components. However, this connection method requires special insulation conditions, complex instruments, and a certain amount of pressure, making installing large workpieces on site impossible.

Compared to the above connection methods, adhesive bonding has the advantages of not being limited by equipment and on-site conditions, not damaging components, and being easy to operate, making it particularly suitable for connecting and fixing components in complex areas [19−22]. Due to their low stress concentration and high specific strength, adhesives are widely used in the aerospace and transportation fields. For example, the bonding area of the fuselage and wings of an aircraft can reach 80% of the surface area of the whole aircraft. The hot melt adhesive used for bonding on aircraft can reach 80% of the maximum strength in just a few seconds [23]. With the increasingly high requirements for extreme high-temperature environments faced by aerospace and other defense industries [24], the demand for high-performance, high-temperature resistant adhesives has become broader and more stringent for many applications. High-temperature resistant adhesion technology is highly suitable for joining engineered ceramics because of its good high-temperature mechanical properties, excellent fatigue resistance, highly matched physical and chemical properties (with ceramic substrates), and high degree of interfacial bonding [25−27]. In addition, adhesion technology is particularly suitable for on-site installation, fixation, and repair of large ceramic workpieces because of its ability to provide effective connections within the entire temperature threshold of extremely environments after curing at room temperature (RT, without the need for postprocessing). High-temperature-resistant adhesives have been widely used for the connection and fixation of C/C [28−30], SiC [31−33], Si3N4 [34], ZrB2 [35], Al2O3 [36−38], mullite, etc., and the applications involve the installation of fairing and the fixation of internal high-temperature sensors, the installation and fixation of insulating tiles/cotton/cloth [39], heat insulation and sealing of engines and tailpipe nozzles, etc.

High-temperature-resistant adhesives can be divided into two main categories. One is ceramic precursor (modified polymer-based) adhesives, and the other is inorganic ceramic adhesives represented by phosphates and silicates. Ceramic precursor-type high-temperature adhesives achieve the connection of ceramics through the transformation of polymers into ceramics. Luan *et al.* [36] used polyborosilazanes to connect alumina, and the joining strength reached 15.73 MPa through the generation of SiC and Si3N4 under the conditions of sintering at 1500 °C for 2 h at a pressure of 1.5 KPa. Qin *et al.* [37] used TiB2 and glass powder-modified polysiloxane to bond alumina, and the shear strength of the joint reached 21 MPa after sintering at 1200 °C for 2 h. Although the ceramic precursor-type adhesive possesses adequate bonding strength after ceramicization, its stability and safety are poor during the transformation period at 400–600 °C, which often prevents its use within this temperature range. In contrast, inorganic ceramic-type high-temperature resistant adhesives have a wider temperature range, greater structural retention, greater mechanical stability, and greater safety. Among them, inorganic phosphate-based adhesives prepared from phosphoric acid and oxides or hydroxides can be cured at RT, with a temperature resistance of up to 1700 °C, and can maintain a certain bonding strength throughout the entire temperature range. Metal cations can be divided into aluminum phosphate adhesives, zirconium phosphate adhesives, magnesium phosphate adhesives, etc. Volceanov *et al.* [40] prepared zirconium phosphate adhesives for bonding magnesite refractory materials according to P2O5/ZrO2 ratios of 2.30, 1.15, and 0.77, and the bonding strength of the three adhesives exceeded 5 MPa. Hao *et al.* [41] prepared an aluminum phosphate adhesive using phosphoric acid and aluminum hydroxide as reaction sources modified with mullite and silica powder, and its bonding strength for mullite ceramics reached 6.58 MPa at 800 °C. Among these types of phosphate adhesives, aluminum phosphate adhesive has been reported to be the most widely used.

In our preliminary work, we investigated the influence of the Al/P ratio on the composition evolution and bonding performance of high-temperature resistant aluminum phosphate adhesives [42]. When the molar ratio of aluminum to phosphorus is not less than 1, the crystal transformation of phosphate in the high-temperature resistant adhesive is simplified, and the high-temperature composition is diversified; its bonding performance is much greater than that of conventional industrial aluminum dihydrogen phosphate. Based on this aluminum phosphate emulsion, we prepared a series of aluminum phosphate-based high temperature-resistant adhesives suitable for C/C, SiC, mullite, TC4, and nickel-based alloys by adjusting the filler type and content [19−22,29,30,43−45]. However, changing the formula solely based on fillers is outdated and has certain limitations in its effectiveness. An excessive variety or dosage of fillers can lead to poor adhesive uniformity, easy settling, and limited storage time. Even some fillers (such as magnesium oxide or copper oxide) can accelerate curing, affecting adhesive operation on large components. Apart from aluminum chromium phosphate, there are few reports on the study of other element-doped aluminum phosphate emulsions [46]. The doping of elements not only changes the viscosity and fluidity of emulsions but also participates in the formation of amorphous macromolecules and affects the network structure of molecules. The type and content of doping elements can affect the thermal stability and decomposition path of the macromolecular network structure, as well as the type and content of phosphates or oxides generated and their crystal transformation, thus further impacting the physical and chemical properties, thermal properties, and mechanical properties of phosphates at high temperatures. Therefore, studying the effect of element doping on aluminum phosphate-based high-temperature resistant adhesives has essential value and application prospects.

The coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of AlPO4 is 5.5×10−6 K−1 (~1000 °C), while that of Al2O3 is approximately (8.1–8.5)×10−6 K−1 (1000–1200 °C) [47−49]. The mismatch of CTE can generate considerable thermal stress during the heating process and affect the bonding effect. The bonding strength of pure aluminum phosphate adhesive for alumina is generally not greater than 5 MPa. Moreover, there are few reports on phosphate-based high-temperature resistant adhesives that can provide high-strength connections for alumina during multiple cold and hot cycles. Therefore, modifying phosphate-based high-temperature resistant adhesives to narrow the difference between their thermal expansion properties and those of alumina is the key to developing unique adhesives for alumina. Considering that ZrO2 has a high CTE of 10.6×10−6 K−1 [50], it is feasible to use it to regulate CTE of aluminum phosphate-based adhesives. On the basis of the preparation of phosphate-based adhesives through acid‒base reactions, zirconium hydroxide was used to modify the reaction between aluminum hydroxide and phosphoric acid. By introducing Zr into the solidified amorphous macromolecular network structure, the polymerization degree, adhesion, and thermal stability of the adhesive are significantly improved. Furthermore, ZrO2 generated during the heating process effectively reduces the difference in CTE between the adhesive matrix and alumina. Finally, a bonding strength of 67.2 MPa was achieved for alumina. The influence of the Zr/Al ratio on the slurry properties, bonding performance, composition evolution, thermal stability, thermal expansibility, and structural evolution of the adhesives was comprehensively studied.

### 2.1 Materials

Al2O3 substrates with dimensions of 40 mm×10 mm×5 mm were obtained from Mingchuang Innovation Materials Co., Ltd., China. All materials for preparing the adhesive were used directly without further treatment, including concentrated orthophosphoric acid (85 wt%, H3PO4, Rhawn Reagent, China), aluminum hydroxide (analytical purity, Al(OH)3, Macklin Reagent, China), zirconium hydroxide (97%, Zr(OH)4, Aladdin Reagent, China), boron carbide (*d* = 2.5 μm, B4C, Zhengxing Abrasive Co., Ltd., China), and silicon powder (*d* = 0.5 μm, Si, Tuoyi Trade Co., Ltd., China).

### 2.2 Preparation of adhesive

First, concentrated phosphoric acid at a concentration of 85 wt% was diluted with water to a concentration of 60 wt% and then placed in a water bath at 85 °C. Subsequently, a quantitative amount of Al(OH)3 was dissolved in the diluted phosphoric acid solution at a molar ratio of 1 : 1 (Al : P) to prepare an adhesive base solution under mechanical stirring. Finally, the aluminum phosphate adhesive was obtained by mixing the base solution, Si, and B4C at a 5 : 0.7 : 0.3 mass ratio under stirring for 0.5–1 h. Based on the above process, zirconium-modified aluminum phosphate adhesives with different contents of doped zirconium were prepared by replacing part of the aluminum hydroxide with zirconium hydroxide at mass ratios of 7 : 3, 5 : 5, and 3 : 7 (aluminum hydroxide : zirconium hydroxide). For ease of narration, the four types of high-temperature-resistant adhesives mentioned above are sequentially named A10Z0, A7Z3, A5Z5, and A3Z7.

### 2.3 Bonding joints and mechanical testing

The bonding surfaces were polished using diamond grinding wheels to reduce the impact of surface roughness and specifically investigate the properties of the adhesive. Before bonding, the Al2O3 substrates were ultrasonically cleaned in alcohol and then dried at 80 °C. The adhesives prepared above were applied to the surfaces of substrates and bonded with an effective bonding surface area of 10 mm × 10 mm. The bonded joints were then subjected to curing at RT, and the curing time was recorded. To clarify the changes in bonding strength during the curing process, shear tests were performed on bonded joints with different curing time with an electronic universal testing machine (CSS-44001, Changchun Testing Machine Factory, China) at a 0.5 mm/min testing rate. After curing, the cured joints were sintered at 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1300, and 1500 °C for 1 h at a heating rate of 5 °C/min and then cooled to RT in a box-type furnace (KSL-1700X, Kejing, China). After that, the joints were also subjected to shear tests with the CSS-44001 machine at a testing rate of 0.5 mm/min. In addition, thermal cycling and shock tests were conducted at 1300 and 1500 °C using a tube furnace (GSL-1700X, Kejing, China). The joints were heated and cooled with the furnace for different numbers of cycles at a rising or falling rate of 5 °C/min, while the joints were directly heated at a given temperature for 10 min and then transferred to an atmospheric environment for thermal shock. After different numbers of thermal cycles and thermal shock cycles, the residual strength of the joints was tested by the CSS-44001 machine under the same conditions. Moreover, the bonding strengths at high temperatures of 800, 1000, and 1200 °C were tested using a high-temperature tensile testing machine (RDL-15, Changchun Testing Machine Factory, China). The pretreated joints were placed in the testing fixture and heated to a specific temperature with the furnace equipped on the machine and then tested after holding for 10 min. The heating rate of the testing machine was between 3 and 20 °C/min, and the heating rate decreased with increasing temperature. In addition, the fracture toughness of the adhesive after treatment at different temperatures was tested through a three-point bending experiment according to standard GBT23806-2009. After treatment at different temperatures, the adhesive samples were cut and polished to 20 mm × 4 mm × 3 mm. The specific operation process is shown in Fig. 1.

### 2.4 Characterizations

The liquid properties of the adhesive were characterized by viscosity and curing time. The viscosity was tested every 0.5 h at room temperature using a rotary viscometer (NDJ-1B, Shanghai Changji, China). The curing time was evaluated by testing the antipuncture effect of the adhesive block at room temperature after different curing time. After curing, the solidified adhesive blocks were subjected to heat treatment, and the density of the adhesive after calcination at different temperatures was tested via the Archimedes method. Additionally, the hardness was tested by using a Cratos W50 Vickers hardness tester (Opton, China) under a pressure of 0.3 kg (HV0.3). The samples for measuring hardness needed to be polished first, and the surface roughness did not exceed 0.4 μm. The thermal expansion test was performed on the calcined samples using thermal mechanical analysis equipment (DIL 402C, Netzsch, Germany) according to GB/T 16535-2008. The calcined adhesive was first ground and then compressed into a long column with a length of 25 mm and a diameter of 6 mm under a pressure of 10 MPa. It was then treated again at the same temperature for 1 h. Meanwhile, the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of the Al2O3 substrate with dimensions of 25 mm × 5 mm × 5 mm was also tested.

The composition of different adhesives under different conditions was tested by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/Max-2500, Rigaku, Japan), and the corresponding chemical bonds were tested by using a Fourier-transform infrared diffractometer (FTIR; NicoletTMIS10, Thermo Fisher, USA). In addition, an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; Escalab 250xi, Thermal Fisher, USA) was used to determine the chemical state of zirconium in different adhesives with different Zr/Al ratios. The thermal properties of various adhesives were tested by a synchronous thermal analyzer (STA-449C, NETZSCH-Gerätebau, Germany) with air flowing at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The microscopic morphology of the joint cross section and the fracture surface morphology of the fractured joints were observed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; S-4800, Hitachi, Japan) equipped with an X-ray energy spectrum analyzer (EDXA). Finally, the crystal structure of the reaction product at the interface between the adhesive and alumina was analyzed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2 F20, FEI, the Netherlands). The thinning process of the samples for TEM was performed using an ion thinning instrument (focused ion beam (FIB); Helios NanoLab 600i FIB/SEM).

### 3.1 Effect of Zr/Al ratio on viscosity and curing time of adhesives

Figure 2 shows the change in viscosity of different adhesives with time. As the stirring time increases, the viscosity of all four adhesives increases, but the viscosity of the adhesive decreases as the amount of zirconium added increases. During the initial four or ten hours of stirring, the viscosity fluctuates up and down with little change because the polymerization reaction inside all adhesives is not yet intense. In the first stage, the viscosities of A10Z0, A7Z3, A5Z5, and A3Z7 are maintained at approximately 1750, 700, 410, and 180 mPa·s, respectively. As time passes, the degree of polymerization of the adhesive begins to increase, resulting in a gradual increase in viscosity. However, introducing zirconium hydroxide significantly weakens the polymerization process at room temperature, resulting in a low rate of increase in viscosity. The viscosity of A10Z0 increases rapidly from 3700 mPa·s at 6 h to 26,500 mPa·s at 12 h. When 30% zirconium hydroxide is added, the change in the viscosity of A7Z3 is similar to that of A10Z0, but the final viscosity of A7Z3 is much lower than that of A10Z0. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the viscosity of A7Z3 increases quickly from 1140 mPa·s at 6 h to 4,100 mPa·s at 11 h, after which the growth trend slows. Finally, it reaches less than 4850 mPa·s after stirring for 20 h. When the ratio of zirconium to aluminum is 1 : 1 (A5Z5), the viscosity increases after 10 h and ultimately remains at approximately 1230 mPa·s. The viscosity of A7Z3 remains at 270 mPa·s at RT, indicating that it does not undergo a polymerization reaction.

Correspondingly, the curing time of various adhesives is evaluated by tweezer pulling, puncturing, and adhesion forces. Figure 3 shows the state of different adhesives after curing for different durations. Four adhesives with the same mass were added to different colored bottle caps and fully vibrated for filling. Initially, all four adhesives show a wire drawing phenomenon, and the adhesion force is weak (Figs. 3(a1)–3(d1)). The outside of the A10Z0 adhesive has almost solidified after standing for 4 h, but the surface clump could still be picked up. The A7Z3 adhesive also becomes very thick after standing for 6 h; it can be pulled up but cannot form a filamentous shape. In addition, A5Z5 and A3Z7 still maintain their original state at 8 h. As time passes, the A10Z0 adhesive can no longer be pulled up, but it is easily punctured after standing for 8 h. It is too difficult to punch 4 h later, so the curing time of the A10Z0 adhesive should be less than 12 h. By comparison, the time for A7Z3 to progress through these two stages is delayed by 4 h. The curing time is further extended with increasing zirconium content. The curing state of A5Z5 after 24 h (Fig. 3(c4)) is not yet as good as that of A10Z0 after 4 h (Fig. 3(a2)), and curing can be achieved after 48 h (Fig. S1(c5) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). For A3Z7, the adhesive cannot cure spontaneously at RT, and there is no curing film on the surface. It can always be picked up within 72 h of standing. Similar to the viscosity test results, the introduction of zirconium weakens the ability of the adhesive matrix to spontaneously polymerize at RT, thereby reducing viscosity and extending curing time.

#### 3.4.1 XRD analysis

According to the previous analysis, the introduction of zirconium affects the polymerization process of the adhesive, which can be confirmed by XRD analysis. Figure 9 shows the XRD patterns of various adhesives after heat treatment from RT to 400 °C. The crystal phases identified from Fig. 9 are shown in Table 1. The broad amorphous diffraction peak (halo peak) is characteristic of this temperature range, and the temperature of the maximum halo peak area corresponding to various adhesives is different. Under consistent sample content and detection conditions, a larger halo peak area indicates a greater degree of polymerization or a greater amount of polymerization products. The temperatures corresponding to the maximum halo peak areas of A10Z0, A7Z3, A5Z5, and A3Z7 are approximately RT, 100 °C, 150 °C, and 400 °C, respectively, which are the same as the treatment temperatures at which they achieve maximum strength. The deep polymerization of A7Z3, A5Z5, and A3Z7 requires high-temperature preheating, and the first increase in the bonding strength of these three adhesives with increasing temperature is related to further polymerization of the matrix. With increasing temperature, the matrix with a macromolecular structure begins to decompose, and the decomposition products of the different adhesives are different. As shown in Fig. 9(a), hexagonal AlPO4 and Al(HP2O7)·2.5H2O first decompose from A10Z0 at 150 °C, and monoclinic AlPO4 then appears at 200 °C. The content of AlPO4 in these two crystal forms increases with increasing temperature, and hexagonal AlPO4 gradually transforms into monoclinic AlPO4 at 400 °C. As shown in Fig. 9(b), the halo peak of A7Z3 in the range of 20°–30° is still very obvious at 200 and 250 °C. Apparently, the introduction of zirconium avoids the direct decomposition of macromolecular network structures into crystals but promotes the formation of amorphous products with smaller molecular structures, which indicates that it improves the thermal stability of the amorphous phosphate structure. In addition, the introduction of zirconium also affects the type of decomposition products. In addition to hexagonal AlPO4, a small amount of ZrP2O7 decomposes, but no monoclinic AlPO4 appears at these temperatures. When the ratio of zirconium to aluminum increases to 1 : 1, as shown in Fig. 9(c), the highest polymerization is achieved at 150 °C, accompanied by the precipitation of Zr(HPO4)4·2H2O and hexagonal AlPO4. Compared with those of the first two adhesives, the peak intensity of hexagonal AlPO4 in A5Z5 is very low, and it is completely transformed into monoclinic AlPO4 again as the temperature increases. Moreover, a large amount of ZrP2O7 is generated at 400 °C. As seen from the XRD pattern of A3Z7 in Fig. 9(d), the amorphous peak area in the range of 20°–35° increases with increasing temperature, reaching its maximum at 400 °C. During the heating process from RT to 250 °C, Zr(HPO4)4·2H2O always exists, and there is no formation of AlPO4; only a small amount of ZrP2O7 decomposes, which indicates that aluminum is fully involved in the formation of network structures, while excess zirconium exists in the form of hydrates. From 250 to 400 °C, the peaks of Zr(HPO4)4·2H2O disappear. It is supposed that some Zr(HPO4)4·2H2O further participates in the generation of a network structure, the other part decomposes into ZrPO4(OH) as a part of cubic AlPO4 precipitates, and the halo peak area increases, which means that zirconium takes part in the formation of a network structure instead of aluminum. From the above analysis, it can be seen that the introduction of zirconium not only improves the degree of polymerization but also enhances the heat resistance of phosphates. With increasing zirconium addition, the amount of amorphous polymerization products and hydrated phosphate products in the adhesive (pretreated) matrix increase, which is the main factor for the difference in bonding strength of the four adhesives at temperatures below 400 °C.

The difference in the Zr/Al ratio also affects the reaction process at elevated temperatures. The composition evolution of the four adhesives varied greatly from 400 to 1500 °C, as shown in Fig. 10. The crystal peaks of A10Z0 do not show apparent changes in the range of 400 to 800 °C (see Fig. 10(a)), while the peak of B4C gradually decreases, and the intermediate BPO4 is formed, which indicates the oxidation of B4C. Two main reactions occur at 1000 °C, including the formation of Al5BO9 and the transformation of other crystal forms to orthorhombic AlPO4, and they proceed more fully from 1000 to 1300 °C. All the AlPO4 is transformed into orthorhombic crystals, and B4C and Si are completely oxidized and participate in further reactions at 1300 °C. After treatment at 1500 °C, only orthorhombic AlPO4 exists in its crystalline form in A10Z0.

As shown in Fig. 10(b), during the heating process from 400 to 800 °C, some amorphous substances still exist in A7Z3, and only a certain amount of Al(PO3)3 decompose at 600 °C. The crystal peak of ZrP2O7 remains inconspicuous until 800 °C, while cubic (30.1° and 50.2°) and monoclinic (24.4°) ZrO2 have large crystal peak strengths and areas, and a small amount of Zr(PO3)4 is generated at 800 °C. This result further indicates that the introduction of zirconium improves the thermal stability of amorphous substances with more polymer chain structures. After heat treatment at 1000 °C, a large amount of monoclinic ZrO2 is identified, which is due to the crystal transformation from cubic ZrO2 to monoclinic ZrO2 during the cooling process, and this phenomenon is more pronounced, with almost no cubic phase for the samples after heat treatment at 1300 °C. In addition, the oxidation degrees of B4C and Si increase from 1000 to 1300 °C; hexagonal AlPO4 is completely transformed into orthorhombic AlPO4, and an increasing amount of Al5BO9 and ZrSiO4 is generated at 1300 °C. The thermal stability of A5Z5 is better than that of A7Z3 in the range of 400–600 °C. The amorphous halo peak at 20°–35° is pronounced, and no new phase crystal peak is generated, as shown in Fig. 10(c). Nevertheless, the bonding strength of A5Z5 gradually decreases from 400 to 600 °C (see Fig. 4), indicating that large-molecule amorphous substances gradually decompose into small-molecule amorphous substances. After heat treatment at 800 °C, the peaks of ZrO2 and Al(PO3)3 appear, and the peak number and intensity of ZrP2O7 and monoclinic AlPO4 increase, both of which are attributed to the crystallization of the amorphous phase. In addition, their amount further increases at 1000 °C. Consistent with the phenomena in A7Z3, due to the crystal transformation of ZrO2, more monoclinic ZrO2 is identified in A5Z5 after heat treatment at 1000 °C. The reaction paths of A5Z5 from 1000 to 1300 °C are similar to those of A7Z3, including the complete oxidation of B4C and Si, the formation of more Al5BO9, the generation of ZrSiO4, and the complete transformation from monoclinic AlPO4 to orthorhombic AlPO4. Certainly, it seems that the content of ZrP2O7 in A5Z5 is significantly greater than that in A7Z3, but it is not easy to measure the ratio accurately via XRD. The proportions of different zirconium species were subsequently and accurately analyzed using XPS. After heating at 1500 °C, the stable crystal composition in A5Z5 is ZrP2O7, AlPO4, ZrO2, ZrSiO4, and Zr2O(PO4)4.

The primary reaction of A3Z7 during the heating process from 400 to 600 °C is also the decomposition of large-molecule amorphous substances into small-molecule amorphous substances since its bonding strength decreases in this temperature range (Fig. 4), and there is no reaction of crystalline substances (Fig. 10(d)). More products (ZrO2, Zr(PO3)4, ZrP2O7, and cubic AlPO4) further precipitated within the range of 600–800 °C, and higher amounts were obtained at 1000 °C. Due to the lowest content of aluminum in A3Z7, no Al5BO9 is generated in A3Z7, which is different from the other three adhesives. However, the reaction process of A3Z7 from 1000 to 1500 °C is similar to that of A5Z5. However, it can be inferred from the number and intensity of the ZrO2 diffraction peaks that the content of ZrO2 in A3Z7 is greater. Finally, in the temperature range from 400 to 1500 °C, the introduction of zirconium mainly promoted the formation of ZrO2, ZrSiO4, and zirconium phosphate, and its content also affected the amount of these products, which closely affected the thermophysical properties of the adhesives at higher temperatures.

#### 3.4.2 FTIR analysis

To conduct a more in-depth analysis of the composition evolution, the IR data were also analyzed in conjunction with the XRD data. In addition, due to the overlap of the P–O absorption peak at 1640 cm−1 with that of –OH (bending vibration), the change in peak intensity at this position from RT to 100 °C is influenced by the presence of hydroxyl groups. Additionally, a certain amount of water is present at temperatures not above 100 °C, as the IR peak of –OH (stretching vibration) at 3400 cm−1 is very apparent. For A7Z3 in Fig. 11(b), the peak intensities of P–O (1110 and 510 cm−1) and Al–O (701 cm−1) decrease from RT to 100 °C, which indicates that A7Z3 undergoes deep polymerization at 100 °C. The absorption peak intensity is almost the lowest at 100 °C, which means that complete curing can be achieved via polymerization at 100 °C. After that, the peak intensity increases with increasing temperature, and the shape becomes increasingly sharp, corresponding to the formation of phosphate crystals. Due to the introduction of Zr–O, the original absorption peak of P=O at 1230 cm−1 became more pronounced. In addition, the peak of –OH at 1640 cm−1 is also more obvious below 100 °C and then decreases with increasing temperature but still occurs at temperatures less than 400 °C.

#### 3.4.3 XPS analysis

To further investigate the combined states of zirconium in different adhesives and the changes in zirconium-containing compounds, XPS analysis was performed on A7Z3, A5Z5, and A3Z7 after heat treatment at 1300 and 1500 °C. Figure 13 shows the XPS spectra of Zr 3d for different adhesives, and the Zr 3d5/2 and Zr 3d3/2 curves are fitted. Table 2 shows the specific peak binding energy. Only two peaks corresponding to ZrO2 with binding energies of 182.5 eV (Zr 3d5/2) and 184.95 eV (Zr 3d3/2) are identified for A7Z3 after heat treatment at 1300 °C, and these two peaks shift to 182.45 eV (Zr 3d5/2) and 184.85 eV (Zr 3d3/2) for A7Z3 after heat treatment at 1500 °C. For A5Z5 after heat treatment at 1300 °C, the peaks of Zr 3d5/2 and Zr 3d3/2 can be separated into two separate peaks, where the peaks at 182.24 eV (Zr 3d5/2) and 184.69 eV (Zr 3d3/2) correspond to ZrO2 and the peaks at 183.03 eV (Zr 3d5/2) and 185.48 eV (Zr 3d3/2) represent zirconium bound to phosphate ions (Zr2P2O7) or silicate ions (ZrSiO4) [57,58]. However, their contents decrease or increase to 44.17 or 55.83 at%, respectively. For A3Z7, zirconium also exists in two chemical states, ZrO2 or ZrP2O7/ZrSiO4, and their contents do not change much at 1300 and 1500 °C, with values of approximately 83% and 16%, respectively. Therefore, all the zirconium added to A7Z3 is converted to ZrO2 at elevated temperatures, most of the zirconium added to A3Z7 is converted to ZrO2, and most of the zirconium added to A5Z5 is converted to ZrP2O7 or ZrSiO4 at 1500 °C. The different forms of zirconium strongly affect the thermal expansion performance of the adhesive.

### 3.5 Effect of Zr/Al ratio on thermal stability of adhesives

To further investigate the effect of zirconium addition on the thermal stability of the adhesives, thermogravimetry–differential scanning calorimetry (TG–DSC) was performed on the different adhesives, as shown in Fig. 14. Without the addition of zirconium, three evident mass loss processes occur in A10Z0, and three endothermic peaks correspond to them, with peak temperatures of 132, 206, and 316 °C. After adding zirconium, the adhesive retains the first two evident mass loss stages, while the third mass loss process becomes remarkably smooth and slow. Moreover, the peak temperatures of these two mass loss stages are delayed to 136 and 234 °C, respectively, and this is independent of the amount of zirconium added. In addition, the addition of zirconium can effectively reduce the weight loss rate, which decreases with increasing amounts of zirconium. For example, the mass loss rates of A10Z0 and A3Z7 in the first stage are 15.2% and 10.7%, respectively, and the mass loss rates of A10Z0 and A3Z7 at 400 °C are 19.4% and 17.7%, respectively. Combined with the composition evolution analysis, the first mass loss of all these adhesives is mainly due to free or adsorbed water volatilization. The second and third mass loss processes of A10Z0 correspond to the decomposition of amorphous phosphates and hydrated phosphate (Al(HP2O7)·2.5H2O), respectively. Combined with the XRD analysis, the second mass loss curves of A7Z3 and A5Z5 are almost identical, mainly representing the decomposition of amorphous phosphates. In addition, a certain amount of Zr(HPO4)4·2H2O in A5Z5 decomposes during this stage. With the same mass loss, the amount of amorphous substances decomposed in A5Z5 decreases, indicating that A5Z5 has improved thermal stability. Moreover, the third mass loss of A7Z3 is mainly due to the decomposition of residual Al(OH)3, while that of A5Z5 still comes from the decomposition of amorphous phosphates. However, the mass change trends and mass loss of these two adhesives in the third stage are also very similar. Therefore, the amorphous phosphates in A5Z5 can withstand higher temperatures. In addition, the second mass loss process of A3Z7 corresponds to the highest temperature, which is attributed to the generation of ZrP2O7 and Zr(HPO4)4·2H2O from the decomposition of amorphous phosphate with a nonmaximum network structure since deep polymerization occurs at 400 °C. The third stage is accompanied by minimal mass loss, indicating that Zr(HPO4)4·2H2O participates in reconstructing large network structures. These results are consistent with the XRD analysis.

The second most significant difference occurs in the range of 761–1500 °C. As the temperature increases, the masses of all four adhesives increase, but the starting temperature for mass increase increases with increasing zirconium content. The increase in mass is mainly due to the oxidation reactions of Si and B4C. The increase in oxidation temperature shows that the addition of zirconium will inhibit the oxidation reaction, possibly because the formation of zirconium reactants (ZrO2, ZrP2O7, ZrSiO4, etc.) occupies more oxygen molecules and reduces the oxygen molecules supplied to the oxidation reaction in the adhesive. The subsequent oxidation reaction requires the entry of external oxygen, which requires a certain amount of time. As shown in Fig. 14, the temperature at which the mass of A10Z0 begins to increase occurs at 761 °C, while that for A3Z7 occurs at 838 °C. Three distinct exothermic peaks appear on the DSC curves of A10Z0, A7Z3, and A5Z5, with peak temperatures of 780, 790, and 798 °C, respectively, and the height and area of the three peaks decrease in sequence. These results indicate that the degree of the oxidation reaction of the adhesive at approximately 800 °C gradually decreases with increasing zirconium content, and even the A3Z7 adhesive has not undergone an oxidation reaction. The second exothermic peak closely follows the first exothermic peak, the position of the second peak also shifts back with increasing zirconium content, and the peak area gradually increases, indicating a lag in the oxidation reaction. The oxidation process lasts until approximately 1200 °C, and the mass growth rate decreases with increasing zirconium content. The maximum mass increase rates for A10Z0, A7Z3, A5Z5, and A3Z7 are 8.1%, 6%, 4.3%, and 1.4%, respectively.

Above 900 °C, the DSC curves exhibit a strong endothermic trend, mainly due to the formation of borosilicate glass, which is consistent with the high yield of borosilicate glass shown by the 1000 °C IR curve. At approximately 1100 °C, except for A10Z0, there are varying degrees of exothermic peaks on the DSC curves of A7Z3, A5Z5, and A3Z7, which should correspond to the crystallization process of a large amount of ZrO2. Subsequently, all these adhesives show a heat absorption state at ~1180 °C, and there is even a peak at 1200 °C for A10Z0. This process, accompanied by a decrease in the TG curve, corresponds to the decomposition of borosilicate glass and volatilization of B2O3. Although the addition of zirconium does not prevent the decomposition of borosilicate glass, it improves the thermal stability of borosilicate glass, as the heat absorption of A7Z3, A5Z5, and A3Z7 is significantly lower than that of the A10Z0 adhesive, and the decrease in the DSC of A10Z0 starts at 900 °C. Therefore, the addition of zirconium also improved the thermal stability of the adhesive at elevated temperatures. In summary, the introduction of zirconium can improve the thermal stability of the adhesive, reduce mass loss, and inhibit the oxidation process but has a weak effect on the generation and decomposition of borosilicate glass.

As mentioned, the optimal polymerization temperatures for A10Z0, A7Z3, A5Z5, and A3Z7 are RT, 100 °C, 150 °C, and 400 °C, respectively. The thermal stability of these adhesives after deep polymerization at the given temperatures was also tested with TG-DSC, as shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM. Without preheating, A10Z0 cured at room temperature has a mass loss of up to 20.26% at 600 °C, accompanied by a three-stage weight loss process, which is consistent with Fig. 14. After deep polymerization at 100 °C for 1 h (A7Z3-100 °C-1 h), A7Z3 maintains good stability before 100 °C and begins to experience mass loss at approximately 115 °C. The total mass loss rate during heating to 600 °C is 7.68%. After deep polymerization at 150 °C for 1 h, A5Z5 is relatively stable within the range of RT–175 °C, with a total mass loss of 6.73%, and no endothermic peak appeared on its DSC curve. For A3Z7-400 °C-1 h, although its mass loss is only 2.83% at 600 °C, its mass continues to decrease from 200 to 600 °C, which should be related to the decomposition of Zr(HPO4)4·2H2O in the A3Z7 adhesive. The above results indicate that A7Z3 and A5Z5 maintain greater stability after deep polymerization at 100 and 150 °C.

### 3.6 Effect of Zr/Al on thermal expansibility of adhesives

Figure 15 shows the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of different adhesives calcined at various temperatures during heating. The CTE of the Al2O3 substrate reaches a stable value of 8×10−6 K−1 at approximately 600 °C and gradually increases to 8.5×10−6 K−1 at 1200 °C. As the heat treatment temperature increases from 800 to 1500 °C, CTEs of all four adhesives first increase (800–1300 °C) and then decrease (1300–1500 °C) with increasing treatment temperature. After heat treatment at 800 °C, CTE gradually decreases with increasing zirconium content. CTEs of A10Z0 and A7Z3 are relatively close, and they can reach stable values of 4.8×10−6 and 4.6×10−6 K−1 at 300 °C, respectively. The thermal responses of A5Z5 and A3Z7 are relatively slow, and their CTEs can reach 4.3×10−6 and 4.0×10−6 K−1 at 400 °C, respectively. For the 800 °C-treated adhesives, the main components that determine their CTEs are phosphates. After heat treatment at 1000 °C, the A7Z3, A5Z5, and A3Z7 adhesives generate more ZrO2 with a higher CTE (10.6×10−6 K−1), so their thermal expansibility becomes greater than that of A10Z0. In addition to ZrO2, ZrP2O7 (with an unusual negative expansion above 1100 °C [59,60]) and ZrSiO4 (4.58×10−6 K−1 at 1500 °C [61,62]) with a lower CTE can also affect the adhesive. Based on the XPS analysis, most of the zirconium-containing compounds in A7Z3 are ZrO2, while some ZrP2O7 and ZrSiO4 exist in A5Z5 and A3Z7. As their proportion in A5Z5 is the highest, the rankings of the CTEs for the 1000 °C-treated adhesives from top to bottom are 6.9×10−6 K−1 for A7Z3, 6.2×10−6 K−1 for A3Z7, and 5.7×10−6 K−1 for A5Z5. This sorting is maintained until 1500 °C.

### 3.7 Structural evolution of A7Z3 adhesive and interfacial reaction

The variation in composition affects the bond structure of the joint, which is discussed here in terms of both microstructure and joint state. As the processing temperature increases, the large holes in the joint gradually disappear; the adhesive layer becomes denser, and the connection state improves, as shown in Figs. 16(a)–16(d). This phenomenon results from the compensation of volume caused by the oxidation reactions inside the adhesive. However, the oxidation reaction does not cause any change in thickness, and the thickness of the adhesive layer remains between approximately 120 and 130 μm at different temperatures. Additionally, as seen from the microstructure of the adhesive matrix in Figs. 16(i)–16(l), the compactness of the adhesive significantly improves with increasing temperature. Throughout the heating process, the interface between the adhesive and the substrate is always well bonded, and no cracks are generated at the interfaces.

### 3.9 Bonding mechanism of A7Z3 adhesive

Figure 21 summarizes the bonding mechanism of the A7Z3 adhesive. A7Z3 mainly relies on physical bonding when the temperature is below 1000 °C, while chemical bonding dominates at temperatures above 1000 °C. Phosphates are the primary substances that play a bonding role in the physical adhesion stage, and their evolution with temperature affects the bonding strength. After complete curing at 100 °C, the macromolecular structure composed of PO4, AlO3, and ZrO4 units and hydroxyl groups has extremely high cohesion and polarity, showing the maximum adhesion performance at lower temperatures. With increasing temperature from 100 to 400 °C, the macromolecular structure gradually decomposes into smaller molecular structures and hexagonal AlPO4, which results in the appearance of pores and cracks within the adhesive matrix, thus leading to a decrease in bonding strength. Within the processing temperature range, the bonded joints fracture both in the adhesive layers and at the interfaces, and as the temperature increases, the area of cracking at the interface increases.

From 400 to 800 °C, most oligomeric amorphous phosphates further decompose into a large amount of cubic ZrO2 and relatively small amounts of monoclinic ZrO2, Zr(PO3)4, and Al(PO3)3. The decomposition process causes further loosening of the adhesive structure, resulting in more pores in the adhesive layer. However, due to the high degree of ceramicization of the adhesive layer at this time, the strength and hardness of the adhesive layer itself significantly improved. Moreover, both Si and B4C undergo a certain degree of oxidation. Although there is no significant volume compensation effect, the generated B2O3 has a high adhesion to the interface. In these cases, the bonding strength of the adhesive treated at 800 °C is significantly improved. When the strength of the adhesive layer and the interface strength increase, the fracture surface occurs both at the adhesive layer and at the interface. Due to the many pore defects in the adhesive layer, the fracture surface fluctuates unevenly.

When the temperature reaches or exceeds 1000 °C, B2O3 and SiO2 in the adhesive begin to react with the matrix to form Al5BO9 and mullite, and chemical bonding begins to occur. As the temperature increases, the interfacial reaction becomes more intense, and the interfacial strength increases. On the other hand, the ceramization and vitrification processes within the adhesive are more thorough:

(1) The content of ceramic phases continues to increase, and the types of ceramic phases have become more straightforward due to their complexity. The cubic ZrO2 and hexagonal AlPO4 are transformed into monoclinic ZrO2 and orthorhombic AlPO4, and further reactions occur between the metaphosphate and alumina to form orthophosphate.

(2) The oxidation of Si and B4C is gradually completed, and the volume compensation caused by oxidation increases the density of the adhesive layer structure. The generated B2O3 reacted with alumina from the adhesive to form Al5BO9, with SiO2 to form borosilicate glass, and some SiO2 reacted with Zr(PO3)4 to form ZrSiO4.

(3) The main components of the adhesive in the range of 1300–1500 °C are monoclinic ZrO2, orthorhombic AlPO4, ZrSiO4, Al5BO9, and borosilicate glass. These substances in a dense state exhibit incredibly high hardness, inherent strength, and a thermal expansion coefficient that matches the alumina substrate.

The strength of the adhesive itself and the interface strength gradually increase with increasing processing temperature, which also leads to the maximum bonding strength of the adhesive. In the chemical bonding stage, the cracking of the adhesive layer mainly occurs in the adhesive layer, and as the processing temperature increases, the macroscopic fracture surface becomes smoother. Therefore, the interface can withstand more damage than the adhesive matrix itself.

## 4 Conclusions

To reduce the thermal expansion difference between aluminum phosphate-based adhesives and alumina ceramics and improve the high-temperature resistance, mechanical performance, and service life of adhesive-bonding technology, zirconium-modified aluminum phosphate-based high-temperature-resistant adhesives were prepared utilizing zirconium hydroxide to regulate the reaction between aluminum hydroxide and phosphoric acid. Four types of adhesives, A10Z0, A7Z3, A5Z5, and A3Z7, were prepared according to the different mass ratios of zirconium hydroxide and aluminum hydroxide. A systematic and detailed study was conducted on the effects of the Zr/Al ratio on the adhesive’s slurry properties, curing effect, bonding performance, thermal stability, thermal expansion, and component evolution with temperature. The addition of zirconium effectively reduces the viscosity of the adhesive slurry and delays the curing rate. As the amount of added zirconium increased, the temperature required for complete curing also increased. After complete curing, as more zirconium was added, the polymerization degree of the adhesive matrix significantly increased, and the initial bonding strength improved. Increasing the Zr/Al ratio could improve the thermal stability of the adhesive matrix, delay the decomposition process, and reduce the quality loss during decomposition. Furthermore, the Zr/Al ratio closely affects the reaction process of adhesives at high temperatures, including the generation and decomposition of macromolecule matrices, the types and contents of decomposition products, and the crystal transformation of different phosphates, significantly affecting the types and amounts of zirconium-containing compounds at high temperatures. As the Zr/Al ratio increased, the ratio of zirconium phosphates to aluminum phosphates gradually increased. When the mass ratio of zirconium hydroxide to aluminum hydroxide was 3 : 7, most of the zirconium-containing compounds in the A7Z3 adhesive at high temperatures were ZrO2, and the amount of zirconium phosphates generated was low. In contrast, more ZrP2O7 was generated in A5Z5 and A7Z3. The thermal expansion matching degree between the A7Z3 adhesive and the alumina substrate was the highest, and after treatment at 1300 °C, the difference in CTE between them was less than 1×10−6 K−1. The RT bonding strength of the A7Z3 adhesive after heat treatment at 1500 °C reached a maximum value of 67.2 MPa. After pretreatment at 1500 °C, the high-temperature bonding strength of the A7Z3 adhesive was not less than 50 MPa within the range of RT–1000 °C. Within the range of RT–1500 °C, the retention rates after 10, 20, and 40 thermal cycles were 67.8%, 33.7%, and 14.4%, respectively. The excellent bonding performance of the A7Z3 adhesive was also attributed to the volume compensation caused by the oxidation of silicon and boron carbide; the formation of zirconium silicate, aluminum borate, and borosilicate glass; chemical reactions at the interface. The A7Z3 adhesive mainly relied on physical bonding when the temperature was below 1000 °C, while chemical bonding dominated at temperatures above 1000 °C. As the temperature increased, the reaction at the interface became more profound; the interface strength became greater, and the fracture of the bonded joints mainly occurred in the adhesive matrix rather than at the interface. The zirconium-modified aluminum phosphate-based high-temperature resistant adhesive had the advantage of adjustable thermal expansion performance, significantly promoting the research and application of novel inorganic high-temperature resistant adhesives.
