## 1 Introduction

Antiferroelectric (AFE) materials undergo complex yet reversible phase transitions between the AFE and ferroelectric (FE) states under applied external electric fields (E-fields), enabling their application in infrared (IR) pyroelectric detectors [1,2], explosive-to-electrical transducers [3], high-energy storage capacitors [4−7], digital displacement transducers [8−10], large-strain actuators [11], electrocaloric cooling devices [12,13], and nonvolatile random access memories [14,15], etc. Driven by the search for AFE materials with high performance, environmental friendliness, and low cost, sodium niobate (NaNbO3; NN), a well-established AFE material, has recently attracted significant research interest [15−21]. However, in pure NN ceramics, the AFE P phase (*Pbcm*, Glazer tilt system: *a*−*a*−*b*+/*a*−*a*−*b*− [22,23]) and FE Q phase (*Pmc*21, Glazer tilt system: *a*−*a*−*b*+) have minimal energy differences [15,24,25], leading to irreversible field-induced phase transitions and the absence of double polarization loops.

Early reports on stabilizing the AFE phase in NN have shown promising progress, but achieving fully reversible double hysteresis loops remains a significant challenge. Several end-members, such as CaHfO3 [26], CaSnO3 [27], and SrSnO3 [15], have been found to induce double loops at room temperature (RT). However, most NN-based ceramics with double polarization loops exhibit negative backward critical fields (*E*FE–AFE r) —a phenomenon known as a partially reversible phase transition. Currently, only a few NN-based ceramics exhibit a completely reversible field-induced phase transition (*E*FE–AFE > 0) at RT. However, these materials require E-fields exceeding 200 kV/cm to trigger the AFE–FE phase transition, which limits their potential applications in large-scale integrated circuits and low-voltage electronics. Therefore, developing novel NN-based AFE systems and uncovering the underlying mechanisms governing low-field reversible AFE–FE phase transitions is of great significance. Additionally, the high-temperature AFE R phase (*Pnma*, Glazer tilt system: *a*−*b*+*c*+/*a*−*b*+*c*+/*a*−*b*0*c*+ [28,29]) has been observed in NN-based ceramics exhibiting double polarization loops [16,25,30−34], but its stabilization mechanism remains unclear.

In this work, guided by density functional theory (DFT) calculations, we design and fabricate *x*Bi2/3SnO3–(1−*x*)NaNbO3 ceramics, overcoming the long-standing challenges of achieving both a fully reversible field-induced phase transition (*E*FE–AFE > 0) and a low operating field (*E*AFE–FE 2/3Ta1/3O3, BiHfO3, and BiScO3), Bi2/3SnO3 enables simultaneous enhancement of polarization and energy efficiency through synergistic mechanisms: Stereochemically active Bi3+ 6s2 lone-pair orbital hybridization combined with Sn4+-induced relaxor behavior [35,36]. Furthermore, we reveal the stabilization mechanism of the AFE R phase and the low-field reversible phase transition from the atomic scale to the phase scale, providing valuable insights into the future development of high-performance AFE ceramics.

### 2.1 Ceramic preparation

*x*Bi2/3SnO3–(1−*x*)NaNbO3 ceramics (*x* = 0.00–0.08) were synthesized via a solid-state reaction. Raw materials included analytically pure Na2CO3 (99.80%), Nb2O5 (99.50%), Bi2O3 (99.00%), and SnO2 (99.50%) (Sinopharm, China) powders, which were weighed in a stoichiometric ratio and then ball-milled in ethanol for 10 h. After ball milling, the obtained slurries were dried, placed in a dryer at 80 °C for one day, and then pressed into cylindrical shapes. The shaped powders were calcined at a heating rate of 5 °C/min to 800 °C, held for 3 h, and then ball-milled again for 4 h. Afterwards, the disc-shaped samples were prepared by manual prepressing followed by cold isostatic pressing at 200 MPa. Finally, sintering was conducted in sealed crucibles with composition-matched sacrificial powder, using a heating rate of 2 °C/min to optimize the temperature (*x* = 0.00: 1330 °C, *x* = 0.02: 1320 °C, *x* = 0.04: 1270 °C, *x* = 0.06: 1240 °C, *x* = 0.08: 1230 °C) with a 3-hour dwell time at each peak temperature.

### 2.2 Characterization methods

X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Smart Lab 9 kW, Rigaku Company, Japan), Raman spectrometer (Renishaw inVia, Renishaw, UK), and transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2 F30, FEI, USA) were employed to identify the P, Q, and R phases in the NN-based ceramics. The TEM samples were prepared via the FIB system of the FEI Helios G4 platform. Before imaging via a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Apreo S, FEI, USA), polished ceramic samples were thermally etched at 1200 °C for 30 min to delineate the grain boundaries. Representative micrographs (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) confirm > 96% relative density and a grain size range of 2.08–4.16 μm. Ferroelectric and dielectric tests (temperature-dependent permittivity and impedance spectroscopy measurements) were carried out via a ferroelectric analyzer (TF2000E, Aixacct, Germany) and an LCR meter (E4980A, Agilent, USA). For ferroelectric measurements, samples (diameter ≈ 10 mm) were polished to a thickness of 0.13 mm and pasted with 3-mm-diameter silver electrodes.

### 2.3 Calculation methods

The DFT simulations were performed via the Vienna *ab initio* simulation (VASP) [37] package, implemented within the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) function [38] and generalized gradient approximation (GGA) [39] for the geometry optimizations and energy calculations of both pure and doped NaNbO3 systems with AFE and FE phases. The AFE and FE phases were modeled via the *Pbcm* and *Pmc*21 space groups, respectively. A supercell structure consisting of 80 atoms was employed for all calculations. The convergence criteria for the residual forces and total energy were set to 10−2/Å and 10−4 eV, respectively, with a plane-wave energy cutoff of 450 eV. The Brillouin zone was represented by Monkhorst–Pack special *k*-point meshes [40] of size 4×9×3 for the AFE phase structures and 3×9×4 for the FE phase. The energy difference (Δ*E*) between the AFE and FE phases was calculated as Δ*E* = (*E*AFE − *E*FE)/16, where *E*AFE and *E*FE represent the total energies of the AFE and FE phases, respectively.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figures 1(a)–1(d) present the polarization–electric field (*P*–*E*) loops and current–electric field (*I*–*E*) curves for the *x*Bi2/3SnO3–(1−*x*)NaNbO3 ceramics. In pure NN ceramics, owing to the irreversible field-induced phase transition, the *P–E* loop and *I–E* curve exhibit FE-like characteristics [25,41−43]. However, when the Bi2/3SnO3 content reaches *x* ≥ 0.04, the samples display typical AFE double *P–E* loops, characterized by four distinct current peaks in the corresponding *I–E* curves. The two peaks at higher fields correspond to the forward critical field *E*AFE–FE, where the initial state transitions to the FE state. The two peaks at lower fields are associated with the backward critical field *E*FE–AFE, where the FE state reverts to the AFE state. Our observed increases in both *E*AFE–FE and *E*FE–AFE with increasing doping level suggest an increased stability of the AFE phase. The permittivity stability against the applied field is very important for direct current (DC)-link capacitor applications. The field-dependent permittivity can be easily calculated from d*P*/d*E*. Figure 1(e) shows the E-field dependence of the calculated permittivity *ε*cal for these ceramics. In a pure NN, *ε*cal decreases rapidly with increasing E-field, limiting its potential application in pulse power systems. In contrast, for compositions with *x* ≥ 0.02, *ε*cal increases with the applied E-field (AFE–FE). The gradually gentler increase in *ε*cal below *E*AFE–FE with increasing *x* can be attributed to the mild AFE–FE phase transition enabled by enhanced relaxation behavior. Figure 1(f) compares the *E*AFE–FE, *E*FE–AFE, and *P*r across various NaNbO3-based ceramics that exhibit double hysteresis loops at RT. Notably, for 0.04 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.08, the ceramics demonstrate reversible field-induced phase transitions (*E*FE–AFE > 0) with *E*AFE–FEs below 200 kV/cm. Compared with other NN-based AFE ceramics, *x*Bi2/3SnO3–(1−*x*)NaNbO3 ceramics exhibit superior potential for integration into large-scale and low-voltage circuits, making them promising candidates for future electronic applications.

All the *x*Bi2/3SnO3–(1−*x*)NaNbO3 ceramics have a perovskite structure without noticeable second phases, as confirmed by XRD analysis (Fig. 2(a)). To gain deeper insight into the AFE phase, high-resolution X-ray diffraction (HR-XRD) measurements were conducted, focusing on specific peaks at 2*θ* = 35.1°–37.8° (Fig. 2(b)), 33.5°–34.5° (Fig. 2(c)), and 53.5°–56.5° (Fig. 2(d)). The presence of the AFE P phase is evident across all ceramic samples, as indicated by the characteristic 1/4-type superlattice diffraction peaks. Furthermore, a 1/6-type superlattice diffraction peak appears in the *x* = 0.04 sample and becomes more pronounced with increasing Bi2/3SnO3 content, suggesting the progressive stabilization of the AFE R (*Pnma*) phase at RT [25,47]. Additionally, the gradual shift of superlattice peaks toward higher angles indicates a contraction of the unit cell, primarily due to the substitution of larger Na+ ions (*r* = 1.39 Å, coordination number (CN) = 12) at A-sites by smaller Bi3+ ions (*r* = 1.36 Å, CN = 12), with an additional contribution from A-site vacancies of the end-member.

Raman spectroscopy is sensitive to the local ionic configuration and shows an obvious difference in active modes used to distinguish the P, Q, and R phases in NN-based ceramics. Figure 2(e) presents the Raman spectra of the synthesized ceramics. Like pure NaNbO3, all samples present eight vibrational modes within the measured range, corresponding to NbO6 rotation, ν6, ν5, ν4, ν2, ν1, ν3, and ν5+ν1, confirming their orthorhombic structure [48,49]. According to group theory, there are 60 Raman active modes in the APE P phase, expressed as 15Ag+17B1g+15B2g+13B3g [50]; the FE Q phase has 57 Raman active modes, denoted as 16A1+13A2+12B1+16B2 [51]; and the Raman active modes in the APE R phase are described as 8Ag+5B1g+7B2g+10B3g [52]. Nevertheless, only 14 modes can be observed after Lorentzian function deconvolution (Fig. 2(f)). The multiple peaks below 300 cm−1 represent a characteristic vibrational fingerprint of the antiferroelectric P-phase, arising from large Nb–O bond length disparities induced by antiparallel Nbs. The composition-dependent shifts of these Raman bands are plotted in Fig. 2(g). When *x* ≤ 0.02, most vibrational modes shift to lower wavenumbers while retaining the total number of active modes. This behavior aligns with the previously observed transition from a coexisting FE Q + AFE P (Q P) phase to a pure AFE P phase in the (Li*x*Na1−*x*)NbO3 solid solution [51]. Concurrently, these spectral changes indicate a decrease in the difference between the B–O bond lengths within the lattice, leading to an improvement in electron localization [16]. With further addition of Bi2/3SnO3, certain Raman modes—particularly those near the 150, 200, and 700 cm−1 of wavenumbers—disappeared, whereas the remaining modes remained nearly unchanged for *x* ≥ 0.04. This observation is in accordance with previous reports about NaNbO3 ceramics, where the R phase emerges upon heating [53]. Figure 2(h) presents the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) variations for selected Raman modes. As *x* increases, the FWHM of ν1 broadens, whereas that of ν5 remains relatively constant for *x* ≥ 0.04. These trends correlate well with the observed structural phase transitions. The increase in the FWHM reflects the increase in relaxation behavior because of local structural disorder. The dielectric peaks (~−100 °C) in all samples shift to higher temperatures, and their corresponding permittivities decrease as the frequency increases. The frequency dispersion and broadened dielectric peaks indicate the relaxation behavior of the sample by incorporating ions (Bi3+ and Sn4+) of different valence states and sizes into A/B sites, which coincides with the Raman analysis. The modified Curie–Weiss law with a dispersion index *γ* and Curie constant *C* is employed to further explore the relaxation behavior [57]:

(1) 1ε−1εm=(T−Tm)γC

where *T*m denotes the temperature corresponding to the maximum permittivity *ε*m. *γ* increases with the addition of Bi2/3SnO3 via linearly fitting the ln(1/*ε*−1/*ε*m) versus ln(*T*−*T*m), as displayed in Fig. 4(a). The increased *γ* indicates that the relaxation behavior of the sample is enhanced [58]. The enhanced relaxation behavior in AFEs leads to a mild AFE–FE phase transition and low energy dissipation, preventing the breakdown caused by drastic structural deformation observed in normal FEs, thereby facilitating the realization of double hysteresis loops [59]. Impedance spectroscopy (Fig. 4(b)) was performed at 600 °C to investigate the conduction mechanism. All these semicircular arcs can be fitted well by a group of parallel R‖CPE (CPE is the constant phase element) equivalent circuit models, as shown in Fig. 4(b). The *E*a in this system is within the range of 0.77–1.19 eV and increases with the addition of Bi2/3SnO3. Generally, the *E*a of oxygen vacancies ranges from 0.5 to 2 eV [62]. The increased *E*a probably means that charge carriers (such as oxygen vacancies) must overcome a larger energy barrier to participate in conductance, thereby contributing to the improved breakdown strength *E*b. Figure 4(d) shows the E-field-dependent leakage current density (LCD) for the *x*Bi2/3SnO3–(1−*x*)NaNbO3 system. The LCD of the doped samples is almost the same and much lower than that of pure NN. The characteristically high leakage current and low breakdown strength of pure NaNbO3 ceramics typically originate from thermally generated defects such as oxygen and sodium vacancies formed during high-temperature sintering. The reduced leakage current observed after Bi2/3SnO3 addition demonstrated that the related vacancy concentration was effectively reduced, primarily through the substitution of Na+ with Bi3+. Furthermore, as discussed above, Bi2/3SnO3 addition enhances electron localization, which is also favorable for increasing *E*b.

The modulation widths of these two structures, which are determined from the intensity profile (in the bottom inset of Fig. 5(b)), are approximately 1.65 and 2.44 nm, respectively. DFT calculations were performed to evaluate the free energy changes and the effects of Bi2/3SnO3 incorporation on the reversibility of the field-induced phase transition, as shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b). The results indicate that after introducing Bi2/3SnO3, the free energy of the AFE P phase is lower than that of the FE Q phase, with the energy difference increasing from 3.35 to 7.20 meV/(f.u.). This larger energy gap enhances the stability of the AFE P phase, allowing the field-induced FE Q phase to more easily revert to the AFE P phase upon removal of the E-field. To further explore the electronic structure, crystal structure analysis and electron localization function (ELF) calculations were conducted (Figs. 6(c)–6(f)) and Fig. S4 in the ESM). The ELF values range from 0 (blue) to 1 (red), where a higher ELF value indicates a higher degree of electron localization [70]. In polyatomic systems, regions with covalent bonding and lone pair electrons generally exhibit high electron localization [71,72]. Figures S4(c) and S4(d) present the ELF maps along the A-site atomic plane for pure NN and 0.08Bi2/3SnO3–0.92NaNbO3 ceramics, respectively. In pure NN, no electron localization (blue) is observed around the Na atoms, indicating the typical ionic bonding nature of Na–O bonds. In contrast, for 0.08Bi2/3SnO3–0.92NaNbO3, a localized electron distribution (ELF > 0, light blue) appears around the Bi–O bonds, suggesting an increase in the covalency of the A–O bonds. Similarly, ELF maps along the B-site atomic plane (Figs. 6(e) and 6(f)) show that the oxygen atoms in pure NN are surrounded by a multilayered confocal ellipsoidal shell electron localization distribution. After modification, the strongly localized electron regions (orange–red) shift noticeably toward the Sn atoms, indicating an increase in the covalency of the B–O bonds. The observed increase in the covalency of both the A–O and B–O bonds contributes to the improvement in *E*b, which is crucial for maintaining double *P*–*E* loops at RT.

## 4 Conclusions

*x*Bi2/3SnO3–(1−*x*)NaNbO3 ceramics were successfully fabricated via traditional solid-state reactions. For compositions within 0.04 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.08, samples exhibit reversible field-induced phase transitions (*E*FE–AFE > 0) with a low operating field *E*AFE–FE below 200 kV/cm and a remarkably low remanent polarization compared with other compositions. Our work demonstrates that achieving a low-field reversible phase transition in NaNbO3 can be optimized by increasing the energy difference between the AFE and FE phases, enhancing the covalency of A–O and B–O bonds, reducing the distortion index, and introducing an appropriate level of dislocations.
