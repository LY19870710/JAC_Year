## 1 Introduction

Molten salt reactor (MSR), one of the six most promising generation IV reactors, has attracted worldwide attention owing to its inherent safety, high fuel utilization efficiency, and low nuclear waste production [1,2]. The structural materials in MSRs are subjected to a combination of challenging environmental conditions, including high temperatures, intense fluoride‒salt corrosion, and severe neutron irradiation [3]. Therefore, silicon carbide (SiC) materials and their ceramic-based composites, which have high-temperature strength, chemical inertness, and favorable neutron characteristics [4−8], are expected to be applied in the structural components of MSRs. However, the synergistic effect of irradiation and corrosion at high temperatures presents one of the most significant challenges limiting the safe and efficient application of SiC. A series of studies conducted at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), Shanghai Institute of Applied Physics (SINAP), and other institutions have demonstrated that high-purity SiC materials exhibit remarkable resistance to corrosion when exposed to FLiNaK molten salt in the absence of irradiation [9−11]. This underscores their potential for application in MSRs. Moreover, evidence from numerous studies indicates that SiC materials maintain exceptional resistance to irradiation damage in the absence of fluoride–salt corrosion at high temperatures [12−16]. However, existing reports do not provide insight into the synergistic effect of irradiation and corrosion on SiC. While the impact of irradiation damage on the corrosion of SiC materials in FLiNaK molten salt has been previously investigated [17,18], the underlying mechanisms remain inadequately explored. Furthermore, the impact of molten salt corrosion on irradiation damage in SiC materials remains unexplored. Comprehensive research into the interactions between fluoride-salt corrosion and irradiation of SiC materials is therefore needed to gain a deeper understanding.

The compatibility of structural materials with molten salt represents a significant challenge for the success of MSR. In previous studies on the corrosion of SiC materials with different microstructures [19,20] in FLiNaK molten salt, three main factors have been identified as contributors: corrosion due to the molten salt itself [10], impurities [20,21], and heterogeneous materials [22]. The dissolution of Si from SiC into salt was a consequence of corrosion, resulting in the formation of a C-rich layer on the surface of SiC [10,20−22]. This could be attributed to the fact that the standard dissolution potential of Si in fluoride salt was more negative than that of C [23]. Although these studies identified the presence of a graphite component in the C-rich layer through spectroscopy tests (including Raman and XPS) [19,21,24,25], the exact structure remains unknown. Furthermore, our previous study revealed the occurrence of irradiation-promoted SiC corrosion [18]. Further findings revealed that irradiation-induced amorphization accelerated the corrosion of SiC in FLiNaK molten salt at 650 °C, a process that may also be influenced by the presence of O impurities [17]. Currently, it remains unclear whether other impurities also contribute to the irradiation-promoted corrosion process and whether the mechanism differs at higher temperatures (≥ 750 °C). The impact of He ion irradiation on corrosion behavior was investigated in a related study [25], which also demonstrated that irradiation-induced amorphization accelerated the corrosion of SiC. However, the results did not explore the influence of corrosion on the evolution of He behavior. Additionally, the SiC materials employed in reactors are subject to intricate interactions with a multitude of He atoms generated by (n, α) nuclear transmutation reactions under neutron irradiation [13,14,16]. The He bubbles degrade the properties of the SiC material, including expansion, hardening, and even fracture.

Therefore, the synergistic damage behavior and mechanism of He ion irradiation and molten salt corrosion in SiC at high temperatures are important for its safe application in MSRs. A combination of SEM/EDS, atomic force microscopy (AFM), Raman spectroscopy, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were employed to analyze the process and mechanism of irradiation-promoted corrosion. Additionally, TEM was employed to elucidate the impact of corrosion on the evolution of He behavior, and the underlying mechanism was derived. The results presented herein provide comprehensive information on the synergistic damage behavior of He ion irradiation and molten salt corrosion in SiC. These findings can serve as a basis for the safe and efficient application of SiC.

## 2 Experimental

The CVD samples employed in this investigation were polycrystalline β-SiC from Rohm & Hass, with a density of 3.21 g∙cm−3, and were cut into 6 mm × 5 mm × 1 mm blocks. Before irradiation, these samples were sequentially polished to a mirror finish on one side via diamond sandpaper and a polishing solution. The ion irradiation experiments were carried out with 400 keV He2+ ions at 750 °C on a 4 MV Pelletron accelerator at SINAP. Successive irradiation at doses of 2×1016 and 1×1017 ions∙cm−2 was used, resulting in peak damage rates of 0.53 and 2.63 displacements per atom (dpa), respectively. These doses were calculated via the stopping and range of ions in matter (SRIM) software, as shown in Fig. 1. The simulations were carried out with the "K–P quick calculation" method. Displacement energies of 20 eV for carbon (C) atoms and 35 eV for silicon (Si) atoms were used in the calculations.

The corrosion experiments of the unirradiated and irradiated SiC samples were performed at 750 °C for 166 h in FLiNaK salt (approximately 70 g) contained in a graphite crucible inside a 316 L stainless steel cell, as shown in Fig. 2. In this study, the irradiation and corrosion testing temperatures were adjusted to avoid temperature effects on the evolution of irradiation defects. The FLiNaK salt was synthesized and purified by the Shanghai Institute of Organic Chemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences (SIOC-CAS). An inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometer (ICP‒OES) was used to analyze impurities in the FLiNaK salt, which was composed of 46.5 mol% LiF, 11.5 mol% NaF, and 42 mol% KF, both before and after the corrosion tests, and the findings are detailed in Table 1. Before the tests, the salt was stored and handled in a glove box under an Ar atmosphere with H₂O and O₂ contents below 5 ppm. All the samples and crucibles were subjected to ultrasonic cleaning in deionized water and anhydrous ethanol for 5 min, followed by baking in a vacuum oven at 90 °C for 2 h to remove potential adsorbates. The steel crucible was subsequently welded within an Ar-filled glove box. After corrosion, the residual salt was carefully removed from the samples via an aluminum nitrate aqueous solution, after which the samples were rinsed with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol.

The surface morphologies were examined via a FE–SEM instrument (Merlin, Zeiss) equipped with an EDS instrument (Oxford Instruments), and AFM was performed with a high-resolution atomic force microscope (Multimode 8, Bruker Nano Inc.). Microstructural characterizations were conducted with a Raman microscope (HR evolution, Horiba) in backscattering geometry using the 532 nm line of a diode-pumped solid-state laser as the excitation source. Cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (XTEM) analyses were performed via a 200 kV transmission electron microscope (Tecnai G2 F20, FEI) equipped with a high-angle-angular-dark-field (HAADF) detector and an EDS system. The irradiated and corroded sample regions were further analyzed via selected-area electron diffraction (SAED). The samples for XTEM were prepared via a focused ion beam (FIB), ensuring a thickness of less than 150 nm, with a protective platinum layer deposited to safeguard against ion beam damage. Furthermore, a 5 keV Ga beam with a current of approximately 8 pA was employed to minimize surface damage during the milling process.

### 3.1 Results

SEM and EDS analyses of virgin, irradiated-only, corroded-only, and irradiated and corroded SiC samples are shown in Fig. 3. As shown in Figs. 3(a)–3(c), no significant changes are observed in the irradiated-only samples compared with the virgin sample. In contrast, the surface morphology of the corroded-only sample reveals significant changes, characterized by extensive denudation and scattered corrosion pits. A comparison of the data in Figs. 3(d)–3(f) reveals a gradual increase in the degree of denudation of the corroded samples with increasing irradiation dose. The EDS map in Fig. 3(g), from the selected area marked in Fig. 3(a), confirms a uniform distribution of surface elements in the virgin sample. A further EDS map after corrosion, shown in Fig. 3(h) from the area marked in Fig. 3(d), reveals a denuded region corresponding to the C-rich structure, which contains a small amount of the O impurity. As illustrated in the EDS map in Fig. 3(i), which is derived from the white box in Fig. 3(e), the distribution of elements on the surface of the irradiated and corroded sample is consistent with that observed in the corroded-only sample. Moreover, the surface area of the C-rich layer in the corroded sample expands as the irradiation dose increases. Table 1 shows a significant increase in the Si content in the FLiNaK salt after corrosion testing, suggesting that the corrosion of SiC is attributed to the loss and dissolution of surface Si into the salt and the subsequent formation of a C-rich layer. This process is also accompanied by partial denudation of the virgin surface.

Raman spectroscopy was used to examine the chemical composition of the SiC surface, as shown in Fig. 5. The Raman spectra of virgin samples exhibit distinct transverse optical (TO) and longitudinal optical (LO) phonon lines at approximately 795 and 970 cm−1, respectively [18,26]. The irradiation results in a pronounced weakening and broadening of both the TO and LO peaks as the dose increases. Additionally, two new broad bands are observed in the ~270 and 535–540 cm−1 regions, which are consistent with the vibration modes of crystalline Si (LA + LO, where LA is the longitudinal optical phonon line) and crystalline Si (TO) according to the location of the Raman scattering peaks [26,27]. In the case of the corroded-only sample, the Raman spectrum reveals new peaks at approximately 1330 and 1580 cm−1, which correspond to the D (disorder peak) and G (graphitic peak) vibration modes of graphite, respectively [17,18,28]. The presence of both SiC and graphite characteristic peaks can be attributed to the sampling location during Raman testing, which is situated in proximity to the edge of the corroded region. The analysis of samples after corrosion revealed that with increasing irradiation dose, the D and G bands of graphite in the corroded regions exhibited progressive intensification. The trend of the graphite peaks is indicative of an increase in corrosion. Overall, the Raman spectral pattern also indicates that corrosion partially removes Si from the SiC surface and generates a C-rich layer. Moreover, the corrosion of SiC at high temperatures increases progressively with increasing irradiation dose.

As shown in Fig. 6, the XTEM image provides insight into the structural damage of the sample only irradiated with a dose of 1×1017 ions∙cm−2. Figure 6(a) is further augmented by He distribution profiles generated by the SRIM-2008 software. Notably, the He bubbles are predominantly distributed at approximately 1160–1340 nm from the surface. However, the actual He bubble distribution extends approximately 100 nm deeper than that predicted by the SRIM simulations. The difference between the SRIM simulation and the actual distribution of He bubbles in Fig. 6(a) may be attributed to the high temperature. The enlarged He bubble region is illustrated in Fig. 6(b), while the SAED pattern shows ordered diffraction spots corresponding to the [011] direction of the face-centered cubic (FCC) lattice. To facilitate identification, Fig. 6(c) has been magnified further and presented under both underfocus and overfocus conditions. Under the underfocus condition, the bubbles appear as white dots, whereas they appear as black dots under the overfocus condition. The bubbles are distributed uniformly throughout the matrix between the gray dashed lines in Fig. 6(a). A notable exception to the uniform distribution of He bubbles is observed along grain boundaries (GBs), where He bubbles are arranged approximately in lines [29]. Figures 10(a) and 10(b) illustrate the relationship between the number density and size of He bubbles in the sample irradiated with only 1×1017 ions∙cm−2 and the corresponding irradiated and corroded samples, respectively. In the irradiated-only sample, a high number density of He bubbles with relatively small sizes, predominantly ranging from 0.4 to 1.0 nm, was observed. In contrast, the irradiated and corroded samples demonstrated a significant decrease in the number density and an increase in size. Notably, in the C-rich layer, while the number density of He bubbles is slightly lower, larger bubbles (> 2.5 nm) are more apparent. To investigate the impact of corrosion on the evolution of He bubbles, the average number density and size of the bubbles are presented in Table 2. After corrosion, the number density decreases significantly, accompanied by a notable increase in size, as shown in Table 2. There is also a slight difference in the average number density and size between the surviving and corroded regions. The observed differences in He bubbles in the two regions near the boundary can be attributed to two factors. First, the porous structure of the C-rich layer facilitates the aggregation and potential escape of He bubbles. Second, the significant differences also play a role. Further statistical analysis conducted approximately 320 nm away from the corrosion boundary in the surviving region yielded a mean bubble number density of (21.8±4.96)×1023 m−3 and a mean size of 1.25±0.14 nm, as shown in the white box in Fig. 9(a). The characteristics of He bubbles in the surviving region far from the boundary are more similar to those of the irradiated-only sample. ### 3.2 Discussion

All the experiments, including SEM/EDS, AFM, Raman and TEM, confirmed that the corrosion of SiC was accelerated by irradiation damage at 750 °C. As shown in Fig. 3, the surfaces of the irradiated and corroded samples clearly denude. Furthermore, the AFM images indicate that the denudation depth increases with increasing irradiation dose. The Raman results also verify that the variation in the peaks of SiC induced by irradiation can be attributed to irradiation-induced defects that disrupt the long-range ordering of the SiC crystals. The presence of these peaks at ~270 and 535–540 cm−1 indicates the formation of homonuclear Si–Si bonds resulting from vacancy/interstitial defects and antisite defects after irradiation [27]. It has been proposed that Si‒Si bonds are susceptible to FLiNaK salt corrosion [24,33].

Moreover, as shown in Figs. 8(b) and 9(c), the presence of Ni at the boundary indicates its interaction with SiC, resulting in the formation of a Ni*x*Si*y* compound during the corrosion process. This implies that the Ni impurities in the salt can also promote the corrosion process of SiC at 750 °C. However, the precise chemical composition remains unclear because of the overwhelming Si signal on the substrate during EDS analysis. On the basis of previous studies, the precipitate is most likely Ni2Si [22,34]. The thermodynamic properties of the reactions between the substances corresponding to the Si-C and Si-Si bonds and the metal fluoride at 750 °C were evaluated via the HSC 6.0 chemistry software. The analysis revealed Reactions (1) and (2):

(1) 2NiF2+2SiC=Ni2Si+SiF4(g)+2CΔG=−239.90kJ∙mol−1

(2) 2NiF2+2Si=Ni2Si+SiF4(g)ΔG=−303.45kJ∙mol−1

The more negative Gibbs free energy change of the Si‒Si bonds indicates that Eq. (2) is thermodynamically more favorable at 750 °C. The Ni impurity reacts preferentially with the homonuclear Si‒Si bonds. These findings confirmed that Ni plays a key role in the irradiation-promoted corrosion process of SiC. SiF4 can further react with F- to form  SiF62−, which serves to stabilize Si in the FLiNaK salt [19]. On the basis of the above results, a schematic of the corrosion process is presented in Fig. 11 to explain the synergistic damage effect of He ion irradiation and molten salt corrosion in SiC at 750 °C. Additionally, O is observed in the EDS maps of both the surface and cross-sections of the samples. Thus, O is also involved in the corrosion process of SiC, which is consistent with previous studies [19,20].

The effect of molten salt corrosion on the behavior of He bubbles is also studied. Compared with those in the He bubbles in the irradiated-only sample, the number density and size of the He bubbles in the irradiated and corroded sample notably change near the boundary (Fig. 9). However, in the surviving region far from the boundary, the number density and size of He bubbles are significantly closer to those of the irradiated-only sample, indicating that molten salt corrosion has a primary influence on the evolution of He bubbles inside or near the corroded region. It is proposed that the loss of Si results in the formation of vacancies or clusters in the corroded SiC. Consequently, there are sufficient vacancies and a distorted crystal structure surrounding them, providing favorable conditions for the migration and coalescence of He bubbles, which is believed to promote the growth of He bubbles at 750 °C. Furthermore, the appearance of linear bubbles can be attributed to the migration and coalescence of He bubbles, which ultimately leads to the formation of linear bubbles. In accordance with the above suppositions, Fig. 11(c) shows a schematic representation of the evolution of He bubbles under corrosion.

## 4 Conclusions

The irradiation and corrosion behavior of CVD SiC in FLiNaK salt at 750 °C for 166 h were investigated. The microstructure of the C-rich layer in SiC after molten salt corrosion at high temperatures was first visualized and characterized via transmission electron microscopy (TEM). SAED and HRTEM confirmed that a graphitic structure is present within the C-rich layer. The surfaces of the irradiated and corroded samples clearly denude, with the denudation depth increasing gradually with increasing irradiation dose. It has been demonstrated that the homonuclear Si‒Si bonds induced by irradiation are susceptible to FLiNaK salt corrosion at 750 °C. Furthermore, the Ni impurity in the salt was found to play a key role in the irradiation-promoted corrosion process of SiC because it reacts preferentially with Si‒Si bonds. After corrosion, the number density and size of the He bubbles clearly changed near or inside the C-rich layer. This phenomenon is attributed to the formation of vacancies or clusters by the loss of Si, which in turn promotes the migration and coalescence of He bubbles. Irradiation and corrosion at high temperatures induce synergistic damage to SiC.
