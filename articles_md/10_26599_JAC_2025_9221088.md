## 1 Introduction

Ferroelectric materials, owing to their off-center crystalline symmetry and associated external field-switchable spontaneous polarization, exhibit a variety of functional phenomena, including dielectric, piezoelectric, pyroelectric, and electrocaloric (EC) properties, which have been found to have broad applications in electronic devices [1−4]. Among these functionalities, ferroelectric refrigeration, which is based on the electrocaloric effect (ECE), is one of the most promising solid-state cooling technologies and has the advantages of high energy efficiency, zero-emission, easy miniaturization, convenient manipulation, and low cost, making it a hotspot of current ferroelectric research [5].

The optimal design of ferroelectric materials is affected by multiple factors, such as chemical composition, crystalline structure, microstructure, and domain configuration, among which chemical composition is the most popular and effective approach for tuning almost all ferroelectric properties [6−8]. The adoption of various ion substitutions can shift the phase transition temperature, adjust the enthalpy change (Δ*H*), induce phase transition diffusion, and even completely change the ferroelectricity (ferroelectric/antiferroelectric/relaxor ferroelectric) so that the properties, including dielectric, ferroelectric, and piezoelectric properties, can be modified for various applications [9−12]. However, it is still difficult to work in some special cases. For example, the large Δ*H* near or even below room temperature (RT) is desirable for most marketable refrigeration applications. However, the large Δ*H* in simple ABO3 materials is always produced at high Curie temperature (*T*C), following the thermodynamic conditions of phase stability [13,14]. Δ*H* of BaTiO3 is 0.9 J/g at 125 °C, and that of PbZrO3 is 4.2 J/g at 234 °C [15,16]. When *T*C is lowered toward RT by ion substitution, Δ*H* significantly decreases at the same time. For Ba0.94La0.04TiO3 with *T*C = 24 °C, Δ*H* decreases to 0.18 J/g, whereas for Pb0.85La0.15Zr0.4Ti0.6O3 with *T*C = 26 °C, Δ*H* is 0.19 J/g [15,17].

Recently, lead scandium tantalate (PbSc0.5Ta0.5O3 (PST)) was reported to have both the large Δ*H* of 1.031 J/g and low *T*C of 27 °C [18]. Later, PbMg0.5W0.5O3 (PMW) with a similar PbB*'*0.5B*''*0.5O3 configuration was discovered to have the even larger Δ*H* of 3.92 J/g at a similar temperature of 36 °C [19]. Unlike traditional ABO3 materials, where various ion substitutions are randomly distributed, PbB*'*0.5B*''*0.5O3 has two kinds of equal amounts but heterovalent ions occupying B-sites and can exhibit fully ordered, partially ordered, or disordered configurations. For PST and PMW, the ordered ion arrangement results in excellent ECE properties. The highly ordered PST ceramics have excellent EC properties, and their multilayer ceramic capacitors (MLCCs) have a very large adiabatic temperature change (Δ*T*) of 5.5 K@290 kV/cm [20]. The properties of PbB*'*0.5B*''*0.5O3 are closely related to the ordering degree (Ω) of the B-site ion distribution. When Ω decreases from 0.96 to 0.81, the EC Δ*T* of the PST ceramic decreases from 4.3 to 3.8 K [20]. However, research on PbB*'*0.5B*''*0.5O3 is still rare, especially research on the effective regulation of Ω and its influence on ferroelectric properties.

In general, the distribution of B-site ions is determined by ion hopping activated by thermal agitation and ordering due to the charge difference between neighboring heterovalent ions. Thus, the PST samples with different Ω values are stable at RT, whereas the distributions of Sc3+ and Ta5+ at B-sites at the nanoscale can be tailored by thermal treatments, including annealing and quenching [21,22]. PST ceramics with high Ω values (Ω ≈ 1) are difficult to achieve because they are not just a function of the annealing time from the initial high-temperature quenched state. Too low a temperature leads to too slow a kinetic process, and too high a temperature enables too much ion hopping randomly, whereas too long a time and too high a temperature of thermal treatment both induce uncontrollable Pb volatilization. In fact, Ω depends in a complex manner on the thermal history of each individual sample [23]. In this work, a PST ceramic with the high Ω of 1 was obtained by carefully controlling the parameters during multiple heat treatment processes, as shown in Fig. 1. Moreover, we explored the large-scale regulation of Ω in PST ceramics and systematically studied its effect on various ferroelectric properties. The variation in Ω provides an efficient method to modify ferroelectric properties, including the phase transition temperature, phase transition enthalpy, transition diffuseness, and relaxation characteristics. In particular, PST ceramics with tunable Ω values exhibit excellent EC properties toward refrigeration application requirements. The sample with Ω = 1 has the giant EC Δ*T* of 4.26 K@60 kV/cm at 37 °C, and that with Ω = 0.51 has the fairly good EC Δ*T* of 1.57 K@60 kV/cm at a rather low temperature of 0 °C. The former is excellent for RT refrigeration, the latter opens up the possibility of low-temperature refrigeration, and the continuous adjustment of the operating temperature facilitates a cascade cooling design. This work proves that lattice ordering is another efficient route for modifying ferroelectric features, i.e., a new method for material design and optimization. Moreover, the ordering-optimized PST ceramics open a new avenue to explore advanced near/below RT refrigeration systems.

## 2 Experimental

Lead oxide (PbO; ≥ 99.9%), scandium oxide (Sc2O3; ≥ 99.9%), and tantalic oxide (Ta2O5; ≥ 99.9%) were adopted as raw materials. The PST bulk ceramics were prepared via the conventional solid-state reaction method. To eliminate the component deviation effect and the generation of pyrochlore, a pre-synthesis method was used to fabricate the PST solid solution. First, ScTaO4 was obtained through a solid-state reaction between Sc2O3 and Ta2O5 powders, which was ball milled for 4 h and prefired at 1450 °C for 4 h. The obtained ScTaO4 and PbO powders were weighed and mixed in a roller mill at 350 r/min for 4 h with zirconia ball media and alcohol. The mixtures were calcined at 900 °C for 4 h and milled again. The dried powders were then dry-pressed into pellets with a diameter of 10 mm under an axial pressure of 2 MPa and then an isostatic pressure of 200 MPa. The green samples were sintered at temperatures ranging from 900 to 1400 °C for 4 h in air, and then all of them were annealed at 900 °C for 190 h to increase the B-site ion ordering via a double crucible conﬁguration with homologous and protective PST powders.

The phase structures of the PST ceramics were characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; SmartLab 9 kW, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα1/2 radiation. The atomic resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) study was performed on a microscope (Titan Themis G3 60-300, FEI, USA). The heat flow curves were measured with a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC; Q2000, TA Instruments, USA), and the specific heat capacity was characterized in modulated DSC mode. The temperature dependence of the dielectric permittivity and loss data were measured by an LCR meter (a type of electronic test equipment used to measure the inductance (*L*), capacitance (*C*), and resistance (*R*) of an electronic component; E4990A, Keysight, USA) equipped with a temperature chamber. The ferroelectric hysteresis loops and current‒electric field curves were measured by using a ferroelectric analyzer (aixACCT TF2000, Germany). The direct ECE measurement was carried out by a typical heat flux measurement, where the DSC was used to provide a different isothermal environment and simultaneously record the Δ*H* under the electric field applied by a direct current (DC) power supply (Trek 677B, USA).

### 3.1 Modulation and nanoscale characterization of the ordering degree

The cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) micrographs of PST ceramics under different heat treatment conditions are shown in Figs. S1(a)–S1(f) and S2(g)–S2(l) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). All the PST ceramic samples exhibit close-packed grains, indicating excellent densification. The grain size distribution plots exhibit a normal distribution for all samples (Figs. S1(a1)–S1(f1) and S2(g1)–S2(l1) in the ESM). As the initial sintering temperature increased, the average grain size (*G*a) increased from 1.08 to 2.03 μm. After the annealing treatment (900 °C for 190 h), the *G*a content further increased slightly (Fig. S3 in the ESM).

To reveal the effect of thermal treatment conditions on Ω, the PST ceramics were first sintered and then annealed again under different conditions. The Ω values analyzed by XRD are shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM. As the initial sintering temperature increases, the Ω of the PST ceramics decreases, as shown in Fig. 2(a). This is because ion hopping intensifies at higher temperatures, which destroys the ordering arrangement of the Sc3+ and Ta5+ ions. To improve Ω, the PST ceramics sintered at different temperatures were again annealed at 900 °C for 190 h together. Owing to the relatively low annealing temperature for a moderate duration, Ω clearly increases for all the samples, and a fully ordered PST ceramic with Ω = 1 is achieved. In other words, we obtained PST ceramics whose Ω values ranged between 0.51 and 1 by adjusting the initial sintering and annealing treatment.

To investigate the arrangement of B-site ions, aberration-corrected STEM was conducted. Atomically resolved high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) images focused on the [001], [110], and [111] zone axes for PST ceramics with Ω = 0.51 and Ω = 1 are selected, as shown in Figs. 2 and S6 in the ESM. The HAADF images at the nanoscale viewed along the [001] zone axis (Fig. 2(b)) are almost the same for the two samples, suggesting that characterizing the ion ordering from the [001] zone axis is difficult. However, the distance between the crystal faces is different. It is 0.2973 nm for Ω = 0.51 and 0.2925 nm for Ω = 1, indicating a slightly larger lattice volume for the disordered sample than for the ordered sample. This finding is consistent with the XRD results in Fig. S5 in the ESM, which shows a continuous decrease in the lattice volume with increasing Ω. In terms of the double perovskite structure along the [110] direction, the HAADF images clearly differ between the two samples, as shown in Figs. 2(c) and 2(d), indicating the different distribution modes of Ta5+ or Sc3+ ions in the PST lattice. Since the intensity of each column is determined by its average atomic number, the columns of Pb2+ (Z* = 82) and Ta5+ (*Z* = 73) ions are much brighter than those of Sc3+ (*Z* = 21) ions in the HAADF images. For the sample with Ω = 0.51, there are disordered regions and ordered regions. In the ordered region, the adjacent spots show obvious dim and bright alternations, where the Sc columns appear much faint compared with the Ta columns. Moreover, the intensity variation of the Sc and Ta columns shows a well-defined periodicity in Fig. S7 in the ESM. In the disordered region, each column has a similar intensity (Fig. 2(e)), indicating that each column contains heterologous elements in a random arrangement. For the PST ceramic with Ω = 1, the HAADF image clearly shows dim and bright spots in the full-view field at the nanoscale, as shown in Figs. 2(d) and 2(f), indicating that the Sc3+ and Ta5+ ions present an alternating and ordered arrangement. These results also indicate that the PST ceramic with Ω = 0.51 has an ordered and disordered configuration for B-site ions and that the sample with Ω = 1 has a fully ordered arrangement of B-site ions.

### 3.2 Phase transition and thermal properties

The temperature dependence of the dielectric constant (*ε*r) for PST ceramics with different Ω values is shown in Fig. 3(a). With increasing Ω, *T*C shifts to higher temperatures, from −7 °C for Ω = 0.51 to 28 °C for Ω = 1. The variation range of *T*C covers RT to below zero, endowing PST ceramics with application potential for the most marketable refrigeration devices. In addition, with increasing Ω, the shape of the dielectric peak becomes sharper, indicating enhanced ferroelectric characteristics. The diffusion exponent (*γ*) was calculated according to the Curie‒Weiss law and is shown in Fig. S10 in the ESM. It is 1.17 for Ω = 1, i.e., a clear feature of a normal ferroelectric with first-order phase transition, whereas, for low Ω, it is 1.34 for Ω = 0.59 and 1.42 for Ω = 0.51, suggesting a weakened ferroelectricity with diffuse phase transition. In addition, the frequency dispersion becomes more notable in the sample with the low Ω, indicating a gradual transition toward a relaxor ferroelectric. The reason for the variation in the ferroelectric characteristics of the PST ceramics may be as follows. On the one hand, the chaotic arrangement of Sc3+ and Ta5+ in disordered PST ceramics destroys long-range ferroelectric ordering and promotes the formation of nanodomains or polar nanoregions (PNRs) [24]. On the other hand, the increase in the lattice volume in the disordered PST ceramics results in lattice strain, which reduces the domain size. In other words, the ferroelectric characteristics of PST ceramics can be regulated by adjusting Ω.

The shift in T*C for different Ω values was also confirmed by the heat flow results, as shown in Fig. 3(b), which also provides the Δ*H* results at the same time. For the fully ordered PST sample with Ω = 1, a sharp endothermic peak is observed, corresponding to the largest Δ*H* of 1.06 J/g. As Ω decreases, the endothermic peak broadens, and Δ*H* decreases, which implies that more diffuse ferroelectric phase transitions occur. For the sample with Ω = 0.51, Δ*H* decreases to 0.16 J/g. Accompanied by the zero-field heat capacity (*C*p) measurements (Fig. S11(c) in the ESM), we access the large transition entropy change (Δ*S*0 ≈ 3.52 J/(kg·K)) for Ω = 1.

### 3.3 Ferroelectric properties and <i>E</i>–<i>T</i> phase diagram of PST ceramics

Since *T*C of the PST ceramics with Ω = 0.51–1 spans a wide temperature range, the polarization–electric field hysteresis loops (*P*–*E*) were measured at a low temperature of −5 °C under 40 kV/cm to compare their polarization behavior (Fig. 4(a)). The PST samples with high Ω values (0.85–1) have regular square *P*–*E* loops of the ferroelectric phase because the test temperature is much lower than their *T*C, whereas the sample with Ω = 0.51 has a clear double hysteresis loop because the test temperature falls within the thermal hysteresis region of the phase transition. The sample with Ω = 1 exhibited a strong ferroelectric feature with a large maximum polarization (*P*max) of 39.1 μC/cm2 and a large remanent polarization (*P*r) of 31.3 μC/cm2, as shown in Fig. 4(a). Both *P*max and *P*r decrease with decreasing Ω (Fig. S14 in the ESM). All the PST samples have a small coercive field (*E*c) of less than 5 kV/cm, indicating easy domain rotation.

*
Fig. 4Polarization characteristics of PST ceramics with different Ω values: (a) P*–*E* loops at −5 °C for samples with different Ω values, (b) *P‒E* loops at different temperatures for sample with Ω = 1, (c) *J‒E* curves at different temperatures for Ω = 1, and (d) contour plots of *J‒E* curves under different temperatures and electric fields for Ω = 1.

To reveal the temperature dependence of the ferroelectricity, the *P‒E* loops were also measured at different temperatures, and Fig. 4(b) shows the typical results for the sample with Ω = 1. The *P‒E* loop shows a saturated square shape at ~0 °C, i.e., a normal ferroelectric phase. The *P‒E* loop gradually becomes slimmer with increasing temperature because the thermal agitation weakens the ferroelectricity. It becomes a double loop when the temperature is near *T*C and enters the thermal hysteresis region of the phase transition. When the temperature is higher than *T*C, the sample transitions to the paraelectric phase accompanied by linear *P‒E* loops. The same evolution rule is observed in all the PST ceramics, indicating no correlation with Ω (Figs. S15 and S16 in the ESM). The double hysteresis loops originate from the bistable state around the phase transition temperature. It appears above *T*1 in Landau–Devonshire theory, i.e., the highest temperature where the low-temperature phase exists in a metastable state under zero field. With increasing temperature, the critical field increases, and the width of each single loop decreases. The former relates to the ease of field-induced transition, and the latter relates to the stability of the field-induced phase. Both of these factors reflect the competition between the field-induced polarization order and the thermally excited disorder. Note that the double hysteresis loops represent a field-induced transition to the ferroelectric phase, which was also observed in BaTiO3 with a first-order phase transition [25]. As already observed and explained by Shebanovs *et* *al.* [26] and then by Crossley *et* *al.* [27], this phenomenon implies a high ECE.

The evolution of the ferroelectric properties with increasing temperature is also confirmed by the current density–electric field (*J*–*E*) curves, and Fig. 4(c) shows the typical results for the PST ceramic with Ω = 1. When the test temperature is far below *T*C, the *J*–*E* curves exhibit a single peak, indicating a ferroelectric feature. When the temperature is close to *T*C, the current density peak sharply decreases and splits into two peaks, resulting in a field-induced phase transition. When the temperature is above *T*C, the two peaks flatten and shift toward high electric fields, i.e., a paraelectric feature. In addition, as Ω decreases, the *J*–*E* curves of the PST ceramics gradually broaden, indicating enhanced relaxor characteristics (Fig. S18 in the ESM).

To further clarify the field-induced phase transition process under the coupling effect of temperature and electric field, the contour plots of current density with temperature and electric field were plotted by extracting data from the lower half of the *J*–*E* curve (+*E*max → −*E*max). The contour plot of *J* serves as the electric field‒temperature phase diagram, and the high *J* bands indicate domain boundaries or phase boundaries. As shown in Fig. 4(d), for the sample with Ω = 1, there is only one high *J* band in the low-temperature region, representing the polarization reversal process in the rhombohedral ferroelectric (FER) phase. As the temperature increases to near *T*C, the high *J* band splits into two branches, representing the field-induced ferroelectric‒paraelectric phase transition. In the high-temperature region, PST always maintains a paraelectric phase. As Ω decreases, the splitting point of the high *J* band moves to lower temperatures, indicating a shift in the phase transition temperature (Fig. S19 in the ESM).

### 3.4 Direct measurement of ECE

ECE was measured by directly measuring the isothermal heat flow with continuous changes in electric fields via DSC (Fig. S20 in the ESM). The Δ*T* values for the PST ceramics with different Ω values were measured in the range of −40–80 °C, and the results at 60 kV/cm are plotted in Fig. 5(a). All the PST ceramics have positive ECEs. The sample with Ω = 1 has a giant EC Δ*T*max = 4.26 K@60 kV/cm, which is greater than that reported previously (Δ*T*max = 3.7 K@40 kV/cm) [18] because of the higher Ω in this work. Moreover, the corresponding Δ*H* of 1.28 J/g in the field-induced phase transition is slightly higher than that of 1.06 J/g obtained under zero electric field in the heat flow curves in Fig. 3(b). This is because under high electric fields, the ferroelectric phase transition is carried out more completely, and the ferroelectric domains are more ordered. In addition, the temperature of the ECE peak (*T*peak = 37 °C) is also higher than *T*C = 28 °C measured under zero field. As Ω decreases, both Δ*T*max and *T*peak decrease. *T*peak decreases to 0 °C for Ω = 0.51, whereas Δ*T*max remains high at 1.57 K, which is the record-high value for all EC materials at such a low temperature in the literature. For example, Δ*T*max is only 0.13 K for the Ba0.6Sr0.4TiO3 ceramic at 1 °C [28], which is less than a tenth of our work. The lower the operating temperature of coolers is, the more difficult it is, and the near/below RT refrigeration equipment has the largest market demand at present. The wide temperature range from 0 to 37 °C in this work covers the requirement of RT refrigeration for the operating temperature. In addition, continuously adjusting the operating temperature by tuning Ω facilitates a cascade refrigeration design that can multiply the cooling capacity in each step. These unique advantages of PST ceramics open a new avenue for the use of EC materials in near/below-RT refrigeration devices. In addition to direct measurements, ECE was also evaluated via indirect thermodynamic calculations based on Maxwell relations. In general, the results are similar to the direct results, including the shape of the Δ*T*–*T* curves and the value of Δ*T*max (Figs. S15–S17 in the ESM).

Figure 5(c) compares the ΔT*max and *T*peak values of the PST ceramics with those of typical EC materials in the literature, including bulk ceramics, single crystals, and MLCCs. Previously, a large ECE response was always obtained at high operating temperatures. For example, Δ*T*max of 5.5 K at 57 °C was obtained in PST MLCCs [20]. The PST ceramics developed in this work always maintain a high ECE performance (Δ*T*max = 1.57–4.26 K) over a wide range of temperatures near/below RT (0–37 °C), which is especially suitable for RT refrigeration devices with great market potential. The excellent EC properties near RT are superior to those of most other materials. For example, Δ*T* is 0.58 K at 30 °C for 0.92Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3–0.08PbTiO3 and 0.42 K at 20 °C for Ba0.65Sr0.35TiO3 [28,29].

In addition to the operating temperature, the applied field is another critical index in practical applications in ECE refrigeration. A comparison of the Δ*T* values for various EC materials under different electric ﬁelds is shown in Fig. 5(d). Considering the feasible integration in electronic systems and the reliability of ferroelectric materials under high fields, it is better to achieve the larger Δ*T* under lower electric fields. Δ*T* is 4.5 K under 145 kV/cm for BaZr0.2Ti0.8O3 bulk ceramic and 5.5 K under 290 kV/cm for PST MLCCs [20,30]. In contrast, Δ*T* reaches 1.69 K for the PST ceramic with Ω = 1 only under a low field of 10 kV/cm, which is greater than Δ*T* of 1.4 K@10 kV/cm for the BaTiO3 ceramic [31]. When the electric field increases to 20 kV/cm, Δ*T* increases dramatically to 3.12 K, which meets the needs of practical refrigeration applications. This value is much higher than that of other materials near RT even under higher electric fields. For example, Δ*T* is only 0.61 K@20 kV/cm for BaTi0.895Sn0.105O3 ceramics and 0.96 K@100 kV/cm for Na(Nb0.92Ta0.08)O3–0.17BaTiO3 ceramics [32,33]. In addition, all the PST ceramics exhibit a series of small *Q*33 values below 0.018 m4/C2, and the weakened electrostrictive activity enhances the performance stability of subsequent devices (Fig. S25 in the ESM) [34]. On the basis of the above analysis, PST ceramics have exceptional EC capabilities for large ∆*T* values, low operating temperatures near/below RT and low electrostrains, confirming the feasibility of the material design strategy for commercial refrigeration applications.

## 4 Conclusions

We carefully adjust Ω for the PST ceramics on a continuously large scale from 0.51 to 1 via multiple heat treatment processes. The wide *T*C ranges from −7 to 28 °C cover the requirement of RT refrigeration for operating temperature. The large Δ*H* of 1.06 J/g and very large Δ*T*max = 4.26 K@60 kV/cm at *T*peak of 37 °C are achieved in highly ordered PST ceramics with Ω = 1. As Ω decreases, both Δ*T*max and *T*peak decrease. *T*peak decreases to 0 °C for Ω = 0.51, whereas Δ*T*max still remains high at 1.57 K, which is the record-high value for almost all reported EC materials at such a low temperature. This work provides a new approach to material design and refrigeration devices, and in particular, the continuous regulation of a wide range of operating temperatures of 0–37 °C contributes to cascade cooling designs. Moreover, the degree of ordering modulation in PST ceramics opens a new avenue to explore advanced near/below RT refrigeration systems.
