## 1 Introduction

Currently, the prevailing 5G communication era has greatly stimulated tremendous progress in microwave devices and has also led to a demand for more stringent dielectric characteristics. Specifically, a low dielectric constant (*ε*r f| ≤ 10 ppm/°C, where *τ*f is the temperature coefficient of the resonant frequency) are required for microwave dielectric ceramics [1,2]. Such performance can shorten the signal transmission time, improve frequency selectivity, reduce insertion loss, and ensure temperature stability in millimeter wave communication. To date, many low-*ε*r ceramics, including Al2O3, Zn2SiO4, CaGa2O4, CaMgGeO4, and Y3Al5O12, have been widely reported because of their ultrahigh *Q*×*f* values [3−7]. However, too high a densification temperature severely restricts their industrial application.

Recently, vanadate compounds with cubic garnet structure have attracted much scientific attention owing to the structural diversity of garnets, and a variety of ceramics with low *ε*r values have been developed. Zhou *et al*. [8] prepared LiCa3ZnV3O12 ceramics via the direct reaction sintering route and obtained remarkable microwave dielectric properties: *ε*r = 6.9, *Q*×*f* = 45,618 GHz, and *τ*f = −39.6 ppm/°C. Ca2YMg2Ge2VO12 ceramics with *ε*r = 10.1, *Q*×*f* = 65,600 GHz, and *τ*f = −53 ppm/°C after sintering at 1300 °C were reported by Duan *et al*. [9]. After the Ca5Mn4(VO4)6 ceramics were sintered at 875 °C, the excellent microwave dielectric properties were *ε*r = 11.2, *Q*×*f* = 33,800 GHz, and *τ*f = −70 ppm/°C [10]. Although vanadate dielectrics have relatively low sintering temperatures, their large and negative *τ*f values still limit their practical application, similar to most low-*ε*r ceramics. Adding those materials with positive *τ*f values into low *ε*r ceramics is an effective method to adjust *τ*f to a near-zero value. However, this method inevitably results in a decrease in the *Q*×*f* value. Consequently, one key challenge facing low-*ε*r ceramics is how to adjust *τ*f to the near-zero value without deteriorating *Q*×*f*.

High-entropy ceramics are defined as ceramics that have a Δ*S*config ≥ 1.5R, where R is the universal gas constant. The term “medium-entropy ceramics” refers to having a Δ*S*config between 1R and 1.5R, and the term “low-entropy ceramics” refers to having a Δ*S*config below 1R. Compared with low-entropy ceramics, high (medium)-entropy ceramics provide a much fresher perspective on performance regulation for material design. By doping compositions with equal amounts into the matrix, high(medium)-entropy ceramics have excellent properties, including energy storage, electromagnetic wave-absorbing, mechanical, and dielectric performance, thus exhibiting great potential for applications in the energy, wave-absorbing, engineering, electrical, and electronic fields. The application of a high-entropy effect to microwave dielectric ceramics can optimize performance by forming a single-phase/solid-phase solution, which is a novel approach for regulating *τ*f to a near zero value. Xiang *et al*. [11] reported that Li(Gd0.2Ho0.2Er0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)GeO4 high-entropy ceramics with olivine structure had excellent microwave performance: *ε*r = 9.0, *Q*×*f* = 11,700 GHz, and *τ*f = −7.4 ppm/°C. Lin reported microwave performances of *ε*r = 22.5, *Q*×*f* = 83,000 GHz, and *τ*f = −1.7 ppm/°C for SrLa (Al0.25Zn0.125Mg0.125Ga0.25Ti0.25)O4 ceramics [12]. Chen *et al*. [13] successfully synthesized fergusonite-structured (La1/5Nd1/5Sm1/5Ho1/5Y1/5)NbO4 high-entropy ceramics (*ε*r = 19.5, *Q*×*f* = 47,770 GHz, and *τ*f = −13.5 ppm/°C). Recently, Xiao *et al*. [14] investigated rutile-structured TiSnNbTaGa2O12 high-entropy ceramics (*ε*r = 33.6, *Q*×*f* = 51,830 GHz, and *τ*f = −0.38 ppm/°C).

A novel compound, Ca3Co2SiV2O12, with a garnet structure, was reported in 2014 by Mill *et al.* [15] and has versatile applications in magnetics, antiferromagnetics, and luminescent materials. However, it is not clear whether Ca3Co2SiV2O12 has potential applications in microwave dielectrics. Based on the analysis of its ionic polarizability and crystal structure, Ca3Co2SiV2O12 should be a low-*ε*r material. Moreover, it has a lower synthesis temperature than other garnet-structured microwave dielectric ceramics, such as germanates, aluminates, and gallates. Consequently, exploring the microwave dielectric properties of Ca3Co2SiV2O12 ceramics is necessary.

A small ionic radius difference is crucial for maintaining the structural stability of medium-entropy ceramics, thus facilitating the formation of a single-phase solid solution. Considering their similar ionic radii (Co2+: 0.65 Å, Zn2+: 0.74 Å, Mg2+: 0.72 Å, Cu2+: 0.73 Å, and CN = 6), Zn2+, Mg2+, and Cu2+ are introduced to the Co-site in Ca3Co2SiV2O12 to produce Ca3(Co0.25Zn0.25Mg0.25Cu0.25)2SiV2O12 (CZMC) medium-entropy ceramics. Moreover, the inclusion of low-melting-point compounds, such as CuO and ZnO, is beneficial for decreasing the sintering temperature of ceramics. Herein, CZMC medium-entropy ceramics are designed on the basis of the medium-entropy strategy. In addition, the inherent correlation between the structure and properties of the ceramics is systematically evaluated, demonstrating the feasibility of the use of the entropy effect to increase the temperature stability and maintain the *Q*×*f*.

## 2 Experimental

Ca3(Co0.25Zn0.25Mg0.25Cu0.25)2SiV2O12 ceramics were fabricated through a solid-state sintering approach employing CaCO3 (≥ 99.5%, Aladdin, China), CoO (≥ 99%, Macklin, China), ZnO (≥ 99%, Macklin, China), MgO (≥ 98%, Macklin, China), CuO (≥ 99.5%, Macklin, China), SiO2 (≥ 99%, Macklin, China), and V2O5 (≥ 99.5%, Macklin, China) as raw materials. These substances were stoichiometrically weighed and ball-milled for 12 h, followed by calcination at 950 °C for 4 h. Then, a repeated ball-milling process of 12 h was applied to the calcined products. After drying and grinding, the resultants were mixed with a 6 wt% polyvinyl alcohol solution homogeneously and pressed into cylindrical pellets (diameter: 10 mm; thickness: 5 mm). The organic binder was discharged by heating at 600 °C for 2 h, and the cylindrical pellets were sintered at 970–1050 °C for 6 h at a heating rate of 5 °C/min by embedding them in powders with the same composition. Finally, these pellets were allowed to cool to room temperature naturally.

The phase structure was inspected via an X-ray powder diffractometer (XRD; D/max2000V, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation. The obtained data were refined via the Rietveld method via the GSAS-EXPGUI software. Raman spectra were collected by means of a Raman spectrometer (Invia, Renishaw, UK). The surface morphology of the sintered samples was examined via a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Mira3, Tescan, Czech Republic). Before SEM examination, the surfaces of the sintered samples were hierarchically polished with P1200#, P1500#, and P2000# sandpaper and then thermally etched for 30 min at a temperature 50 °C lower than the optimum sintering temperature. The average grain size of the sintered bodies was determined via Nano Measurer software via the linear intercept method. The bulk density (*ρ*bu) was measured via the Archimedes drainage method. The relative density (*ρ*re) was calculated via Eqs. (1) and (2):

(1) ρre=ρbuρth×100%

(2) ρth=AZNAV

where *ρ*th refers to the theoretical density and *A* and *Z* are the atomic weight (g/mol) and molecular number, respectively; *N*A and *V* represent the Avogadro constant (mol−1) and unit cell volume (cm3), respectively. AC impedance spectroscopy analysis was performed with an impedance analyzer (4294A, Agilent, USA), and the obtained data were modeled with ZView-2 software. The microwave dielectric properties were determined via a vector network analyzer (N5230C, Keysight Technologies, USA) according to the Hakki‒Coleman dielectric resonator method [16]. The *τ*f (ppm/°C) was evaluated in the temperature range from 25 to 85 °C in a temperature chamber (MC-710AGP, ESPEC, Japan) via Eq. (3):

(3) τf=f85−f25(82−25)×f25×106

where *f*25 and *f*85 are the resonant frequencies at temperatures of 25 and 85 °C, respectively.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1 shows the densities of the CZMC ceramics, which include the bulk density and relative density, sintered at 970–1050 °C. The bulk density increases from 2.83 to 3.69 g/cm3 as the sintering temperature increases from 970 to 1010 °C, corresponding to an increase in the relative density from 73.1% to 95.4% (the theoretical density is 3.86 g/cm3). Both the bulk density and the relative density subsequently tend to decrease overall at higher sintering temperatures. In our previous study, we reported that dense Ca3Co2SiV2O12 ceramics could not be obtained at a sintering temperature of 1160 °C (93.7% relative density). Therefore, an increase in Δ*S*config can effectively reduce the sintering temperature of ceramics and promote densification. The reason is that multiple components occupying the same lattice site cause compositional disorder and lead to severe lattice distortion. The lattice distortion produced by the medium-entropy effect introduces defects (e.g., vacancies and dislocations) that act as diffusion pathways. These defects can reduce the energy barrier for atomic movement and allow atoms to diffuse more easily, thus reducing the sintering temperature [17].

Like the XRD method, Raman spectroscopy can also be utilized to investigate the local crystal structure evolution of materials. For the garnet structure with the Ia*3*d* space group, twenty-five Raman modes can be obtained through group theoretical calculations. However, as shown in Fig. 6(a), the measured number of Raman modes of the CZMC ceramics is lower than the calculated number. Only six modes of Raman activity are found by fitting the Raman data via the Gaussian−Lorentzian function (Fig. 6(b) and Fig. S2 in the ESM). Generally, the absence of certain Raman patterns in experiments is attributed to instrument resolution, low-intensity peaks, and overlapping peaks [20−22]. In the garnet structure, the Raman vibration modes are usually divided into two types: exterior and internal vibration modes [23]. The former derives from the translational motion of cations, whereas the latter is always influenced by the [C–O]4 tetrahedron. For the CZMC ceramics, the Raman modes in the range of 50–300 cm−1 are caused by the translational motion of Ca2+ cations. In the range of 300–600 cm−1, the Raman-active peaks are attributed to the rotational vibrations of the [Si/V–O]4 tetrahedron. The symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of the [Si/V–O]4 tetrahedron lead to the appearance of Raman modes above 600 cm−1. Surprisingly, two additional Raman peaks appear in the sample sintered at 1050 °C. However, no secondary phase is found in the CMZC ceramics at any sintering temperature based on the XRD and SEM results. A possible reason for this phenomenon is that high sintering temperatures might activate certain low-energy vibrational modes, making them visible in Raman spectra. According to Refs. [24,25], two analogous Raman peaks are found in garnet ceramics Ca3Ta1.5Ga3.5O12 and Ca3Cr2Ge3O12, which correspond to the rotational and bending vibrations of [Ge–O]4 (or [Ga–O]4), respectively. Therefore, the two additional Raman peaks in the current system are speculated to be associated with the rotational and bending vibrations of the [Si/V–O]4 tetrahedron. Additionally, some features of the mode at approximately 838 cm−1, such as the Raman shift and full width at half-maximum (FWHM), are regarded as the main factors affecting the microwave dielectric properties [26,27].

Figure 7 shows the sintering temperature dependence of the relative density, ε*r, and Raman shift of the CZMC ceramics. The *ε*r of the ceramics tends to first increase but then decrease as a function of the sintering temperature, corresponding to the variation in the relative density. The maximum *ε*r value of 10.89 is achieved at 1010 °C, at which the maximum relative density value is 95.4%. Generally, the relative density and secondary phase significantly affect the *ε*r of dielectric ceramics. Overall, the effect of the secondary phase can be neglected since all the samples have a pure phase. Therefore, the relative density is a key determinant of the *ε*r of CZMC ceramics. The theoretical dielectric constant (*ε*th) and the corrected dielectric constant (*ε*corr) of CZMC ceramics can be calculated via Eqs. (6) and (7), respectively [28,29]:

*
Fig. 7Sintering temperature dependence of the relative density, dielectric constant, and Raman shift of the CZMC ceramics.

(6) εth=3Vm+8πα3Vm−4πα

(7) εcorr=εr(1+1.5P)

where V*m and *α* are the unit cell volume and the polarizability of the molecules, respectively. *P* refers to the porosity (= 1 − *ρ*re). The obtained *ε*th and *ε*corr values are 11.69 and 11.66, respectively, which are similar to the maximum *ε*r values of the CZMC ceramics. A negative and small dielectric constant deviation value (* Δ=(εr−εth)/εth=−6.8%) indicates that the medium-entropy strategy at the B-site of the garnet structure does not result in the generation of the “Rattling effect”. The “Rattling effect” was first proposed by Dunitz and Orgel [30], indicating the off-center displacement of ferroelectric and antiferroelectric materials due to the loosening of the cations in the center of the octahedron when the size of the cations decreases. For the garnet structure, the displacement of cations will cause twisting deformation of the polyhedron and lead to unusually large polarizability. However, several studies have revealed that the “Rattling effect” generally appears in A-site-substituted garnets, such as Mg3Y2Ge3O12 and Y3−x*Mg*x*Al(Oct)2Al(Tet)3−*x*Si*x*O12 [31,32]. Our current results are in accordance with the previous conclusions. Moreover, Eq. (7) further shows that the relative density, or the pores in ceramics, has a remarkable influence on *ε*r because the *ε*r of air (~1) is significantly smaller than that of CZMC ceramics.

In addition, the *ε*r of the CZMC ceramics tends to differ from that of the Raman shift when the sintering temperature increases from 970 to 1050 °C. This correlation exists in many ceramic systems, such as TiSnNbTaGa2O12, Ca1−*x*Sr*x*MgSi2O6, and CaY2Al4SiO12 [14,33,34]. *ε*r is closely related to the rigidity of the oxygen polyhedron. In general, the occurrence of a low-angle shift implies decreased rigidity of the oxygen polyhedron, corresponding to an increase in *ε*r [35].

Figure 8 shows the sintering temperature dependence of the relative density, *Q*×*f*, and FWHM of the CZMC ceramics. As the sintering temperature increases, the *Q*×*f* initially increases from 23,490 to 59,200 GHz. Moreover, *Q*×*f* reaches its highest value at 1010 °C. When the sintering temperature exceeds 1010 °C, the value of *Q*×*f* gradually decreases. This variation is identical to that of the relative density but opposite to that of the FWHM. The *Q*×*f* of ceramics is commonly determined by extrinsic and intrinsic factors. The extrinsic factors mainly include phase configuration, relative density, and grain size, whereas the intrinsic contributors primarily include lattice vibration and ion polarization. No secondary phase exists in the CZMC ceramics. Thus, the relative density and grain size are representative extrinsic factors. Dense ceramics with large grain sizes are advantageous for improving the *Q*×*f*, whereas ceramics with lower densities exhibit a reduced *Q*×*f* due to higher porosity [36]. Generally, a larger grain size means a smaller grain boundary, leading to a reduction in lattice imperfection; thus, the *Q*×*f* value increases. However, overlarge grains always contain more defects and impurities at grain boundaries due to excessive grain growth, resulting in a decrease in the *Q*×*f* value. As a result, the relationship between *Q*×*f* and the grain size is not very strict. In the present work, the CZMC ceramics with the largest grain size do not have the highest *Q*×*f* values. Combined with the density results shown in Fig. 1, the density rather than the grain size should be a dominantly extrinsic factor for the current ceramics. Increasing the sintering temperature promotes the densification of ceramics and reduces their number of defects, thus improving the *Q*×*f*. However, a high sintering temperature can also result in the generation of more defects, including vacancies from atom volatilization and grain boundary defects, due to abnormal grain growth, ultimately leading to a reduction in the density and hence the *Q*×*f*. In addition, the *Q*×*f* of ceramics is strongly associated with the lattice anharmonic vibration [37]. As shown in Eqs. (8) and (9), increasing the FWHM indicates that the damping behavior of some lattice vibrational modes is increased, leading to increased intrinsic dielectric loss. Therefore, when the FWHM value is at its minimum, the *Q*×*f*  is at its maximum.

Here, the intrinsic relationship between the microwave dielectric properties and chemical bond characteristics in CZMC ceramics is further analyzed on the basis of the calculation of the bond energy [38]. All the calculation formulas are given in the ESM, and the bond energy (*E*b) of the CZMC ceramics is shown in Fig. 9(b). In addition, the corresponding bond lengths of the CZMC ceramics sintered at different temperatures are shown in Table S2 in the ESM. The crystal structural stability was reported to be dependent on the *E*b and the thermal expansion coefficient (*α*L) [14,39]. A larger *E*b and smaller *α*L normally indicate a more stable structure, leading to a smaller |*τ*f| value. *α*L is commonly considered to be a constant of (8–10) ppm/°C. The resulting data indicate that the B–O bond contributes 42.8% of the total *E*b, proving to be pivotal in manipulating the τf value. Through calculation and analysis, the B‒O bond energy of nonentropic Ca3Co2SiV2O12 ceramics accounts for only 19.5% of the total *E*b. Therefore, the B-site bonding energy is improved by the medium-entropy strategy. A larger *E*b makes CZMC ceramics have a more stable structure, hence resulting in a near-zero τ*f*.

Figure 10(a) shows that the contribution rates of Ca1–O, Ca2–O, B–O (B = Co, Zn, Mg, or Cu), Si–O, and V–O chemical bonds to bond ionicity (*f**i*) are 19.21%, 18.50%, 17.80%, 21.04%, and 23.45%, respectively. In general, the *f**i* of chemical bonds reflects their correlation with the *ε*r of materials. Therefore, the V‒O bond plays a crucial role in the *ε*r of CZMC ceramics because it has the highest *f**i*. The level of lattice energy (*U*) indicates the binding ability of cations and anions in compounds. Therefore, a higher *U* signifies a stronger chemical bond and a larger *Q*×*f*. Figure 10(b) shows the contribution rates of Ca1‒O, Ca2‒O, B‒O (B = Co, Zn, Mg, Cu), Si‒O, and V‒O chemical bonds to *U* in the CZMC compounds. The largest contribution rate (63.3%) of the V‒O bond to U implies that the *Q*×*f* value of the CZMC ceramics is most affected by the V‒O bond.

To investigate the correlation between the dielectric performance and the applied electrical signal, AC impedance spectroscopy was conducted, providing insight into the contributions of the grain and grain boundaries to the investigated ceramics. Figure 11 shows the AC impedance spectra of the CZMC ceramics sintered at 1010 °C. The *Z'* and *Z''* in these images are the real and imaginary components of the complex impedance (*Z**), respectively. At 340 °C, the impedance spectra exhibit a compressed semicircle, indicating that the grain resistance holds the leading station. In this state, the vacancies in the ceramics are more likely to aggregate at the grain boundaries, forming conductive pathways at the grain boundaries and thus lowering the resistance of the grain boundaries. When the temperature exceeds 340 °C, the impedance spectra exhibit two compressed semicircles. The peak in the low-frequency band refers to the grain boundary resistance, whereas the peak in the high-frequency band represents the grain resistance [40]. Under these conditions, it is difficult to generate a continuous conductive pathway at the grain boundaries. Therefore, the grain boundary resistance increases. Moreover, as the test temperature increased from 340 to 500 °C, the radius of the semicircle gradually decreased, indicating a significant decrease in the impedance of the samples.

For the CMZC ceramics, an equivalent circuit (inset of Fig. 11(a)) is proposed, including three resistances (R*1, *R*2, *R*3) and two constant phase elements (CPEs). The results fitted by the model are in excellent agreement with the experimental data, validating the credibility of the equivalent circuit. The calculated conductivity (*σ*) is shown in Fig. 11(e). Obviously, there is a good linear correlation between ln*σ* and 1000/*T*, revealing that *σ* is generally regulated by the Arrhenius law:

(10) σ=σ0exp⁡(−EakbT)

where *σ*0, *E*a, *k*b, and *T* represent the preexponential factor, the activation energy, the Boltzmann constant, and the temperature (K), respectively. Good linearity can eliminate other dominant possibilities, such as electronic conductivity and grain boundary conductivity, and is consistent with the Arrhenius relationship for thermally activated processes. In addition, the *E*a value of 1.16 eV corresponds to the typical activation energy for oxygen vacancy migration (0.6–1.3 eV) [41,42]. Thus, the conductivity of the CZMC ceramics can be attributed to the diffusion of thermally activated oxygen vacancies. Moreover, a high *E*a value indicates a low defect concentration in the sample, contributing to its low dielectric loss, namely, high *Q*×*f*.

In summary, at a sintering temperature of 1010 °C, the CZMC ceramics exhibit high densification and optimal microwave dielectric properties (*ε*r = 10.89, *Q*×*f* = 59,200 GHz, and *τ*f = −9.6 ppm/°C). Table 3 lists a performance comparison of several related microwave dielectric ceramics. In general, it is critical for microwave dielectric ceramics to have a high *Q*×*f* and a near-zero *τ*f simultaneously in practical applications. High *Q*×*f* values can be observed for the Ca3Y2Ge3O12, Ca3CoZrGe3O12, and Y2CaZrGa4O12 ceramics, but large *τ*f values evidently restrict their practical application. Compared with other high-entropy ceramics, CZMC ceramics exhibit good cooperation in terms of sintering temperature and performance, including a relatively low sintering temperature, a high *Q*×*f* value, and a *τ*f value approaching zero. Notably, the *Q*×*f* value of the CZMC ceramics is approximately double that of the pure Ca3Co2SiV2O12 ceramics via the medium-entropy strategy. Moreover, the sintering temperature is effectively reduced from 1160 to 1010 °C. This finding illustrates the potential of CZMC ceramics as promising candidates in millimeter wave communications.

## 4 Conclusions

A medium-entropy strategy was used to produce Ca3(Co0.25Zn0.25Mg0.25Cu0.25)2SiV2O12 ceramics via the solid-state sintering route. The resultant ceramics were proven to be single-phase garnet structure on the basis of XRD, structural refinement, and entropy effect analysis. The SEM results revealed that the ceramics possessed a dense microstructure with fine grains, resulting in a relative density of 95.4% when sintered at 1010 °C. The Raman spectra of the ceramics revealed an obvious negative correlation between *ε*r and the Raman shift at approximately 838 cm−1, similar to the correlation between the FWHM and the *Q*×*f*. AC impedance spectroscopy revealed that a low defect concentration contributed to reducing the dielectric loss of the ceramics. The B–O bond had the strongest contribution to the total bond energy, thus playing an important role in manipulating the *τ*f of the ceramics. Moreover, the V–O bond significantly influences the *ε*r and the *Q*×*f* of the CZMC ceramics. Notably, the Ca3(Co0.25Zn0.25Mg0.25Cu0.25)2SiV2O12 ceramics sintered at 1010 °C had superior performance, with *ε*r = 10.89, *Q*×*f* = 59,200 GHz, and *τ*f = −9.6 ppm/°C. The above results demonstrate that the Ca3(Co0.25Zn0.25Mg0.25Cu0.25)2SiV2O12 ceramics have extensive application prospects in the field of millimeter wave communications.
