## 1 Introduction

With the increasing demand for intelligent devices, the requirements for multifunctional materials are growing increasingly urgent [1−9]. In particular, as promising multifunctional materials, various capacitor materials dominate critical applications in communication systems and electronic devices [10−12]. Among these, voltage is regarded as a highly promising driver for modulating physical properties such as electrical conductivity owing to its ease of control in device applications. This offers an effective means for tuning the dynamic response of circuits and electronic systems [13,14]. However, electromagnetic radiation is inevitable in intelligent devices, which affects the normal use of electronic devices and seriously threatens human health and the living environment [15−34]. Therefore, the development of efficient electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding materials that can reduce or control electromagnetic radiation pollution is urgently needed [35,36]. Compared with traditional EMI shielding materials with constant EMI shielding performance, EMI shielding materials with adjustable shielding effectiveness can be used in intelligent devices and meet the complex applications of emerging communication technologies and electronic instruments for different degrees of EMI shielding effects [37−41]. Zhou *et al*. [42] developed a multifunctional material with a wood–bamboo multilayer structure, where a bamboo layer with excellent EMI shielding and mechanical properties is bonded to the wood surface. The uniform integration of copper nanoparticles endows the bamboo layer with excellent EMI shielding effectiveness and good hydrophobic properties. The wood–bamboo hybrid materials demonstrated excellent flexural strength (70.1 MPa) and flame-retardant performance. Nie *et al*. [43] reported that rock wool/reduced graphene oxide (RW-rGO) composites presented excellent EMI shielding performance (> 25 dB) and good hydrophobicity (water contact angle > 130°). Moreover, the RW-rGO composites exhibit good thermal insulation and fire-retardant ability. At present, the regulation of the EMI shielding performance of many materials mainly relies on physical deformation or simple changes in the doping ratio. This type of regulation often requires significant external stimuli, making it impossible to achieve low-power consumption and a reversible real-time dynamic response. A fundamental challenge lies in the fact that most traditional conductive materials follow Ohm’s law, showing a linear current–voltage relationship, which inherently limits dynamic control. Although some nonohmic materials, such as semiconductors and superconductors, allow for tunability, they usually require large electric fields or complex structural modifications, which hinder their actual integration into low-power devices.

Bi2Se3 ceramics have a unique layered structure and are also typical topological insulators. Topological insulators can achieve less dissipative transport in surface states because the presence of gapless surface states is protected by time reversal symmetry [44−48]. In particular, Bi2Se3 has a topological insulating electronic phase generated by strong spin–orbit coupling, which gives it potential for use in electronic devices as well as information transport materials [49−51]. Bi2Se3 has an appropriate bandgap (~0.3 eV) and appropriate conductivity, making it an ideal candidate for intelligent electronic devices [52−54].

In this work, hexagonal Bi2Se3 topological insulator nanosheets with various thicknesses were synthesized via a chemical reduction method. By leveraging the unique surface states of Bi2Se3 and tailoring their nanosheet morphology, precise control over conductivity was realized through bias voltage regulation. This approach enables tunable EMI shielding and capacitive performance. The Bi2Se3 nanosheets have significant tunable capacitance and unique tunable EMI shielding performance. The capacitance of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets decreases with increasing frequency within the range of 25–40 kHz. Moreover, as the bias voltage increases, the capacitance tends to decrease. Moreover, Bi2Se3 nanosheets with a thin thickness exhibit an EMI shielding effectiveness exceeding 25 dB over the entire frequency range of 2–18 GHz with a bias voltage of 5 V, benefiting from the enhanced electrical conductivity. The photoelectric properties of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets are such that when the bias voltage is 15 V and the light power density is 200 mW/cm2, the maximum photocurrent of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets reaches 17.39 mA, which is an increase of 43%. The increase in the photocurrent can further regulate the EMI performance of Bi2Se3 on the basis of voltage, thereby expanding the tunable range. At a bias voltage of 15 V and an optical power density of 200 mW/cm2, the average total EMI shielding effectiveness (SET) of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets increases to 62.8 dB. Bi2Se3-based shields exhibit a wide tunability window under low applied voltages, benefiting from their nonohmic conduction mechanism and topological surface states. This unique characteristic not only provides a new pathway for designing energy-efficient adaptive shielding materials but also opens opportunities for dual-function devices that combine tunable capacitance and intelligent electromagnetic response for next-generation intelligent electronic devices.

### 2.1 Materials

Sodium selenite (Na2SeO3, 99%) was purchased from Shanghai Adamas-beta Co., Ltd. Bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (Bi(NO3)3·5H2O, 99%) was obtained from Shandong Xiya Chemical Co., Ltd. Potassium hydroxide (KOH, AR) was purchased from Beijing Xiandai Dongfang Fine Chemicals Co., Ltd. Sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, CP) was obtained from Xilong Chemical Co., Ltd. Ethylene glycol (EG, AR) was obtained from Tianjin Fuyu Fine Chemicals Co., Ltd. Ethylene glycol was analytically pure and used without further purification.

### 2.2 Preparation of Bi2Se3

Na2SeO3 (0.75 mM), Bi(NO3)3·5H2O (0.5 mM), KOH (0.4 g), and CMC (0.4 g) were uniformly mixed in 50 mL of ethylene glycol. The mixture was then heated in an oil bath at 190 °C and stirred vigorously for 8 or 30 min to obtain a black solution. After naturally cooling to approximately 25 °C, the samples were centrifuged at 7000 r/min and washed three times with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol. Finally, the material was dried at 60 °C for 8 h and ground into powder. With stirring times of 8 and 30 min, the prepared samples were labeled P1 and P2, respectively.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The morphology of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets was verified by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Hitachi S4800), a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEOL JEM-2100F), a high-resolution TEM (HRTEM; JEOL JEM-2100F), and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. An energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS; Horiba 7021-H) mapping was used to confirm the element composition. The phase and composition were analyzed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Bruker D8 Advance) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5406) in the scanning range of 20°–80°. An X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; Thermo Escalab 250XI) was used to examine the elemental composition and valence state of the materials. The effects of permittivity (real part *ε*ʹ and imaginary part *ε*ʺ) and the capacitance (*C*p) of Bi2Se3 nanosheets on the bias voltage were tested via an Agilent 4294A instrument. A source meter (Keithley, 2400) was used to measure the direct current (DC) electrical properties. A CHF-XM500 xenon lamp (Trust-tech) was used as the light source. An SM206-SOLAR solar power meter was used to measure the optical power density.

### 2.4 Theory of tunable EMI shielding

The total EMI shielding effectiveness (SET) was determined by a combination of absorption effectiveness (SEA) and reflection effectiveness (SER), as given by Eq. (1) [55,56]:

(1) SET=SER+SEA

The equations for calculating the reflectance efficiency SER and the absorption efficiency SEA are defined by Eqs. (2) and (3) [57]:

(2) SER=20log⁡μ0σ42πfμϵ0

(3) SEA=8.686d(πfμσ)12

where *σ* is the electrical conductivity; *μ*0 is the vacuum permeability; *d* is the shielding thickness of the material; * f is the frequency; μ* is the magnetic permeability of the material, which can be expressed as * μ=  μ0μr ( μ0 = 4π×10−7 H/m; μ*r is the relative permeability and  μr= 1 for nonmagnetic Bi2Se3);  ϵ0 is the vacuum permittivity.

If the material thickness is greater than the skin depth or SEA > 15 dB, the effect of multiple reflection effectiveness (SEM) can be neglected. The total EMI shielding effectiveness SET is calculated as Eq. (4):

(4) SET=8.686d(πfμσ)12+20log⁡μ0σ42πfμϵ0

### 2.5 Theory of photoelectric conversion

The responsivity (*R*) is also an important parameter that describes the performance of the photoconversion detector, and the specific formula is given by Eqs. (5) and (6) [58]:

(5) Iph=Iillumination−Idark

(6) R=IphPirrA

where *I*ph is the photocurrent, which is the difference between the bright current (*I*illumination) and the dark current (*I*dark); *P*irr is the optical power density; *A* is the actual effective illumination area of the photodetector.

The detection rate (*D**) is one of the key parameters for evaluating the photodetection performance of a photodetector and is a measure of the ability to detect weak light signals, defined as Eq. (7):

(7) D∗=(SΔf)1/2NEP

where *S* is the actual effective illumination area of the photodetector; NEP and Δ*f* are the noise and electrical bandwidth (in Hz), respectively. Approximating *I*dark as noise, the equation for the detection rate can be expressed as Eq. (7) [59,60]:

(8) D∗=(Iillumination−Idark)×S1/2PirrA(2eIdark)1/2

where e is the absolute value of elementary charge.

## 3 Results and discussion

SEM images of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets corresponding to P1 are shown in Figs. 1(a) and 1(b), whereas the SEM image of P2 is shown in Fig. 1(c). SEM images of P2 of different sizes are shown in Fig. S1(a) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Bi2Se3 nanosheets clearly have a regular hexagonal structure, and the nanosheets of P1 are thinner (~30 nm) than the nanosheets of P2 (~110 nm). The growth of layered nanosheets is attributed mainly to a self-assembly fusion process. In the reaction system, ethylene glycol (EG) serves as the reducing agent. Selenite ions (SeO32−) are initially reduced to elemental selenium (Se0). The newly formed Se0 possesses high reactivity in the alkaline environment provided by KOH, which readily undergoes a disproportionation reaction to form selenide ions (Se2−). These Se2− ions then react with Bi3+ ions in the solution, leading to the formation of thin hexagonal Bi2Se3 nanosheets. Figure 1(d) shows a TEM image of P1, and Fig. S1(b) in the ESM shows a TEM image of P2, confirming that both samples have a nanosheet structure. The corresponding HRTEM image in Fig. 1(e) captured on a nanosheet shows a lattice spacing of 0.356 nm, corresponding to the (101) crystal planes of Bi2Se3 [61]. The SAED result shown in the inset of Fig. 1(f) reveals bright symmetric spots, confirming the good crystallinity of the nanosheets and the formation of a single-crystal structure. The morphology and corresponding element mapping results of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets in Figs. 1(g)–1(i) confirm that the uniform nanosheets are composed of Bi and Se without impurities. The EDS analysis of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets in Fig. S2(a) in the ESM confirms that the nanosheets are composed of Bi and Se. The diffraction peaks (Fig. 1(j)) of the XRD pattern match well with those of Bi2Se3 with a space group of * R3¯m, which is consistent with the standard card of Bi2Se3 (JCPDS 33-0214). The sharp diffraction peaks without impurity peaks indicate that the sample is a pure Bi2Se3 crystal phase with high crystallinity. The XPS survey spectrum of Bi2Se3 is shown in Fig. S2(b) in the ESM, with characteristic peaks corresponding to Bi and Se. Figure 1(k) shows the high-resolution XPS spectrum of Bi 4f. The peaks located at 162.98 and 157.68 eV correspond to Bi 4f5/2 and 4f7/2 of Bi3+, respectively. The peaks at 163.98 and 158.78 eV belong to Bi0. In addition, the high-resolution XPS spectrum in Fig. 1(l) belongs to Se 3d. The peaks at 54.88 and 53.98 eV correspond to Se 3d5/2 and Se 3d3/2, respectively [62]. The XPS results further confirmed the composition and verified the formation of Bi2Se3.

Bi2Se3 has a quintuple layer (QL) structure characterized by a Se–Bi–Se–Bi–Se atomic sequence within each fundamental unit. Figure S3 in the ESM presents structural models for 6-QL and 10-QL configurations. Comparative electronic structure analysis revealed thickness-dependent band gap variations, with 0.46 eV for the 6-QL system (Fig. 2(a)) and 0.57 eV for the 10-QL system (Fig. 2(b)). As the thickness increases, the Bi2Se3 band continuously shifts toward lower binding energies. For a structure with 6 QLs, the Fermi level approaches the conduction band, whereas for a structure with 10 QLs, the Fermi level approaches the valence band. Inside a topological insulator, the Fermi level is located between the conduction band and the valence band, exhibiting insulator properties. Owing to its special quantum states protected by topology, its surface can carry current and exhibit metallic conductive behavior, which is directly reflected in the evolution of the electronic band structure and density of states (DOS) with different thicknesses. Further examination of the partial density of states (PDOS) in Figs. 2(c) and 2(d) demonstrates that within the ±5 eV energy window relative to the Fermi level (denoted by vertical dashed lines), the electronic states near the Fermi level are predominantly contributed by p-orbitals. The PDOS exhibits a prominent increase in intensity as the QL count increases from 6 to 10. This trend correlates with an increase in the electronic density of states resulting from increased material dimensionality. Owing to the unique properties of topological insulators, which conduct electricity on the surface while remaining insulated internally, thinner nanosheets have a larger specific surface area. As a result, the surface state effect is enhanced, leading to improved conductivity and a higher dielectric constant (ε*′). As shown in Table S1 in the ESM, the existence of surface states is indirectly proven.

Figures 3(a) and 3(b) show the dielectric parameters of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets. The quantitative profiles of P1 and P2 versus the bias voltage at 25 kHz are presented. When no bias voltage is applied, the ε*′ of P1 is 323.5, and that of P2 is 32.3. As the bias voltage clearly increases, *ε*′ continuously decreases. As the bias voltage continues to increase, the Bi2Se3 nanosheets transition from semiconductors to conductors, and the low-frequency *ε*′ of the composite material becomes negative. This notable suppression of *ε*′ under an electric field is attributed to the increased carrier density and enhanced electrical conductivity within the Bi2Se3 nanosheets, which significantly attenuate the polarization capability of the material. More importantly, such pronounced and continuous tunability of the dielectric response via a simple bias voltage not only provides a fundamental mechanism for designing novel variable capacitors but also lays the foundation for achieving dynamically adjustable EMI shielding performance, as the impedance and loss characteristics of materials can be precisely modulated in real time. When no bias voltage is applied, the *ε*″ of P1 is 6.9×105. As the applied voltage increases, *ε*″ increases to 1.4×106. When no bias voltage is applied, the *ε*″ of P2 is 36.5. As the applied voltage increases, *ε*″ increases to 72.4. The pronounced dependence of the permittivity on the DC bias voltage endows Bi2Se3 nanosheets with exceptional voltage-controlled capacitance (*C*p). The capacitance was tested via an impedance analyzer according to the schematic shown in Fig. 3(c). For characterization, the P1 and P2 powders were uniaxially pressed into slices with thicknesses of 1.7 and 0.9 mm, respectively. Figures 3(d) and 3(e) show the *C*p values of P1 and P2 within the frequency range of 25–40 kHz under different bias voltages. When the frequency is increased from 25 to 40 kHz, as the voltage increases, the capacitance of P1 decreases and even becomes negative because of the improved conductivity of the material. When it reaches above 6 V, the *C*p value remains essentially stable. The conductivity of sample P1 is too high, and it is not suitable for use as a variable capacitor. The capacitance of P2 decreases monotonically. As the bias voltage increases, the decrease in capacitance is also quite obvious. P2 is suitable for adjustable capacitors. Figure 3(f) illustrates the relationship between the *C*p values of P2 and the applied voltage at a frequency of 25 kHz. For P2 at 25 kHz, the capacitance changes by only 5.3 pF within the 0–10 V range, with the *C*p value decreasing from 38.1 pF without bias voltage to 32.8 pF with a 10-V bias voltage, representing a maximum variation of 16.2%. The results indicate that P2 has an outstanding capacitance tuning capability under low voltages at low frequencies. This performance presents a considerable advantage over traditional bulk ferroelectrics, which often necessitate bias voltages in the kilovolt range for effective modulation, whereas Bi2Se3 achieves this advantage with considerably lower driving voltages [63]. Bi2Se3 only requires a bias voltage of 0–10 V to adjust the capacitor. The capacitance of Bi2Se3 changed by approximately 16.2% over a voltage modulation range of 0–10 V, which is comparable to previous work [64]. As the applied bias voltage increased, the electrical conductivity increased. This increase in conductivity enhances the concentration of free charge carriers within the material. Under an alternating electric field, these accelerated charge carriers migrate rapidly, leading to a decrease in permittivity (*ε*′) and a reduction in capacitance [65]. Figure 3(g) shows the current response of P1 as a function of the applied voltage (*I*–*V* curves). The aggregation of Bi2Se3 nanosheets exhibited a monotonic nonohmic law response to voltage. As the voltage increases to 1 V, the corresponding current increases to 32 mA. Figure S4(a) in the ESM shows that the resistance decreases from 42.55 to 30 Ω. P2 exhibits comparable current evolution under an applied voltage, as shown in Fig. 3(h). As the voltage increases to 1 V, the corresponding current increases to 4.5 mA. Figure S4(b) in the ESM shows that the resistance decreases from 306 Ω in the forward direction to 232 Ω and from 215 Ω in the reverse direction. Figure 3(i) shows that the conductivity of P1 increases from 0.337 to 0.458 S/m. The conductivity of P2 increased from 0.024 to 0.033 S/m. The electrical transport characteristics of P1 and P2 are reproducible, and all the curves in ten consecutive bias voltage scans nearly overlap with each other, proving that the material has stable electrical transport characteristics. The unique electrical transport behavior is attributed to the gate voltage effect of disordered topological insulator nanosheets [14].

As shown in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b), P1 can maintain stable ON and OFF states with an almost constant ON/OFF resistance ratio of ~1.9, and P2 can also maintain stable ON and OFF states with an almost constant ON/OFF resistance ratio of ~871.5. This suggests that the electrical response of Bi2Se3 nanosheet aggregates is robust upon repeated electric field switching. The Bi2Se3 nanosheet photodetectors exhibit distinct *I*–*V* characteristics under illumination gradients, as shown in Fig. 4(c). The linearity of the *I*–*V* curves indicates that there is good ohmic contact between the electrode and the Bi2Se3 nanosheet. The photocurrent monotonically increases with increasing optical power density over the range of 0–200 mW/cm2. Furthermore, at a given optical power density, the photocurrent also shows a linear dependence on the applied bias voltage. When the optical power density increases, the number of photons passing through the unit area per unit time increases, and the number of encounters between photons and holes increases, increasing the bright current. By increasing the bias voltage, which is equivalent to improving the kinetic energy of photoelectrons, the bright current also increases. At a bias voltage of 15 V and an optical power density of 200 mW/cm2, the maximum photocurrent of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets is 17.39 mA, which is a 43% increase in the current compared with 0 mW/cm2. These results indicate that the Bi2Se3 nanosheets have good photovoltaic conversion ability. The electrode responsivity of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets was calculated via Eqs. (5) and (6). The bias voltage dependence of the photodetector responsivity of Bi2Se3 nanosheets under various optical power densities is characterized in Fig. 4(d). The responsivity of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets increases with increasing bias voltage when the optical power density is constant. The carrier migration inside the Bi2Se3 nanosheets becomes stronger as the bias voltage increases. At a certain bias voltage, the responsivity decreases as the optical power density increases. When the bias voltage is 15 V and the optical power density is 20 mW/cm2, the responsivity of the Bi2Se3 nanosheet photodetector reaches 84.44 mA/W1. This also indicates that the Bi2Se3 nanosheet photodetector has high sensitivity under weak illumination. Figure 4(e) shows how the detection rate of the Bi2Se3 nanosheet photodetector gradually increases with increasing bias voltage. When the bias voltage is constant, the detection rate of the Bi2Se3 nanosheet photodetector increases with increasing optical power density, and the maximum increase is one order of magnitude. At a bias voltage of 15 V and an optical power density of 200 mW/cm2, the maximum detection rate reaches 1.44×109 Jones, and a photodetector with a large *D** value indicates that it has a better ability to detect low light. The progressive conductivity enhancement of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets under increasing illumination (0–200 mW/cm2) is shown in Fig. 4(f). Moreover, the conductivity also increases with increasing bias voltage.

Figures 5(a)–5(d) show the variation in the SET values with frequency for P1 and P2 at different voltages with a sample thickness of 1.0 mm. The SET values of both samples increase with frequency, and the overall SET values of both samples increase with increasing voltage. When voltage was continuously added to the samples to adjust their shielding performance, the SET values of sample P1 were all greater than 20 dB. For sample P2, when the voltage was increased to 7 V, the SET values were also greater than 20 dB within the range of 2–18 GHz. By applying a voltage, the shielding performance of this material can be effectively regulated, and as the voltage increases, the shielding performance of the nanosheet also continuously strengthens. Figures 5(b) and 5(e) show that when a 0.1-V or 5-V bias voltage is applied, the SET values of P1 are always greater than 25 dB, and the values of P2 are greater than 15 dB in the range of 2–18 GHz. This proves that by applying a bias voltage, the shielding performance of the material can be effectively regulated. As the voltage increases, the shielding performance is continuously strengthened.

The SET values of Bi2Se3 nanosheets with a thickness of 1.42 mm under irradiation by light sources with different optical power densities at a 5-V bias voltage are all > 57 dB in the 2–18 GHz range. The EMI shielding effectiveness of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets also increased with increasing optical power density. The EMI shielding effectiveness at a 6–15 V bias voltage is shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM. Remarkably, a 172% increase in tunable EMI shielding efficiency is observed at a bias voltage of 5 V under an optical power density of 200 mW/cm2. The SET was calculated according to Eq. (1). Since the SEA is negligible and the electrical conductivity remains constant at 5 V, the SET is inversely proportional to the frequency within the 2–18 GHz range. The essence of achieving tunable EMI shielding with Bi2Se3 nanosheets lies in the ability to continuously modulate their macroscopic electrical conductivity via an applied bias voltage, thereby dynamically manipulating the balance between absorption loss and reflection loss to achieve reversible control over the total shielding effectiveness.

Figure 5(f) shows that the SET of Bi2Se3 nanosheets at a 2-GHz frequency increases with increasing optical power density, whereas the SET increases with increasing bias voltage, indicating that Bi2Se3 nanosheets still have excellent EMI shielding capability at low frequencies. An increase in the photocurrent can further regulate the EMI performance of Bi2Se3 on the basis of voltage, thus expanding the tunable range. The SEA and SER of Bi2Se3 were calculated under applied bias voltages from 8 to 15 V. As revealed in Fig. S6 in the ESM, both the SEA and SER increase with increasing bias voltage at a constant frequency. The tunable EMI shielding performance of Bi2Se3 is governed primarily by the SER. As shown in Fig. 5(g), Bi2Se3 can be applied to microwave switch devices, tuning circuits in communication equipment, and smart wearable devices.

Compared with other tunable EMI shielding materials [40,41,66−69], Bi2Se3 nanosheets have a significant advantage in tunable EMI shielding performance. The thickness of the sample is only 1.42 mm. At a bias voltage of 15 V and an optical power density of 200 mW/cm2, the average SET of the Bi2Se3 nanosheets increases to 62.8 dB. Moreover, Bi2Se3 nanosheets can achieve a wide range of EMI shielding performance adjustments from 23.9 to 62.8 dB.

## 4 Conclusions

Bi2Se3 nanosheets with different thicknesses were prepared via the polyol reduction method by changing the reaction time. Moreover, the nanosheets possess a single-crystal structure. The Bi2Se3 nanosheet aggregates exhibit remarkable capacitance tunability under an applied bias voltage. In the range of 2–18 GHz, the SET values completely exceed 15 dB, resulting in superior EMI shielding performance. The shielding performance of the material can be effectively tuned by applying a bias voltage, with an adjustable shielding efficiency exceeding 99%. In this work, the optoelectronic properties of Bi2Se3 nanosheets were also investigated, and the maximum bright current was 17.39 mA at a bias voltage of 15 V and an optical power density of 200 mW/cm2, which is a 43% increase in the current compared with 0 mW/cm2. The responsivity is 84.44 mA/W at an optical power density of only 20 mW/cm2, and the maximum detection rate can reach 1.44×109 Jones. Under 5 V bias voltage, the SET values of Bi2Se3 exceed 57 dB. Therefore, Bi2Se3 nanosheets have great potential for applications in areas such as microelectronic devices, communication systems, and intelligent devices.
