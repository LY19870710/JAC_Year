## 1 Introduction

The development of a new generation of ultrahigh-temperature structural materials (above 2000 °C) still faces significant challenges in response to harsher service conditions for future aerospace vehicles, including faster speeds, stronger stresses, and higher temperatures [1]. Ultrahigh-temperature ceramics (UHTCs) are an emerging class of materials that have the potential to resist formidable thermal loads and large mechanical denudation because of their high melting temperature (above 3000 °C), good chemical stability, and excellent ablation resistance [2−4].

Among these UHTCs, HfC and ZrC showed high melting points (HfC: ~3890 °C; ZrC: ~3540 °C) and good high-temperature stability, and their oxidation products also had high melting points (HfO2: 2810 1C and ZrO2: 2670 °C), both ensuring the use of HfC/ZrC in ultrahigh-temperature and oxygen-containing environments (above 2000 °C) [5,6]. The monocarbide HfC/ZrC is a preferred choice for surface coating and modified components to improve the oxidation, corrosion, and heat shock resistance of substrates over a wide range of temperatures [7], including graphite [8,9], C/SiC [10,11], and C/C composites [12−15]. However, the volume change from t-Hf/ZrO2 (high-temperature phase) to m-Hf/ZrO2 (room-temperature phase) during the oxidation cooling process damaged the integrity of the oxide films. These Hf/Zr-based oxide films showed “pesting” and loose structures after prolonged low-temperature [16] and high-temperature [17] oxidation, respectively, both of which limit their further application. To overcome these issues associated with traditional monocarbides such as HfC/ZrC, the current focus and difficulties of research are how to design and prepare carbides that can be stably used for a long time under extreme conditions.

The compositional design of medium-/high-entropy carbides (MECs/HECs, ≥ 3/4 metal cations) has significantly widened due to the countless combinations of multiple principal elements, giving them special properties that cannot be achieved in traditional monocarbides [18−21]. Wang *et al*. [22] reported that compared with those of monocarbides (HfC, ZrC, TaC, and NbC), the entropy stabilization effect of (Hf–Ta–Zr–Nb)C was synergistic with multiple principal elements in terms of oxidation resistance, improving the onset oxidation temperature (*T*o) during thermogravimetric (TG) analysis. Lun *et al*. [23] studied the TG oxidation behavior of nonstoichiometric (Zr,Hf,Ti)C*x* carbides and HfC and reported that the synergistic effect of (Zr,Hf,Ti)C*x* also increased its onset oxidation temperature. Additionally, as reported by Tan *et al*. [24], the synergistic effect promoted the formation of a dense oxide and inhibited the crack initiation of the (Ti–Zr–Hf–Nb–Ta)C ceramic compared with that of the ZrC ceramic in a 1200 °C water vapor atmosphere. Although these results proved that the synergistic effect of multiple principal elements within MECs/HECs improved their oxidation resistance compared with traditional monocarbides, some doubts between MEC ceramics and the corresponding multi-phase carbide ceramics with the same cation and proportion urgently need to be answered. Is there a difference in the oxidation behavior between MECs and multi-phase carbides, and what role does entropy stabilization play in MECs? In other words, however, it is currently unclear whether the antioxidation advantages resulting from the synergistic effect of multiple principal elements within MECs exist compared to those of multi-phase carbides.

Thus, the purpose of this work was to compare the oxidation behaviors of (Hf1/3Zr1/3Ti1/3)C medium-entropy carbide (HZT-MEC) powders and equimolar HfC–ZrC–TiC multi-phase carbide (HZT-MPC) powders. The oxygen adsorption energies of HZT-MEC and HZT-MPC and the solid solution energies of multi-phase oxides of ZrO2, HfO2, and TiO2 (subsequently referred to as the Hf–Zr–Ti–O system) during oxidation were studied by first-principles calculations (VASP software 5.4.4), which helped to understand their different initial oxidation behaviors.

## 2 Experimental

Figure 1 shows a schematic diagram of the preparation of the HZT-MPC and HZT-MEC powders. Without any high-temperature heat treatment, commercial HfC, ZrC, and TiC powders were ground by a bench planetary mill for 6–8 h at an equal molar ratio (1 : 1 : 1, all raw powders > 99.5%, 1–3 μm) using Y2O3-stabilized ZrO2 balls and ethanol as milling media. The mixed slurries were dried at 60–80 °C for 6–8 h and then sieved through a 300-mesh sieve to obtain the HZT-MPC powders.

The HZT-MEC powders were prepared using a carbothermal reduction reaction. Commercial HfO2, ZrO2, and TiO2 powders (≥ 99.0%, 1–3 µm) and graphite (≥ 99.9%, 1–3 µm) were ground via a bench planetary mill for 6–8 h. After experiencing the same grinding and drying processes as HZT-MPC powders, the mixed oxide powders were wrapped in a graphite crucible with graphite paper, heated in a heat treatment furnace under the protection of Ar gas at 2100 °C with a ramp rate of 3–5 °C/min and maintained for 2–4 h before cooling naturally. The synthesized HZT-MEC powders were ground by a bench planetary mill for 2–4 h, with the same grinding and screening details as previously mentioned. The carbothermal reduction reaction for the HZT-MEC powders is expressed by Eq. (1):

(1) HfO2(s)+ZrO2(s)+TiO2(s)+9C(s)=3(Hf1/3Zr1/3Ti1/3)C+6CO(g)

The phase identification of the raw HZT-MPC and HZT-MEC powders was investigated by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X’Pert Pro MPD, Panalytical, the Netherlands and D8 DISCOVER A25, Bruker AXS, Germany) using Cu Kα radiation (λ* = 1.5418 Å). The microstructure and corresponding elemental distribution of the raw carbide powders were observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; NovaTM Nano SEM 450, FEI, USA) operated at 15–20 kV accelerating voltage and equipped with an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS; INCA, Oxford, UK). The structure and composition of the synthesized HZT-MEC powders were analyzed by a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Themis Z, FEI, USA). A thermogravimetric analyzer (STA 449F5, Netzsch, Germany) was used to study the oxidation behavior of the obtained HZT-MEC and HZT-MPC powders (approximately 15 mg) in dry air from room temperature to different temperatures, including the maximum oxidation rate temperature (*T*m), peak oxidation temperature (*T*p), and 1300 °C. The samples were kept at room temperature for 20–30 min, then heated at a rate of 10 °C/min to the set temperatures, and finally brought to room temperature at a rate of approximately 15–20 °C/min. The thermogravimetric analysis crucible was too small (*ϕ*5 mm × 3 mm) to provide sufficient powders for XRD detection. Therefore, similar to the SEM and TEM analysis of the raw HZT-MEC and HZT-MPC powders, the structure and composition of the oxides in the HZT-MEC and HZT-MPC powders after TG oxidation were studied by a combination of SEM (NovaTM Nano SEM 450) and TEM (Themis Z and Talos F200X, FEI, USA).

The first-principles calculations were based on density functional theory (DFT) through the VASP software to study the initial oxidation behavior of HZT-MEC and HZT-MPC, as well as the solid solution process of the Hf–Zr–Ti–O system. The ionic core–valence electron interaction was produced by projector-augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotentials, and the electronic exchange-correlation potential was treated under the generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) parametrized by the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) scheme [25,26]. The cutoff energy was set to 450 eV after the optimization tests [27]. The Monkorst–Pack scheme was used for *k*-point sampling, and the *k*-points of different crystals met *k*a × *a* ≈ *k*b × *b* ≈ *k*c × *c* ≈ 20–30 Gamma-centered grids corresponding to the first Brillouin zone, where *a*, *b*, and *c* are the lattice parameters of different crystal models [28]. The simulation considered spin polarization. The convergence criterion of our self-consistent calculations for ionic relaxations was 10−5 eV between two consecutive steps [27]. All atomic positions and the size of the unit cell were optimized until the atomic forces were less than 0.02 eV/A [25]. The electronic conﬁgurations of Hf, Zr, Ti, C, and O were 5s25p65d26s2, 4s24p64d25s2, 3s23p63d24s2, 2s22p2, and 2 s22p4, respectively. Table 1 shows the relevant calculation parameters [25−29].

The HZT-MPC powders exhibit significant elemental clustering even after prolonged grinding (Fig. 2(a)), while the HZT-MEC powders have a uniform compositional distribution after carbothermal reduction at 2100 °C (Fig. 2(b)). ### 3.2 TG oxidation test

Figure 4 shows the TG and derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) curves of HZT-MPC, HZT-MEC, and three monocarbide (HfC, ZrC, and TiC) powders from room temperature to 1300 °C. All curves undergo the same oxidation horizontal platform, passing through *T*o and *T*m and then gradually increasing to *T*p, as shown in Figs. 4(a) and 4(c). The *T*o is determined by the temperature corresponding to the intersection point where the tangent of the platform intersects with the tangent at the TG curve inflection point [22]. When the DTG reaches its maximum, the corresponding point and temperature are identified as the TG curve inflection point and *T*m, respectively. The temperature located at the TG maximum is called *T*p. *T*o, *T*m, and *T*p of HZT-MPC, HZT-MEC, and the three monocarbide powders are shown in Table 2. The raw powders undergo a process from carbide crystal to amorphous oxycarbide and then to oxide crystal, which induces an increase and then a decrease in the TG curves for the HZT-MPC and HZT-MEC powders [23]. The transformation process is explained in detail later.

The whole TG and DTG curves of the HZT-MPC powders are on the left of those of the HZT-MEC powders, which implies that the oxidation of the former occurs earlier than that of the latter, as shown in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b). Before *T*p, the rate of increase in TG reflects the oxidation rate of the carbide powders. The DTG is the first-order derivative of the TG, of which the value represents the change rate of TG. The DTG maximum of HZT-MPC (0.10) is greater than that of HZT-MEC (0.09), implying that the oxidation of the former is faster than that of the latter. Although the HZT-MPC and HZT-MEC powders return to the same TG endpoint (HZT-MPC: 13.8%; HZT-MEC: 14.0%), the compositional difference causes their different oxidation paths after *T*p (Fig. 4(a)). From 760 to 920 °C (marked by red circles in Fig. 4(b)), the DTG value of HZT-MEC ≤ 0 reflects a gradual decrease in the TG curve of HZT-MEC powders after *T*p and then becomes stable, while the value of HZT-MPC ≥ 0 shows that the TG curve of HZT-MPC powders rises slowly to a dynamic equilibrium after the first decline, forming a bimodal distribution (Fig. 4(a)). Hence, the HZT-MPC powders undergo a new carbide oxidation process at 760–920 °C, forming a second *T*p at 920 °C. In addition, the TG curve of HZT-MEC fluctuates slightly after *T*p, which may be related to oxides with oxygen vacancies further oxidizing to nonstoichiometric oxides [23].

According to the TG and DTG curves of the three monocarbides (HfC, ZrC, and TiC), as shown in Figs. 4(c) and 4(d), the oxidation of TiC occurs significantly later than that of HfC and ZrC. *T*m of TiC is 715 °C (Fig. 4(c)), corresponding to the valley temperature of HZT-MPC (710 °C), marked by the black arrow in Fig. 4(a). Hence, HfC and ZrC preferentially oxidize during the TG oxidation of HZT-MPC powders, and then, the oxidation of TiC is delayed after 710 °C, which explains why the TG curve of HZT-MPC powders has a bimodal distribution. The same bimodal distribution also occurred in the delayed oxidation of TaC within HfC–ZrC–TaC-related powders [28]. The presence of three monocarbides within HZT-MPC powders produces the “preferential oxidation” of HfC/ZrC, followed by the formation of multi-phase oxides (HfO2, ZrO2, and TiO2) and their solid solution reactions. However, the single peak characteristic of the HZT-MEC powders indicates that the entropy stabilization effect enhances the synergistic oxidation ability of multiple principal elements [22], which suppresses the “preferential oxidation” of HfC/ZrC and the “delayed oxidation” of TiC, increasing the onset oxidation temperature of the HZT-MEC powders compared to that of the HZT-MPC powders. In addition, *T*o of the HZT-MEC powders is among those of the HfC, ZrC, and TiC powders, implying that the oxidation resistance of the HZT-MEC powders is superior to that of the HZT-MPC powders.

Figure 5 shows SEM images of the HZT-MPC and HZT-MEC powders after TG oxidation at *T*m, *T*p, and 1300 °C. A combination of SEM and TEM was used for phase analysis of the TG oxidation powders. SEM with a large scanning area indicates that the oxides within the HZT-MPC powders both have significant elemental clustering (Figs. 5(a)–5(c)), while the oxides within the HZT-MEC powders have a uniform compositional distribution (Figs. 5(d)–5(f)). Since it is difficult to obtain the oxide crystal structure via SEM, TEM was used to further obtain the structural and compositional information of the HZT-MPC and HZT-MEC powders after TG oxidation at different temperatures.

Similar to the elemental results in the SEM images, Figs. 6 and 7 show that the oxides of the HZT-MPC powders still have significant elemental clustering (Figs. 6(a) and 6(b) and Fig. 7(a)), while the oxides within the HZT-MEC powders have compositional uniformity (Figs. 6(c), 6(d), and 7(f)). At the initial stage of oxidation, O atoms first replace C atoms or occupy C vacancies to form amorphous oxycarbide [23,31]. As a result, some Zr/Hf–O–C amorphous oxycarbide areas appear at *T*m within the HZT-MPC powders, as marked by the area 1 in Fig. 6(a1). When the concentration of oxygen reaches the critical phase transition conditions, the oxycarbide areas are oxidized into grains [31], including the ZrO2 grains (the area 2 in Fig. 6(a1)) and some polycrystalline HfO2 grains (Fig. 6(a2)). As oxidation continues to *T*p, the higher temperature promotes the formation of polycrystalline Ti-doped (Hf,Zr)O2 through the solid solution of partial multi-phase oxides (HfO2, ZrO2, and TiO2), as shown in Fig. 6(b1). In addition, the presence of TiC (Fig. 6(b2)) confirms that the oxidation of TiC is delayed above 710 °C and that the TG curve of HZT-MPC has a bimodal distribution (Fig. 4(a)). The compositional uniformity and entropy stabilization effect of HZT-MEC promote the oxidation of the raw powders from carbide crystals to amorphous oxycarbide (Zr–Hf–Ti–O–C) and then to oxide crystals (Ti-doped (Hf,Zr)O2 and (Hf,Zr)TiO4) without multi-phase oxides (HfO2, ZrO2, and TiO2) and the “delayed oxidation” of TiC. This process induces a single peak characteristic of HZT-MEC powders, as shown in Fig. 4(a).

The appearance of amorphous and polycrystalline oxycarbide implies the low crystallinity of the oxides after TG oxidation at T*m and *T*p. As oxidation progresses to 1300 °C, crystal oxides form within the HZT-MPC and HZT-MEC powders. Based on the element distribution (Fig. 7(a)), the HAADF image of HZT-MPC oxides is divided into four regions, A–D, as indicated by the white square regions in Fig. 7(a). In region A, the high proportion of Hf (Fig. 7(h)) and a typical monoclinic structure (Fig. 7(b)) demonstrate that this phase is m-HfO2 (JCPDS#74-1506). The fringe spacing of 0.360 nm corresponds to the *d*-space of the (111) planes of m-HfO2. The high Ti content and orthorhombic structure indicate that the phase is o-TiO2 (JCPDS#21-1236) in the region B (Fig. 7(c)). Due to this phase adhering to the carbon film, there is partial amorphous carbon at the bottom of Fig. 7(c). In the region C, the ratio of Hf to Zr is closer and higher than that of Ti. A small amount of TiO2 diffuses into the solutions of HfO2 and ZrO2 (m-(Hf,Zr)O2) without changing its crystal structure (Fig. 7(d)). The fringe spacing of 0.520 nm corresponds to a *d*-space of 0.517 nm for the (010) planes of m-HfO2 of 0.521 nm for m-ZrO2 (JCPDS#86-1451), as shown in Fig. 7(d), and a typical monoclinic structure is along the [10* 1−] zone axis. As a result, Ti-doped m-(Hf,Zr)O2 forms. The solid solubility of Ti atoms in the Hf–Zr–Ti system (abbreviated as  STi) is lower than 10% (Fig. 7(i)). As much TiO2 diffuses into m-(Hf,Zr)O2 ( STi is approximately 45%), a phase transition occurs from Ti-doped m-(Hf,Zr)O2 to o-(Hf,Zr)TiO4 (o-HfTiO4: JCPDS#74-1287; o-ZrTiO4: JCPDS#74-1504) in the region D.

The different onset oxidation temperatures of the three monocarbides (Fig. 4(c) and Table 2) imply that HZT-MPC powders form multi-phase oxides (ZrO2, HfO2, and TiO2) after oxidation, followed by solid solution reactions. In contrast, the compositional uniformity of the original HZT-MEC powders at the macroscale promote rapid interdiffusion of oxides, resulting in the absence of ZrO2, HfO2, and TiO2 after oxidation. However, the segregation of Ti indicates that the supersaturated solid solution of TiO2 in (Hf,Zr)O2 induces phase separation (Fig. 7(f)). Hence, the Ti-poor phase of Ti-doped m-(Hf,Zr)O2 (the region E;  STi is 15.1%) and the Ti-rich phase of o-(Hf,Zr)TiO4 (the region F;  STi is 28.0%) appear in Figs. 7(f)–7(h). The fringe spacings of Ti-doped m-(Hf,Zr)O2 correspond to d*(011) and *d*(100) of m-Hf/ZrO2, while the fringe spacings of o-(Hf,Zr)TiO4 correspond to *d*(101) and *d*(110) of o-Hf/ZrTiO4. In conclusion, as  STi increases, TiO2 diffuses to m-(Hf,Zr)O2, producing a phase transition from Ti-doped m-(Hf,Zr)O2 to o-(Hf,Zr)TiO4 [32], where the maximum  STi in Hf/ZrO2 is approximately 20% [33].

### 3.3 Initial oxidation behavior analysis

The oxygen adsorption (O-adsorption) process of HZT-MPC and HZT-MEC was simulated by the first-principles calculations to study their initial oxidation behaviors. Figure 8 shows the adsorption models of HZT-MPC and HZT-MEC. Considering that the original HZT-MPC powders were composed of HfC, ZrC, and TiC, the oxidation behavior of HZT-MPC was simulated by three monocarbides. Wang J, and Wang SQ [25] noted that the surface energy of the (111) plane was greater than that of the (100) and (110) planes, which implied that chemisorption at the (111) plane occurred earlier than that at the (100) and (110) planes. The value of the adsorption energy (*E*ads) depends not only on the content of a crystal plane but also on whether the crystal plane is preferentially adsorbed. In addition, Cheng *et al*. [34] used (111) for the simulation of ZrC. The (111) plane is not only the strongest diffraction peak of the HZT-MPC and HZT-MEC powders in Fig. 1(a) but also an easily adsorbed plane. Hence, it is set as the adsorption surface for simulation [25,34].

The top sites (T) are located at the top of each surface cation; the bridge sites (B) are the top sites in the middle of two different surface cations (Hf–Zr, Hf–Ti, Zr–Ti, Hf–Hf, Zr–Zr, and Ti–Ti; partial sites are shown in the blue dashed line); the hollow site (H) is the gap point above all cations (Hf–Zr–Ti, as shown in the red dashed line) [35]. (2) Eads=Eslab+o−Eslab−EO2/2

where * Eslab,  EO2, and  Eslab+o are the total energies of the adsorption models, the individual oxygen molecule, and the oxygen adsorption configuration after relaxation, respectively.

Figure 9 shows the different initial oxidation behaviors of HZT-MPC and HZT-MEC. Table 3 shows the calculated  Eads of different initial adsorption sites, with a smaller  Eads implying stronger chemisorption. The minimum  Eads values of the HfC (Hf-H: −6.16 eV) and ZrC (Zr-H: −6.13 eV) configurations are close and smaller than that of the Ti-H configuration for TiC (−5.64 eV), as shown in Fig. 9(a). This proves the preferential oxidation of HfC/ZrC over TiC within HZT-MPC and the three monocarbide powders (Figs. 4(a) and 4(c)). Figure 9(b) shows that the O atom preferentially bonds with Hf and Zr atoms (Hf–Zr–Zr-H conﬁguration in Table 3) during the initial oxidation of HZT-MEC.  Eads of the Hf–Zr–Zr-H configuration for HZT-MEC (−5.98 eV, Fig. 6(b)) is among those of HfC (−6.16 eV), ZrC (−6.13 eV), and TiC (−5.64 eV). Hence, the oxygen adsorption sequence is HfC ≥ ZrC > HZT-MEC > TiC. Surface adsorption generally occurs in the unoxidized stage at low temperatures. As the oxidation temperature and oxygen content increase, which promotes more adsorption processes, the smaller  Eads of HfC/ZrC indicates that HZT-MPC may tend to oxidize more easily than HZT-MEC [35].

The solid solution process of the Hf−Zr−Ti−O system was simulated by first-principles calculations, as shown in Figs. 9(c) and 9(d). The HfO2, ZrO2, and TiO2 parent crystals included 16 O atoms and 8 Hf/Zr/Ti atoms. The solid solution binding energy (E*bi) is expressed by Eqs. (3) and (4) [36]:

(3) Ebi=[Eall−(xEHf+yEZr+zETi+mEO)]/(x+y+z+m)

(4) Si=Ni/(NHf+NZr+NTi)

where *N**i* is the number of Hf, Zr or Ti in the system; *E*all is the energy of the solution structure; * Si is the solid solubility of the solid solution atoms;  EHf,  EZr,  ETi, and  EO are the energies of an individual Hf, Zr, Ti, and O atom, respectively; x*, *y*, *z*, and *m* are the corresponding numbers (*N*).

*E*bi reflects the interdiffusion ability of HfO2, ZrO2, and TiO2, with a smaller value implying a greater solid solution ability [37]. The simulation considered the influence of the occupied positions of solid solution atoms in the parent crystals on *E*bi. *E*bi of Zr-doped HfO2 is lower than that of other *E*bi of interdiffusion processes, and the binding energy curves of Hf-doped ZrO2 and Zr-doped HfO2 are connected, indicating that HfO2 and ZrO2 preferentially form a limitless solid solution of (Hf,Zr)O2 at the start of oxidation. The energies of Hf/Zr-doped TiO2 are always greater than those of Ti-doped Hf/ZrO2, (Fig. 9(d)). Therefore, TiO2 is prone to diffusion to (Hf,Zr)O2. However, the supersaturated solid solution of TiO2 in (Hf,Zr)O2 induces a phase separation from Ti-doped (Hf,Zr)O2 to (Hf,Zr)TiO4 as  STi increases (Fig. 9(e)).

Figure 10 shows the schematic and macroscopic morphologies of three monocarbides, HZT-MPC and HZT-MEC powders. HZT-MPC powders have an uneven compositional distribution, including HfC, ZrC, and TiC powders, with reduced color depth (marked by black, gray, and light gray in Fig. 10(a), respectively). At the start of oxidation, the inconsistent oxidation of the HZT-MPC powders results in the “preferential oxidation” of the HfC/ZrC powders compared to the TiC powders. HfO2/ZrO2 preferentially forms and TiO2 lags. As much TiO2 appears, the solid solution of multi-phase oxides produces Ti-doped (Hf,Zr)O2 and (Hf,Zr)TiO4. However, the formation of multi-phase oxides slows their reaction processes. Hence, the TG curve of the HZT-MPC powders has a bimodal distribution (Fig. 4(a)), and multi-phase oxides (HfO2, ZrO2, and TiO2) appear (Figs. 5(a)–5(c), Figs. 6(a) and 6(b), and Fig. 7(a), respectively).

For HZT-MEC powders, the entropy stabilization effect suppresses the “preferential oxidation” of HfC/ZrC and the “delayed oxidation” of TiC, promoting the synergistic oxidation ability of each element. A uniform compositional distribution at the atomic scale promotes the rapid diffusion of TiO2 to (Hf,Zr)O2, forming Ti-doped (Hf,Zr)O2 and (Hf,Zr)TiO4, as shown in Fig. 10(b). Hence, the TG curve of the HZT-MEC powders has a single peak in Fig. 4(a), and there is no appearance of multi-phase oxides (HfO2, ZrO2, and TiO2) in Figs. 5(c), 5(d), and Fig. 6(f) at different temperatures. Additionally, Fig. 10(c) shows that HZT-MPC powders are yellowish-brown after oxidation, similar to the monocarbides, while HZT-MEC powders are milky white, which also demonstrates that the oxides within HZT-MPC powders form through the solid solution among multi-phase oxides.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, we studied the oxidation behaviors of HZT-MPC and HZT-MEC powders. For HZT-MEC, O atoms preferred to adsorb at the sites where Hf and Zr aggregated together to form the Hf–Zr-rich oxide (Hf,Zr)O2. A uniform compositional distribution at the atomic scale promoted the rapid diffusion of TiO2 to (Hf,Zr)O2, forming Ti-doped (Hf,Zr)O2 and (Hf,Zr)TiO4. Within the three monocarbides, the oxidation of TiC occurred significantly later than that of HfC/ZrC. The uneven compositional distribution of HZT-MPC (composed of three monocarbides) led to inconsistent oxidation over time, with HfC/ZrC preferentially oxidizing and TiC lagging, thus producing multi-phase oxides (HfO2, ZrO2, and TiO2). Their solid solution reactions delayed the formation of Ti-doped (Hf,Zr)O2 and (Hf,Zr)TiO4. Hence, the entropy stabilization effect of HZT-MEC increased the synergistic oxidation ability of multiple principal elements. The research results of this article are generalized and applicable to high-entropy ceramics.
