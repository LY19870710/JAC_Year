## 1 Introduction

Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) are a new type of fuel cell that directly converts chemical energy into electrical energy and have received considerable attention in recent years [1,2]. High-temperature (800–1000 °C) SOFCs are efficient, pollution-free electrochemical converters with broad fuel adaptability (hydrocarbons, alcohols, and ammonia) [3]. However, at intermediate temperatures (500–700 °C), material degradation and a decrease in cathode performance compromise the efficiency of SOFCs [4,5]. Sluggish oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) kinetics at the cathode are widely recognized as key factors limiting the performance of SOFCs [6,7]. Therefore, many researchers are dedicated to developing novel cathode materials with superior electrochemical performance.

In previous studies, cobalt-based perovskites, such as GdBaCo2O6−*δ* [8], PrBa0.8Ca0.2Co2O6 [9], Ba0.5Sr0.5Co0.8Fe0.2O3−*δ* [10], La0.6Sr0.4Co1−*x*Fe*x*O3−*δ* [11], and Sm0.5Sr0.5CoO3−*δ* [12], have been widely used as cathode materials for SOFCs because of their high electrical conductivity, excellent oxygen transport properties, and remarkable catalytic activity [13]. However, cobalt-based cathode materials suffer from several limitations, such as a high thermal expansion coefficient (TEC), high volatility, high cost, and poor stability [14,15]. Compared with cobalt-based materials, Fe-based materials are promising cathode candidates because of their excellent structural stability, high chemical compatibility with electrolytes, and low thermal expansion coefficient [16,17]. The superior electrocatalytic performance of Fe-based materials in the ORR originates from their favorable ion diffusion kinetics and the variability of their Fe oxidation states [18,19].

Owing to the distortion of their crystal structures, perovskite oxides are classified into single perovskites, double perovskites, and Ruddlesden‒Popper (R‒P) layered perovskites [20]. Among them, R–P layered perovskites have been widely investigated as cathode materials for SOFCs owing to their high oxygen–ion conductivity and promising electrocatalytic performance [21]. The distinctive layered structure, composed of alternating rock-salt and perovskite layers, maintains significant oxygen nonstoichiometry [22], thus enhancing the oxygen exchange kinetics. These materials concurrently demonstrate superior structural stability and exceptional resistance to phase transitions [23,24]. Sr3Fe2O7−*δ* (SFO) consists of SrO rock‒salt layers and double-layered SrFeO3−*δ* perovskites [25]. Recent studies have shown that it exhibits both high structural stability and excellent oxygen ion conductivity. However, it is susceptible to the slow ORR process with decreasing temperature. Additionally, during long-term durability tests, the sustainability of Sr-rich oxides can be compromised by Sr segregation, which severely affects the performance of SOFCs [26].

Doping is a widely used strategy to simultaneously increase the structural stability and electrocatalytic activity of materials. To improve the stability of SFO, Huan *et al*. [27] synthesized SrEu2Fe2O7−*δ* (SEF) via Eu doping at the A site. The results showed that the doped SEF exhibited high catalytic activity and improved stability. Song *et al*. [28] systematically investigated the influence of Cu doping on the catalytic properties of Sr2.7Pr0.3Fe2O7−*δ*. The experimental results indicated that Cu doping significantly enhances both the electrochemical activity and the oxygen-ion transport properties, suggesting that Cu is a promising cathode material for SOFCs. Murata *et al*. [29] demonstrated that Nd substitution for Pr in Pr2−*x*Nd*x*NiO4+*δ* significantly improved long-term stability, with the highest electrochemical performance observed. Kim and Manthiram [30] demonstrated that replacing 10% Sr with Nd in Sr2.7Ln0.3Fe1.4Co0.6O7−*δ* effectively suppressed the structural instability of SFO. In Ln*x*Sr1−*x*Co*x*Fe1−*x*O3−*δ* (Ln = La, Pr, Nd, and Sm), the Nd-doped sample demonstrated both the maximum electrical conductivity and the best catalytic activity for the ORR among all the investigated compositions [31]. The above experiments suggest that Nd doping may enhance the electrical conductivity by optimizing the localized hopping behavior of charge carriers. Concurrently, it could improve oxygen adsorption/diffusion processes, thereby increasing the electrochemical performance while suppressing phase decomposition, maintaining structural stability.

In this work, a Nd-doped Sr2.9Nd0.1Fe2O7−*δ* (SNFO) material was successfully synthesized through the sol‒gel method. This study systematically investigated the impact of Nd incorporation on the phase stability, oxygen desorption characteristics, ORR kinetics, and electrochemical properties of the SNFO cathode. The results demonstrated that Nd doping significantly increased the oxygen vacancy concentration; thus, the SNFO cathode material exhibited favorable electrocatalytic activity toward the ORR.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

SFO and SNFO cathode materials were synthesized via the sol‒gel method. Stoichiometric amounts of Sr(NO3)2, Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, and Nd(NO3)3·6H2O were dissolved in deionized water, followed by the addition of citric acid (CA) and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as chelating agents at a molar ratio of total metal ions : EDTA : CA = 1 : 1 : 2. After complete dissolution, the pH of the solution was adjusted to 7–8 with aqueous ammonia. The mixture was then heated under continuous stirring until a viscous gel formed. The gel was dried at 180 °C to obtain the precursor powder. The obtained precursor powder was heated to remove carbon, and the decarbonized powder was placed in a high-temperature furnace. The temperature was increased to 1100 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C·min−1 for high-temperature calcination for 12 h. After natural cooling, the target product was obtained.

### 2.2 Cell fabrication

The Ce0.9Gd0.1O1.95 (GDC) (Ningbo SOFCMAN Energy Technology, China) electrolyte powder was pressed under 10–12 MPa. The compact was sintered in a furnace (KSL-1700X, Hefei Kejing Material Technology Co., Ltd., China) by heating first to 1200 °C at 5 °C·min−1, held for 24 h, then to 1400 °C at the same rate, and held for 12 h. After natural cooling, the sample was ground to a 0.32–0.35 mm thickness for testing. The cathode material was weighed and transferred to an agate mortar for grinding. The powder particles were refined, a suitable amount of terpineol (containing 5% ethyl cellulose) was added, and grinding was continued until a homogeneous slurry formed. The slurry was even coated onto the central 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm region on both sides of the GDC electrolyte, and then the sample was dried in an oven at 80 °C. The dried electrode was subsequently sintered in a muffle furnace (KSL-1100X, Hefei Kejing Material Technology Co., Ltd., China) at 900 °C for 4 h. Finally, silver paste was applied, and silver wires were attached to both sides of the electrode to assemble a symmetrical cell with an effective area of 0.25 cm2. For the three-electrode configuration, the cathode material served as the working electrode (WE), while silver paste was coated at a symmetrical position as the counter electrode (CE). A silver wire was placed near the WE surface to function as the reference electrode (RE). The anode-supported half-cells (Ni-YSZ|YSZ|GDC)(ZrO2·Y2O3 (YSZ)) were procured from Ningbo SOFCMAN Energy Technology Co., Ltd., China. The thicknesses were ~300 μm for the NiO-YSZ anode layer, ~10 μm for the YSZ electrolyte layer, and ~4 μm for the GDC interlayer. The cathode slurry was coated on the GDC buffer layer and calcined at 900 °C for 4 h.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The phase structure and composition of the powder were detected via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; AXS D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany). The XRD data were refined via Rietica (version 1.7.7.8) software. The microstructure and elemental distribution of the material were observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; S4800, Hitachi, Japan) and a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HR-TEM; JEOL-2100, JEOL, Japan). The valence state of the surface elements of the sample was characterized via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Kratos Axis Supra TM, Kratos Analytical Ltd., UK). The oxygen temperature-programmed desorption test (O2-TPD) was carried out using a chemisorption instrument (TP-5076, Tianjin Xianquan Co. Ltd., China) to detect the oxygen desorption ability. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the samples was performed at 50–800 °C in air via a thermal analyzer (SETARAM, Setsys Evolution 16, France).

### 2.4 Electrochemical tests

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed using an Autolab workstation (PGSTAT302 N) under the conditions of a frequency range from 10−2 to 106 Hz and an alternating current amplitude of 10 mV. The EIS data were fitted via Zview software. To probe the ORR kinetics at the cathode, EIS was performed under various oxygen partial pressures (0.08–0.16 atm), and the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) was analyzed with MATLAB R2018a. The electrical conductivity of the sintered rod-shaped samples was measured via the DC four-probe technique over the temperature range of 100–800 °C in air via a Keithley DAQ6510 data acquisition system. The electrical conductivity was measured as a function of time at 800 °C under an oxygen partial pressure (* pO2) of 0.21 atm, followed by a switch in  pO2 from 0.21 to 0.02 atm. The oxygen surface exchange coefficient (k*ₑₓ) and chemical diffusion coefficient (*D*them) were determined via the electrical conductivity relaxation (ECR) method. Single-cell performance serves as a key metric for evaluating cathode materials for SOFCs. The output performance of the single cells was determined via a workstation (DH7002A). Humidified H2 (3 vol% H2O) was supplied as fuel, while ambient air was used as an oxidant.

### 2.5 Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

In this study, DFT calculations were carried out via the Vienna *ab initio* Simulation Package (VASP). The Perdew–Burke–Emzerhof generalized gradient approximation (PBE-GGA) was employed to handle the exchange‒correlation interactions [32]. To accurately predict the electronic structure, the *U*eff value for the Fe atoms was set to 5.0 eV (the *U*eff parameter has been applied to rectify inaccuracies in standard DFT’s treatment of strongly correlated electronic systems), and the convergence energy threshold was set to 10−5 eV. A 5 × 5 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack *k*-point grid was used simultaneously. In the geometric optimization step, a force convergence criterion of 0.05 eV·Å−1 was adopted. Previous studies have shown that the (001) surface of SFO is more stable than the (100), (110), (111), and (011) surfaces [33]. Therefore, the (001) surface was selected as the adsorption surface in this study, and the perovskite layer of the material was identified as the reactive active region. On this basis, different adsorption sites were designed by comprehensively considering the exposure of the alkaline earth layer and the transition metal layer. Moreover, Nd doping was performed at the alkaline earth sites of the perovskite layer to investigate the relevant property changes. The calculation expression for the oxygen vacancy formation energy is shown in Eq. (1):

(1) ΔEVO=EVO−E(perfect)+12E(O2)

where *E*VO and *E*(perfect) represent the electronic energies of the material with and without oxygen vacancies, respectively. O2 is the chemical potential of the oxygen atoms, which is calculated from the triplet state energy of the oxygen molecules. The adsorption energy and dissociation energy of molecular oxygen were calculated via Eq. (2):

(2) Eads=E(adsorbate−adsorbent)−E(adsorbate)−E(O2)

where *E*(ads), *E*(adsorbate-adsorbent), and *E*(adsorbate) represent the adsorption energy, the calculated total energies of the slab with and without bound oxygen, respectively, and *E*(O2) records the predicted energy of triplet O2 in a 10 Å cubic cell.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of the SFO and SNFO samples. The diffraction data of the SFO and SNFO were indexed to a tetragonal structure (space group *I*4/*mmm*) with no detectable impurity phases. To obtain clearer structural information, the XRD patterns of the SFO and SNFO were refined via the Rietveld method (Figs. 1(b) and 1(c)), and the refined cell parameters and corresponding reliability factors are summarized in Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The reasonable reliability factors (*R*p, *R*wp, and *χ*2  are used to assess the refinement quality) reflect good consistency between the refined results and experimental data. Furthermore, a decrease in the lattice parameters and cell volume is observed with Nd3+ doping, which is attributable to the ionic radius mismatch (Nd3+: 1.27 Å 2+: 1.44 Å) [34,35]. To study the compatibility between the cathode and the electrolyte at high temperature, the SFO/SNFO and GDC powder were ground at a ratio of 1 : 1 and then calcined at 1100 °C for 12 h. The XRD patterns of the mixtures are shown in Fig. 1(d). All diffraction peaks observed in the mixtures originate from the characteristic phases of SFO, SNFO, and GDC, and no extraneous phases or impurities are detected. These findings confirm the favorable chemical compatibility of the cathode material with the electrolyte at elevated temperatures.

Furthermore, O2-TPD analysis was employed to probe the oxygen adsorption capacity of the SFO and SNFO materials. As shown in Fig. 3(c), both the SFO and SNFO samples exhibit two distinct desorption peaks within the temperature range of 50–950 °C. The first desorption peak at 300–500 °C is attributed to α-oxygen, which originates from the desorption of surface oxygen and lattice oxygen loss. This phenomenon is due to the reduction of Fe4+ to Fe3+, which facilitates the diffusion of lattice oxygen from the bulk phase to the surface [40]. The second desorption peak located at 600–850 °C is attributed to β-O2, which correlates with the Fe3+ ions to Fe2+ ions. Notably, the area of the *α*-oxygen desorption peak of SNFO is larger than that of SFO, indicating that SNFO has better oxygen adsorption capacity. In addition, the oxygen nonstoichiometric (*δ*) value of the sample at high temperatures was obtained from the TGA curves, as shown in Fig. 3(d). The first stage, from room temperature to approximately 200 °C, is the physically adsorbed water in the samples. When the temperature exceeds 300 °C, the weight loss is due mainly to the release of lattice oxygen and the formation of oxygen vacancies [41,42]. The weight loss rates are 2.97% and 6.83% for SFO and SNFO, respectively, upon heating to 900 °C. Furthermore, the *δ* values of SFO and SNFO were derived from TGA data and oxygen nonstoichiometry (*δ*0) at room temperature as determined by iodometric titration [43]. Obviously, SNFO oxide has a higher *δ* value than parent SFO does, implying that SNFO can produce more oxygen vacancies at SOFC operating temperatures. In general, a higher oxygen vacancy concentration is beneficial for faster oxygen ion migration in the bulk electrode, thereby promoting the electrochemical activity for the ORR of the cathode.

Figure 4(a) shows the relationships between the electrical conductivities of the SFO and SNFO and the temperature. When the temperature is lower than 450 °C, the electrical conductivity increases with increasing temperature, indicating the conductive behavior of semiconductor materials [44]. However, the electrical conductivity decreases with increasing temperature above 450 °C. This phenomenon reveals metal-like conduction, which is attributed to the loss of lattice oxygen and the partial reduction of iron ions [45]. The electrical conductivity of SNFO is lower than that of SFO. This may be because the doping of Nd3+ increases the oxygen vacancy concentration in the oxide, which obstructs the electron transport path in Fe3+–O2−Fe4+. This leads to a decrease in the carrier concentration, which in turn reduces the electronic conductivity of the electrode material [46]. The ORR processes of the cathode are intimately associated with the oxygen bulk diffusion coefficient (*D*chem) and the surface exchange coefficient (*k*ex) of the material [47]. The *D*chem and *k*ex values of the SFO and SNFO oxides were investigated via the ECR method. The ECR is used to investigate the *D*chem and *k*ex of the SFO and SNFO. Figure 4(b) shows the normalized ECR curves of the SFO and SNFO samples at 700 °C. On the basis of the fitting results, the corresponding *D*chem and *k*ex values of the SNFO are 2.99×10−5 and 2.00×10−3 cm·s−1, respectively, which are greater than those of pristine SFO (*D*chem: 2.19×10−5 cm·s−1, *k*ex: 8.9×10−4 cm·s−1). This result indicates that Nd doping can effectively increase the oxygen transport capacity and ORR kinetics of a material. Notably, the *D*chem and *k*ex values (700 °C) of SNFO are obviously greater than those of other Fe-based cathode materials (Figs. 4(c) and 4(d)) [48−52].

The ORR performance of the SNFO cathode was further studied on the basis of symmetric cells (SNFO|CGO|SNFO)(Ce0.9Gd0.1O1.95 (CGO)). The electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) of the SFO and SNFO cathodes at 700 °C are shown in Fig. 5(a). The difference between the intercepts of the left and right ends on the real axis represents the total polarization resistance (*R*p) of the electrode. The *R*p values of the SNFO and SFO electrodes are 0.20 and 0.41 Ω·cm2 at 700 °C, respectively, indicating that the SNFO cathode possesses superior electrocatalytic performance. Importantly, the SNFO cathode has a lower *R*p, which exceeds those of some reported Fe-based cathodes for SOFCs (Fig. 5(b)) [53−57]. Moreover, the Arrhenius plots of the SFO and SNFO cathodes are presented in Fig. 5(c). The calculated activation energy (*E*a) value of SNFO is 0.97 eV, which is lower than that of the parent SFO (1.30 eV). The smaller *R*p and *E*a values indicate that the SNFO cathode has excellent electrocatalytic performance and reduces the energy barrier for the electrode reaction [58]. Moreover, to further investigate the possible application of the electrode in SOFCs, full cells with the configuration Ni-YSZ|YSZ|CGO|SNFO(SFO) were fabricated, and the output performance was measured. Figures 5(d) and 5(e) display typical *I*‒*V*‒*P* (*I* represents current density, *V* represents voltage, and *P* represents power density) curves of single cells with SFO and SNFO air electrodes at 600‒700 °C. The fuel cell with the SNFO cathode presents a higher peak power density (PPD) than that with the SFO cathode at the same temperature, which can reach 803 mW·cm−2 for SNFO and 415 mW·cm−2 for SFO at 700 °C. Notably, compared with some Fe-based cathode materials, SNFO achieves superior PPD values at 700 °C under analogous operating conditions (Fig. 5(f)). Additionally, an SEM image of the cross-section of the complete cell with the SNFO cathode is shown in Fig. 5(g). The single cell comprises a porous SNFO cathode (~18 µm), a CGO buffer layer ( ~4 µm), a dense YSZ electrolyte (~10 µm) forming a dense thin film that only permits the passage of oxygen ions while blocking electrons and gases, and a Ni-YSZ anode layer (~300 µm). The fuel cell exhibits robust interfacial contact and no observable delamination between layers, which ensures good gas transport and effective charge transfer. Moreover, the short-term durability of the fuel cell incorporating the SNFO cathode was further investigated, as displayed in Fig. 5(h). The current density of the cell remains at a steady value over 100 h of testing, demonstrating the operational stability of the SNFO cathode in SOFCs.

To clarify the rate-determining step for the ORR on the electrode, the EIS spectra of the SNFO cathode were evaluated at 700 °C under different * pO2 and are depicted in Fig. 6(a). The EIS data were fitted via Zview software with an equivalent circuit (R*0–(*R*HF//CPE1)–(*R*MF//CPE2)–(*R*LF//CPE3), where *R*0 is the total ohmic resistance generated by the electrolyte, the electrode, and the device; *R*HF, *R*MF, and *R*LF are the high-frequency, medium-frequency, and low-frequency resistances, respectively; and CPE1, CPE2, and CPE3 are the corresponding constant phase elements, respectively. As shown in Fig. 6(a), the EIS spectra exhibit three distinct regions: a high-frequency (HF), medium-frequency (MF), and low-frequency (LF) region, indicating the presence of three distinct ORR processes on the cathode. The relationship between the polarization resistance and * pO2 can be expressed as follows: R*p = *k*(* pO2)−n*, where *k* is a constant, and *n* reflects the reaction step for the ORR [59]. In general, *n* = 1 corresponds to the adsorption and diffusion of molecular oxygen on the cathode surface; *n* = 0.5 represents the adsorption‒dissociation process of oxygen; *n* = 0.375 is associated with the charge transfer reaction at the three-phase boundary (TPB); *n* = 0.25 is related to the charge transfer reaction; and *n* = 0 can be attributed to the migration of oxygen ions from the TPB to the electrolyte. Figure 6(b) shows the fitting curves of *R*HF, *R*MF, and *R*LF of the SNFO cathode as a function of * pO2 at 700 °C. The fitted n* values for *R*HF, *R*MF, and *R*LF are 0.34, 0.46, and 1.49, respectively, originating from the charge transfer reaction at the TPB interface, molecular oxygen adsorption‒dissociation, and the oxygen adsorption‒diffusion process [60]. To further investigate the ORR process of the electrode, the EIS of the SNFO cathode under different * pO2 values was explored via the DRT technique (Fig. 6(d)). The observed insensitivity of the high-frequency (P*HF) and low-frequency (*P*LF) peaks to oxygen partial pressures, in contrast to the distinct dependence exhibited by the medium-frequency peak (*P*MF), further demonstrates that the oxygen adsorption‒dissociation process is the rate-determining step of the electrode reaction.

In the equation, *R**i* represents the resistance, *n**i* denotes the degree of similarity to an ideal capacitor, and *Q**i* represents the constant phase element and *i* denotes a specific process within a high, medium, or low frequency band. For the high-frequency process, the values of *C**i* and *f**i* are approximately 10−4 F·cm−2 and 104–105 Hz, respectively. These values are associated with the oxygen ion transfer process [61]. In the medium-frequency process, *C**i* and *f**i* are approximately 10−4 F·cm−2 and 103–104 Hz, respectively, and are closely related to the oxygen adsorption‒desorption process. For the low-frequency process, *C**i* and *f**i* are in the ranges of approximately 1 F·cm−2 and 10–102 Hz, respectively, corresponding to the transport of oxygen ions through the adsorption‒diffusion of molecular oxygen [62].

The polarization performance of a material is a critical parameter for evaluating electrode activity [63]. Figure 7(a) presents the Tafel plots of the SNFO electrode measured at 600–700 °C. The polarization overpotential of the electrode can be described via the Butler‒Volmer equation: *η* = *R**T*ln(*i*)/*α*ZF − *R**T*ln(*i*0)/*α*ZF, where *η* denotes the electrode overpotential, *R* the gas constant, *T* the absolute temperature, *F* the Faraday constant, *α* the charge transfer coefficient, and *Z* the electron transfer number for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), as demonstrated previously [64]. Generally, the exchange current density (*i*0) fundamentally reflects intrinsic ORR kinetics and the electrocatalytic performance of the electrode. The *i*0 value of the SNFO cathode is 27.59 mA·cm−2 at 700 °C, outperforming some reported cathode materials for SOFCs, such as Pr2CuO4 (17.4 mA·cm−2) [65] and Ca2Fe1.3Mn0.7O5+*δ* (18.2 mA·cm−2) [66]. Moreover, to evaluate the bifunctional activity of the SNFO electrode, the polarization curves of the SNFO electrode in the ORR and oxygen evolution reaction (OER) modes were obtained (Fig. 7(a)). During operation in ORR mode, a measured cathode overpotential of 33.51 mV corresponds to a current density of 87.04 mA·cm−2 at 700 °C. Under OER conditions, an applied cathode overpotential *η* = 29.47 mV produces a current density of *i* = 87.94 mA·cm−2 at 700 °C. These results demonstrate good catalytic activity of the SNFO electrode in both SOEC and SOFC modes.

To systematically examine the effect of Nd doping on the ORR kinetics of SFO cathodes, we performed DFT calculations to compare the oxygen vacancy formation energies of SFO and SNFO. Figure 8(a) shows four representative oxygen vacancy configurations, and the results are summarized in Fig. 8(b) and Table 1. Combined, the results of iodometric titration and TGA reveal that Nd doping increases the oxygen vacancy concentration in SNFO, with preferential formation of vacancies in  VO2 and a reduction in the formation energy to 0.01 eV compared with that of undoped SFO. Figure 8(c) compares the oxygen adsorption energies (E*O2,ads) of SFO and SNFO. For SFO, the *E*ads values are 0.20 eV (Sr site) and 0.10 eV (Fe site), whereas those of SNFO are 0.72 eV (Sr), −0.04 eV (Fe), and −0.14 eV (Nd sites). The negative values at the Fe/Nd sites confirmed that spontaneous O2 adsorption was induced by Nd doping. Figure 8(d) reveals that Nd substitution promotes Sr dissociation toward Nd dopants while reducing the Fe dissociation energy from 3.78 to 3.45 eV, confirming that Nd doping creates additional catalytically active sites in SNFO and thereby enhances the ORR activity of the cathode. The density of states (DOS) for SFO and SNFO are presented in Fig. S6 in the ESM. The O 2p-band center, commonly used as a descriptor for cathode ORR activity [67], was calculated to be −2.19 eV (SFO-VO3) and −2.25 eV (SNFO-VO3), as shown in Fig. 8(e). The shift of the SNFO energy band away from the center may be attributed to Nd doping. Furthermore, Fig. 8(f) shows that Nd doping reduces the charge transfer energy (Δ*E*CT) from −3.816 to −3.729 eV. This 0.087 eV decrease in Δ*E*CT likely contributes to the ORR performance of the SNFO catalyst.

The electronic localization function (ELF) was further employed to quantify the degree of electron localization. Comparative analysis between SFO and SNFO (Fig. 8(g)) reveals that SNFO results in increased electron density accumulation around Fe sites with concomitant delocalization characteristics, which is attributable to strengthened metal‒oxygen bonding that facilitates electron hopping. ## 4 Conclusions

In summary, both SFO and SNFO cathode materials were successfully synthesized via the sol‒gel method and systematically investigated as oxygen electrodes for SOFCs. The experimental results demonstrate that Nd doping effectively promotes oxygen vacancy formation, enhancing the oxygen adsorption capacity and electrochemical performance. Notably, the SNFO cathode exhibited an *R*p of 0.20 Ω·cm2 and a PPD of 803 mW·cm−2 at 700 °C. DRT analysis combined with EIS under varying  pO2 revealed that the rate-determining step of the electrode is oxygen surface adsorption and dissociation processes. Furthermore, DFT calculations confirmed that Nd doping reduces both the oxygen vacancy formation energy and the adsorption energy. These findings collectively suggest that SNFO is a promising high-activity cathode catalyst for SOFCs.
