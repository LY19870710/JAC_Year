## 1 Introduction

Electrostriction refers to a phenomenon in ceramics where strain varies proportionally with the square of the applied electric field or polarization [1−5]. Electromechanical ceramics, which are capable of converting electrical energy into mechanical energy and vice versa, combine high electrostrain with low hysteresis, offering an excellent platform for applications in electronics and optoelectronics [6−9]. The electric field-induced strain in lead-based ferroelectric materials, such as PZT and relaxor-PbTiO3 (Pb(Zn1/3Nb2/3)O3–PbTiO3 and Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3–PbTiO3), has garnered increasing attention over the past few decades and has also been widely used in sensors, motors, actuators, transducers, artificial muscles, and medical microrobots [1,10−13]. However, these lead-based ceramics typically contain high levels of lead, raising significant concerns regarding environmental safety and human health. Consequently, owing to the toxicity of lead and its compounds, interest in developing environmentally friendly, lead-free alternatives is increasing.

Among the wide range of lead-free ceramic materials proposed as alternatives to traditional lead-based compounds are sodium–bismuth titanate ((Bi,Na)TiO3 (BNT)), potassium-bismuth titanate ((K,Na)NbO3 (KNN)), barium titanate BaTiO3 (BTO) and its derivatives (BZT and BST) [7,14−20]. These materials all belong to the perovskite-type family with the general formula ABO₃ and are characterized not only by high unipolar strain but also by thermal stability. Notably, there is growing industrial demand for ultra large lead-free ceramics in high-power, high-energy applications, such as ultrasonic transducers, actuators, sonar systems, energy harvesters, and large-scale sensors. Particularly in green energy systems—for example, piezoelectric energy harvesters embedded in infrastructure—larger monolithic ceramics are often required to capture mechanical energy efficiently. Gel casting has emerged as a highly promising shaping technique for the fabrication of ultralarge lead-free ceramics because of its low cost, high packing density, enhanced green body strength, and excellent machinability [21,22]. Notably, it integrates ceramic powder processing with polymer chemistry, thus overcoming several limitations of other complex shape-forming methods, such as injection molding, slip casting, or uniaxial pressing [23]. Additionally, studies on humidity-assisted strategies for the synthesis of high-quality ceramics remain scarce. Vu *et al*. [24] developed porous mechanical ceramics capable of controlling humidity, noting that the pore size of the raw materials must lie within the appropriate mesoporous range. However, lead-free electromechanical ceramics often suffer from volatilization during the sintering process, leading to deviations in stoichiometry and subsequent degradation of functional properties [22]. Furthermore, the conventional solid-state synthesis route involves prolonged drying times during gel casting, which is time-consuming and can be a potential source of cracking [25,26].

Herein, we introduce a humidity-assisted strategy for the synthesis of a series of colossal lead-free electromechanical ceramics, including BTO, BST, BZT, BNT, and KNN. Using this method, we successfully fabricated monolithic ceramic samples as large as 10 cm × 10 cm. By tuning the humidity, the drying temperature was reduced to as low as 20 °C, significantly shortening the processing time. Interestingly, a tunable dielectric constant ranging from hundreds to thousands was also achieved simply by adjusting the porosity. More importantly, under an AC bias, these ceramics exhibited excellent electrostrictive performance. This work demonstrates the promising potential of lead-free ceramics for practical applications in electromechanical devices.

### 2.1 Manufacturing of dense ceramics

Large dense ceramics were fabricated via a gelcasting technique, with Isobam 104 (Kuraray, Japan; M.W. 55,000–65,000) serving as both the dispersant and the gelling agent. Commercial high-purity powders of BTO, BST, BZT, BNT, and KNN (Sigma‒Aldrich) were employed as starting materials. The ceramic slurry was prepared by ball-milling a mixture of ceramic powders (35–48 vol%) and Isobam 104 (0.3–1.0 wt%) in deionized water at 600 r/min for 4 h. The resulting slurry was thoroughly degassed and cast into plastic molds. Gelation was carried out in sealed molds for two days to ensure complete gelation, followed by drying at 20 °C and 85% relative humidity for approximately two days to remove ~90% of the water content. Subsequent drying was performed in a ventilated oven at 50 °C for one day or until the sample’s weight stabilized. Upon complete drying, typical crack-free green bodies with dimensions of approximately 10 cm × 10 cm were obtained. These green bodies were then sintered in a ceramic powder bed at elevated temperatures for durations ranging from several hours to several tens of hours in air, yielding dense ceramic components. ### 2.2 Manufacturing of porous ceramics

Commercial BT powder was used as the raw material. Similarly, Isobam104 was used as both the dispersant and the gelling agent. Dodecyltrimethylammonium chloride (DTAC; Sigma Aldrich) was used as the foaming agent. Taking BTO as an example, during the gelcasting process, barium titanate slurries with different solid loads and different contents of DTAC were prepared by milling for one hour in a planetary ball milling machine. Wet foams were fabricated via direct foaming with a kitchen mixer at 600 r/min for 6 min after adding DTAC into the slurries. The wet foams were then cast into plastic molds. After gelating for approximately 24 h, the samples were demolded and dried at room temperature for 2 d. Finally, the samples were held at 600 °C for 2 h to remove the organic matter and then sintered at 1307 °C for 30 min, followed by sintering at 1147 °C for 10 h. After the cooling process, the porous ceramics were obtained. The influences of the solids load and DTAC concentration on the foaming ability and microstructure of the ceramic foams were investigated. Finally, by controlling the sintering protocol to obtain ceramic green bodies that are partially sintered, it is possible to obtain ceramics with different porosities. A set of BTO green bodies prepared with a 46 vol% solid load and 0.3 wt% isobam slurries were sintered at temperatures of ~1147–1337 °C for the studies.

### 2.3 Material characterizations

The micro/nanostructures and elemental compositions of these samples were investigated via a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; JSM6700F, JEOL) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDX). An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Rigaku, Japan) was subsequently employed to determine the lattice parameters. The bulk density of the ceramics was measured via Archimedes' principle.

Cylindrical disks with a diameter of 10 mm were cut from the large ceramic samples via an ultrasonic cutter. Silver electrodes were applied to the samples by sintering silver paste at 800 °C for 15 min, followed by soldering with copper wires. The room-temperature dielectric constant and dielectric loss were measured via a precision impedance analyzer (4294A, Agilent, USA). The temperature-dependent dielectric properties were investigated via a high-precision LCR meter (HP 4284A, Hewlett-Packard Co., USA) over the temperature range of 30–500 °C. Polarization–electric field (*P*–*E*) hysteresis loops were recorded via a Radiant Precision Multiferroic II system. Finally, a custom-designed setup was employed to evaluate the electrostrictive response for actuator applications.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(a) shows a schematic diagram of ceramic production via the gel-casting technique. During a typical gel-casting process, ceramic powders are first ground via ball milling and then mixed with a gelling agent solution to form a castable slurry; the slurry is poured into a mold and solidified after drying, followed by a sintering procedure. Notably, gelling agent solution preparation, which consists of a solubilized gel former and a solvent, is crucial for large crack-free ceramic bulks. Another factor is the rheological behavior of the ceramic slurries, especially the trade-off between the shear rate and viscosity [22]. Isobam can generally be used as both a gelling agent and dispersant owing to its innocuous and environmentally friendly characteristics (Fig. 1(b)) [27]. For porous ceramic production, a foaming agent as an additive was introduced before the casting process (Fig. 1(c)) [23,25]. Such gel casting has several remarkable advantages, such as low cost, high yields and high green capacity, indicating the potential of mass production of supercolossal high-quality ceramics. Figures 1(d) and 1(e) show photographs of dense and porous BTO ceramics obtained via gel casting, indicating the successful fabrication of monolithic wafers. The corresponding microstructures were further investigated via SEM (Figs. 1(f) and 1(g)). The former has a dense surface morphology with numerous uniform nanoscale particles, whereas the latter consists of hollow spheres ranging from tens to hundreds of micrometres in diameter. As shown in Fig. 1(h), EDX analysis suggests the presence of Ba, Ti, and O. The approximate ratio of Ba to Ti is 1.01, which is very close to the stoichiometric ratio of the formula. The dense BTO ceramic also reveals excellent ferroelectric performance, with a spontaneous polarization of 29.8 μC/cm2 (Fig. 1(i)). This value is comparable to the 26 μC/cm2 value reported for BTO bulks and less than the 50 μC/cm2 value reported for strained BTO thin films [28,29].

In general, crack initiation is always observed in ceramics because of the large stress, grain boundaries, and voids present during the synthesis and sintering process, which usually results in performance degradation. Thus, degassing is the key step for successful gel casting, allowing the achievement of wafer-scale crack-free ceramics. Figure 2 shows the degassing effect on the BTO ceramics. It is clear that numerous cracks are present in the samples without degassing, whereas their counterparts with degassing are completely monolithic without any cracks. As shown in Figs. 2(c) and 2(d), SEM analysis further confirmed the existence of voids in the former. In contrast, the degassing BTO ceramics exhibit smooth and dense surfaces, which are beneficial for accessing electronic components. In addition, the densities of BTO without and with the degassing process are 3.60 and 4.15 g/cm3, respectively (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). Apparently, the degassing process effectively removes gases to obtain highly dense BTO ceramics. Each density was recorded 5 times, and the results were averaged to minimize measurement errors. Obviously, degassing efficiently improves the density of the green body, thereby reducing cracking.

Another key factor, such as humidity, is also attributed to crack evolution in the BTO bulk. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the BTO samples were dried in a ventilated oven at 50 °C, leading to disastrous results (Fig. S2 in the ESM). In contrast, while maintaining a humidity of 50%, regardless of whether 40 or 60 C is used, 2-inch BTO monolithic materials without any cracks can be obtained (Figs. 3(b) and 3(c)). Figure 3(d) shows the weight evolution of the BTO samples dried at 50% humidity at 40 and 60 °C. If the mixture is dried in an ambient environment, it will take more than 7 d to reduce the amount of solvent. When drying in a humidity-controlled chamber, only 11 h are needed, which is far superior to that of the ambient environment. More importantly, we can significantly reduce the drying temperature to 20 °C by increasing the humidity to 85%, further allowing for 10 cm × 10 cm crack-free BTO ceramics (Figs. 3(e) and 3(f)). Cracks typically occur when shrinkage within a ceramic piece occurs unevenly, exceeding the inherent strength of the material to resist such deformation. The presence of humidity, which acts as a protective agent while maintaining equal gas pressure, effectively ensures the uniformity of the drying process. Additionally, the vapor pressure of water in the surrounding environment regulates the evaporation rate within the ceramic body, preventing the formation of cracks caused by rapid water evaporation.

By utilizing such a general strategy, a series of wafer-scale electromechanical ceramics, such as BST, BZT, BNT, and KNN, are synthesized. Figures 4(a)–4(e) show scanning electron microscopy images of BTO, BST, BZT, BNT, and KNN. The corresponding insets show their bulk samples with two-sided polishing. Notably, all the SEM images display a dense microstructure with a relative density of greater than 93%. In addition, the grain size varies according to the ceramic material, ranging from 1.5–3.5 μm. The difference in grain morphology is mainly associated with the slurries with particle morphologies during the gel-casting process. The irregular polyhedral particles always possess lower Hausdroff dimensionality, which, in turn, might increase the slurry viscosity, thereby hindering particle mobility and dense packing [30]. The density values of all the lead-free ceramics are 5.8810, 5.2325, 5.9420, 5.6695, and 4.0002 g∙cm−3, respectively (Fig. 4(f)).

Figure 5(a) shows the typical XRD patterns of lead-free ceramics. All the XRD diffraction peaks could be well indexed to a pure perovskite structure, indicating the formation of single solid solutions in all the samples [31−33]. Taking (110)/(101) as an example, a remarkable variation in peak position is observed, which should be related to the difference in the radius and charge of positive ions. The lattice parameters are calculated and listed in Fig. 5(b). Obviously, both a* and *c* exhibit similar trends. Moreover, the tetragonality (*c*/*a*) reveals a small variation owing to the various components. Figure 5(c) displays the dielectric constants and losses of all the samples over a wide frequency range from 103 to 108 Hz. Notably, both BZT and BTO ceramics demonstrate a larger dielectric constant of over 3000, with a decreasing trend with increasing frequency [20,33−35]. Interestingly, their dielectric constant gradually decreases at high frequencies, whereas the loss significantly increases. In fact, the dipole reversal at high frequency cannot keep up with the electric field, thereby creating a relaxation, which might be attributed to the incremental loss [36,37]. Figure 4(d) shows the values of their dielectric constant and loss at a frequency of 10 kHz. Although BNT has a slightly high dielectric loss of approximately 5.1%, the losses of the other materials are less than 3%.

Figures 6(a)–6(e) reveal the temperature and frequency of the dielectric performances for the BTO, BST, BZT, BNT, and KNN ceramics. Notably, all the samples, with the exception of BNT, exhibit typical ferroelectric characteristics, and all the curves almost overlap with increasing temperature near the Curie temperature (T*c) [29,38,39]. In contrast, the BNT ceramic displays remarkable relaxor behavior, where *T*c significantly shifts toward higher temperatures, thereby leading to broader dielectric maxima [40]. Interestingly, these lead-free ceramics demonstrate distinct values of *T*c, ranging from tens to hundreds. Among them, BST has the lowest *T*c of 31.3 °C, whereas BTO has a higher value of 121.8 °C, which is associated with the Sr doping-induced flattened free-energy profile [37,41,42]. Both BNT and KNN clearly have large values above 300.0 °C [33,43]. In particular, the Curie temperature of KNN is approximately 407.6 °C, which is much better than those of lead-based (Pb,La)(Zr,Ti)O3 (PLZT, 123.6–151.3 °C), PZN–PT (130–221 °C), and PMN–PT (185–215 °C) (Fig. 6(f)) [44−46]. Moreover, it is also comparable or superior to Pb(In1/3Nb2/3)O3–Pb(Zn1/3Nb2/3)O3–PbTiO3 (PIN–PZN–PT, 330 °C) and Pb(Zr,Ti)O3 (PZT, 419.3 °C) [38,47]. Therefore, high-performance lead-free electromechanical ceramics with high Curie temperatures, such as KNN and BNT, can be considered promising candidates for PZT replacement, which cannot avoid the problem of lead metal contamination.

In addition, by using a similar strategy, we can obtain ultralarge lead-free porous ceramics simply by adjusting their porosity during the synthesis process. Taking BTO as an example, we synthesized a series of porous BTO ceramics with porosities ranging from 7.7% to 72.8%, as shown in Figs. 7(a)–7(c) and Fig. S3 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Apparently, the corresponding dielectric constant of BTO dramatically decreases with increasing porosity, whereas the dielectric loss generally increases. The dielectric constants of the BTO samples with porosities of 67.7% and 72.8% are as low as 594.1 and 489.3, respectively, which are nearly an order of magnitude lower than those of dense BTO (4215.7 at 10 kHz). Owing to the interconversion between electrical and mechanical energy in these electromechanical ceramics, they have been widely used in sensors, actuators, and transducers [9]. In fact, the total strain *ε* in one ferroelectric material can be defined as *ε* = *ε*piezo + *ε*striction *+ ε*thermal *+ ε*pressure = *d*33*E* − *ME*2 + *λT* + *a*el*σ*, where *d*33, *E*, *M*, *λ*, *T*, *a*el, and *σ* are the piezoelectric coefficient, the electric field, the electrostriction coefficient, the dilatation coefficient, the temperature, the elastic coefficient, and the stress, respectively [49]. At room temperature and without an applied bias or pressure, the strain is related only to electrostriction and hence can be simplified: *ε* = *ME*2. Therefore, we designed a smart facility to directly measure the microscopic strain of these ceramics via laser interferometry, thereby allowing accurate measurements of their electrostriction under an AC bias [50]. Figure 8(a) shows a schematic illustration of electrical field-induced strain. The displacement responses as functions of the AC electric field are characterized by laser feedback and then recorded by one oscilloscope. Figure 8(b) shows the longitudinal displacement responses of the BNT ceramics as the AC bias varies. The inset shows the corresponding photograph of our experimental setup. There is little doubt that the relationship between the strain and AC bias is quadratic, indicating that the electromechanical behavior here is dominated by electrostriction. As shown in Fig. 8(c), as the AC voltage changed from 0.1 to 5 kHz, we found little significant change in the magnitude of the electrostrictive response (Fig. 8(d)), indicating the condition of a steady mechanical boundary.

The calculated electrostrictive coefficient (*M*) of the BNT ceramic is 125 nm2∙V−2, which is higher than that of most of the other electrostrictive ceramics listed in Table 2 and comparable to that of the widely used electrostrictive ceramic, i.e., Pb(Zn2/3Nb1/3)O3–0.08PbTiO3 (PZN–PT) (120 nm2∙V−2) [51]. A major factor behind the outstanding electrostrain performance is the fabrication process used to achieve dense and stoichiometrically accurate BNT perovskite ceramics. The *M*BNT is comparable to typical electrostrictive polymers, such as poly(vinylidene fluoride–trifluoroethylene) (P(VDF-TrFE)), which have *M* values ranging from 113–180 nm2∙V−2 [50]. More importantly, BNT has the large *T*C of ~350 °C (Fig. 7), which is significantly higher than 200 °C for PZN–PT and 125 °C for P(VDF-TrFE) [51,52]. Fig. 9 benchmarks various electrostrictive ceramics in terms of their electrostrictive coefficients and Curie temperatures. BNT can be considered a highly competitive substitute for lead-based electrostrictive ceramics, e.g., Pb(Zr0.52Ti0.48)TiO3 and Pb(Zn2/3Nb1/3)O3–0.08PbTiO3, to be used in various advanced actuators, sensors, and motors owing to their strong electrostrictive response and high Curie temperature [1,53].

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, we reported a facile, time-efficient, and humidity-assisted approach for synthesizing a series of lead-free electromechanical ceramics. Monolithic ceramic samples as large as 10 cm × 10 cm in size were successfully fabricated, and their microstructure, dielectric, and ferroelectric properties were systematically investigated. Notably, the use of humidity not only reduced the drying temperature to room temperature but also significantly shortened the processing time. Taking BaTiO3 as an example, a tunable dielectric constant ranging from 489.3 to 4215.7 was achieved by adjusting the porosity. Furthermore, under AC voltages ranging from 0.1 to 5 kHz, the ceramics exhibited excellent electrostrictive performance, with the strain of approximately 0.46% and electrostrictive coefficient of 125 nm2∙V−2. This work not only presents a scalable strategy for fabricating large-scale lead-free ceramics but also highlights their significant potential for practical applications in electromechanical devices.
