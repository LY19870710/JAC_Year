## 1 Introduction

Boron carbide (B4C) ceramic has been widely applied in various fields such as military, civil, and nuclear fields due to its light weight, ultra-high hardness, excellent wear resistance, and good neutron absorption capability [1−5]. However, the low fracture toughness (2.0–3.0 MPa·m1/2) and flexural strength (250–450 MPa) as well as the poor sinterability severely hinder the wide application of B4C ceramic [6,7]. In addition, the ultra-high hardness and brittle nature make the conventional machining of B4C ceramic by diamond lapping or grinding extremely difficult and limited to simple shapes. Electrical discharge machining (EDM) is an attractive method to machine bulk ceramics into complex shapes with high accuracy and low cost [8]. Unfortunately, the low electrical conductivity of B4C ceramic around 100 S/m is not sufficient for achieving excellent EDM processing efficiency [9,10].

The addition of second phase particles is effective and convenient to improve the densification, fracture toughness, and strength of B4C ceramic [11−14]. Among the commonly used second phase particles (refractory borides, carbides, and nitrides), TiB2 is one of the most promising candidates owing to its moderate density, high hardness, and excellent electrical conductivity [15,16]. The addition of TiB2 enhances the fracture toughness and strength of the B4C ceramic. Moreover, the electrically conductive B4C–TiB2 composites satisfy the demand for EDM and exhibit excellent machinability [17]. Reactive sintering is a promising approach to obtain B4C–TiB2 composite with fine microstructure and high sintering activity. Wang *et al*. [18] prepared B4C–TiB2 composites by reactive sintering of B, Ti, and graphite. However, this method was costly and the reaction process was uncontrollable. Ding *et al*. [19] prepared B4C–TiB2 composites by reactive hot-press sintering of TiC–B mixtures. The composition of the composites was fixed and the theoretical density was high up to 3.34 g/cm3. Popov *et al.* [20] prepared B4C–TiB2 composites by hot pressing using B4C and TiC as the raw materials. However, the densification and mechanical properties of the composites were adversely affected by the presence of residual C in the final composites.

It is a challenge to derive high performance B4C–TiB2 composites containing low TiB2 contents [21,22]. The B4C–23 vol% TiB2 composite fabricated using spark plasma sintering (SPS) effectively improves the fracture toughness and flexural strength from 3.4 MPa·m1/2 and 417 MPa to 6.9 MPa·m1/2 and 520 MPa, respectively, compared with pure B4C ceramic [23]. Further increasing the content of TiB2 to 30 vol%, a stable EDM process was achieved in B4C–TiB2 composites [24]. However, due to the lower intrinsic hardness and higher specific gravity of TiB2 than B4C, such a high volume content of TiB2 is detrimental to the light weight and ultra-high hardness of B4C ceramic. In our previous works [25,26], a novel microstructure, i.e., sub-micron-sized TiB2 grains surrounding B4C matrix to form a three-dimensional (3D) interconnected network, has been proposed to achieve high electrical conductivity together with excellent mechanical properties. The B4C–TiB2 composite was constructed by reactive spark plasma sintering (RSPS) using B4C, TiC, and B as raw materials. The Vickers hardness of the composite maintained 28.9 GPa with enhanced fracture toughness (5.28 MPa·m1/2) with only 15 vol% TiB2. The electrical conductivity reached 2.79×104 S/m and the material removal rate (MRR) value could be also remarkably increased to 8.22 mm3/min, owing to the interconnected intergranular TiB2 network. However, the high proportion of B4C particles inhibits the *in-situ* reaction between TiC and B, which may also react with TiC. As a result, a small amount of residual C exists in the B4C–TiB2 composite, which has degenerated the comprehensive mechanical properties of the composite.

To overcome this shortcoming, a two-step method based on the novel selective matrix grain growth (SMGG) strategy was established to fabricate B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics. First, (B4C–TiB2) precursor powders with uniform size distribution were synthesized from the boronizing process of TiC. Then, the precursor powders were mixed with commercial B4C powders to fabricate B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics. The effects of particle size matching between initial TiC and B powders on microstructure, mechanical and electrical properties of synthesized precursor powders and derived composites were investigated in detail to elucidate the reaction mechanism in TiC–B system and SMGG mechanism. Further, the machinability of sintered B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics by wire-EDM (WEDM) was also evaluated.

### 2.1 Powder preparation

The starting materials consisted of three kinds of B4C powders with average particle sizes of 0.5, 3.12, and 10.29 µm (Jiangxi Nutpool Industrial Co., Ltd., China), three kinds of TiC powders with average particle sizes of 0.05 (Hefei Kaier Nano Energy Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China), 0.8 and 3.0 µm (Changsha Langfeng Metallic Material Co., Ltd., China), and amorphous B powder with an average particle size of 0.9 µm (Dandong Chemical Engineering Institute Co., Ltd., China). A schematic for the synthesis of B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics via a two-step method is illustrated in Fig. 1. The molar ratio of B4C/TiC/B was determined by Reaction (1). First, (B4C–TiB2) precursor powders were synthesized from TiC and B mixture powders via a carbide boronizing process.

Considering the impurity of raw B powders, the actual amount of B powders was 10 wt% more than the calculated amount. The mixture powders were ball-mixed in polythene bottles at 60 r/min for 24 h using absolute ethanol and ZrO2 balls as the mixing medium. The slurries were then evaporated in a rotary evaporator at 65 ℃, followed by evaporation in a drying oven at 90 ℃ for 24 h. Subsequently, the dried mixture powders were sieved with a 200 mesh sieve to avoid powder segregation and agglomeration. The as-received powders were loaded into a stainless steel die and dry-pressed at 30 MPa into a disk with a diameter of 30 mm and a thickness of about 5 mm using a digital tablet press. The disks were loaded into a graphite die and heated in a spark plasma sintering furnace (SPS-20T-10, Shanghai Chen Hua Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China) at 1200 ℃ for 10 min in vacuum with a heating rate of 100 ℃/min. To ensure electric current passage, a uniaxial pressure of 8 MPa was applied. The (B4C–TiB2) precursor powders with different TiC particle sizes were referred to as TCn*. The heated disk was crushed with a mortar and then sieved through a 200 mesh sieve. Subsequently, the as-prepared (B4C–TiB2) precursor powders were mixed with commercial B4C powders in appropriate proportions to form B4C–15 vol% TiB2 composite ceramics. Finally, the B4C–(B4C–TiB2) mixture powders were placed in a graphite mold with an inner diameter of 40 mm, and sintering was done using the same SPS furnace at 2000 ℃ for 16 min at the pressure of 50 MPa in vacuum. The samples were heated to the desired temperature at the heating rate of 100 ℃/min. Meanwhile, a uniaxial pressure of 8 MPa was applied to the graphite punch to ensure that electric current passed through the powders. When the sintering temperature reached 2000 ℃, the applied pressure was gradually increased from 8 to 50 MPa in 1 min. Then, the pressure was maintained until the end of the sintering process. B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics prepared with different particle sizes of B4C powders and TiC powders were referred to as B*m*C*n*. ### 2.2 Characterization

The sintered specimens were ground to remove graphite foil residual and the reaction layer by the diamond grinding disk. The bulk densities of the sintered specimens were determined by the standard Archimedes method. The theoretical densities of B4C and TiB2 are 2.52 and 4.52 g/cm3, respectively, which were used to calculate the theoretical densities of the composites by the rule of mixture based on the calculated composition. The phase compositions were identified by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan). The particle size distribution was measured using a laser particle analyser (ZS90, Malvern, UK). The average grain size was measured from the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images using the linear intercept method. The microstructure was observed by an analytical high-resolution field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, MIRA3 XMU, TESCAN, Czech Republic) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEM-2100F, JEOL, Japan). The testing of electrical resistivity was conducted by a four-probe method instrument (HPS2662, Helpass Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., China) on mirror-polished surface of ceramic samples (40 mm in outer diameter and 2 mm in thickness) at room temperature. The average values of at least 10 tests were reported for each sample to ensure accuracy. The machinability of the B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics was evaluated by MRR using an electrical discharge machine (DK77, Ningbo Zhongyuan Machine Tool Co., Ltd., China). The MRR was determined by the cutting rate (*v*), cutting width (*b*), and thickness (*h*) of the sample using the equation: MRR = *v* × *b* × *h*. The detailed experimental procedures were described in our previous study [25]. The Vickers hardness (*H*V) and fracture toughness (*K*IC) were determined using a hardness tester (HVS, Shanghai Shangcai Testermachine Co., Ltd., China) with an indentation load of 19.61 N and dwell time of 15 s. The fracture toughness was calculated using Evans equation [27]. Ten tests were performed on each sample. The bulk ceramics were machined into rectangular bars (2 mm × 3 mm × 25 mm) for three-point bending tests on an electromechanical universal testing machine (AGS-X, Shimadzu Instruments Manufacturing Co., Ltd., China) with a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min and a span of 20 mm to measure the flexural strength. At least eight bars were tested and five optimum values were chosen to calculate the flexural strength.

### 3.1 Preparation of (B<sub>4</sub>C–TiB<sub>2</sub>) precursor powders

The possible reactions among TiC and B during the reactive sintering process were presented as Reactions (2)–(5):

(2) TiC+6B=B4C+TiB2

(3) TiC+2B=TiB2+C

(4) TiC+1/2B4C=TiB2+3/2C

(5) 4B+C=B4C

According to our previous studies [25,28], the calculated Gibbs free energy (Δ*G*) values for Reactions (2)–(5) are all below 0, indicating that the reactions were all thermodynamically favorable during the sintering temperatures. As known to all, the reaction with lower Δ*G* has higher priority [29]. Reaction (2) was the most preferential with the lowest Δ*G* values. Consequently, Reaction (2) would be activated at first. However, when B was deficient or had a poor match in particle size of TiC. Reaction (3) was more likely to activate than Reaction (2). As a result, minor unreacted TiC and residual C would appear. As the reaction proceeded, Reactions (4) and (5) might occur and contribute to the formation of B4C and TiB2.

Figure 2(a) shows the XRD patterns of the as-synthesized (B4C–TiB2) precursor powders. Particle size measurement in Figs. 2(b)–2(d) indicated the average grain sizes of TC3, TC0.8, and TC0.05 were 1.13, 0.56, and 0.21 µm, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the main phases were B4C and TiB2. The crystallinity of the synthesized TiB2 powders was comparable to that of the standard JCPDS card 35-0741 for TiB2, indicating that TiB2 prepared by *in-situ* reaction of TiC–B had good crystallinity. The degree of crystallinity of TiB2 for TC3, TC0.8, and TC0.05 was 91.58%, 93.41%, and 97.77%, respectively. Reducing TiC particle size was beneficial for obtaining powders with a high crystallinity. The relatively weak characteristic peaks of B4C were attributed to its lower reference intensity ratio (RIR, ~0.4). Remaining TiC was detected in the precursor powders prepared from TiC with sizes of 0.8 and 3.0 µm. Our previous research indicated that B would diffuse into TiC to form fine TiB2 and B4C during the reaction between TiC and B [30]. When the initial TiC powder was micro-sized, the generated nano-TiB2 agglomerated together with TiC as the skeleton, as shown in Figs. 2(e) and 2(g). As a result, a part of TiC was covered, which hindered its further reaction with B. A similar phenomenon was reported in the synthesis of the (B4C–TiB2) precursor powders using TiC and B powder mixtures at 1400 ℃ for 1 h [31]. The average particle size of the resulted powders was nearly 3.0 μm. The smaller particle size of (B4C–TiB2) precursor powders prepared in this study was mainly due to the lower synthesis temperature of 1200 ℃ and short SPS time. No residual TiC was detected in the nano-sized starting TiC situation, implying a complete reaction between TiC and B. The solid phase reaction of TiC–B was a process controlled by diffusion. Although the pulsed current will induce the mass transport of the elements and promote the formation of precursor powders [32], both large particles and low sintering temperatures will result in an incomplete reaction between micro-sized TiC and B. In summary, pure (B4C–TiB2) precursor powders with uniform size distribution were obtained using 0.05 µm TiC and 0.9 µm B as raw materials.

### 3.2 Phase compositions and microstructure of B<sub>4</sub>C–TiB<sub>2</sub> composite ceramics

The phase compositions of the as-sintered B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics are presented in Fig. 4. Diffraction peaks of B4C and TiB2 were detected in all samples. It was worth mentioning that TiC was not detected in the B*m*TC0.8 series and B*m*TC3 series, while it was still present in the TC0.8 and TC3 precursor powders. As previously analyzed, the *in-situ* TiB2 particles were wrapped around the surface of TiC particles to prevent B from diffusing into TiC, causing B powders not being able to fully participate during the reaction of TiC–B. As a result, few B powders were still present in the TC0.8 and TC3 precursor powders. During the sintering process, TiC reacted with residual B and was completely converted into B4C and TiB2. In summary, B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics could be successfully fabricated via a two-step method.

To investigate the influence of particle size matching on the microstructure of B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics, the back-scattering electron (BSE) images of the polished surfaces are shown in Fig. 5. The dark phase shows the B4C matrix, while the white phase is TiB2. Figure 6 shows the average grain size of TiB2 of the as-sintered composite ceramics. The average grain size of TiB2 in B0.5TCn* series was in the range of (0.62±0.21)–(1.75±0.31) μm. When the B4C particle size was 3.12 μm, the average grain size of TiB2 in B3TC*n* series was increased and lay in the range of (0.75±0.28)–(1.91±0.34) μm. Further increased B4C particle size to 10.29 μm, the average grain size of TiB2 in B10TC*n* series was in the range of (0.82±0.23)–(2.28±0.46) μm. Large TiB2 grains started to appear with increasing B4C particle size. It has been confirmed that grain boundary diffusion is the primary mechanism of densification and TiB2 grain growth [33,34]. The composites with larger B4C grains have a lower volume fraction of grain boundaries and TiB2 grains were more likely to contact with each other and grow up. As a result, small TiB2 grains tended to aggregate at the grain junctions and the triple points of large B4C grains in sample B10TC0.05 (Fig. 5(c)). Most TiB2 grains were interconnected and clustered around the B4C grain boundaries, forming a 3D interconnected intergranular TiB2 network. Therefore, interconnected TiB2 grains established an excellent conductive path for the passing of electrical current, which was beneficial to the improvement of electrical conductivity. In comparison, TiB2 grains were randomly distributed in the B4C matrix and impeded the formation of conductive networks in sample B0.5TC3 (Fig. 5(g)). These results indicated that the microstructure of B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics could be effectively modulated by regulating the combination of the initial powders with different particle sizes. It is well known that microstructure plays a critical role in the performance of composites. Therefore, the mechanical and electrical properties of the composites can be modified by particle size coupling of the starting powders.

### 3.3 Mechanical properties of B<sub>4</sub>C–TiB<sub>2</sub> composite ceramics

Figure 10 presents the relative density (RD) of the as-sintered B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics. When the B4C particle size was 0.5 and 3.12 µm, the B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics were highly densified (RD ≥ 98.73%). Further increasing B4C particle size to 10.29 µm led to a slight decrease in the RD value, which was found to lie in the range of 97.45%–98.08% for the B10TC*n* series. This was mainly attributed to the lower surface energy of larger B4C particles, which reduced the sintering driving force and resulted in a decrease in the RD of the composites [36].

As shown in Fig. 11, the Vickers hardness of the Bm*TC0.8 series was in the range of (30.54±0.68)–(32.01±0.51) GPa, which was slightly higher than that of the B*m*TC0.05 series. This was because that the composites with higher density and uniform dispersion of TiB2 particles can release the stress and bear a larger load, thus increasing the hardness [37]. When the TiC particle size increased to 3.0 µm, the hardness of the B*m*TC3 series was decreased in the range of 0.38–0.57 GPa compared to those of the B*m*TC0.8 series. This could be attributed to the coarse microstructure and lower density. Hardness is affected primarily by composition, densities, and grain size. The hardness of the B0.5TCn series was in the range of (30.61±0.45)–(31.24±0.34) GPa. When the B4C particle size was 3.12 μm, the hardness of the B3TC*n* series was increased and lay in the range of (31.29±0.46)–(32.01±0.51) GPa. Further increased B4C particle size to 10.29 μm, the hardness of the B10TC*n* series was decreased in the range of 0.99–1.47 GPa. According to the Hall–Petch relationship, grain size reduction will cause an increase in hardness. In addition, a more uniform microstructure of composites is beneficial to higher hardness. As a result, the B3TC*n* series obtained a higher Vickers hardness than the B10TC*n* series and B0.5TC*n* series owing to higher RD, finer grain size, and uniform microstructure. Considering the standard deviation of the reported hardness values, the particle size matching between B4C and TiC has little effect on the hardness.

Figure 13 shows the fracture toughness of the as-sintered B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics. The fracture toughness gradually increased with the increasing TiC particle size. The variation in fracture toughness was attributed to the following factors. First, larger TiB2 grains result in a stronger closure force on the crack surfaces [42]. It means that crack propagation at a constant crack length will consume more energy, thereby improving the fracture toughness. Second, the residual stress increases with increasing TiB2 grain size, which will induce crack deviation or impedance during crack propagation [14]. With the increasing B4C particle size, the fracture toughness first increased and then decreased. The increase in B4C grain size makes the crack propagation path more tortuous, which can dissipate the crack energy and improve the fracture toughness. When the B4C particle size increased to 10.29 μm, a 3D network structure formed in the B10TCn* series, which resulted in lower residual stress [43]. As a result, the reduction in residual stress reduced the amount of energy expended in crack propagation, thereby slightly reducing the fracture toughness.

To further explore the toughening mechanism of B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics, the magnified images of the crack propagation path in samples B3TC0.05 and B3TC3 are shown in Fig. 14. Due to the mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients of B4C (4.5×10−6 K−1) and TiB2 (8.1×10−6 K−1) [44], resulting in tensile stress on the surface of TiB2 grain and compressive stress on the surface of the B4C grain. The residual stress between B4C and TiB2 grains is around 1 GPa, which is strong enough to break the interface and cause microcracks [45]. A large number of microcracks originally existed and extended, adding many new crack surfaces and absorbing the elastic strain energy, thereby causing the increase in fracture toughness. Toughening mechanisms of crack deflection, crack bridging and crack branching were induced. These crack extension behaviors reduced crack tip stress and increased energy dissipation during crack propagation, acting as a significant impediment to crack extension and thereafter improving the fracture toughness [46]. As shown in Fig. 14(a), the crack propagated along a relatively straight line in sample B3TC0.05. In comparison, the crack propagation path was more tortuous, and the crack width was reduced, as shown in Fig. 14(b), indicating more energy dissipating. Based on this consideration, sample B3TC3 should obtain a higher fracture toughness of 6.57±0.14 MPa·m1/2 than that (5.97±0.27 MPa·m1/2) of sample B3TC0.05.

### 3.4 Electrical properties of B<sub>4</sub>C–TiB<sub>2</sub> composite ceramics

As shown in Fig. 15, the electrical conductivity of the B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics ranged from (0.14–0.15)×104 S/m for B0.5TC*n* series, from (0.61–0.67)×104 S/m for B3TC*n* series, and from (1.42–4.25)×104 S/m for B10TC*n* series. The electrical conductivity increased significantly with the increasing B4C particle size. It is well known that the grain size is inversely proportional to the grain boundary areas. Therefore, the fixed TiB2 content distributed in the B4C matrix with smaller grain boundary areas was more likely to form a 3D conductive network, which greatly promoted electron passing by providing perfectly intergranular conductive pathways. When the B4C particle size was 0.5 and 3.12 µm, the variation of TiC particle size hardly affected the electrical conductivity. This was mainly due to TiB2 grains randomly distributed in the B4C matrix and unable to form continuous conductive pathways. However, when the B4C particle size was 10.29 µm, the electrical conductivity significantly increased to 1.42×104, 2.45×104, and 4.25×104 S/m for samples B10TC3, B10TC0.8, and B10TC0.05, respectively, with the highest increase rate above 5 times compared with those of B3TC*n* series. Since the volume content of TiB2 was fixed, a smaller grain size means a much more abundant number of grains. Therefore, smaller TiB2 grains were easier to connect with each other, providing more conductive channels and improving the electrical conductivity. Huang *et al.* [47] used B4C and TiB2 powders as raw materials to fabricate B4C‒20 vol% TiB2 composite via pulsed electric current sintering at 2000 ℃. The composite had an electrical conductivity of 6.73×103 S/m. Wang *et al.* [48] prepared B4C–TiB2 composites with 10–20 vol% TiB2 by hot pressing using B4C and TiB2 powders as raw materials. The electrical conductivity of as-sintered composites was in the range of (0.46–1.95)×103 S/m. In this study, the electrical conductivity of the B4C‒15 vol% TiB2 composites was maximized to 4.25×104 S/m due to the highly interconnected intergranular TiB2 network. These results indicate that the SMGG is an effective strategy to achieve excellent electrical conductivity of the composite ceramics.

The MRR values of the Bm*TC0.05 series were measured to evaluate the machinability of B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics. As shown in Fig. 16, the sample B10TC0.05 exhibited excellent machinability and the processing accuracy was relatively high. As shown in Fig. 16(d), when the B4C particle size was 0.5 µm, the MRR was 6.63 mm3/min. With increasing B4C particle size to 3.12 and 10.29 µm, the MRR increased to 7.21 and 8.68 mm3/min, respectively. The electrical conductivity of pure B4C ceramic is 120 S/m with a relatively low MRR of only 0.578 mm3/min [49]. By introducing TiB2 into B4C matrix, the machinability of as-sintered composites was highly improved owing to the remarkably enhanced electrical conductivity. The maximum electrical conductivity and MRR achieved 2.79×104 S/m and 8.22 mm3/min in the previous B4C–15 vol% TiB2 composite fabricated via a one-step method, which was lower than those of sample B10TC0.05 [25]. This may be attributed to the smaller grain size of TiB2 and diminished TiB2 agglomeration, resulting in the formation of a more perfect conductive network. These results demonstrate that the B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics prepared by the SMGG method in this work with high electrical conductivity are beneficial to machining complex shape parts.

## 4 Conclusions

B4C–15 vol% TiB2 composite ceramics were successfully prepared via the SMGG strategy with commercial B4C, TiC, and amorphous B powders as raw materials. First, B4C–TiB2 precursor powders were designed and synthesized from boronizing process of TiC at 1200 ℃ to eliminate incomplete reactions. Then the as-synthesized B4C–TiB2 precursor powders were densified with B4C to obtain B4C–TiB2 composites by SPS at 2000 ℃. During the sintering, large B4C grains preferred to grow at the cost of small B4C grains, and residual small TiB2 grains tended to surround large B4C grains to form a 3D conductive network. The electrical conductivity and mechanical properties of the ceramics can be adjusted by matching the particle size of raw B4C and TiC powders. Coarse B4C and ultrafine TiC powders are beneficial for improving electrical conductivity, while the combination of medium sized B4C and TiC powders is more favorable to mechanical properties. The flexural strength, Vickers hardness, fracture toughness, and electrical conductivity of the prepared B4C–15 vol% TiB2 composite ceramics were 629–827 MPa, 30.16–32.01 GPa, 5.75–6.57 MPa·m1/2, and 1.42×103–4.25×104 S/m, respectively.
