## 1 Introduction

With the rapid development of 5G and 6G communication technologies, microwave dielectric ceramics have become essential materials widely used in microwave components such as filters, oscillators, and dielectric antennas [1,2]. To meet the demands of high-frequency wireless communication, microwave dielectric ceramics should possess a low permittivity (*ε*r  50,000 GHz) to achieve low dielectric loss and enhance frequency selection characteristics, and a near-zero resonant frequency temperature coefficient (*τ*f ≈ 0 ppm/°C) to ensure the frequency stability of the device over a broad temperature range [3,4]. The collaborative optimization of these properties is crucial for advancing the next generation of communication equipment, enabling high performance and reliability.

Olivine-type microwave dielectric ceramics, characterized by the general structural formula A2BO4, have demonstrated significant advantages for high-frequency signal transmission, including extremely low loss and high transmission efficiency, attributed to their low *ε*r ( 100,000 GHz) [5,6]. Xiang *et al*. [7] reported an olivine-structured CaMgGeO4 ceramics with a low *ε*r (6.71) and ultrahigh *Q*×*f* (124,900 GHz). However, its relatively large negative *τ*f of −73.7 ppm/°C and the high cost of raw materials limit its practical application. Thus, developing low-cost ceramic materials with ultralow dielectric loss and nearly zero *τ*f value remains an ongoing research challenge. The olivine structure consists of two octahedra ([A(1)O6] and [A(2)O6]) and a tetrahedron [BO4], in which the [A(1)O6] octahedra (4*a* site) are shared edges along the *b*-axis, the [A(2)O6] octahedra (4*c* site) are connected by corners, and the [BO4] tetrahedra fill the gap between [AO6] and [A’O6] [8]. Typically, the cations are disordered in the octahedral position. However, when there is a significant difference in their ionic radii, the ions occupying the A-sites become ordered. Specifically, the A(1) cation, which has a larger radius, tends to occupy the 4*a* site preferentially, whereas the A(2) cation, with a smaller radius, is more likely to occupy the 4*c* site. Daoudi *et al*. [9] reported that an increase in the radius difference between Ca2+ and RE3+ promotes the formation of an A-site ordered structure in gallium-based olivine CaREGaO4 (RE = rare-earth elements), and a high temperature could transform it into a tetragonal perovskite-like structure (K2NiF4 type). Clark *et al*. [10] subsequently prepared an olivine-structured CaYGaO4 with a fully ordered structure. The ordering of the structure significantly impacts the *Q*×*f* value of ceramics, such as spinels [11−13] and perovskites [14,15]. However, the microwave dielectric properties of CaYGaO4 ceramics have not yet been clarified.

Xiao *et al*. [16] reported a series of CaREAlO4 (RE = Nd, Sm, and Y) ceramics with a K2NiF4 structure, for which the *τ*f values can change from negative to positive (−12 to 6 ppm/°C), but the *Q*×*f* values are relatively low (17,980–51,060 GHz). Additionally, Fang *et al*. [17] reported that CaLaGaO4 ceramics undergo an irreversible phase transition from orthorhombic olivine to tetragonal K2NiF4 structure when sintered above 1260 °C, resulting in a decrease in *Q*×*f* from 52,300 to 32,500 GHz and an increase in *τ*f from −50.5 to 2.3 ppm/°C. Therefore, on the basis of the structural characteristics and performance advantages of orthorhombic olivine and tetragonal K2NiF4 ceramics, it is anticipated that through the ordered and evolutionary design of the structure, A2BO4 ceramics can achieve both ultrahigh *Q*×*f* values and near-zero *τ*f values. In this work, we prepared novel CaYGaO4 ceramics featuring an ordered olivine structure. To explore the structural changes in the CaYGaO4 ceramic, Al3+ (*r* = 0.39 Å, CN = 4) was employed for the substitution of Ga3+ (*r* = 0.47 Å, *CN* = 4), with the expectation of finding the limit of B-site substitution in CaYGaO4-based ceramics, as well as the driving factors for the high *Q*×*f* and near-zero *τ*f. Therefore, CaYGa1−*x*Al*x*O4 (*x* = 0–1) ceramics were prepared via a conventional solid-phase reaction method. The impacts of the phase composition, microstructure, structural evolution, and chemical bonding characteristics of CaYGa1−*x*Al*x*O4 on the microwave dielectric properties were systematically investigated. Additionally, the potential of olivine ceramics (CaYGaO4) for C-band applications was evaluated and validated through the design and simulation of a cylindrical dielectric resonator antenna (CDRA).

## 2 Experimental

The traditional solid-state reaction method was used to synthesize CaYGa1−*x*Al*x*O4 (*x* = 0–1) ceramics. This reagent consists of high-purity powders (99.99%, Aladdin) of CaCO3, Y2O3, Ga2O3, and Al2O3, and the Y2O3 powders were calcined at 1000 °C for 2 h before use. To achieve uniform mixing, the powders were combined with ZrO2 balls and C2H6O in a specific ratio and then milled in a nylon jar for 6 h using a planetary ball mill. The powders were calcined at 1100 °C for 4 h to facilitate the initial synthesis of the target phase and were subsequently ball-milled again. The calcined powders were mixed with 5 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) binder to form pellets, which were pressed into cylindrical molds to obtain several samples (10 mm in diameter and 5 mm in height). Finally, the samples were calcined at 550 °C to remove PVA, followed by sintering at temperatures ranging from 1340 to 1440 °C for 6 h.

A powder X-ray diffractometer (PXRD; X’pert PRO, PANalytical) was used to characterize the phase composition of the materials. The Rietveld profile refinement method was used to analyze the crystal structure of the ceramics using the Fullprof program. A high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM; JEM-2100F) was used to measure high-resolution transmission electron microscopy and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns. Room-temperature Raman spectra were obtained using a laser Raman spectrometer (DXR, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with a 780 nm laser excitation source to analyze the natural surface of the samples. The microstructures of the sintered ceramics were examined by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; S-4800, Japan). The average grain size of the ceramics was determined using the Nano Measurer software. The bulk density of the sintered ceramics was measured using the Archimedes method and compared with the theoretical density obtained via Rietveld refinement. The variations in *ε*r and tan*δ*, which are dependent on temperature, were characterized at frequencies ranging from 5 kHz to 1 MHz using a dielectric measurement system (DMS-300, Partulab, China). The DC resistivity of the ceramics across a range of temperatures was assessed with a high-temperature resistivity measurement system (RMS1000I, Partulab, China). The linear thermal expansion coefficient (*α*L) was evaluated in the temperature range of 50–500 °C using a thermal dilatometer (DIL402C, Netzsch, Germany). The microwave dielectric properties were measured using the TE011 method, which employs a network analyzer (N5230A, Agilent) and a temperature chamber (Delta 9023, Delta Design, Belgium).

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of the CaYGa1−*x*Al*x*O4 (*x* = 0–1) ceramics sintered at optimal temperatures. The optimal sintering temperature for ceramics is determined by changes in density and properties, as illustrated in Fig. S2 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Three-phase regions, including pure CaYGaO4 (orthorhombic, *Pnma*), the composite of CaYGaO4 and CaYAlO4 phases, and pure CaYGaO4 (tetragonal, *I*4/*mmm*), are detected. In the range of 0 ≤ *x* 3+ in CaYGa1−*x*Al*x*O4 is less than 40%. This limitation is due to the significantly smaller ionic radius of Al3+ (*r* = 0.39 Å, CN = 4) than that of Ga3+ (*r* = 0.47 Å, CN = 4) [18]. Within the range of 0.4 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.8, the diffraction peaks (103) and (110) of the tetragonal CaYAlO4 phase (PDF#81-1587) were observed, with their relative intensities increasing as the value of *x* increased. When *x* > 0.8, a pure tetragonal CaYAlO4 phase was obtained.

The Rietveld refinements of the CaYGa1−*x*Al*x*O4 (*x* = 0–1) ceramics are shown in Figs. 1(b)–1(d) and Fig. S1 in the ESM, with the crystallographic structural parameters listed in Tables S1 and S2 in the ESM. Low *R* factors (*R*wp, *R*p, and *χ*2) indicate a close correspondence between the calculated and observed patterns. As *x* increases, the proportion of the orthorhombic olivine phase gradually decreases, the proportion of the tetragonal K2NiF4 phase gradually increases, and the unit cell volume (*V*) gradually decreases, as indicated in Fig. 1(e) and Table S1 in the ESM. On the basis of the crystal structure schematic (Fig. 1(f)), orthorhombic olivine is clearly composed of [CaO6] octahedra, [YO6] octahedra, and [GaO4] tetrahedra, whereas the tetragonal K2NiF4 phase consists of [CaO9] dodecahedra and [AlO6] octahedra with two unequal Al–O bond lengths (2.026 and 1.823 Å). Owing to the smaller ionic radius of Al3+ (0.39 Å for CN = 4, 0.535 Å for CN = 6), it prefers to coordinate with six oxygen atoms to form [AlO6] octahedra instead of [AlO4] tetrahedra. In CaYGaO4, the larger Ca2+ cations typically occupy the A(1) sites, whereas the smaller Y3+ cations fill the A(2) sites (with Shannon radii of 1.00 Å for Ca2+ and 0.90 Å for Y3+) [19]. The charge arrangement between the A(1) and A(2) sites, which correspond to 2+ and 3+ charges, respectively, is crucial for the ordering of gallium-based olivine [20].

Raman spectroscopy was performed to better understand the impact of substituting Al3+ for Ga3+ at the B-site in the phonon mode. Group theory predicts 36 different Raman vibrational modes (11Ag + 7B1g + 11B2g + 7B3g) for orthorhombic olivine (*x* = 0 and 0.2) [21]. As shown in Fig. 2, only 15 Raman-active modes were observed and fitted due to limitations such as the low intensity of some modes or peak overlap. The Raman mode intensity of the *x* = 0 sample is considerably greater than that of the other samples. This could indicate a greater degree of order in the CaYGaO4 structure, as strong and narrow Raman modes are typically associated with ordered structures [13]. On the basis of group theory and a comparison of the Raman spectra of similar structures, it has been determined that vibration modes below 300 cm−1 correspond to external modes, whereas vibration modes above 300 cm−1 correspond to internal modes [22]. The bands at 647 and 682 cm−1 (Ag mode) are related to the stretching modes of two different types of Ga–O bonds in the tetrahedra [23]. Four Raman peaks (330, 374, 419, and 531 cm−1) belonging to the tetragonal K2NiF4 phase appeared with increasing Al3+ substitution (*x* ≥ 0.4) [24], indicating a change in the structure and the coexistence of two crystalline phases. As *x* approached 0.9, the Raman vibration peak of orthorhombic olivine disappeared completely, and only the vibration peak of the tetragonal K2NiF4 phase was detected, which is consistent with the XRD results.

Figure 3 shows room-temperature transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of the CaYGaO4 and CaYGa0.1Al0.9O4 ceramics. Figure 3(b) clearly illustrates the superlattice reflections, demonstrating the ordered distribution of Ca2+ (4*a*) and Y3+ (4*c*) at the octahedral sites in the CaYGaO4 (*x* = 0) ceramic. The lattice fringe of the HRTEM image (Fig. 3(c)) of CaYGaO4 was 3.941 Å for the (111) plane, which is consistent with the XRD data and further demonstrates its orthorhombic olivine structure. For the CaYGa0.1Al0.9O4 (*x* = 0.9) ceramic, the SAED pattern indicates a notable change in its crystal symmetry (Fig. 3(e)). The lattice constants evolve to *a* = *b* ≠ *c*, whereas the interaxial angles remain at 90°, which is characteristic of the tetragonal crystal system [25]. The distance of the (101) plane along the [1* 3¯1] crystal direction, as measured by HRTEM, is 3.511 Å (Fig. 3(f)). This measurement is close to the standard value of 3.487 Å for the K2NiF4-type structure. Additionally, the fast Fourier transform (FFT) and inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT) maps do not show any secondary diffraction rings or defect fringes. The absence of significant lattice distortion confirms the single-phase characteristics of the ceramic.

The microstructures of the CaYGa1−x*Al*x*O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 1) ceramics are nearly pore-free and exhibit high density (Figs. 4(a)–4(g)). As the concentration of Al3+ increases, the average grain size tends to decrease (6.41–4.65 μm). In the range of 0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2, the CaYGa1−*x*Al*x*O4 ceramics exhibit sheet-like grains with an orthorhombic olivine phase. Within the phase evolution region (0.4 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.8), distinct sheet grains of the orthorhombic phase can be observed alongside rod-shaped grains of the tetragonal phase. As the value of *x* increases, the concentration of rod-shaped grains in the tetragonal phase gradually increases. Finally, when *x* ≥ 0.9, the ceramics fully transform into the single tetragonal K2NiF4 phase. Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis (Fig. 4(h)) of the CaYGa0.4Al0.6O4 (*x* = 0.6) ceramics further confirmed that the sheet-like grains belong to the orthorhombic olivine phase and that the rod-like grains belong to the tetragonal K2NiF4 phase in the two-phase region. Figure 4(i) shows the trends in the bulk and relative densities of the CaYGa1−*x*Al*x*O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 1) ceramics as a function of the Al3+ content. As the value of *x* increases, the bulk density of the ceramics increases steadily from 4.23 to 4.53 g/cm3. In parallel, the relative density increases from 95.48% to 98.05%. This high relative density corresponds with the dense microstructures observed in the SEM images.

The values of *ε*r, *Q*×*f*, and density initially increase, then reach a saturation point, and subsequently decrease slightly as the sintering temperature increases, as illustrated in Fig. S2 in the ESM. This behavior is typical and may be attributed to the reduction in the number of pores and the secondary growth of the grains during the sintering process [26]. Figure 5(a) shows the variation in *ε*r with *x* value. The *ε*r of CaYGa1−*x*Al*x*O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 1) steadily increases from 8.3 to 16.2 as *x* increases. The permittivity of ceramics is influenced primarily by two main factors: internal parameters, which include structural properties and polarizability, and external parameters, which involve the presence of a second phase and porosity [27]. However, the effect of porosity can be considered negligible because these ceramics typically have high relative densities exceeding 95% (Fig. 4(i)). As *x* increases, the calculated permittivity (*ε*r(C−M)) trends similarly to the measured *ε*r (Fig. 5(a)). The *Q*×*f* value of CaYGa1−*x*Al*x*O4 decreases from 125,530 (*x* = 0) to 49,490 GHz (*x* = 0.9) and then slightly increases to 50,660 GHz (*x* = 1) with increasing *x* (Fig. 6(a)). The frequency distributions of the test results are presented in Table S4 in the ESM. The lowest *Q*×*f* value obtained at *x* = 0.9 might be related to its proximity to the phase transition point. The fully ordered CaYGaO4 ceramics with an olivine structure presented the highest *Q*×*f* value. The dielectric loss curves of the CaYGaO4 and CaYAlO4 ceramics at low frequencies (5 kHz–1 MHz) show strong temperature stability at lower temperatures, but the loss in CaYGaO4 is lower. Generally, *Q*×*f* is influenced by intrinsic and extrinsic loss factors [32,33]. The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the Raman mode is closely associated with the damping coefficient of the lattice vibration. The FWHM values of the Ag (647 cm−1, orthorhombic olivine) and A1g (419 cm−1, tetragonal K2NiF4) modes increase with increasing *x*, which is opposite to that of the *Q*×*f* mode (Fig. 6(b)). This indicates that the lattice vibration damping coefficient in the tetragonal K2NiF4 structure is greater than that of olivine, resulting in a lower *Q*×*f* for this system than for olivine. Resistivity refers to a material’s ability to generate free carriers that move in response to an applied electric field. A high resistivity reduces leakage conduction loss and enhances the *Q*×*f* value and frequency selectivity [34,35]. Figures 6(c) and 6(d) show the DC resistivity data of CaYGaO4 and CaYAlO4. The DC resistivity of the CaYGaO4 ceramics is 6.2×107 Ω·cm, which is higher than that of the CaYAlO4 ceramics (3.3×106 Ω·cm). Additionally, the conductive activation energy (*E*a) in CaYGaO4 is 1.28 eV, which is also greater than the *E*a (1.21 eV) found in the CaYAlO4 ceramic. This phenomenon corresponds to the relatively high *Q*×*f* value of the CaYGaO4 ceramic.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, a series of CaYGa1−*x*Al*x*O4 (*x* = 0–1) ceramics were synthesized via the solid-state reaction method. The phase composition, structural changes, and factors influencing the microwave dielectric properties were investigated. The crystal structures of the CaYGa1−*x*Al*x*O4 ceramics exhibited orthorhombic olivine structures when *x* ≤ 0.2, whereas tetragonal K2NiF4 structures were observed for *x* ≥ 0.9, based on the XRD, SEM, and Raman spectroscopy data. Complex phases of orthorhombic olivine and tetragonal K2NiF4 structures were detected in the 0.4 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.8 range. Notably, CaYGaO4 (*x* = 0) olivine features a fully ordered structure with larger Ca2+ cations occupying the A(1) site (4*a*) and smaller Y3+ cations filling the A(2) site (4*c*) in the octahedra. By controlling the content of Al3+, orthorhombic material can be transformed into a tetragonal K2NiF4 structure, allowing significant regulation of its microwave dielectric properties (*ε*r = 8.3–16.2, *Q*×*f* = 125,530–50,660 GHz, and *τ*f = −50.9 to 2.9 ppm/°C). Ultralow loss (tan*δ* = 1.38×10−4, *Q*×*f* = 125,530 GHz, *f* = 17.3 GHz) and a near-zero resonant frequency temperature coefficient (*τ*f = −0.5 ppm/°C) were obtained at *x* = 0 and 0.9, respectively. From the TEM, Raman, and P–V–L complex chemical bond theories, it is concluded that the main factors influencing the dielectric properties are ionic order, phonon absorption, and ionic polarization. A CDRA with a resonance frequency of 5.33 GHz (S11 = −25.8 dB) was designed and simulated using a CaYGaO4 ceramic. High radiation efficiency (> 90%) and gain (5.36–6.15 dBi) are achieved within the range of 5.065–5.747 GHz, which satisfies the working requirements of a C-band antenna for 5G communications.
