## 1 Introduction

Since the 2011 accident at the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power plant in Japan, there has been a growing focus on enhancing fault tolerance for accidents in nuclear power industry. One promising approach involves using SiC fibers reinforced SiC ceramic matrix composites (SiCf/SiC) as accident tolerance fuel cladding (ATFC) materials due to their low neutron activity, neutron irradiation resistance, high-temperature strength, high-temperature creep resistance, and excellent thermal conductivity [1,2]. When a loss-of-coolant accident (LOCA) occurs in the pressurized water reactor (PWR), SiC reacts with steam at high temperatures to form a dense oxide film that protects the interior [3]. However, this oxide protective film is difficult to remain stable for long time under normal operating conditions of PWR. SiO2 and Si(OH)4 react with water or hydrolyze to form water-soluble [SiO3]2− and [H2SiO4]2−, respectively [4−8], probably leading to the early failure of SiCf/SiC. Qin *et al*. [9] found that improving the grain size and crystallinity of SiC is an effective way to alleviate the early hydrothermal corrosion of SiC. However, with the increase of the dissolved oxygen (DO) content, the corrosion process of SiC is significantly accelerated. Before the complete oxidation, SiC will delaminate and exfoliate due to grain boundary corrosion.

The researchers considered preparing corrosion mitigation coatings on the surface of SiCf/SiC to reduce its susceptibility to corrosion. Several alloy and compound coating candidates, such as Cr, CrN, TiN, ZrN, NiCr, and Ni, have been investigated [10−12]. However, the results were not satisfactory, as these materials exhibited cracking and loss of corrosion resistance after irradiation, mainly due to weak bonding with the substrate and the oxidation of certain coatings in high DO hydrothermal environments [13,14].

In recent years, Ti3SiC2, a ternary transition metal carbide belonging to the MAX phase family, has garnered attention from nuclear materials researchers. Ti3SiC2 can form a dense oxide layer, including rutile grains, in hydrothermal environments with DO, effectively preventing the infiltration of corrosive media. Moreover, Ti3SiC2 exhibits a very low corrosion rate due to the strong bonding of its TiC6 units [15−17]. On the other hand, it is well known that Ti3SiC2 is better compared to SiC in terms of structural stability and radiation damage tolerance after neutron irradiation, making it an attractive choice for corrosion mitigation coating [2,18−24].

Currently, the research on Ti3SiC2 as a SiCf/SiC corrosion mitigation coating is limited, partly due to the immature preparation techniques. In the past, Ti3SiC2 coatings were commonly prepared using chemical vapor deposition (CVD) [25−27]. However, controlling the phase composition of the coatings through CVD has proven challenging, leading to a decline in relevant studies over the past two decades. Alternative approaches, such as the plasma spray technique using Ti–SiC–C mixed particles or direct use of Ti3SiC2 particles for coatings, have been explored, but issues such as large pores, impurities, thermal decomposition, and oxidation of coatings have hindered their applications [28−31].

When there is a large difference in the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) between the materials used for the coating and SiC, the primary requirement for the coating is a high interfacial bonding strength with the SiC substrate. In our previous study, we successfully prepared Ti3SiC2 interphase for SiCf/SiC using molten salt synthesis (MSS) [32]. The interphase prepared by this process has relatively high bonding strength with the SiC fiber, exhibiting unique toughening mechanisms instead of interface debonding and fiber pulling out. However, compared with the Ti3SiC2 interphase, the Ti3SiC2-based corrosion mitigation coating lacks the protection of the matrix, so it needs higher bonding strength to resist thermal stress and peeling. Therefore, in addition to the Ti3SiC2 monolithic structure, the multi-layered structures containing TiC were designed in the corrosion mitigation coatings to enhance the bonding strength. Building on our understanding of the synthesis mechanism, this work adopted the MSS process to *in-situ* prepare structurally tunable Ti3SiC2-based coatings on the chemical vapor infiltration (CVI) SiCf/SiC. The performance of coatings with different compositions and microstructures was thoroughly evaluated, focusing on mechanical properties, thermal conductivity, and hydrothermal corrosion resistance.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

The stacked 2D SiC fiber flat woven fabric was utilized as the preform, and PyC was used as interphase materials. SiCf/SiC was obtained after the preform was densified by the CVI process [32]. The preparation procedures of the Ti3SiC2-based coatings are as follows: first, put the SiCf/SiC substrate into the corundum crucible and buried the substrate with the raw material powders; second, the crucible was annealed in a tube furnace provided by HF-Kejing; finally, the annealed sample was placed in deionized water for ultrasonic cleaning, to remove residual powders. The raw material powders consist of silicon powders (300 mesh, 99.9%), titanium powders (300 mesh, 99.8%), and NaCl (99.5%). The above three reagents were mixed with ethanol by a horizontal ball milling machine for 24 h. Then the raw material powders can be obtained by drying ethanol and sieving. The annealing process was carried out under the protection of Ar gas. According to our previous work [32,33], Ti and Si can react with the CVI-SiC matrix to form TiC and Ti5Si3C*x* intermediate phases, which can be converted to Ti3SiC2. NaCl as the molten salt can dissolve Ti and Si particles and enhance the diffusion and migration rate of the two types of atoms, which will help to synthesize monolithic Ti3SiC2 below 1300 ℃. ### 2.2 Hydrothermal corrosion experiment

The hydrothermal corrosion experiment was conducted in a high-temperature and high-pressure water environment corrosion test system. The system consisted of a connected circulating water system and an autoclave. To shorten the time required for the corrosion, more severe test conditions than the PWR environment were used: The circulating water system maintained approximately 8000 ppb of DO in high-purity water, with a controlled pH of around 7. Stainless steel wire was used to fix the coated SiCf/SiC samples and control samples in the autoclave simultaneously. All samples were immersed in the high-temperature water environment. The water in the autoclave was heated to 360 ℃ by a temperature control system and pressurized to 18.6 MPa. Corrosion experiments were conducted for 8, 16, and 24 h, to analyze the corrosion process of the Ti3SiC2 coating. According to Zhu *et al*. [17], 24-h corrosion exposure in such ultra-high DO (UHDO) and high-temperature environment corresponds to a corrosion effect equivalent to service of 250 d in the PWR environment.

### 2.3 Characterizations

Morphological characterization of the samples was performed using the Gemini 500 scanning electron microscope (SEM) by Carl Zeiss, equipped with an energy dispersion spectrometer (EDS) by Oxford Instruments to analyze the content of elements. Prior to the SEM characterization, the samples were surface polished using the Tegrapol-25 automatic grinding and polishing machine by Struers. Cross-sectional slice specimens containing the coating and substrate were prepared using a focused ion beam scanning electron microscope (FIB-SEM; Helios G4 CX) by FEI. Phase interface structures in the slice specimens were observed using a double spherical aberration corrected transmission electron microscope (AC-TEM; Themis Z) by FEI.

The tensile bonding strength and shear bonding strength of the coating at room temperature were determined according to GB/T 39685-2020 (the national standard of China). The sample size (length × width × thickness) is 20 mm × 4 mm × 4 mm. Cross-bonding with stainless-steel bars of the same size was achieved using epoxy resin structural adhesive. Both tests were performed using a SANS CMT4304 electronic universal testing machine by MTS with a loading rate of 0.5 mm/min and a bonding area of 16 mm2 for the cross-bonded specimen. The tensile bonding strength and shear bonding strength of the coating sample were calculated using Eqs. (1) and (2):

(1) σt=PtA

(2) τ=PsA

where *σ*t is the tensile bonding strength (MPa), * τ is the shear bonding strength (MPa), P*t and *P*s are the maximum tensile and shear load (N), respectively, and * A is the bonding area (mm2).

The thermal diffusivity of the sample was measured using the LFA1000 laser thermal conductivity meter by LINSEIS. The sample size was ϕ*12.7 mm × 3 mm cylindrical sample. The thermal diffusion coefficient of SiCf/SiC in the stacking direction was measured within a test range from room temperature to 800 ℃, with a heating rate of 10 ℃·min−1 and a test step of 50 ℃, all conducted under the protection of Ar gas. The thermal diffusivity data at different temperatures were used to calculate the corresponding thermal conductivity using the formula:

(3) λ=α⋅ρ⋅cp

where *λ* is the thermal conductivity (W/(m·K)), *α* is the thermal diffusion coefficient (mm2/s), *ρ* is the density (g/cm3), and *c**p* is the specific heat capacity (J/(g·K)).

The flexural strength of the sample at room temperature was tested using a three-point flexural test in accordance with ASTM C1161 standard. The specimen size (length × width × thickness) was 70 mm × 5 mm × 2 mm. The three-point flexural test was conducted using a Model 3345 electronic universal testing machine by Instron Company with a loading rate of 0.5 mm/min and a span of support roll set at 64 mm to avoid interlaminar shear failure of the specimen. Five parallel specimens were prepared for all mechanical tests to obtain standard deviations.

### 3.1 Coating structure design and control

As shown in Fig. 1, SiC was deposited on the surface of Ti3SiC2 ceramics using the CVI process at 1000 ℃. After cooling down to room temperature, interfacial debonding occurred between the two phases, leading to the peeling of CVI-SiC from the surface and coating failure. This experimental result indicates that the thermal mismatch between SiC and Ti3SiC2 is sufficient to cause debonding of the two phases.

*
Fig. 1SEM images of interface debonding because of thermal mismatch between Ti3SiC2 and CVI-SiC: (a) cross section and (b) surface.

To address the thermal mismatch problem between SiC and Ti3SiC2, a TiC layer can serve as a transition layer due to its CTE between those of SiC and Ti3SiC2. Additionally, TiC plays a role in the synthesis of Ti3SiC2, acting as an intermediate phase [33,34]. The above factors make TiC an ideal candidate for alleviating the thermal mismatch issue. Furthermore, considering the potential benefits of improved hydrothermal corrosion mitigation, a multilayer coating with a structure of TiC/Ti3SiC2/Ti5Si3Cx* can be designed. Ti5Si3C*x* is known for its low oxygen atom diffusion rate, enhancing the oxidation resistance of the substrate [35]. However, further research is required to establish its resistance to hydrothermal corrosion.

In accordance with the orthogonal experiments listed in Table 1, sixteen sets of coating specimens with different process parameters are prepared on SiCf/SiC surfaces. The process parameters are adjusted by varying the powder Si/Ti molar ratios, annealing temperatures, and annealing time. After grouping the specimens based on different powder Si/Ti molar ratios, the polished cross-sectional backscattered electron (BSE) photographs were taken and are shown in Fig. 2. The demarcation lines between the phases in Fig. 2 are plotted based on the *Z*-contrast. Qualitative observation of the BSE photographs reveals significant differences in the morphology among the different groups of specimens. Notably, the specimens with a Si/Ti molar ratio of 1.33 : 3 exhibited large-size pores, while the specimens in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b) show small-size pores, and the specimens in Fig. 2(d) are denser. The thickness of each material phase, including TiC, Ti3SiC2, and Ti5Si3C*x*, as well as the total coating thickness, are measured using the scale bar in the images. When performing thickness measurements, EDS elemental semi-quantitative analysis and elemental surface profiles are used to help determine phase boundaries. The representative EDS results are shown in Figs. S1 and S2 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). To ensure reliable data, each set of samples contained two parallel specimens. The thicknesses are averaged over three different locations within a specimen.

*
Fig. 2BSE images of polished cross section of sixteen specimens in orthogonal experiment, grouped according to different powder Si/Ti molar ratios: (a) 0/3, (b) 0.67/3, (c) 1.33/3, and (d) 2/3.

The thickness data of the coatings are summarized in Table 2, representing the average value of multiple measurements. To analyze the effects of three parameters, namely powder Si/Ti molar ratio, annealing temperature, and annealing time, on the five indicators (thickness of TiC layer, thickness of Ti5Si3Cx* layer, thickness of Ti3SiC2 layer, total thickness of coatings, and ratio of Ti3SiC2 layer thickness to total thickness), non-parametric tests are employed. Kruskal–Wallis test statistic is used to analyze the significance of the effect of the powder Si/Ti molar ratio and annealing time, as they have more than two parameter levels. On the other hand, Mann–Whitney test is used for the annealing temperature parameter, which has two parameter levels.

Kruskal–Wallis test results of the Si/Ti molar ratio in the raw material powders on the thickness indexes are presented in Table S1 in the ESM. Among the three indexes (TiC thickness, Ti5Si3C*x* thickness, and total thickness), the *p*-values are less than 0.05, indicating that the Si/Ti molar ratio in the raw material powders has a significant effect on the TiC thickness, Ti5Si3C*x* thickness, and total thickness. Kruskal–Wallis test results of the annealing time on the thickness indexes are shown in Table S2 in the ESM. The *p*-values for all the indexes are not less than 0.05, indicating that the effect of the annealing time on all the thickness indexes is not significant. However, the *p*-values for the Ti3SiC2 thickness (*p* = 0.068) and total coating thickness (*p* = 0.056) are close to 0.05, which suggests that the annealing time may still have some effect on the synthesis of Ti3SiC2 and the total thickness of the coating, considering the possible measurement errors.

Mann–Whitney test results of the annealing temperature on the five indexes are shown in Table S3 in the ESM. The *p*-values for all the indexes are much larger than 0.05, indicating that the effect of the annealing temperature on all the indexes of the coatings does not show significance. The small range of annealing temperatures in the orthogonal experiments (1200 and 1300 ℃) may account for the non-significant results in the test.

In summary, the Si/Ti molar ratio in raw material powders directly influences the thickness of TiC with Ti5Si3C*x*. Within the range of 0/3 to 1.66/3, the median TiC thickness gradually increases with the increase of the Si content, but it decreases to the lowest value when the powder Si/Ti molar ratio reaches 2/3. However, the relationship among the Si/Ti molar ratio and the Ti5Si3C*x* thickness, as well as the total thickness, does not show a simple monotonically increasing or decreasing pattern. Additionally, the annealing time affects the thickness of Ti3SiC2 generated by the reaction between TiC and Ti5Si3C*x*, showing a parabolic relationship. Meanwhile, the annealing time exhibits a monotonically increasing relationship with the total coating thickness. Regarding the annealing temperature, it does not significantly affect the thickness index between the two levels of 1200 and 1300 ℃. Nevertheless, the BSE photographs in Fig. 2 reveal that the coatings have fewer pores at higher annealing temperatures. This feature is particularly evident in the group of four specimens with the Si/Ti molar ratio of 2/3. From the above relationships between process parameters and thickness indexes, it can be observed that there is an apparent interaction effect between Si/Ti molar ratio, annealing time, and temperatures. The structure of the coating is subject to the combined effect of three factors, and the relationship between the single factor and the thickness indexes is not fixed but rather changes with the alteration of other factors.

### 3.2 Structures and bonding strength

Specimens of No. 7, No. 6, and No. 10 in the orthogonal experiments are selected as representatives of the three structures: Ti3SiC2 monolithic coating (TSC), TiC/Ti3SiC2 multi-layered coating (TC–TSC), and TiC/Ti3SiC2/Ti5Si3C*x* multi-layered coating (TC–TSC–TS), respectively. Figure 3 shows their cross-sectional morphology after polishing treatment. The TSC specimen exhibits high density, the TC–TSC specimen has more pores, and the TC–TSC–TS specimen displays a few large-sized pores at the Ti3SiC2/Ti5Si3C*x* phase boundary and small-sized pores at the TiC/Ti3SiC2 phase boundary. TEM-BF images in Fig. 4(a) reveal irregular-shaped pores of around 100–200 nm in size in the TC–TSC–TS specimen, often located at grain boundaries, possibly due to incomplete sintering. Figure 4(b) displays the microstructure of the SiC/TiC phase boundary, indicating the opportunity for a co-lattice interface formation due to the diffusion of Ti atoms into SiC. At both TiC/Ti3SiC2 and Ti3SiC2/Ti5Si3C*x* phase boundaries, a Ti3C2 transition layer is observed (Figs. 4(c) and 4(d)). In contrast, Figs. 4(e) and 4(f) reveal the microstructures of the TSC specimen and the SiC/Ti3SiC2 phase boundary, respectively, with no observable pores and a tooth-like interface profile, suggesting a tooth-meshing structure formation between Ti3SiC2 and SiC at the micro- and nanoscale.

The tensile bonding strength of ZrC, SiC, and MoSi2 coatings by other techniques are considerably lower (around 10–23 MPa [36,37]) than those of the Ti3SiC2-based coatings prepared by MSS in this study. The in-situ* synthesized Ti3SiC2 phases exhibit a tooth-meshing structure with SiC, which partially transformed shear stresses at the boundary into tensile stresses on Ti3SiC2 “teeth” and compressive stresses on SiC “teeth” This stress transformation avoids the shear damage caused by the thermal stress on the SiC/Ti3SiC2 phase boundary. Additionally, the tensile and compressive stress on the “teeth” improve the static friction on the “tooth meshing surfaces”, enhancing the tensile bonding strength of the coating.

Furthermore, the TiC layer between SiC and Ti3SiC2 acts as a stress transition layer, effectively alleviating thermal stress, and thereby improving the bonding strength of the coatings. The TC–TSC–TS specimen demonstrates the most significant effects, with increased tensile and shear bonding strength by 54% and 77%, respectively, compared to the TSC specimen. The presence of more pores in the TC–TSC specimen adversely affects bonding strength, leading to only 31% improvement in the shear bonding strength compared to the TSC specimen and no improvement in tensile bonding strength.

### 3.3 Structures and thermal conductivity

The thermal diffusivity of SiCf/SiC substrates and SiCf/SiC covered with coatings of three different structures were measured from room temperature to 800 ℃, and the thermal conductivity was calculated based on the thermal diffusivity and specific heat capacity, as shown in Fig. 6. It is important to note that the same batch of as-received SiCf/SiC composites are used as the substrate for all specimens with density of 2.80 g/cm3 and open porosity of 4.77%, ensuring consistency in intrinsic thermal conductivity. The thermal conductivities of the two as-received SiCf/SiC specimens are similar at most of the temperature points, except for a difference observed at 800 ℃. Thus, the differences in the thermal conductivity between the different specimens are only affected by the coatings of different structures. The Ti3SiC2 layer with high thermal conductivity characteristics positively impacts the overall thermal conductivity enhancement of the composites. Thus, SiCf/SiC with the TSC coating demonstrates thermal conductivity enhancement of 10%–12% compared to as-received SiCf/SiC.

*
Fig. 6(a) Thermal diffusivity and (b) thermal conductivity of as-received SiCf/SiC and three types of coated SiCf/SiC.

However, the TC–TSC coated specimen shows only a 3% enhancement in the thermal conductivity, while the TC–TSC–TS coating had a negative effect on the thermal conductivity of the substrate. TiC, Ti3SiC2, and Ti5Si3Cx* are all inherently high thermal conductivity ceramic materials, indicating that the effect of different coatings on the thermal conductivity of SiCf/SiC mainly relies on microstructural differences. First, pores in the coatings reduce the effective area for heat flow transfer. Second, the scattering effect of phase boundaries on carriers leads to interfacial thermal resistance. Analyzing the structures of the three coatings shown in Fig. 3, the TSC specimen has lower porosity, and the heat flow from the coating into the SiC substrate needs to pass through only one layer of interface. Therefore, the TSC specimen exhibits the highest thermal conductivity of the three. The TC–TSC specimen, with two layers of interfaces and higher porosity, exhibits lower thermal conductivity than the former one. The TC–TSC–TS coating with three layers of interfaces experiences a complete offset of its intrinsic high thermal conductivity by interfacial thermal resistance, resulting in a negative effect on the thermal conductivity of the composites. Based on thermal conductivity considerations, coatings used for the SiCf/SiC cladding materials should ideally adopt monolithic structures to avoid the adverse impact of interfacial thermal resistance resulting from multi-layered structures on the thermal conductivity of the materials.

### 3.4 Hydrothermal corrosion characteristics

TSC-coated SiCf/SiC samples were utilized to conduct hydrothermal corrosion experiments and subsequent flexural strength tests. Additionally, the coating was piloted on SiCf/SiC nuclear cladding tubes. Figure 7 illustrates the appearance of the samples throughout the process. The SiCf/SiC substrate is initially black. Upon being covered with the TSC coating, it acquires a silver-white appearance with a metallic luster. After the corrosion experiments, the sample turns gray-white, and some powders can be observed falling off the surface when touched. As for the nuclear cladding tubes, the outer layer, inner layer, and end surface are all covered with a silver-white coating.

*
Fig. 7Macro photos of (a) three-point flexural test specimens and (b) cladding tubes.

After subjecting the TSC specimens to corrosion in neutral circulating water with 8000 ppb DO and a temperature of 360 ℃ for 8, 16, and 24 h, the BSE photographs combined with EDS along the thickness direction are presented in Fig. 8. The line-scan spectra of the element O indicate that the left side represents the corroded region of the sample surface. The corrosion layer of Ti3SiC2 is measured to be 0.8, 1.5, and 3.8 μm in thickness, respectively. Analyzing the full spectrum of the point scan and the semi-quantitative results, it can be observed that the Ti3SiC2 phase in the region on the right side of the corrosion layer demarcation line still maintains normal stoichiometric ratios. Conversely, the corrosion layer displays a higher content of oxygen and a lower content of carbon and silicon. These results indicate that the corrosion of Ti3SiC2 in a hydrothermal environment follows the chemical reactions of Eqs. (4)–(9) based on Refs. [16,17]. Specifically, the TiC6 unit reacts with aqueous oxygen to form a solid oxide layer that remains on the specimen’s surface. Additionally, Si atoms are stripped out of the crystals, producing water-soluble Si oxides, while the C atoms recombine to form free carbon or CO.

The microstructure of the TSC specimen after the hydrothermal corrosion of 8 h is depicted in Fig. 9. In the BSE photographs of the polished cross section of the specimen, Ti3SiC2 is observed to cover the surface of the SiC substrate, effectively isolating SiC from the aqueous environment. At the outer side of the Ti3SiC2 layer, there are fragmented oxides formed due to the oxidation of Ti5Si3Cx* present on the surface of the TSC specimen. The presence of a little bit of Ti5Si3C*x* on the surface of the TSC specimen can be attributed to the feature of the MSS process. During the synthesis of Ti3SiC2 by MSS, Ti and Si particles that were not dissolved in the molten salt reacted with each other, resulting in the formation of Ti5Si3 particles. These particles settled and accumulated on the upper surface of the specimen under the effect of gravity. Although most of the Ti5Si3 particles were removed during the subsequent ultrasonic cleaning process, some particles were fixed on the surface of the specimen during annealing and then turned into Ti5Si3C*x*, making it difficult to completely eliminate them [32]. During hydrothermal corrosion for 8 h, Ti5Si3C*x* is fully oxidized, and the oxidation products have a loose structure, making it difficult to form a dense protective film. The above phenomenon indicates that Ti5Si3C*x* does not possess the same resistance to the hydrothermal corrosion as Ti3SiC2, despite having the same elemental species. The TiC6 units in Ti3SiC2, which are ionically bonded, exhibit excellent corrosion resistance. However, the Si atomic layer, which is covalently bonded to the TiC6 units, is the first area where corrosion occurs [17]. In contrast, the crystal structure of Ti5Si3C*x* is primarily composed of covalent bonds, which could be the main reason for its poor resistance to hydrothermal corrosion.

After corrosion of 16 h, the TSC coatings, in general, are not significantly affected, but a phenomenon of UHDO liquid water erosion is observed along the longitudinal cracks, penetrating into the interior of the coatings, as shown in Fig. 10. Analyzing the EDS mapping images, it can be observed that the corrosion depth of the O element in the non-cracked region is about 1 μm, while in the cracked region, the intrusion depth extends to 2–3 μm. Additionally, the distribution of C and Ti elements in the coating region remains homogeneous, while the O and Si elements exhibit complementary distributions. This observation confirms that during the corrosion, UHDO liquid water invades the coating along the cracks, leading to oxidation of the cracked region. Simultaneously, liquid water carries away the Si elements from the oxidized region. It is worth noting that in Fig. 10(b), a laminar texture is observed along the longitudinal direction in Ti3SiC2 grains between the two cracks. Such oriented grains expose the fragile Si atomic layer to the corrosive medium and are therefore more susceptible to corrosion. Besides, this laminar texture is generated when the grains experience tensile stress perpendicular to the basal plane. Considering that the Si atomic layer in Ti3SiC2 serves as an easy water–oxygen diffusion channel, the stress corrosion cracking phenomenon in Ti3SiC2 coatings is formed under the coupling effect of tensile stress and hydrothermal corrosion.

Figure 12 presents the mass change per unit area of the average TSC specimen and SiCf/SiC substrate as a function of corrosion time. Under the same corrosion conditions, the SiCf/SiC substrate exhibits a linear mass decrease, while the TSC specimen shows a linear mass increase, resulting in corrosion rates of −0.0087 and 0.0341 mg/cm², respectively. It is important to note that this constant corrosion rate is evident only during the corrosion period of 8–24 h. The mass change within the first 8 h of corrosion does not follow this linear relationship with the corrosion time. Specifically, the mass of the SiCf/SiC substrate increases, while the mass of the TSC specimen decreases during this early stage of corrosion. The initial non-linear behavior in the mass change can be attributed to the effects of dissolved oxygen and H ions in the hydrothermal environment. UHDO in the neutral water environment accelerates the generation of the solid oxide film during the early corrosion stage, while H ions facilitate the dissolution of the solid oxide film [4,5]. Thus, the use of the UHDO neutral water environment in this study can simulate the long-term corrosion effects on Ti3SiC2 through short-term corrosion experiments, but the corrosion ability of SiC is relatively weaker, leading to a mass gain in the early corrosion period of the SiCf/SiC substrate, which corresponds to the generation of a significant amount of solid oxide film, and a phenomenon also observed in the experimental results of Qin et al*. [9]. The sudden decrease in the mass of the TSC specimen during the early corrosion stage is attributed to the presence of residual unwashed Ti5Si3C*x* on the upper surface of the specimen. After the hydrothermal corrosion of 8 h, Ti5Si3C*x* is completely oxidized, presenting a powdery state that can be easily detached, thereby causing a decrease in the mass of the specimen and resulting in a significant increase in the standard deviation of the mass.

### 3.5 Effect of stress corrosion coupling on strength

Figure 13(a) presents the flexural strength of as-received SiCf/SiC, SiCf/SiC after simple annealing, and SiCf/SiC with TSC coating after the three-point bending test. The simple annealing condition is the same as that of the MSS process, involving holding at 1300 ℃ for 5 h, and the specimens have average density of 2.84 g/cm3 and open porosity of 5.73%. The test results indicate that the flexural strength of the as-received specimens is essentially the same as that of the specimens after annealing, implying that the mechanical properties of SiCf/SiC are not affected by thermal damage under this annealing condition.

However, the flexural strength of the specimen with the TSC coating decreased by 14% compared to the as-received specimen. Since the effect of the thermal damage is excluded, the most likely reason for this reduction is the substrate strength decreased due to the thermal stress. A simplified finite element model of the TSC specimen was constructed to calculate the residual thermal stress distribution in the specimen cross section after the specimen was cooled from 1300 ℃ to room temperature, as shown in Fig. 14. Given that CTE of Ti3SiC2 is about twice that of CVI-SiC, the maximum tensile stress suffered by the Ti3SiC2 coating is about 3.0 GPa, and the minimum tensile stress is about 0.4 GPa, while the maximum compressive stress suffered by the SiCf/SiC substrate is 3.3 GPa. In the real specimen, the internal structure of SiCf/SiC is much more complicated, and while the finite element calculation cannot accurately compute the true value of stress, it does indicate the existence of GPa-level thermal stress around the interface between Ti3SiC2 and the CVI-SiC matrix.

In our previous work, we used the He-Hutchinson criterion analysis to determine that the SiC/Ti3SiC2 interface forms a strongly bonded interface. As a result, cracks tend to penetrate the interface along their original expansion direction rather than deflecting along the interfacial direction when passing through [32]. The bonding strength study in this work also confirmed the robust bonding properties between SiC and Ti3SiC2. Therefore, the tensile stress in Ti3SiC2 could not be released through interfacial debonding. Bao et al*. [38,39] investigated the impact of surface pre-stress on the strength of ceramic materials. They found that compressive pre-stress in the surface layer can prevent cracks from emerging and counteract applied tensile stress, thereby enhancing the strength. Conversely, tensile pre-stress on the surface can add to the externally supplied tensile stress, promoting the crack propagation and reducing the strength.

The residual bending strength and strength retention of the TSC specimens and the as-received SiC specimens as a control group after hydrothermal corrosion with different time duration are shown in Fig. 13(b). The strength of both specimens decreases after corrosion of 8 h, and the strength retention of both specimens is about 78%. With the further increase of the corrosion time, the residual strength of TSC specimens gradually increases, and the residual strength of SiC specimens gradually decreases. Finally, after the hydrothermal corrosion of 24 h, both specimens reach the same residual strength, but the strength retention of the TSC specimen increases to 83%, whereas the strength retention of the SiC specimen decreases to 71%. Figure 15 shows the load–displacement curves of the TSC specimens in the three-point bending test and the fracture morphology of the specimens at the end of the test. The load–displacement curves still have the linear zone in the front section and the nonlinear zone in the back section. Thus, the preparation process of the coating, as well as the hydrothermal corrosion, did not change the original mechanical behavior of SiCf/SiC. In addition, fiber pullout can be observed from the fracture specimen, and the pullout length is large, indicating that the Ti3SiC2 coated SiCf/SiC still exhibits good toughness.

The observed phenomena indicate that, under the current corrosion conditions, the mechanical properties of the SiCf/SiC composites are primarily influenced by structural changes in the coating and matrix regions. Although both TSC and SiC specimens exhibit the same strength retention rate after the hydrothermal corrosion of 8 h, their strength trend diverges significantly in subsequent corrosion, suggesting different underlying reasons for the strength decreasing of the two specimens in early corrosion. The continuous strength decreases in SiC specimens can be attributed to the gradual erosion of the matrix with increasing the corrosion time. In contrast, the TSC specimens experience a sudden strength decrease in the early stage of corrosion, followed by a gradual increase over time. This behavior can be explained through the following deductions: After preparing the Ti3SiC2 coating with strong bonding ability using the MSS process, the surface of the specimens gains a high pre-existing tensile stress. As a result, during the initial 8 h of hydrothermal corrosion, microcracks emerge on the surface of the TSC coating due to the combined effect of stress and corrosion. These microcracks significantly reduce the bending strength of the specimen, acting in conjunction with the surface’s pre-existing tensile stress. As the corrosion time increases, the coating gradually develops penetrating cracks under the influence of stress-corrosion cracking. These cracks release the thermal stresses, resulting in a rebound of the flexural strength due to the release of the surface tensile stress. The number of stress corrosion cracking sites increases, leading to a more comprehensive release of the tensile stress. Additionally, the SiCf/SiC substrate remains protected by the coating and is not subjected to hydrothermal corrosion, contributing to the gradual rise in the flexural strength with increasing the corrosion time.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, structurally tunable Ti3SiC2-based corrosion mitigation coatings were prepared on the surface of SiCf/SiC using the MSS process. The effects of the Si/Ti molar ratio in raw material powders, annealing time, and annealing temperatures on the phase and structure of coatings were investigated. It is found that the powder Si/Ti molar ratio is the main factor affecting the thickness of the two intermediate-phase layers of TiC and Ti5Si3C*x*, and the annealing time is the main factor affecting the thickness of Ti3SiC2. The annealing temperature can affect the porosity of the coating by influencing the sintering rate.

Ti3SiC2 prepared by diffusion reaction can form a strong bond with CVI-SiC matrix, and the tensile bond strength of Ti3SiC2 monolithic coating reaches 26.9 MPa, and the shear bond strength is up to 30.8 MPa. Incorporating TiC as a transition layer at multi-layered coatings further enhances these strength to 41.3 and 51.4 MPa. The bond strength of the three coatings can meet the requirements of typical working conditions.

Ti3SiC2 has high intrinsic thermal conductivity, and Ti3SiC2 monolithic coating enhances the thermal conductivity of SiCf/SiC by 10%–12%. Due to the high interfacial thermal resistance of the multi-layered structure, TiC/Ti3SiC2 coating enhances the thermal conductivity of SiCf/SiC by 3%, while TiC/Ti3SiC2/Ti5Si3C*x* coating reduces the thermal conductivity by 4%.

The 24 h corrosion experiments in the UHDO environment equivalently simulated the corrosion of Ti3SiC2 in service for 250 d under the PWR environment. The thickness of the corrosion layer on the surface of Ti3SiC2 coating is only 3.8 μm, and the internal SiCf/SiC substrate is not corroded under the protection of the coating. In contrast, the Ti5Si3C*x* layer is completely oxidized after only corrosion experiments of 8 h and thus cannot resist hydrothermal corrosion. The coating shows stress corrosion cracking under the coupling of thermal stress and hydrothermal corrosion. However, the stress corrosion cracking releases the residual tensile stress of the coating and increases the flexural strength of SiCf/SiC. The results of the hydrothermal corrosion experiments prove that the Ti3SiC2 coating can play a role in protecting SiCf/SiC, and the strength retention rate of SiCf/SiC under the coating protection after corrosion is 83%, compared with 71% in the control group. Overall Ti3SiC2 monolithic coating has higher thermal conductivity, TiC/Ti3SiC2 multi-layered coating has higher bonding strength, and both have desirable resistance to hydrothermal corrosion.
