## 1 Introduction

Solid-state lasers with high peak and high time-average powers are indispensable tools for numerous fundamental and applied studies [1−3]. As important devices in laser systems, Faraday isolators can control polarization and isolation with high beam quality on the basis of the nonreciprocity of the Faraday effect. The lack of high-performance Faraday isolators is now limiting the advancement of laser technologies and applications [4]. Tb3Al5O12 (TAG) has superior Verdet constant, thermal conductivity, and thermal‒optical properties compared with those of commercial TGG crystals [5−7], which are now dominant in the isolator market. As TAG is an incongruent melting material, the fabrication of TAG crystals with large apertures and high optical quality is rather difficult [8−11]. Transparent TAG ceramics can be fabricated without melting, so TAG ceramics are considered one of the most promising magneto–optical materials for isolators applied in the 400–1500 nm wavelength band.

Research has confirmed that TAG ceramics can be applied in high-power laser isolators [12−15]. To further improve the performance of TAG ceramics, many works have been carried out to reduce the optical loss and increase the size of TAG ceramics [16−21]. Some researchers have identified secondary phases in TAG ceramics, whether the ceramics were fabricated via a solid-state reaction sintering method or nonreactive sintering from coprecipitated powders [22,23]. According to the phase diagram of Tb2O3–Al2O3 [24], the solid solution range of TAG is quite narrow. This implies that a minor deviation in the stoichiometric ratio may result in a significant secondary phase in the obtained TAG ceramics. Thus, in actual experimental and production operations, reaching a completely accurate stoichiometric ratio is quite difficult, secondary phases usually exist and deteriorate the optical quality of TAG ceramic products. Our group reported that Y2O3 can suppress the secondary phase and increase the optical transmittance of TAG ceramics, resulting from the increase in solubility [25]. However, when the amount of added Y was greater than 20 at%, the additives apparently reduced the magneto–optical and thermal properties.

Sc3+ and Tb3+ belong to the same main group, and the difference in the effective ion radius between Tb3+ (1.04 Å) and Sc3+ (0.87 Å) at the 8-coordinate site is ~16%. Therefore, on the one hand, Sc3+ is likely to partially occupy the Tb3+ site in TAG ceramics. On the other hand, Sc3+ can heavily occupy the 6-coordinate Al3+ site in TAG. Tb3Sc2Al3O12 is a material that has been extensively studied [11,26,27], although the difference between the ion radii of Sc3+ (0.745 Å) and Al3+ (0.535 Å) is ~28%. If Sc3+ tends to occupy both Tb3+ and Al3+ sites, it is possible that a slight deviation in the Tb/Al ratio will not result in a secondary phase in TAG ceramics after sufficient Sc3+ is added because Sc3+ might “automatically” fill either of the vacant sites. (Tb1−*x*Sc*x*)3(Al1−*y*Sc*y*)2Al3O12 (TSAG) ceramics were fabricated by Shin-Etsu Chemical Co., Ltd., as shown in their patents [28,29], and they reported that Sc2O3 can improve the optical quality of TAG ceramics. However, a detailed analysis of the mechanism and more characterization are still needed.

In this work, TSAG ceramics with 0, 2, 4, and 6 wt% Sc2O3 additives were fabricated via the solid-state reaction sintering under vacuum combined with hot isostatic pressing (HIP) posttreatment. The phase and crystal structure, sintering kinetics, microstructure, optical transmittance, and magneto–optical Verdet constant of the ceramics were studied in detail. First-principles calculations on TAG ceramics were performed to investigate the formation energy and concentration of anti-site defects (ADs) and Sc substitution. The mechanism by which Sc2O3 eliminates the secondary phases in TAG was investigated by combining experiments and calculations. These results indicate that Sc2O3 is an effective additive for improving the optical quality of TAG ceramics.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

High-purity micron Tb4O7 powder (99.999%, Sheeny Metal Materials Co., Ltd., China), high-purity submicron α-Al2O3 powder (99.99%, Sumitomo Chemical Co., Ltd., Japan), and high-purity micron Sc2O3 powder (99.99%, Shanghai Jingyun Material Technology Co., Ltd., China) were used as the raw powders. The raw powders were weighed according to TAG with additional 0, 2, 4, and 6 wt% Sc2O3 and ball milled in anhydrous alcohol for 15 h. Tetraethyl orthosilicate (0.3 wt%; 99.999%, Alfa Aesar Chemical Co., Ltd., China) was added as the sintering aid. The milled slurries were dried and calcined at 1200 °C for 2 h. The calcined powders were further ball milled in anhydrous alcohol for 15 h with 0.5 wt% polyvinyl butyral (PVB) as a binder. The slurries were subsequently dried, sieved and uniaxially pressed into pellets under 20 MPa and cold isostatically pressed under 250 MPa for 5 min. The obtained green bodies were calcined at 800 °C for 4 h to remove the binder. Then, the calcined green bodies were presintered under vacuum and subjected to HIP in argon. After annealing in a muffle furnace at 1300 °C for 20 h and mirror polishing, transparent TSAG ceramics were obtained.

### 2.2 Sample characterizations

Phase characterizations were performed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany) equipped with a copper target X-ray tube. The scanning rate was 3 (°)/s of 10°–80° for phase identification and 0.35 (°)/s of 10°–110° for structure refinement, and the step size was 0.02°. The compositions, lattice parameters, and theoretical densities of the TSAG ceramics were calculated from the XRD patterns via Rietveld refinements of the crystallographic structure. An X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB 250, Thermo Fisher, UK) was used to characterize the chemical structure of the TSAG ceramics. The obtained binding energy was calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. The microstructures of the thermally etched surfaces were characterized via energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDS) mappings by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; SU-8220, Hitachi, Japan). To characterize the microstructures of the fabricated ceramics, the polished TSAG ceramic samples were thermally etched in a muffle furnace at 1300 °C for 3 h. The average grain sizes of the ceramics were measured via the linear intercept method. The in-line transmittances were characterized with an ultraviolet‒visible–near-infrared (UV‒VIS–NIR) spectrophotometer (Cary-5000, Varian, USA). A transmission optical microscope equipped with two polarizers (BX51TF, Olympus, Japan) was also used to characterize the microstructures of the ceramics. The Verdet constants of the TSAG ceramics at 633 nm were characterized via an extinction method with a Faraday effect experimental instrument (FD-FZ-C, Fudan Tianxin, China). The Archimedes method was used to measure the density of the ceramics. Linear shrinkages of the ceramics were calculated from the volume shrinkage, assuming that the sintering shrinkage of the ceramics was isotropic.

### 2.3 Calculation procedure

First-principles calculations of TAG were performed via the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) code with the projector augmented-wave method and a cell model consisting of 160 atoms [30−32]. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) [33] generalized gradient approximation was used as the exchange and correlation functionals. Convergence of the plane wave basis set energy cutoff (550 eV) and *k*-point sampling (2 × 2 × 2 *k*-point meshes of the Monkhorst–Pack type) [34] were implemented. Tb 4f, 6s, Al 3s, 3p, O 2s 2p electrons were described as valance electrons. All geometry optimizations were performed via a conjugate gradient method with a force tolerance of 0.01 eV/Å. TAG has a cubic crystal structure (space group *Ia*3*d*). The conventional cell contains 160 atoms with Tb in 24c 8-coordinated sites, Al in 16a 6-coordinated, and 24d 4-coordinated sites, and O in 96h sites. We compared all the lattice constants and optimized atomic sites obtained from our calculations with the experimental results in the literature. The difference between the experimental and calculated results is within 2%. In particular, for TAG, the calculated lattice constant is 12.14 Å, which is very close to the experimental value of 12.00 Å [35].

### 3.1 Experimental results

Figure 1 shows the FESEM micrographs and XRD patterns of the mixed oxide powders after calcination at 1200 °C for 2 h and then ball milling. Powders with different amounts of added Sc2O3 have similar morphologies. Starting Tb4O7, Al2O3, and Sc2O3 powders are well mixed and ball milled to submicron sizes. Figure 1(e) shows that after calcination at 1200 °C for 2 h, the powder without the Sc2O3 additive had multiple phases, but no garnet peak was detected. With increasing amounts of Sc2O3, the intensity of the garnet peaks increases. This result indicates that the Sc2O3 additive can promote the solid-state reaction in TSAG, which may be attributed to a higher diffusion coefficient and a lower activation energy of diffusion.

The FESEM micrographs of the TSAG ceramics presintered at 1550 °C for 3 h are shown in Fig. 3. Pores can be seen in all the ceramics with different Sc contents. When the amount of added Sc2O3 was greater than 4 wt%, as shown in Figs. 3(c) and 3(d), no secondary phase was detected. The different phase compositions between the ceramics with and without the Sc2O3 additive may explain the difference in the densification rate during the intermediate sintering stage, as shown in Fig. 2. Figure 4 shows the EDS analysis of the TAG ceramic sample and confirms that the secondary phase is most likely to be Al2O3, which may be derived from the deviation of powder weighing and abrasion from the Al2O3 balls and jars during the ball milling process. This result shows that the Sc2O3 additive can effectively eliminate the Al2O3 secondary phase in TSAG ceramics.

To further characterize the residual secondary phase particles and pores in the TSAG ceramics, an optical microscope was used. The optical microscope micrographs of the TSAG ceramics with 0 and 2 wt% Sc2O3 are not shown because the light is difficult to transmit because of the poor optical quality of these ceramics. Figures 6(a) and 6(c) show that there are numerous scattering points in these ceramics, which are difficult to detect via FESEM. The FESEM detects ~10 nm of the ceramic surface, whereas the optical microscope can detect micron-scale thicknesses inside the ceramics, so the detected volume and the information in one optical transmission micrograph are much greater than those in an FESEM micrograph. Hence, it is not surprising that the pores and secondary phase can be detected by an optical microscope in transparent ceramics, although the magnification is much lower than that of FESEM. After crossing the polarizers under the optical microscope, secondary phase particles can be seen in Figs. 6(b) and 6(d) because of the birefringence of the secondary phase and inhomogeneous areas. The number of secondary phase particles in 6 wt% Sc2O3:TAG ceramics was lower than that in 4 wt% Sc2O3:TAG ceramics. Once again, the results show that the Sc2O3 additive helps to suppress the secondary phases in the TSAG ceramics.

The TSAG ceramics with 6 wt% Sc2O3 possess the highest in-line transmittance of 73.6% at 1064 nm; however, this transmittance is still lower than the theoretical transmittance (~82.6%) because the optical scattering source includes residual pores, secondary phase particles, and inhomogeneous areas. An optimization of the sintering schedule will further improve the optical quality of TSAG ceramics.

To determine how the Sc2O3 additive suppresses the secondary phase, XRD characterizations were conducted. All the TSAG ceramics exhibit a single garnet phase because of the low concentration of secondary phases, as shown in Fig. 7(c). The Rietveld refinement results are shown in Table 1. The nominal composition was obtained by assuming that Sc can occupy both the Tb site and the Al site at a proportion of 3 : 5 and that the occupied Al site is the Al 16a site. The refined occupation of Sc essentially meets our assumption. The refined lattice parameters and theoretical density change with increasing Sc2O3 content, as shown in Fig. 7(d). As the ionic radius of Sc3+ is larger than that of Al3+ and smaller than that of Tb3+, the occupation of different sites by Sc3+ will cause opposite changes in the lattice parameters. When the amount of Sc2O3 was greater than 2 wt%, the lattice parameter tended to increase with increasing amount of Sc2O3. However, the lattice parameter of the 2 wt% Sc2O3:TAG ceramics is slightly lower than that of TAG, which implies that the replacement of Tb3+ by Sc3+ dominates the lattice parameter variation at this concentration. The ratio of ScTb/ScAl (the ratio of Sc ions at the Tb site and the Al 16a site) shown in Table 1 is in good accordance with the changes in the lattice parameters. This result may also explain the evolution of the microstructure shown in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b). As there is excess Al2O3 in TAG, Sc preferentially occupies the Tb site, and the Al2O3 secondary phase is reduced. When the Sc2O3 content further increases, most of the secondary phase particles are eliminated, and the ScTb/ScAl ratio gradually approaches 3 : 5, which is the Tb/Al stoichiometric ratio of TAG. The refined composition also revealed that the Tb/Al ratios were 0.586, 0.582, and 0.598 in the TSAG ceramics with 2, 4, and 6 wt% Sc2O3, respectively. These values deviate from the standard stoichiometric ratio of TAG, which is 0.6. This result confirms that the solid solution range can be increased by the addition of Sc2O3.

The Verdet constant represents the ability of materials to rotate the polarization plane of light and is one of the most important performance parameters for magneto–optical ceramics. For paramagnetic ceramics, the Verdet constant can be calculated as Eq. (2):

(2) V=θBL

where θ* is the Faraday rotation angle, *L* is the sample length along the direction of light transmission, *V* is the Verdet constant, and *B* is the magnetic field intensity. The slopes of the fitting lines in Fig. 9(a) represent the Verdet constants of the TSAG ceramics with different Sc2O3 contents, and the values are shown in Fig. 9(b). As the concentration of magneto–optical ions (Tb3+) decreases with increasing Sc content, the Verdet constant decreases from −188.1 to −161.4 rad·T−1·m−1 at 633 nm. All the values are higher than those of commercial TGG (−136 rad·T−1·m−1) and TSAG crystals (−152 rad·T−1·m−1) [8], and the results show that Sc2O3 is an effective additive that improves the optical quality of TAG and maintains superiority in terms of its magneto–optical properties. In addition, by optimizing the Tb/Al ratio while ensuring high optical quality, the amount of Sc2O3 can be further reduced to obtain TSAG ceramics with high Verdet constants.

### 3.2 Calculation results

To determine the solubility of TAG and the limit of Sc replacement and to further understand the mechanism by which Sc2O3 suppresses secondary phases, first-principles calculations of antisite defects (ADs) and Sc replacement were performed. The calculation process is similar to that of Lu3Al5O12 (LuAG) [42], and Y3Al5O12 (YAG) [43]. The formation energies of ADs and Sc replacement in TAG can be calculated from the total energies of the unit cells according to Eq. (3) [44]:

(3) Ef(defect) =Etot(defect)−Etot(TAG)−∑i=Tb, Al, Scniμi

where *E*f(defect) is the formation energy of the defects, *E*tot(defect) is the total energy of the unit cell containing ADs or Sc replacement, *E*tot(TAG) is the total energy of the perfect TAG, *n**i* is the number of defects involved in the potentials of these species, and *μ**i* is the corresponding chemical potential of related elements (Tb, Al, and Sc), which strongly depends on the experimental conditions. To simplify the calculation, we choose Tb2O3 and Al2O3 as the reference materials for the Tb2O3-dominant and Al2O3-dominant environments, respectively.

The enthalpies of the formation of TAGs from corundum Al2O3 and cubic Tb2O3 were determined via Reaction (4):

(4) 32Tb2O3+52Al2O3→Tb3Al5O12

The enthalpy of the formation Δ*H*f(Tb3Al5O12) is −1.11 eV, which is obtained by calculating the difference in total energy between the products and reactants. The chemical potentials of Tb2O3 and Al2O3 can be determined as Reaction (5):

(5) 32μTb2O3+52μAl2O3= \DeltaHf(TAG)

For the Tb2O3-rich condition, * μAl2O3= 0 eV and  μAl2O3= −0.444 eV; for the Al2O3-rich condition,  μAl2O3= −0.74 eV and  μAl2O3= 0 eV. To ensure the stability of the TAG phase and avoid the formation of other phases, such as Al2O3 or TbAlO3 (TAP), the chemical potential values are limited by Reactions (5) and (6):

(6) 12μTb2O3+12μAl2O3\lt  \DeltaHf(TAP)

(7) μTb2O3≤0 andμAl2O3≤0

The enthalpy of formation of TAP ΔH*f(TAP) is calculated to be −0.178 eV, so the obtained *μ* values meet the requirements given by Reactions (6) and (7).

The formation energies for ADs are calculated from Eq. (3) and are shown in Table 2. TbAl,16a, TbAl,24d, and AlTb are possible ADs in TAG. In both Tb2O3-rich and Al2O3-rich situations, *E*f for TbAl,16a is the lowest. *E*f for AlTb is the highest in the Tb2O3-rich situation, and that for TbAl,24d is the highest in the Al2O3-rich situation. These calculated values are in good accordance with those of other RE3Al5O12 works [42,43,45], in which *E*f of REAl,16a in RE-rich situations is the lowest, with a value of 1.0–2.0 eV. These results indicate that Tb excess is more energetically favorable than Al excess in TAG is, which may partly explain why the secondary phase detected in TAG ceramics was often Al2O3 rather than TAP in this and previous works [22,23,46]. The defect reaction volumes are also shown in Table 2. When the ion radius is *r*(Tb) > *r*(Al 16a) > *r*(Al 24d), the relationship is Δ*V*(TbAl,24d) > Δ*V*(TbAl,16a) > 0 > Δ*V*(AlTb).

The concentrations of the ADs and Sc replacement are determined by Eq. (8) [47]:

(8) cNsite=exp(−EfkT)

where *c* is the defect concentration, *N*site is the number of sites in the lattice, *k* is the Boltzmann constant, and *T* is the fabrication temperature. Figures 11(a) and 11(b) show the calculated fractions of the ADs and Sc replacement in TAG at different temperatures, respectively. The results predict that the percent concentration of TbAl,16a in the Tb2O3-rich situation has the highest value among ADs, which is 0.418% at 2023 K (near the upper limit temperature for the fabrication of TAG ceramics), and this value is smaller than that of LuAl,16a in the Lu2O3-rich situation for LuAG calculated via the same method, which is ~1% at this temperature [42]. When the temperature was 1823 K, the percentage concentration of TbAl,16a in the Tb2O3-rich state decreased to 0.228%. For TbAl,24d, the percent concentration is lower than 0.001% at 2023 K under Tb2O3-rich conditions, indicating that Tb is unlikely to occupy the Al 24d site, which is also in accordance with the XRD refinement. For AlTb in the Al2O3-rich situation, the percent concentration is lower than 0.0005% at 2023 K, and such a value is nearly negligible in practical fabrication processes. At 2023 K, the calculated range of Tb/Al (0.6000–0.6027) in TAG is smaller than that of Lu/Al (0.5998–0.6052) in LuAG [42]. Therefore, the solid-solution range of TAG is relatively small, especially the tolerance of excess Al2O3.

For the Sc replacement, in the Tb2O3-rich situation, the formation energy for ScAl,16a is quite low, so the concentration should be high. However, only one Sc ion replacement in the unit cell was considered during the calculation, so the formation energy may not be very precise for high concentrations, and Eq. (8) is also not applicable under high-concentration conditions. Therefore, a quantitative calculation of ScAl,16a in a Tb2O3-rich situation is not available. However, in the Tb2O3-rich situation, the concentration of ScAl,16a is quite high, and Fig. 11(b) shows that the percentage concentration of ScTb is 2.5% at 2023 K, which is also not negligible. This calculation result implies that if Tb3(Al1−x*Sc*x*)5O12 is prepared, there will be a deficiency in the Al sites because some of the Sc will enter the Tb site, and the TAP phase may be found. A similar phenomenon has been reported by some researchers in the fabrication of TSAG and Lu3(Al1−*x*Sc*x*)5O12 crystals [48,49]. In the Al2O3-rich situation, the percent concentration is 17.9% for ScTb and 24.8% for ScAl,16a at 1823 K. Hence, the ratio of the atoms occupying the Tb site and the Al site ScTb/ScAl is 3.6/5 at this temperature, which is higher than the standard stoichiometric 3/5 in TAG, indicating that Sc can compensate for the deficient Tb, so that the Al2O3 secondary phase can be suppressed. The range of Tb/Al becomes 0.5332–0.9747 at 2023 K after the adequate quantity of Sc is substituted, which is much wider than that in TAG (0.6000–0.6027) calculated from ADs. The calculation is consistent with the experimental results above. The solid-solution range of TAG can be increased, and the secondary phase can be suppressed by Sc doping, regardless of which component is present in excess. Conceivably, Sc2O3 is an effective additive for increasing the stability of the optical quality of ceramics in mass production.

Figure 11(c) shows a comparison between the lattice parameters of the experimental and calculated TSAG ceramics with different Sc2O3 contents. The result shows a consistent changing trend, and the overestimation of the calculated values is a typical issue for DFT calculations with PBE functionals. However, the difference between the calculated and experimental lattice constants is within 1%.

Considering that the difference in the effective ion radius between Tb3+ and Sc3+ (~16% at the 8-coordinate site) is smaller than that between Al3+ and Sc3+ (~28% at the 6-coordinate site), the fact that Sc can heavily occupy the Al 16a site but can only occupy the Tb site in a small amount according to Fig. 11(b) is confusing. To determine the Sc3+ occupation preference, the difference between the electron distributions before and after the replacement of 8-coordinate, 6-coordinate, and 4-coordinate Sc3+ sites for Tb, Al 16a, and Al 24d was calculated, as shown in Fig. 12. The electrons are depleted in the blue region, whereas in the yellow region, the electrons accumulate after Sc3+ replacement. Electrons deplete around O2− sites after Sc3+ occupation of the Tb sites but accumulate after Sc3+ occupation of the Al 16a and 24d sites. This indicates that the ionicity of TAG is reduced after Sc3+ occupation of the Tb sites but enhanced after Sc3+ occupation of the Al sites, which can be attributed to the fact that the electronegativity of Sc (1.36) is lower than that of Al (1.61) and greater than that of Tb (1.1). The decrease in ionicity may imply a greater formation energy for Sc3+ to replace Tb sites.

## 4 Conclusions

Transparent magneto–optical (Tb1−*x*Sc*x*)3(Al1−*y*Sc*y*)2Al3O12 ceramics with 0, 2, 4, and 6 wt% Sc2O3 contents were fabricated via solid-state reactive sintering combined with HIP posttreatment. The solid-state reaction promoted the powder calcination process by Sc2O3. Sc2O3 increases the densification activation energy of TSAG ceramics in the intermediate sintering stage. The microstructures indicated that Sc2O3 was highly effective for removing secondary phases and that the optical quality of the TSAG ceramics improved. Both the experimental XRD results and first-principles calculations confirmed the mechanism by which Sc2O3 increases the solid-solution range of TAG and that Sc can occupy both the Tb site and the Al 16a site at high concentrations. The chemical state of both Tb3+ and Sc3+ barely changed with the amount of Sc2O3 added to the TSAG ceramics, and Tb4+ was hardly detected. The Verdet constant of the TSAG ceramics at 633 nm decreased by 12.6% after adding 4 wt% Sc2O3 and by 14% after adding 6 wt% Sc2O3. TSAG ceramics with both high optical quality and magneto–optical properties were obtained after optimizing the component and fabrication process. The inline transmittance of the TSAG ceramics with 4 wt% Sc2O3 reached 82.2% at 1064 nm and 81.2% at 633 nm. These results demonstrate that Sc substitution significantly benefits the fabrication of TSAG ceramics with both high optical quality and a high Verdet constant.
