## 1 Introduction

In contemporary times, global concerns about surface water pollution have become widely acknowledged, and the necessity for a sustainable ecological environment is increasingly urgent [1,2]. Tetracycline hydrochloride (TC-HCl) is a widely used tetracycline antibiotic, primarily for treating various bacterial infections [3]. However, the persistence of TC-HCl in the environment, particularly in aquatic systems, due to its misuse and improper disposal has become a significant problem [4]. To address these pressing issues, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have emerged as widely studied water treatment methods that utilize powerful oxidizing agents, such as hydroxyl radicals (·OH), to degrade various organic contaminants in water [5−6].

Among various AOPs, the photo-Fenton technology has garnered significant attention [7]. Fenton and photo-Fenton reactions are recognized as effective and promising methods for the degradation of hazardous organic pollutants owing to their straightforward operation, high ·OH radical generation rate, and low cost [8]. However, Fenton-based technology typically requires the external addition of H2O2 and Fe2+ ions, which often leads to incomplete mineralization of organic pollutants and generates significant amounts of iron sludge, hindering its widespread application [9]. Notably, the *in-situ* generation and utilization of H2O2 can greatly mitigate the risks and costs associated with H2O2 synthesis, transportation, and storage. Consequently, developing a Fenton-based process that does not rely on the external addition of H2O2 and Fe2+ but still achieves enhanced degradation and mineralization performance is of considerable importance. Recent studies have shown that the photocatalysis-assisted self-Fenton process can effectively overcome the limitations of traditional Fenton reactions [10]. By integrating photocatalysis with the Fenton process, electrons generated by photocatalysts through light irradiation can effectively reduce Fe3+ to Fe2+, thereby enabling a self-sustaining Fenton cycle [11]. Furthermore, it not only eliminates the dependence on the external addition of H2O2 and strictly acidic conditions but also allows for the continuous generation of ·OH radicals under light irradiation, significantly enhancing the reaction efficiency [12]. By combining the advantages of photocatalysis and Fenton catalysis, this approach achieves more efficient treatment outcomes in environmental remediation, particularly in the degradation of organic pollutants [13].

MIL-100(Fe) is a multifunctional metal-organic framework (MOF) material characterized by its open framework structure and high surface area. It provides abundant active sites conducive to the adsorption of organic pollutants and reaction substances, which could subsequently enhance the efficiency of the photocatalysis-assisted self-Fenton reaction. Moreover, Fe3+ ions in MIL-100(Fe) could be reduced to Fe2+ under light irradiation, which is crucial for the continuous operation of the self-Fenton reaction. However, there are still challenges in the development of MIL-100(Fe) photocatalysts. For example, high-temperature and high-pressure conditions are usually needed during synthesis, leading to high energy consumption [14]. Next, the use of corrosive strong acids such as HF in the preparation process poses health threats and is not environmentally friendly, which limits its potential for large-scale production [15]. Additionally, these materials generally exhibit poor structural stability, especially in humid environments where they are prone to structural collapse or degradation [16]. This instability limits its long-term use in aqueous-phase photocatalytic reactions. Furthermore, the photocatalytic efficiency of MIL-100(Fe) in the visible light region remains constrained because of its limited light absorption range, which hinders its effective utilization of solar energy.

By introducing a second metal ion, bimetallic MOFs could provide possibilities to modulate their band structures and enhance the absorption of visible light to improve their photocatalytic performance, especially under solar light irradiation [17]. Additionally, introducing a second metal ion could create additional sites for trapping electrons or holes, which helps minimize the recombination of photo-generated electron–hole pairs and enhances the separation and migration of charge carriers to promote their effective utilization [18]. Furthermore, bimetallic coordination centers could enhance the structural strength of MOFs to increase their chemical and thermal stability, which would reduce their degradation during photocatalytic reactions [19]. As a result, bimetallic MOFs typically demonstrate superior stability and reusability during prolonged photocatalytic reactions. If the secondary metal ion introduced is a valence-variable metal ion, it may further promote the Fe3+/Fe2+ redox cycle and even improve the photocatalysis–self-Fenton performance through its own participation by enhancing the ·OH production efficiency. However, no systematic investigations on this approach have been reported.

In this work, a series of bimetallic MIL-100(Fe/Ti) and MIL-100(Fe/V) samples were synthesized, incorporating Ti and V as secondary metal ions. The aim was to develop MIL-100(Fe)-based bimetallic MOFs that exhibited enhanced degradation and mineralization capabilities for organic pollutants. An eco-friendly precipitation process at room temperature was developed to synthesize these MIL-100(Fe)-based bimetallic MOFs without the use of corrosive strong acids. Both the MIL-100(Fe/Ti) and MIL-100(Fe/V) bimetallic MOFs exhibited improved performance in removing organic pollutants during the photocatalysis–self-Fenton process under visible light exposure. In particular, the bimetallic MIL-100(Fe/V) MOF photocatalyst, synthesized by doping MIL-100(Fe) with V, exhibited superior efficiency in removing organic pollutants compared with both the MIL-100(Fe) and MIL-100(Fe/Ti) MOF photocatalysts. This was particularly evident in its significantly enhanced performance in treating TC-HCl at a high initial concentration of 100 mg/L. The enhanced TC-HCl adsorption and mineralization effects of the MIL-100(Fe/V) MOF photocatalyst during the photocatalysis–self-Fenton process was found to be directly related to V-doping. More pores and larger surface areas can be created to enhance the pollutant adsorption and subsequent degradation, while the mixed valence states of V4+ and V5+ further enhance the charge carrier separation, participate in the self-Fenton reaction to generate more ·OH, and facilitate the reduction of Fe3+ through a spontaneous redox cycle to accelerate the regeneration of Fe2+ than Ti-doping without mixed valence states. Thus, this work clearly illustrates the performance enhancement mechanism of a second metal with mixed valence states and indicates that it could be a promising approach to create MIL-100(Fe)-based bimetallic MOFs to further enhance their photocatalysis–self-Fenton reaction performance.

### 2.1 Chemicals and materials

1,3,5-Benzenetricarboxylic acid (C6H3(CO2H)3), ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4·7H2O), titanium sulfate (Ti(SO4)2), and rhodamine B (RhB) were all sourced from Aladdin Biochemical Co., China. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), vanadyl sulfate (VOSO4), and TC-HCl were supplied by Macklin Biochemical Co., China. Deionized (DI) water, with a resistivity greater than 18 MΩ·cm, was obtained from a water purification system (ChengDu Pure Technology Co., Ltd., China). Ethanol (EtOH, 99.7%, Chengdu Colon Chemical Co., Ltd., China) was employed for sample cleaning. All reagents used were of analytical grade and were utilized without further purification.

### 2.2 Sample preparation

Figure 1 schematically presents the MIL-100(Fe) MOF sample preparation process. Initially, 2.1 g of C6H3(CO2H)3 was dissolved in 37.5 mL of 1 M NaOH solution to yield solution A. Meanwhile, a corresponding amount of FeSO4·7H2O (with molar ratios of C6H3(CO2H)3 to FeSO4·7H2O of 1 : 0.5, 1 : 1.0, 1 : 1.5, and 1 : 2.0) was dissolved in 40 mL of DI water to constitute solution B. With a constant pressure separatory funnel, solution B was then gently dropped into solution A at a rate of 2 mL/min at room temperature under continuous magnetic stirring. After a 24 h reaction, the resulting product was separated by centrifugation and then washed sequentially with ethanol and DI water. The sample was subsequently dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h and ground to obtain the MIL-100-Fe*x* samples, where *x* = 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2. Ti- and V-doped MIL-100(Fe) bimetallic MOF samples were prepared with the same procedure, while Ti(SO4)2 and VOSO4 were used to provide Ti and V sources, respectively. The molar ratios of C6H3(CO2H)3 to FeSO4·7H2O+Ti(SO4)2 and of C6H3(CO2H)3 to (FeSO4·7H2O+VOSO4) were both set at 1 : 1.5, and a series of Fe/Ti or Fe/V molar ratios were used to obtain MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti*y* (*y* = 1, 2, 4, and 6) and MIL-100-Fe1.5-V*z* (*z* = 0.5, 1, 2, and 4) samples, respectively; their preparation parameters are summarized in Tables S1 and S2 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Details of the chemicals and materials, material characterization, photoelectrochemical measurements, and photocatalytic decomposition experiments can be found in the ESM.

### 2.3 Sample characterizations

The crystal structures of the samples were investigated using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Empyrean Alpha-1, Malvern Panalytic, the Netherlands) with a scanning rate of 0.02 (°)/s. Energy dispersive X-ray (EDS) elemental analysis was conducted on a scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM; JEM-2100F, JEOL, Japan). The morphology of the samples was examined through a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-7610F, JEOL, Japan). The surface chemical compositions and binding energies were investigated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) on a K-Alpha spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, USA), which utilizes a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV). The surface’s functional groups of the samples were analyzed by measuring the FTIR spectra in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 via a Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, USA). The specific surface area of the samples was determined via nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms and measured with a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) apparatus (Micromeritics APSP 2460 analyzer, USA). The ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) diffuse reflectance spectra (DRS) of the samples were recorded via a UV–vis spectrometer (UV-3600PLUS, Shimadzu Co., Ltd., Japan), with BaSO4 powder serving as the reference material.

### 2.4 Photoelectrochemical measurements

Photoelectrochemical measurements were performed via a CHI 760E electrochemical analyzer (Shanghai Chenhua Instrument Ltd., China), with a 0.2 M Na2SO4 solution employed as the electrolyte, at room temperature. A typical three-electrode setup was utilized, consisting of a working electrode (FTO glass loaded with the photocatalyst sample), a counter electrode (Pt wire), and a reference electrode (saturated Ag/AgCl electrode). The working electrode was fabricated via a slurry coating method. Initially, the photocatalyst sample, acetylene black, and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) were blended at a weight ratio of 8 : 1 : 1, followed by dispersion in N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) to achieve a homogeneous slurry. The resulting slurry was then applied to the surface of an FTO glass with an active area of approximately 1 cm × 1 cm. The coated substrate was dried in air at 60 °C for 12 h to obtain the working electrode. The light source employed was a 300 W xenon lamp (PLS-SXE-300+, Beijing Perfect Light Technology Co., Ltd., China). The photocurrent transient response was recorded by monitoring the open circuit voltage during “on” and “off” light irradiation over a 30 s interval. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed within a frequency range of 0.01 Hz−100 kHz. Mott–Schottky (M–S) analysis was conducted at 2000 and 3000 Hz to determine the flat band potential of the photocatalyst sample.

### 2.5 Photocatalytic decomposition experiments

The photocatalytic performance of the MIL-100-Fe*x* (*x* = 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2), MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti*y* (*y* = 1, 2, 4, and 6), and MIL-100-Fe1.5-V*z* (*z* = 0.5, 1, 2, and 4) samples was initially evaluated based on their degradation of RhB (20 mg/L) under visible light irradiation. In a standard procedure, 4 mg of the sample was first introduced into 40 mL of RhB solution (20 mg/L), and the mixture was magnetically stirred in dark for 60 min to reach adsorption–desorption equilibrium. Next, the suspension was exposed to visible light irradiation using a xenon lamp equipped with a filter allowing wavelengths ranging from 380 to 720 nm. Throughout the reaction, 4 mL of the suspension was withdrawn at 20-min intervals, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 r/min for 5 min. The resulting supernatant (3 mL) was then analyzed using a UV-3600PLUS UV–Vis spectrophotometer to determine the RhB concentration. The MIL-100-Fe1.5, MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti4, and MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 samples were subsequently employed for the degradation of TC-HCl at a high concentration of 100 mg/L. The reaction conditions were identical to those used in the RhB degradation experiment, with the sole distinction being the dark adsorption duration, which was extended to 80 min.

### 3.1 Creation of MIL-100(Fe)-based photocatalysts by a simple precipitation process

The preparation of MIL-100(Fe)-based MOFs typically involves a hydrothermal method [20,21], which necessitates exposure to elevated temperatures during the process. In this work, we found that MIL-100(Fe)-based MOFs could be successfully created through a simple precipitation process at room temperature, which removed the high-temperature treatment and subsequently simplified the synthesis process, reduced energy consumption, and lowered the synthesis cost. Figure 2(a) presents the XRD patterns of MIL-100-Fe*x* powder samples synthesized via the simple precipitation method developed in this study, where *x* = 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2. The characteristic diffraction peaks of MIL-100(Fe) were observed at 2*θ* = 6.3°, 10.2°, 11.0°, and 20.0° in all the MIL-100-Fe*x* samples, which aligns well with those reported for MIL-100(Fe) produced using conventional hydrothermal synthesis [22]. Thus, the XRD analysis results confirmed that pure-phase MIL-100(Fe) could be successfully synthesized via this simple precipitation process at room temperature. Moreover, with increasing Fe2+ to C6H3(CO2H)3 molar ratio, the MIL-100-Fe*x* samples showed a higher diffraction peak intensity, suggesting that a higher Fe2+ concentration could enhance the crystallinity of the sample. Figure S1(a) in the ESM shows the photocatalytic RhB degradation performance of MIL-100-Fe*x* (*x* = 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2) photocatalysts under visible light illumination, which showed that the MIL-100-Fe1.5 sample demonstrated the highest photocatalytic RhB degradation efficiency within 60 min. Thus, the molar ratio of C6H3(CO2H)3 to FeSO4·7H2O/Ti(SO4)2 or C6H3(CO2H)3 to FeSO4·7H2O/VOSO4 was chosen at 1 : 1.5 for the synthesis of Ti or V-doped MIL-100(Fe) bimetallic MOFs.

### 3.2 Morphology, surface area, and pore structure of MIL-100(Fe)-based photocatalysts

Figures 3(a)–3(c) display SEM images of the MIL-100-Fe1.5, MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti4, and MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 samples prepared through a simple room-temperature precipitation method. All the samples retained the characteristic octahedral morphology of MIL-100(Fe) [23,24], suggesting that Ti or V doping had a minimal effect on the structural appearance of MIL-100-Fe1.5. However, notable differences were observed in their grain and aggregation sizes. Specifically, the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample exhibited significantly smaller grain and aggregation sizes than MIL-100-Fe1.5 and MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti4. This implies that the introduction of V into the MIL-100-Fe1.5 framework may hinder the grain growth.

Thus, these MIL-100(Fe)-based MOFs showed strong potential for the photocatalytic degradation of organic pollutants in water systems.

### 3.3 Surface properties and chemical compositions of MIL-100(Fe)-based photocatalysts

Figure 4 presents the FTIR spectra of MIL-100-Fe1.5, MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti4, and MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 samples. The spectra revealed that MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti4 and MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 exhibited stretching vibration peaks similar to those of MIL-100-Fe1.5. Specifically, the peak at approximately 485 cm−1 corresponded to Fe–O stretching vibrations, while the one at approximately 711 cm−1 was attributed to the Fe3O4 structure. The peak near 760 cm−1 was attributed to C–H stretching vibrations in benzene rings, and the peak at ~1378 cm-1 arose from C–O stretching vibrations. Additionally, the ~1450 cm−1 peak was associated with –OH bending vibrations, the ~1629 cm−1 peak corresponded to C=O stretching, and the ~3445 cm−1 peak reflected -OH stretching vibrations [26]. These results indicated that Ti or V doping did not significantly alter the surface functional groups of the MIL-100-Fe1.5 framework.

These diagrams clearly revealed that the photogenerated holes lacked sufficient oxidative potential to interact with water to produce hydroxyl ·OH radicals with strong oxidation properties.

### 3.6 Photocatalysis–self-Fenton degradation and mineralization of high-concentration aqueous TC-HCl by MIL-100-Fe<sub>1.5</sub>-V<sub>1</sub> MOF photocatalyst

Figure S4 in the ESM depicts the photocatalytic performance of the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 MOF for degrading TC-HCl with an initial concentration of TC-HCl of 20 mg/L and a low photocatalyst loading concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. Thus, the initial TC-HCl : photocatalyst weight ratio was 1 : 5, which was higher than that in most previous reports [44−46]. After 80 min of adsorption in dark, ~83% of TC-HCl was removed with such a low photocatalyst loading concentration (~10% of the typical concentration used in Refs. [47,48]). This observation demonstrated that the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 MOF photocatalyst had a superior adsorption ability for TC-HCl in dark, which was beneficial for its subsequent photocatalytic degradation under visible light irradiation. After only ~20 min under visible light irradiation, almost all TC-HCl (20 mg/L) was removed from the TC-HCl aqueous solution. This performance was much better than that in some previous reports [49,50], which indicated that our sample could have good TC-HCl removal performance even when much higher TC-HCl contaminant concentrations were present in aqueous environments.

Figure 8(a) illustrates the TC-HCl removal efficiencies of the MIL-100-Fe1.5, MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti4, and MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 samples compared with that of a control sample (MIL-100-Fe1.5-h) prepared with a conventional hydrothermal method [51]. The experiments were conducted with an initial TC-HCl concentration of 100 mg/L and a photocatalyst loading as low as 0.1 mg/mL. Thus, the initial TC-HCl:photocatalyst weight ratio was as high as 1 : 1, and such a high TC-HCl : photocatalyst weight ratio had not been explored in previous studies. As MOF photocatalysts with high specific surface area values, all of them showed good TC-HCl adsorption performance in dark. After reaching adsorption/desorption equilibrium in dark for 80 min, ~8%, ~9%, and ~11% of TC-HCl was removed from the MIL-100-Fe1.5-h, MIL-100-Fe1.5, and MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti4 samples through adsorption, respectively, which suggested that their TC-HCl adsorption capacitances reached ~80, ~90, and ~110 mg/g, respectively. The MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample showed a much greater TC-HCl adsorption ability. After reaching adsorption/desorption equilibrium in dark for 80 min, ~33% of TC-HCl was removed by the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample through adsorption, which suggested that its TC-HCl adsorption capacitances reached ~330 mg/g. Upon exposure to visible light irradiation, the TC-HCl concentrations consistently decreased for all the samples as the treatment time increased, indicating their photocatalytic degradation capability. After 1 h of irradiation, the removal efficiencies of TC-HCl were approximately 14.5%, 21.2%, 31.7%, and 61.1% for MIL-100-Fe1.5-h, MIL-100-Fe1.5, MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti4, and MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 samples, respectively. These results clearly demonstrated that the MIL-100-Fe1.5 sample synthesized by the simple precipitation process at room temperature had better TC-HCl removal performance than the MIL-100-Fe1.5-h sample synthesized by the conventional hydrothermal process. Both Ti and V doping improved the TC-HCl removal performance of the MIL-100-Fe1.5 MOF photocatalyst, with V doping, particularly with its mixed valence states, offering the most significant enhancement.

These findings suggest that the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample holds considerable promise for practical applications in real water environments.

### 3.7 Active species involved in TC-HCl degradation process, working mechanism, and TC-HCl degradation pathway

To better understand the photocatalytic mechanism of the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample, spin trapping experiments of electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) were performed to identify the active species involved in the degradation of TC-HCl. For this purpose, 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine N-oxide (TEMPO) was employed to capture h+ (in water), while 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) was used to trap ·O2− (in methanol) and ·OH (in water). Figure 9(a) displays the EPR spectra corresponding to the TEMPO-h+ signal, which revealed a significant reduction in the intensity of the characteristic TEMPO peak upon visible light exposure. This finding confirmed the generation of h+ by the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample [58]. Figure 9(b) shows the EPR spectra for the DMPO-·O2− signal, and an increased intensity of the DMPO-·O2− peak was observed under visible light, providing further evidence for the formation of ·O2− by the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample [59]. These results aligned with the expected energy band structure of the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 material.

As shown in Fig. 9(c), strong characteristic DMPO-·OH peaks occurred under visible light illumination, which verified the production of ·OH with a strong oxidation capability (2.27 V vs. NHE) by the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample [60]. Interestingly, its photogenerated holes cannot react with water to generate ·OH radicals, as determined by its relatively low VBM (1.72 V vs. NHE). Thus, this observation suggested that ·OH radicals must be produced by other approaches. Although ·OH could not be directly produced through water oxidation by its photogenerated holes, Fe2+ and V5+ in the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample could facilitate the generation of highly oxidative ·OH radicals through the photo-Fenton reaction [61,62]. Moreover, the V5+/V4+ redox pair effectively facilitated the rapid conversion of Fe3+/Fe2+. Both factors contributed to the significantly greater ·OH generation by the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample than by the other bimetallic MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti4 samples. As illustrated in Fig. 9(d), the intensity of the characteristic DMPO-·OH signal peak produced by the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample was notably greater than that of the MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti4 sample under visible light irradiation with identical test conditions, which clearly indicated that many more ·OH radicals were produced by the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample than by the MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti4 sample.

To examine the degradation effects of these active species, a free radical capture experiment was carried out, and the results are shown in Fig. S7 in the ESM. It clearly demonstrated that the photocatalytic RhB degradation performance of the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample was largely suppressed by the addition of either isopropanol (IPA, the ·OH scavenger) or ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA, the h+ scavenger) and moderately suppressed by the addition of BQ (the ·O2− scavenger). These results clearly demonstrated that both ·OH and h+ played major roles in the strong oxidation capability of the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample, while ·O2− played the minor role. This observation was consistent with the EPR analysis results.

Reactions (4)–(12) summarize the potential processes involved in the photocatalysis–self-Fenton reactions of the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample [62,63]:

(4) H2O+h+→1/2H2O2+H+

(5) O2+e−→⋅O2−

(6) ⋅O2−+e−+2H+→H2O2

(7) H2O2+Fe2+→Fe3++OH−+⋅OH

(8) Fe3++e−→Fe2+

(9) ⋅O2−+V5++2H2O→2H2O2+V4+

(10) Fe3++V4+→Fe2++V5+

(11) V5++H2O2→V4++⋅OH+OH−

(12) V5++e−→V4+

Thus, highly oxidative ·OH radicals were produced by the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample under visible light illumination, during which both Fe2+ and V5+ directly contributed to their effective generation. Therefore, it possessed a strong organic degradation capability through the photocatalysis–self-Fenton process, as demonstrated by its effective removal of high concentrations of TC-HCl in water.

The photocatalytic degradation of TC-HCl by the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample was further examined using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) to identify intermediate products and investigate the degradation pathway. Figure S8 in the ESM presents the LC–MS spectrum of the TC-HCl solution after 1 h of photocatalysis–self-Fenton treatment with the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample. Based on the LC–MS data, a potential pathway for the photocatalytic degradation of TC-HCl under visible light was proposed and is illustrated schematically in Fig. 10. The presence of TC-HCl was indicated by the presence of the substance with mass-to-charge ratio (m*/*z*) = 445, and its degradation process involved several main steps. First, oxidation occurred [64], which led to the cleavage of carbon–carbon double bonds to generate an intermediate product with *m*/*z* = 475. Then, demethylation and deamination occurred [65], which led to the formation of an intermediate product with *m*/*z* = 365. Next, decarboxylation occurred to form an intermediate with *m*/z = 251 [66]. Then, further demethylation led to the formation of an intermediate with *m*/*z* = 181. Finally, these intermediates could be further mineralized into H2O and CO2, along with the formation of some other low-molecular-weight products. Figure S9 in the ESM presents the total organic carbon (TOC) contents of the TC-HCl solution after being treated with the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample for different time intervals. A continuous decrease in the TOC content was observed with increasing treatment time, which clearly demonstrated that the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample effectively mineralized TC-HCl through the photocatalysis–self-Fenton reaction.

## 4 Conclusions

An eco-friendly precipitation process was developed to create MIL-100(Fe)-based bimetallic MOFs at room temperature without the use of corrosive strong acids. Both Ti and V were chosen as secondary metal ion species, and MIL-100(Fe/Ti) and MIL-100(Fe/V) bimetallic MOFs demonstrated enhanced organic pollutant removal performances than the MIL-100(Fe) MOF during the photocatalysis–self-Fenton process under visible light illumination. In particular, the incorporation of vanadium not only increased the porosity and specific surface area of the samples but also accelerated the photogenerated charge carrier separation with its coexisting V4+ and V5+ valence states. Furthermore, these V4+/V5+ species participated in the photocatalysis–self-Fenton process to generate more ·OH radicals and facilitated the Fe3+ reduction to regenerate Fe2+ through a spontaneous redox cycle. Thus, the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample demonstrated an even better photocatalysis–self-Fenton performance than the MIL-100-Fe1.5-Ti4 sample. Its TC-HCl removal rate reached ~3.34 mg/(g∙min) by the photocatalysis–self-Fenton process without the addition of H2O2, which was even higher than that of most reported MIL-100(Fe)-based photocatalysts with the assistance of H2O2 addition. It demonstrated a good structure and performance stability, and it also performed well in a tap water matrix. Furthermore, TC-HCl could be effectively mineralized through the photocatalysis–self-Fenton reaction. Thus, the MIL-100-Fe1.5-V1 sample exhibited the potential for practical applications in real water matrices. These findings demonstrated that the introduction of a second metal species with mixed valence states into MIL-100(Fe) could be a promising approach to form bimetallic MOFs with significantly enhanced photocatalysis–self-Fenton performance for environmental remediation.
