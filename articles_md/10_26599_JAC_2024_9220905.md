## 1 Introduction

The manufacturing of cutting-edge components such as throats or nozzles serving at temperatures above 2500 K in aerospace applications is inseparable from the support of (ultra)high-temperature ceramic. More specifically, transition metal ceramic such as ZrB2 [1−3], NbB2 [4], HfC [5,6], TaC [6], WC [7], etc., and their high-entropy compounds [8,9] play a key role here due to their high melting point, high thermal stability, and high hardness, but common failures such as low machinability, low fracture toughness, low damage tolerance, and low thermal shock resistance also greatly limit their practical applications [10].

Nevertheless, a new class of ternary transition metal ceramic, the MAX phase, may overcome these difficulties [11,12]. In this kind of materials, M represents transition metals, A mostly represents group A elements, and X is carbon or nitrogen. Due to the introduction of A, the MAX phases possess much better machinability [13], greater damage tolerance [14], and greater fracture toughness [15] compared to the MX ceramic, and some of them also have higher oxidation/ablation resistance [16,17], where Ti3AlC2 (TAC) is a typical example [18].

Although it is believed that Ti3AlC2 has potential for use as an anti-ablation material in aero-engines due to its protective TiO2–Al2TiO5–Al2O3 oxidation layer and low linear ablation rate [18−21], potential safety hazards still exist within the first few seconds after the ignition of the aero-engine as well as during the ablation tests for the Ti3AlC2 ceramic when the temperature is below 1400 °C [18]. A superfast temperature increase always occurs, generating super high thermal stress. Once this thermal stress exceeds the ultimate fracture stress of Ti3AlC2, Ti3AlC2 will rupture before exhibiting excellent ablation properties in the ultrahigh temperature range, making the abovementioned advantages unavailable. Therefore, considering the relatively low critical temperature difference of Ti3AlC2 [22], improving the thermal shock resistance is necessary. However, although many studies have investigated the intrinsic thermal shock resistance of different MAX phases [11,22,63,68−76], the methods for improving the thermal shock resistance still lack systematic understanding, which is urgent to clarify.

On the other hand, it is also essential to possess superior strength for the structural material used at elevated temperatures. Acquiring a textured microstructure with a high orientation degree of the MAX phase is an effective way to obtain higher strength [23−31], as well as different solid solutions [32−38] or second phase additions [39] of Ti3AlC2. Although there has been great progress in improving the strength of Ti3AlC2, the research that simultaneously improves the high-temperature strength and thermal shock resistance of the MAX phase is rare, which might be difficult to realize due to Eq. (1) [40,41]:

(1) Rst=σκα2E

where *R*st is the thermal stress crack stability that reflects the thermal shock resistance; *σ* is the strength; *κ* is the thermal conductivity; *α* is the thermal expansion coefficient; *E* is the elastic modulus. An approach that can improve strength may also lead to a higher elastic modulus or lower thermal conductivity, together with the opposite variation trend between the thermal expansion coefficient and the elastic modulus, making it difficult to determine a proper way of improving strength and thermal shock resistance synchronously [42,43].

Among the different potential additions that can achieve the above goals, tungsten (W)-based addition may have enough ability to improve *R*st and meet the above requirements due to its high thermal conductivity, low thermal expansion coefficient, and potential ability to improve the mechanical properties of MAX phases. Zheng *et al.* [44] combined Ti3(AlSi)C2 with W metal and obtained better high-temperature strength through second-phase strengthening; Yang *et al.* [45] used Ti, Al, TiC, and WO3 to fabricate (Ti,W)3AlC2 for the first time with significant Al2O3 and acquired superior mechanical properties at room temperature. Although it may be difficult to confirm the mechanism of the W solid solution strengthening due to the evident strengthening effect of *in situ* Al2O3, it does not influence the conclusion that W is beneficial to the mechanical properties of Ti3AlC2, either as a solid solution or secondary phase.

Based on the survey and analysis above, tungsten carbide (WC), another potential additive incorporated in Ti3AlC2, has not been reported. Thus, in this study, WC with high thermal conductivity and low thermal expansion coefficient was used in combination with Ti3AlC2 to fabricate textured W-containing ceramic of Ti3AlC2 through spark plasma sintering (SPS). The phase evolution and microstructure of the ceramic were similar to those reported by Yang *et al.* [45]. As the W content increased, (Ti,W)3AlC2 formed with better room-temperature mechanical properties. The high-temperature strength was also significantly improved, and better thermal shock resistance of the ceramic was successfully achieved together with well-regulated thermophysical properties. Scanning transmission electron microscopy high-angle annular dark-field imaging (STEM-HADDF) showed that W atoms randomly (disorderly) replaced Ti atoms in the M sites of Ti3AlC2, providing a good reference for the establishment of crystal models, and calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) indicated a greater fracture energy of (Ti,W)3AlC2 at different crystal planes, increasing the resistance to transgranular fracture. Due to the shrinkage of the (Ti,W)3AlC2 lattice, a lower mismatch degree of (Ti,W)3AlC2/Al2O3 at different interfaces was observed, which provided stronger interface bonding compared to that of Ti3AlC2/Al2O3, improving the resistance to intergranular fracture together with the more balanced stress distributions at the interfaces.

The results of the present study may serve as an effective reference for simultaneously improving the high-temperature strength and thermal shock resistance of the MAX phases, providing additional opportunities for use in high-temperature areas or aerospace applications, and the mechanisms revealed may also provide insight into the material design of the MAX phases.

### 2.1 Synthesis of bulks

Ti3AlC2 powder (~10 μm, self-made with Ti3Al powder and carbon black powder [46]) and nanosized WC powder (~0.4 μm, self-made by carbonization of nanosized tungsten powder [47]) were chosen as starting materials. The powders were put into stainless steel jars for 24 h of milling with absolute ethyl alcohol as milling media and cemented carbide as a milling ball, and the ball-to-material ratio was 8 : 1. Then, the wet mixture was put into a vacuum oven for 2 h drying and sealed for safekeeping. Next, the powder was put into graphite dies and sintered by a spark plasma sintering (SPS) system at 1200 °C and 35 MPa for 5 min. Afterward, the as-sintered samples were processed to different sizes and tested.

### 2.2 Test methods

A mechanical property testing system with a heating furnace (SinoTest, DF13T) was used to determine the fracture strength (specimen size 5 mm × 5 mm × 25 mm, including samples for testing room temperature strength, high-temperature strength, and thermal shock retained strength). High-temperature samples and thermal shock samples were heated in air at a 30 °C/min heating rate and held for 5 min at the specified temperatures. Then, the tests began in the furnace or began after quenching in 20 °C water), and the fracture toughness was determined by a single-edge notch beam (SENB; 2.5 mm × 5 mm × 25 mm, the notch was approximately 2.5 mm deep and 0.2 mm wide) using the 3-point bending method with a 20 mm testing span in light of GB/T 6569-2006 together with Refs. [48−53]. All tests above were performed using polished textured top surfaces (TTSs) as the top and bottom, while TSSs (textured side surface) were used as the side surface, and the results were given by the average value of four tests. The elastic modulus was obtained through the nanoindentation method (KLA G200, conducted through GB/T 25898-2010, average value of four tests) at room temperature and the pulse excitation method (RFDA-HT1600, average value of three tests, conducted through GB/T 34186-2017) at high temperatures, where the specimen size was 60 mm × 20 mm × 3 mm; the thermal diffusivity (TD) was measured by the Linseis LFA-1000 instrument with a specimen size of *φ*12.7 mm × 2 mm (average value of five tests), while the thermal capacity (TC) was measured by the Netzsch STA449F3 instrument with a specimen size of *ϕ*5.1 mm × 2 mm. The thermal expansion coefficient (TEC) was determined by the Netzsch DIL-402C instrument with the specimen size of *ϕ*6 mm × 12 mm. The focused ion beam (FIB) equipment used was Thermo-Scientific Helios 5 CX. The apparent density of the specimen was measured by the Archimedes method, and the porosity was measured and calculated by the Image-Pro software on the back-scattered electron (BSE)/energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) image of the specimen; see the details in Fig. S5 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)**.**

### 2.3 Characterizations

A DX-2700 X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Cu Kα radiation) was employed to investigate the phase evolution of the samples with 2*θ* ranging from 5° to 90°, step angle 0.06° and 0.5 s per step. The orientation degree was determined by the Lotgering factor (LF), which can be calculated through Eq. (1):

(2) LF=(P−P0)/(1−P0)

where *P* and *P*0 are the ratios of * ∑I(00l)/∑I(hkl) for the textured samples and standard PDF cards, respectively, and higher f*l indicates a greater degree of texture. A JSM-7900F scanning electron microscope (SEM) was used to observe the morphology of both the TTS and TSS samples under the EDS mode; the working voltage ranged from 2 to 15 kV and the working distance varied from 2 to 10 mm. A Talos F200X transmission electron microscope (TEM), a Talos F200X high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) with selected area electron diffraction (SAED), and a FEI Themis Z double spherical aberration corrected field emission transmission electron microscope with high-angle annular dark field (STEM-HAADF) were employed to determine the crystal structures and interfaces of Ti3AlC2 and (Ti,W)3AlC2. TEM determination, fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern analysis, and stress contribution analysis were performed with the GMS 3 software. A LabRAM HR Evolution Raman microscope was utilized to identify the differences between the vibration models; the phase diagram was calculated through Thermal-Calc software, and the atomic ratios of Ti, Al, C, and W were subjected to Ti3AlC2+WC, which is Ti : Al : C : W = 3 : 1 : (2+*x*) : *x*.

### 2.4 DFT methodology

The Cambridge sequential total energy package (CASTEP) [54] module was chosen to study the formation energies and electron density differences of Ti3AlC2 and (Ti,W)3AlC2. The fracture energies that could further explain the solid-solution strengthening mechanism of (Ti,W)3AlC2 during the transgranular fracture were mainly calculated. Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof generalized gradient approximation (GGA-PBE) was selected as the electronic exchange-correlation functional, and the interactions of electrons with ion cores were represented by on-the-fly generation (OTFG) ultrasoft pseudopotentials, in which the plane-wave cutoff energy, *k*-point separation, and self-consistent field (SCF) tolerance were 550 eV, 0.025 Å−1, and 5×10−6 eV/atom, respectively. All the structures were fully optimized with Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS), and the energy convergence tolerance, maximum force, stress, and displacement were selected to be within 5.0×10−6 eV/atom, 0.01 eV/Å, 0.02 GPa, and 5.0×10−4 Å, respectively. The unit cells were enlarged to 2 × 2 × 1 for the fracture energy calculation along [1000] and 6 × 1 × 1 for [0001]; the different unit cells of 4 × 4 × 1, 3 × 3 × 1, 3 × 2 × 1, and 2 × 2 × 1 were used for calculating the fracture energies of Ti1–Al with one Ti atom substituted by one W atom to simulate solid solutions with different W amounts. In addition, a 3 × 3 × 1 cell with one Ti atom substituted by one W atom was used to calculate the formation energy, and the electron density difference was calculated to reveal the chemical bonding of (Ti,W)3AlC2 and make comparison with that of pristine Ti3AlC2. The fracture energy was defined by splitting the bulk into two complementary parts of surfaces, which was calculated through Eq. (3):

(3) Efra+b=1S0(Eslaba+Eslabb−Ebulk)

where * Efra+b is the fracture energy; S*0 is the surface area;  Eslaba and  Eslabb are the total energies of the two symmetric complementary slabs that are fully relaxed respectively;  Ebulk is the total energy of the bulk units. Three types of bonds existing along the Ti3AlC2[1000] direction were considered: the Ti1–Al layer, Ti1–C layer, and Ti2–C layer (Ti1 represents the outer Ti atoms bonded with Al atoms, and Ti2 represents the inner Ti atoms), and the other two types of bonds existing along the Ti3AlC2[0001] were considered as well. The details of the unit cells and model establishment methods, as well as the specific calculation values, are given in Fig. S2 in the ESM.

### 3.1 Synthesis and characterization of textured bulks

Figure 1 shows the sketch of the experimental procedure. It is widely reported that TAC tends to crash along basal planes due to the weak bonding between Ti and Al, and a flake-like morphology can be easily obtained [11]. When the aspect ratio of the flakes reaches a certain value, the flakes will have a strong tendency to spontaneously stack as poker cards and expose their (000l) planes on the top, which is identified as TTS, exhibiting very high (000l) diffraction peaks; in contrast, TSS will show very poor (000l) diffraction peaks. In such circumstances, the flakes will stack more uniformly when vertical stress is applied, leading to a textured bulk with a high orientation degree (OD) after sintering.

### 3.2 Integrative properties of Ti3AlC2 with WC

The room-temperature properties of Ti3AlC2 with different WC additions are given in Fig. 4. The fracture strength and fracture toughness (Figs. 4(a) and 4(b)) both show the highest values at 5 wt% WC addition, which are 1146.2 MPa and 11.78 MPa·m1/2, respectively, which are both higher than those reported in studies without magnetic field regulation [23,24,31,39,55] and better than those in some reports with magnetic field regulation [27,28,56]. Generally, the strength and toughness of the ceramic exhibit the same trend except for the marked change in grain size [57,58]; thus, both of them show turning points above 5 wt% WC when the solid solution effect is weaker than the increasing and unfavorable effect of TiC [59] together with the decreasing orientation degree caused by the cubic structure of TiC. Vicker hardness and elastic modulus of the specimens increase with increasing WC addition (Figs. 4(c) and 4(d)), which is not only attributed to the increasing TiC hard phase [78] but also may be due to the enhanced chemical bonding of Ti–Al indicated by the DFT calculations (Fig. S4 in the ESM). Additionally, the densities of the specimens also increase in the light of the heavy W, and relative densities of approximately 99% are determined by calculating the porosity of (Ti,W)3AlC2 (Fig. 4(e) and Fig. S5 in the ESM)**.**

The electron density difference of (Ti,W)3AlC2  (Fig. S4 in the ESM) may also support the analysis above, as well as the Raman spectra of the two specimens (Fig. S6 in the ESM), as the greater Raman shifts of different vibration modes in 5WC suggest that the corresponding atoms need more energy to break the atomic bonding and reach the excited states. Figure 5(e) shows the high-temperature elastic modulus curves. Due to the different test methods (the nanoindentation method for Fig. 5(a) and the pulse excitation method for Fig. 5(e)), the elastic moduli of both specimens at room temperature and high temperatures have a small error; a higher value of (Ti,W)3AlC2 is obtained in both methods, and the trends of the elastic modulus with increasing temperature accords well with Ref. [61]. Figure 5(f) shows the thermal expansion curves of TAC (pink) and 5WC (purple). Due to the higher atomic bonding after solid solution treatment, the 5WC specimen has a lower value of *α* = 10.29 μK−1, which is good for thermal shock resistance. Figure 5(g) shows the high-temperature strengths of the TAC and 5WC specimens. The strength values in both kinds of specimens remain almost unchanged below 873 K, and only a small decrease can be found. Above 873 K, the strengths of both the TAC and 5WC specimens clearly decrease with increasing the temperature, but the strength of the 5WC specimen is always greater than that of the TAC specimen in the full temperature range from 298 to 1173 K. Furthermore, based on Eq. (1) in Section 2.2, the *R*st values of both TAC and 5WC with increasing the temperature are shown in Fig. 5(h). Tables S1–S2 in the ESM exhibit the corresponding data for calculating *R*st, and the results indicate that 5WC has a higher thermal shock resistance with a higher *R*st value from 373 to 1173 K. To verify this theory, we heated both specimens to different temperatures, quenched them in 25 °C water, and then measured their retained strength to evaluate the thermal shock resistance, and the results are shown in Fig. 5(i). For the TAC specimen, the retained strength gradually decreases below 673 K and plummets at 773 K, and the critical temperature difference of the thermal shock is slightly greater than that of the anisotropic bulk Ti3AlC2 [62]. When the quenching temperature is above 773 K, the retained strength decreases to a flat stage, which is also consistent with Refs. [62,63]. For the 5WC specimen, the retained strength gradually decreases below 773 K and plummets at 873 K, and the starting temperature of thermal shock is 100 °C higher than that of the TAC specimen. When the quenching temperature is above 873 K, the retained strength of 5WC decreases to a flat stage but is approximately 40–90 MPa (approximately 20%–30%) greater than that of TAC. Despite the lower *R*st value at 298 K, the 5WC specimen with the W solid solution shows better thermal shock resistance than the TAC specimen due to the higher critical temperature difference and higher retained strength, which verifies the theory of a higher *R*st value for the 5WC specimen.

### 3.3 Strengthening/toughening mechanisms of addition of WC to Ti3AlC2: Transgranular fracture

The results above show better mechanical properties of textured (Ti,W)3AlC2, which is consistent with our expectations. However, the underlying strengthening and toughening mechanisms remain unknown, which is highly important and must be revealed, either for explaining the superior mechanical properties or guiding the design of other subsequent materials.

Considering the two basic modes of ceramic fracture, transgranular fracture and intergranular fracture, both modes are observed in MAX phase fracture [26, 64], as shown in Figs. S8 and S11 in the ESM. For the transgranular fracture, considering a textured specimen set at the horizontal plane with TTS as the top and bottom, the fracture surfaces are along or within the range of the [1000] and [0001] directions of Ti3AlC2, which are two extreme cases, as shown in Fig. 6(a), and the core question is whether the solid solution of tungsten atoms strengthens the bonding, or the fracture energy, along [1000] and [0001] [77].

### 3.4 Strengthening/toughening mechanisms of addition of WC to Ti3AlC2 alloys: Intergranular fracture

The critical factor of intergranular fracture is the variation in the mismatch degree before and after the W solid solution at certain interfaces, which determines the variation in the interface strength and further influences the resistance to intergranular crack propagation. For the Ti3AlC2/Ti3AlC2 and (Ti,W)3AlC2/(Ti,W)3AlC2 interfaces, the plane spacings at the interface change simultaneously to the same extent due to the uniform distribution of W in the Ti3AlC2 grains, making little sense for investigating the intergranular fracture. Therefore, the key point should be the mismatch degree of the Ti3AlC2/Al2O3 and (Ti,W)3AlC2/Al2O3 interfaces, and the solid solution changes the lattice parameters of Ti3AlC2 instead of Al2O3, leading to a variation in the mismatch degree and further influencing the interface bonding.

Based on the above discussion, multiple interfaces between Ti3AlC2 (or (Ti,W)3AlC2) and Al2O3 are observed, as shown in Figs. 10(a)–10(d). The corresponding orientation relationships of the interfaces are given in Figs. 10(e)–10(h). In the first case of Figs. 10(a), 10(e), and 10(i), Ti3AlC2(1000) and Al2O3(113) are at approximately 150°; the spacing of Al2O3 (113) is measured as 2.086 Å, and that of Ti3AlC2(1000) is approximately 2.660 Å. Due to the plane angle, the spacing of Al2O3(113) has to be multiplied by a trigonometric function relation, which is 2.409 Å along the Ti3AlC2(1000) direction, and the mismatch degree of the two planes is approximately 9.45%, showing a semicoherent state. After introduction of W, the (1000) planes will shrink by approximately 2.19% based on previous lattice characterization and discussion, which will further generate a spacing value lower than 2.660 Å for Ti3AlC2(1000). By having this lower value, the mismatch degree, in this case, will decrease slightly, and the whole interface will move a little step forward but still move a great deal of distance to the coherent state, which leads to stronger interface binding. Similarly, the 2.11 Å spacing of Al2O3(113) and 2.238 Å spacing of Ti3AlC2(0008) form another semicoherent interface with a mismatch degree of 5.72% (Figs. 10(b), 10(f), and 10(j)); the 2.51 Å spacing of Al2O3(104) and 2.305 Å spacing of (Ti,W)3AlC2(0008) at a 28° angle form a coherent interface with a mismatch degree of 3.83% (Figs. 10(c), 10(g), and 10(k)); finally, as shown in Figs. 10(d), 10(h), and 10(l), the 2.381 Å spacing of Al2O3(110) and 2.339 Å spacing of (Ti,W)3AlC2(0008) form another coherent interface with a mismatch degree of 1.62%. However, some (0008) planes of (Ti,W)3AlC2 have larger spacings compared to Ti3AlC2 due to the distortion at different interfaces with different orientation relationships, and all situations will have lower mismatch degrees when W atoms exist in the Ti3AlC2 lattice, which will reinforce the binding of the relevant interfaces to increase the resistance of the ceramic to intergranular fracture. More observations should be made in the future to verify this theory, but at least based on the orientation relationship observations in this study, the solid solution of tungsten in Ti3AlC2 will surely improve the resistance to intergranular fracture.

### 3.5 Mechanism of improving thermal shock resistance

The mechanism of improving the thermal shock resistance can be well guided by the determination of *R*st in Eq. (1). The nature of the thermal shock can be described as a thermal stress higher than the ultimate fracture stress in a solid, and an effective way to avoid the above results is to improve the ultimate fracture stress or/and decrease the thermal stress.

The ultimate fracture stress, which is also referred to as the fracture strength, is verified to improve due to the W solid solution effect through both intergranular and transgranular methods; for the thermal stress, first, higher thermal conductivity can conduct heat faster to decrease the temperature difference and further lower the thermal stress. At 5WC, the thermal conductivity is lower than TAC at lower temperatures but higher at elevated temperatures, which are determined quantitatively; second, a lower thermal expansion coefficient can decrease the expansion and compression/tension among particles during fast temperature variation, further lowering the thermal stress. At 5WC, a lower thermal expansion coefficient is acquired than that at TAC. Last, a lower elastic modulus may cause the solid to more easily release its thermal stress through elastic deformation. Unfortunately, the higher elastic modulus of 5WC is due to the super-high elastic modulus of W. All the parameters above are quantized to calculate the value of *R*st at different temperatures and obtain the variation curve of *R*st (Fig. 5(h)), which shows a higher value of 5WC and corresponds well with its higher critical temperature difference and thermal shock retained strength (Fig. 5(i)). Thus, the mechanism of improving the thermal shock resistance is determined through the mutual effect of the four parameters above, and the regulation of these different parameters enables 5WC to bear greater thermal stress compared to TAC, leading to better thermal shock resistance.

The flexural strength of the current 5WC seems to be the highest among the recent works without magnetic field regulation, and both the thermal shock retained strength and critical temperature difference of 5WC are evidently improved compared with the current TAC, which are all superior to those of the existing Ti3AlC2 materials marked by full lines in Fig. 13(b).

## 4 Conclusions

(Ti,W)3AlC2 with a small amount of TiC and Al2O3 was successfully synthesized from Ti3AlC2 and WC, and the mechanical properties were evidently improved. A flexural strength of 1146 MPa (298 K)–785 MPa (1173 K), fracture toughness of 11.78 MPa·m1/2, hardness of 5.81 GPa, elastic modulus of 280 GPa (298 K)–228 GPa (1273 K), thermal conductivity of 13.82 W/(m·K) (298 K)–12.92 W/(m·K) (1173 K), and thermal expansion coefficient of 10.29 μK are obtained for 5WC, and higher WC additions lead to a decrease in strength and toughness due to an increase in the hard TiC phase and a decrease in the orientation degree. The thermal shock resistance of the 5WC is also improved, with a 20%–30% increase in the thermal shock strength and an approximately 100 K increase in the critical temperature difference, indicating that 5WC could be used for applications involving rapid temperature variations. The resistance of transgranular fracture is verified to improve through DFT calculations, while the resistance of intergranular fracture is also improved through qualitative analysis of the mismatch degree and stress distribution at interfaces. Together, these two mechanisms contribute to the better strength, better toughness, and, to some extent, better thermophysical properties of 5WC, resulting in better thermal shock resistance of the ceramic.
