## 1 Introduction

Functionally graded materials (FGMs) are among a class of novel materials in which compositions/constituents and/or microstructures gradually vary with volume, resulting in corresponding changes in properties and functions tailored for enhanced performance [1−4]. FGMs can be classified into continuous FGMs, where the transitions are smooth throughout the graded structure, and discontinuous FGMs, wherein compositions and/or microstructures change in a stepwise mode with the presence of an interface [5]. Owing to changes in their composition and structure, as well as the existence of interfaces separating distinct material regions or layers in FGMs, their manufacturing has been challenging [6]. Functionally graded additive manufacturing (FGAM) is a layer-by-layer manufacturing technique that can achieve the desired material composition and organization within a single volume to enable gradational functionalities [7]. Unlike traditional production methods, FGAM can produce freeform structures with customizable site-specific properties at strategic locations [8].

The FGAM of ceramic-based materials remains a complicated issue compared with those of other classes of materials, which often suffer from improper bonding between individual layers and mismatches in the thermal properties of graded compositions during postprocessing [9]. A digital light processing (DLP) system based on photopolymerization is widely utilized in ceramic printing due to its enhanced precision, faster printing speed, and ability to produce smoother surface finishes [10,11]. This technique typically employs a light source with a specific ultraviolet (UV) wavelength to selectively cure the surface of a ceramic slurry, which consists of photopolymerizable monomers and photoinitiators (PIs), within a vat [12,13]. Continuous film-supply DLP technologies have been developed to overcome the challenges of contamination-driven property degradation in FGMs and large amounts of material waste during printing with vat photopolymerization systems [14,15]. Film-type DLP systems have better slurry supply controllability because of the doctor blade module, well-equipped cleaning processes (stamping module), and fast processing [16]. However, irrespective of the system type, all DLP printers expose the ceramic slurry to UV light for photocuring, where light is scattered after it impinges on ceramic particles suspended in the resins, leading to excessive curing beyond targeted regions. This scattering-induced overcuring can not only deteriorate the shape fidelity of the FGM structures but also pose post-processing challenges and cause property degradation in the final part. Therefore, these issues must be addressed in the FGAM of ceramic materials during DLP printing.

Zirconia (ZrO2) ceramics are widely used in dentistry because of their excellent mechanical properties, outstanding resistance to corrosion and high temperatures, good biocompatibility, and nontoxicity to the human body [17−21]. In addition to the mechanical and biological criteria, color and translucency are two other major aesthetic requirements of dental crowns [22,23]. A natural tooth has a gradient appearance in both color and translucency, in that it is yellowish-red with a large opacity at the bottom (cervical), while the top part (incisor) is white and translucent. Therefore, an ideal ZrO2 crown must possess both color and translucency gradation features to satisfy the aesthetic requirements of patients.

Several advancements have been made to achieve optimal color and translucency in ZrO2 crowns; however, these aims have been addressed separately. For color, various shading techniques have been adapted to facilitate the natural tooth-like appearance of ZrO2 crowns. These techniques are applied in one of three ways: (1) Metal oxides are mixed with the starting ZrO2 powder at the production stage to obtain pre-colored green-stage blocks; (2) before sintering, green-stage frameworks are infiltrated with specific coloring liquids; (3) after sintering, zirconia is painted with liners that require firing in a traditional dental ceramic furnace [24]. Techniques (2) and (3) are easy to apply; however, the final shades are often inhomogeneous, fade over time, and can lead to a decrease in the mechanical properties of ZrO2 crowns [25,26]. Hence, doping with metal oxides such as Fe2O3, Bi2O3, CeO2, and Er2O3 to color ZrO2 during the powder stage is preferable [27−29]. Compared with other metal oxides, mixing iron oxide (Fe2O3) with zirconia powder has been studied extensively and found to be significantly more effective in producing natural teeth with minimal deterioration in mechanical properties [30,31].

Similarly, the translucency of ZrO2 has been pursued via several strategies: (1) reducing residual porosity by adding a glassy phase or sintering additive [32], (2) refining the microstructure so that grain boundaries do not interfere with light, (3) increasing the grain size considerably to obtain fewer grain boundaries [33], and/or (4) introducing significant amounts of the cubic phase, which is optically isotropic and does not induce birefringence [34]. Until recently, increasing the doped-yttria content in the ZrO2 powder has tended to occur because of its role in stabilizing the cubic phase at room temperature, which is more translucent than the tetragonal phase of ZrO2. The advantage of the cubic phase is that it has a symmetrical microstructure with the same refractive index (*n)* in all directions, which improves the translucency of the material by transmitting light efficiently [35]. Many studies have reported the potential of 4 mol% yttria-stabilized zirconia (4YSZ) and 5 mol% yttria-stabilized zirconia (5YSZ) for the fabrication of highly translucent dental prostheses [36−39].

In this study, we focused on simultaneously achieving color and translucency gradation in ZrO2 dental crowns and removing the printing and postprocessing challenges in FGM crown fabrication via an in-house-developed film-type DLP system (Fig. 1(a)) that operates via the flashing technique. Color gradation was achieved by controlling the amount of Fe2O3 doped into the ZrO2 powders, and translucency gradation was achieved via three different grades of yttria-stabilized ZrO2 powders (3YSZ, 4YSZ, and 5YSZ). Although few studies have reported color gradation by doping various rare earth oxides [40,41] as well as translucency gradation [42] in dental zirconia, both of these features have been investigated separately; hence, there is a need to combine these gradient characteristics.

By doping the Fe2O3 color pigment into the ZrO2 slurry, the optical properties are significantly changed, and hence, the curability of the slurry is also affected. Colored slurries absorb a large portion of the supplied UV radiation and reduce the curing depth, eventually necessitating curing the slurry for longer curing time to eliminate the risk of layer detachment during printing. Although longer curing time solves the curing depth challenge to some extent, the loss of structural accuracy and material deposition in undesirable regions caused by scattering are unavoidable under extreme printing conditions. Furthermore, excessive curing can result in serious issues during postprocessing and affect the color and translucency parameters of sintered FGM parts. To overcome the challenges resulting from scattering, we implemented a flashing illumination technique to cure the ceramic slurry in stages [43]. Free-radical photopolymerization occurs in three stages: photoinitiation, propagation, and termination [44]. A key characteristic of this process is that light exposure is solely responsible for generating free radicals, whereas polymer-chain growth continues even after the light source is removed (postillumination) [45,46]. During flashing, the flash duration controls radical formation exclusively within the UV-exposed region, restricting its diffusion into unexposed areas due to scattering. Moreover, the intervals between flashes, i.e., off-time flashes, allow for full radical consumption, termination of the polymerization reaction, and the development of a partially solidified layer with lower scattering ability [43,47]. This study is significant because it aids in advancing the FGAM process for ceramic-based FGMs by removing process limitations and enabling three-dimensional (3D)-printing FGM ZrO2 dental crowns with simultaneous color and translucency gradation features that are highly desirable in esthetic dentistry, which has never been reported before.

### 2.1 Materials

Three ZrO2 powders with doped yttria contents of 3, 4, and 5 mol% (3YSZ, 4YSZ, and 5YSZ; TZ-PX-245, TZ-PX-524, and Zpex Smile, respectively, Tosoh, Japan) were used for preparing the ceramic slurries. Iron oxide (Fe2O3) (iron(III) oxide nanopowder, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was used as a pigment. Monomer resins of trimethylolpropane triacrylate (TMPTA) and 1,6-hexanediol diacrylate (HDDA), and a nonreactive diluent, polypropylene glycol (PPG; P 400, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were used. Bis(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl)-phenylphosphine oxide (Irgacure 819; BASF, Germany) was used as the PI, and DISPERBYK-111 (BYK111; Altana AG, Germany) was added as a dispersant.

### 2.2 Slurry preparation and printability

The zirconia powders were milled overnight with various contents of Fe2O3 in anhydrous alcohol (99.9%, Samchun Pure Chemical, Republic of Korea) via 2 mm ZrO2 balls at 280 r/min. The milled suspensions were vacuum dried at 30 °C and sieved with a 56 µm mesh to obtain Fe2O3-doped ZrO2 powders. BYK111 dispersant optimization was performed for 40 vol% slurries of 3YSZ, 4YSZ, and 5YSZ without Fe2O3, and the solid loading was increased to the optimized BYK111 amount. The resin system of the ceramic slurry was composed of HDDA and TMPTA monomers (weight ratio of 3 : 17), a PPG diluent, and PI (Irgacure 819), which constituted 20% and 0.8% of the total resin, respectively. Slurries were homogeneously mixed for 10 min in a planetary mixer (ARE-310, THINKY, USA) at 2000 r/min. The curability of all groups of slurries based on varying Fe2O3 content (S1 to S7) (Table 1) was checked via an in-house-built film-type DLP system operated at a wavelength of 405 nm and an intensity of 30 mW·cm−2 in continuous mode.

### 2.3 Post-processing and color measurements

The printed disc samples were debinded in a nitrogen (N2) atmosphere up to 600 °C to remove binder elements based on thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) results. All brown bodies were air-sintered at 1500 °C for 2 h. Color data were recorded in terms of the CIELAB coordinates *L**, *a**, and *b**. The color difference (Δ*E**) was calculated using the CIEDE2000 equation to assess the color differences between the samples (Eq. (1)) [48].

(1) ΔE∗=(ΔL∗)2+(Δa∗)2+(Δb∗)2

where *L** represents the lightness on a scale of 0 (black) to 100 (white); *a** corresponds to the green‒red axis; *b** represents the blue‒yellow axis.

### 2.4 FGM crown fabrication

To print the FGM crown, a structural accuracy assessment was initially performed by curing the slurry with flash illumination and comparing the results with those of continuously cured layers at the same curing time. A single layer of window-like porous structure with pore sizes of 3 mm × 3 mm, 2 mm × 2 mm, and 1 mm × 1 mm was cured with flashing (0.2 s flash time and 1 s off-time in between), and cure depths as well as geometric overgrowths were measured. An FGM crown with color and translucency gradation was printed according to the composition shown in Fig. 1(b) with a layer thickness of 15 µm through flashing and continuous illumination. The printed crowns were debinded in N2 until 600 °C and air-sintered at 1500 °C.

### 2.5 Characterizations

Samples of all FGM compositions were air-sintered at 1450, 1500, and 1550 °C to define the optimum sintering temperature and impact of Fe2O3 doping. The sintering shrinkage was measured at these temperatures, and the sintered densities were calculated via the Archimedes method (XPE205, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). Flexural bending strengths were measured using a universal testing machine (RB-305 MICROLOAD, R&B, Republic of Korea) based on the ASTM C1161 standard. The microstructures of FGM compositions were analyzed via a low-voltage scanning electron microscope (SEM; Merlin Compact, Carl Zeiss, Germany). The translucency parameter (TP) was calculated via the color differences against ideally black and white backgrounds according to Eq. (2):

(2) TP=(LB∗−LW∗)2+(aB∗−aW∗)2+(bB∗−bW∗)2

where  LB∗,aB∗, and  bB∗ represent color space values over ideally black background, and  Lw∗,aw∗, and  bw∗ represent the respective values taken over ideally white background.

### 3.1 Characterization of raw ZrO<sub>2</sub> powders

Figure 2(a) shows SEM images of 3YSZ, 4YSZ, and 5YSZ powders. Although the primary particle size of all powders was on the nanoscale, large agglomerates formed in all ZrO2 grades, and the magnified SEM images of these agglomerates revealed the presence of nanosized particles in all powders. The presence of these agglomerates can be observed in the particle size analysis (PSA) curves in Fig. 2(b), where the *d*50 values for 3YSZ, 4YSZ, and 5YSZ powders were 0.75, 0.82, and 0.98 µm, respectively. However, the concentration of most particles was less than 0.5 µm. In addition to the 3YSZ and 4YSZ powders, the 5YSZ powder contained approximately 4 wt% binder elements, as shown in the TGA graphs in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Therefore, the 5YSZ powder was calcined at 800 °C for 2 h to remove these binders before slurry preparation. Figure 2(c) shows the XRD patterns of the 3YSZ, 4YSZ, and 5YSZ powders composed of mixed tetragonal zirconia (t-ZrO2) and cubic zirconia (c-ZrO2) phases. However, the cubic phase replaced the tetragonal phase with increasing yttria content of the powders, as clearly observed in the peaks of the 4YSZ and 5YSZ powders.

Material selection was carefully performed to achieve graded features of translucency in ZrO2 dental crowns. We pursued the strategy of introducing significant amounts of c-ZrO2 for translucency control, which is optically isotropic and does not induce birefringence [34]. It offers a similar refractive index in all directions and effectively improves the translucency of ZrO2 by transmitting light. Therefore, we used three ZrO2 powders doped with varying amounts of yttria (3YSZ, 4YSZ, and 5YSZ samples) to achieve graded translucency in the final part.

### 3.2 Optimization of rheological properties

The rheologies of the 3YSZ, 4YSZ, and 5YSZ slurries are shown in Fig. 3. Initially, the amount of BYK111 dispersant was optimized for a 40 vol% slurry of each ZrO2 powder. Figures 3(a)–3(c) show the optimum BYK111 content, where the minimum viscosities were recorded with 3, 4, and 5 wt% BYK111 for the 3YSZ, 4YSZ, and 5YSZ slurries, respectively. At the optimized BYK111 amount, the solid loading was increased to 43 vol% for all ZrO2 powders, where the slurries presented viscosities below 10 Pa·s with desirable shear thinning behavior.

The powder content was not increased further because the slurries resulted in high viscosities in the case of the 45 vol% solid amounts (Fig. 3(f)), which was not desirable for the film-supply type DLP printing system.

Slurry optimization is necessary in film-type DLP printing systems to achieve smooth flow and printability. The dispersant BYK111 was coated onto the powder particles to prevent particle agglomeration and ensure uniform dispersion. The slurry stability conditions were determined based on viscosity measurements, as the slurry was fed through the thin opening of the doctor blade in our DLP system (Fig. 1(a)) and spread over the film. A very low content of BYK111 was insufficient to fully coat the powder, and an excessive amount of dispersant beyond the optimum amount could lead to flocculation caused by strong van der Waals forces [49]. Although higher solid loading is necessary to achieve high sintered density and mechanical properties of the final parts, very high viscosity makes it difficult for the slurry to be transferred through the doctor blade in our DLP system, in addition to layer detachment during the printing process. Therefore, 45 vol% slurry was not selected during the printing of green bodies.

### 3.3 Curing behavior and printability of Fe<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>-doped slurries

The curing depths of the 3YSZ and 4YSZ powders doped with varying Fe2O3 contents at curing time under UV light (continuous illumination), ranging from 1 to 14 s (with 2 s increments beyond 2 s), are shown in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b). Overall, both materials exhibited similar curing-depth trends.

The curability of the material in a DLP system depends on its optical properties and total energy dose supplied by the UV source. The incorporation of Fe2O3 into ZrO2 has been reported to enhance the hydrothermal stability of sintered ceramics [30]. This enhanced stability is attributed to the resistance of abundant cubic and stabilized tetragonal phases to their transformation into the monoclinic phase. Typically, accelerated transformation rates are linked to unstable, yttria-depleted tetragonal grains located near cubic grains, as these grains are more susceptible to hydrothermal degradation [52,53]. In the present study, 5YSZ constituted 40% of the total crown composition and presented a higher cubic phase content, which was responsible for the increased translucency in the incisal part of the crown. This increased cubic content likely contributes to improved phase stability and unaffected translucency features within oral conditions. Furthermore, the presence of stabilized 3YSZ and 4YSZ suggests that the tetragonal grains in these regions also exhibit resistance to monoclinic transformation. In support of these observations, Willems *et al.* [30] and Abualsaud *et al*. [54] demonstrated that 1 mol% Fe2O3-doped ZrO2 did not reach monoclinic phase saturation even after 400 h of hydrothermal aging, indicating a significant retention of the tetragonal phase. These findings suggest that cubic grains may surround and protect the remaining tetragonal grains, effectively delaying or preventing hydrothermal degradation. These results highlight the dual functionality of Fe2O3 as an effective coloring agent in dental zirconia, offering both enhanced aesthetic properties and reduced susceptibility to hydrothermal aging.

In contrast, no cracks or delamination were observed in the flash-printed samples after sintering (Fig. 8(d)). The FGM crowns were more uniform, with a clear indication of gradation in both color and translucency.

Although multilayered zirconia blocks with gradation characteristics have already been commercialized (such as Katana STML or ZirCAD Prime), their processing through conventional computer aided design/computer aided manufacturing (CAD–CAM) or milling techniques leads to waste of raw materials, wear of the cutting instruments, and inability to include different shades or phases of material in the same model [55]. With the superiority of additive manufacturing (AM) technology over conventional CAD–CAM, DLP has emerged as a major applied technique because of its proven printing speed, comparatively higher resolutions, and cost effectiveness by reducing material waster and lead time. However, FGM crown fabrication through DLP has faced serious challenges because of light scattering during photopolymerization. This scattering leads to overcuring, which is influenced by the light intensity of the UV source and the optical properties of the material slurry [56−59]. The intensity was kept constant in our DLP system (30 mW·cm−2); therefore, the only concern was the optical properties of the slurries, which changed upon Fe2O3 doping of the 3YSZ and 4YSZ compositions. The decrease in the curing depth necessitates longer curing time to print a stable layer, consequently leading to large overgrowths originating from UV scattering. Radiative transfer theory (RTT) models light traveling through scattering and absorbing media as Eq. (4) [60]:

(4) ∂I(r,s)∂s=−kI(r,s)+σs∫4πP(s,s′)I(r,s′)dΩ

where I*(*r*, *s*) represents the radiance value at the position *r* in the *s* direction; *k* represents coefficient of absorption that quantifies the medium’s energy loss; *σ*s represents scattering coefficient that measurs how strongly particles scatter light; *P*(*s, s′*) represents a phase function that specifies the angular spread of scattered light, and *s* represents the path length of the light.

Under continuous curing, light exposure is maintained for an extended period, allowing photons to repeatedly interact with powder particles of the ceramic material, which increases cumulative scattering and broadens the spread of light laterally. Conversely, flashing illumination supplies short light bursts that significantly limit photon exposure time and hence reduce the likelihood of scattering. Furthermore, continuous curing produces an isotropic scattering effect, meaning that photons are scattered equally in all directions, which in turn generates a high number of radicals. However, fewer radicals are produced per unit time and volume during flashing, confining the reaction to the intended area. In addition, a partially cured layer is formed with every successive flash, which immobilizes the powder particles through the formation of a denser cured layer. This densification decreases the effective scattering coefficient (*σ*s) in subsequent flashes [61], causing the light to be more confined in the vertical direction and increasing the overall precision.

Therefore, continuous curing was replaced with flashing to supply the UV dose in a 0.2 s small flash and a 1 s off-time to improve the printing accuracy [43]. The flash time below 0.2 s was neglected first, because it would increase the total printing time for a single layer, and second, it might not be enough to provide the critical energy dose necessary to initiate the reaction. Similarly, longer off-time values significantly reduce overcuring [43]; however, FGM fabrication takes excessive periods, which makes the implementation of the flashing technique unfeasible. Figure 8(b) shows that scattering leads to large overcuring, and the inside hollow region of the crown is fully blocked, while the structure possesses its design features during flashing (Fig. 8(c)) at the same curing time. During sintering, most trapped uncured slurries in the hollow region of the continuously printed crown cause delamination at the interfaces because of the difference in shrinkage between the cured FGM compositions and trapped powders inside the hollow region. However, no cracks or delamination were observed in the flash-printed FGM crown.

For process comparison, we calculated an average time for a single cycle of layer printing under maximum curing time conditions through both illumination methods. It took approximately 37 and 97 s for continuous and flashing illumination, respectively, when a single layer of 3YSZ or 4YSZ doped with 0.10 wt% Fe2O3 was printed for total curing time of 12 s, indicating a considerable difference in processing speed between the two methods. However, the lead time for any of the adopted methods is still much shorter, given its potential for mass production with multiple sample fabrication at the same time, unlike conventional subtractive manufacturing methods, including CAD–CAM techniques [55,62]. Furthermore, this gap between continuous and flashing illumination decreases further when the slurry does not require longer curing time. On the other hand, the accuracy achieved through the flashing method (less than 10% overgrowth) for the above composition was far superior to that achieved through the continuous method (approximately 88% overgrowth), which signifies the applicability of the flashing mechanism. In the future, further research is required to accelerate the flashing illumination process.

The larger grains in 4YSZ and 5YSZ in particular were attributed to the higher yttria content in these ZrO2 compositions, which maintained a high fraction of cubic phases, as indicated by the XRD peaks of these materials in Fig. 10(b). Fe2O3 doping was also found to promote grain growth, although very little, and introduced residual porosity in 3YSZ compositions containing 0.08 and 0.10 wt% Fe2O3. Notably, Fe2O3 was not detected in the XRD patterns because of the low concentrations used for coloring purposes.

All FGM compositions met the minimum flexural strength requirements outlined in ISO 6872 : 2024 for dental ceramic materials. Although conventionally fabricated multilayered 3YSZ zirconia doped with Fe2O3—designed to replicate the natural shade gradient of teeth—has demonstrated higher flexural strength values, typically ranging from 900 to 1100 MPa [63,64]. These conventional systems, however, lack the design flexibility and patient-specific customization achievable through AM technology. Digital DLP-based AM zirconia, particularly 3YSZ, has been reported to achieve flexural strength values of approximately 800 MPa, although performance is strongly influenced by factors such as solid content in the slurry, curing time, printing orientation, and sintering conditions [54,65,66]. In the present study, the highest flexural strength (~750 MPa) was recorded for undoped 3YSZ, which is reasonable given the 43 vol% solid loading used. Increasing the yttria content and Fe2O3 doping resulted in further deterioration of the mechanical properties. The 5YSZ sample had the lowest strength (549.1 MPa) at 1500 °C, which aligns with values reported by Romario et al.* [42]. This reduction in strength is attributed primarily to the high fraction of c-ZrO2, as confirmed by the XRD patterns in Fig. 10(b), which is known to compromise the mechanical performance compared with the tetragonal phase in 3YSZ and 4YSZ. The increased yttria content promotes grain growth (Fig. 10(a)), which ultimately degrades the mechanical properties. Similarly, there was a slight degradation in the mechanical properties (for both 3SYZ and 4YSZ samples) with the inclusion of Fe2O3, although this degradation was not significant at lower doping contents. Fe2O3 melts at approximately 900 °C, well before the ZrO2 sintering temperature, which is distributed on the grain boundaries and promotes both grain growth together with residual porosity in the structure (Fig. 10), which is the primary reason behind the decrease in strength [30]. Figure S2 in the ESM shows that the flexural strengths of the 3YSZ and 4YSZ (undoped) samples were approximately 745 and 708 MPa, respectively, which were reduced to 678.72 MPa in the case of 0.10 wt% Fe2O3-doped 3YSZ and up to 670.19 MPa in the 4YSZ material doped with 0.06 wt% Fe2O3. Notably, flexural bending tests were conducted for every FGM composition separately after sintering in this study. While it is clearly acknowledged as a limitation, an in-depth analysis is suggested in the future to consider all the FGM compositions in a single part with the indicated interfaces to better understand the mechanical behavior of the graded structure after sintering.

The shrinkage decreased with increasing amounts of yttria (3YSZ > 4YSZ > 3YSZ) and with increasing amounts of Fe2O3 doped into the 3YSZ and 4YSZ samples. The obvious reason for the decrease in shrinkage could be grain growth attributed to increased amounts of yttria or Fe2O3 restricting grain compaction. The Fe2O3 inclusion could enhance shrinkage because of its early melt-down temperature; however, pore generation restricted further densification. Furthermore, the higher shrinkage value in the vertical (*Z*) direction was clearly caused by the layered pattern formed during the printing process, where interlayer voids were present and the particles were bonded under a relatively weak binding force compared with the *XY* direction [67,68].

The 5YSZ discs were more translucent with considerably better background visibility, because 5YSZ contained a large composition of c-ZrO2 phase (verified through XRD in Fig. 10(b)), which has the ability to pass through light effectively due to the isotropic nature of grain boundaries.

## 4 Conclusions

This study implemented a DLP-flashing strategy for overcoming process limitations in the FGAM of ceramic materials and addressing the aesthetic demands of dental prostheses by fabricating ZrO2 dental crowns with simultaneous color and translucency gradation. Optimized ceramic slurries were obtained with BYK111 amounts of 3, 4, and 5 wt% for 3YSZ, 4YSZ, and 5YSZ materials, respectively. The curing depth dramatically decreased with extreme overcuring owing to scattering caused by the doping of Fe2O3 into the 3YSZ and 4YSZ samples. However, the CIELAB parameters indicated a successful change in the color of the final sintered parts. Furthermore, flashing illumination (0.2 s flash time and 1 s off-time) effectively reduced the degree of scattering induced overcuring by 14%, which was ~80% under continuous curing in slurries doped with high amounts of Fe2O3, such as 0.06 wt% and above. In addition, the sintered FGM crown exhibited no cracks, unlike those under continuous printing conditions. The flexural bending strength and sintering shrinkage were slightly affected by the amount of yttria doped into ZrO2 materials. The *TP* values confirmed the translucency gradation, with a maximum *TP* value of ~22 for 5YSZ and 0.10 wt% doped 3YSZ (TP ≈ 11). FGM ZrO2 dental crowns with simultaneous color and translucency gradation were successfully fabricated via a DLP flashing-based FGAM process. In the future, further research should focus on in-depth investigations of the biocompatibility of the color- and translucency-graded ZrO2 dental crowns and speed up the DLP-flashing-based FGAM process.
