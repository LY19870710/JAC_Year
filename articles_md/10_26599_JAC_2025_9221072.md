## 1 Introduction

In recent years, the development of radioisotope thermophotovoltaic (RTPV) systems has attracted increasing attention. In an RTPV system, emitter is the key device to determine the energy conversion efficiency [1]. The working principle of emitters is to convert heat energy into radiant energy and output photons. The magnitude of photon output directly affects the selective emissivity and spectral efficiency of the emitter [2]. An improvement in the energy conversion efficiency of RTPV systems can be achieved by increasing the emissivity and spectral efficiency of the emitters [3]. Among the many types of emitters, selective emitters are widely used because of their excellent selective emissivity and spectral efficiency. The key to developing selective emitters with high spectral efficiency is to match the emission wavelength of the selective emitter with the absorption spectrum of existing photovoltaic (PV) cells such as GaSb, InGaSb, and InGaAs [4]. Selective emitters can be categorized into three groups: (a) bulk emitters, (b) emitters incorporating rare-earth recent composites or dopants, and (c) photonic crystal (PhC) emitters [1]. However, among the three types of emitters, the bulk emitters with the highest temperature resistance exhibit the lowest selectivity, while the photonic crystal emitters with poor temperature resistance exhibit the greatest selectivity [5−7]. In contrast, emitters incorporating rare-earth composites or dopants not only possess excellent high-temperature resistance but also exhibit excellent selective emission performance at high temperatures, making them excellent candidate materials for selective emitters [8−10].

Monocline-structured rare-earth tantalates (RETaO4) demonstrate remarkable optical properties, high-temperature resistance, and excellent chemical stability [11−15], making them viable candidates as RTPV-selective emitter materials. ErTaO4 exhibits excellent emission properties, which are caused by the energy level transition of Er3+. The ground state electrons of Er3+ transition from the 4I15/2 energy level to the 4I13/2 energy level, producing a continuous strong light spectrum in the range of 1.53–1.57 μm [16,17]. The luminescence of Er3+ can precisely match the spectral response range of the maximum PV convertible emission power of GaSb batteries (1.40–1.60 μm) [18], which can make the emission spectrum of Er3+ able to be effectively absorbed and utilized by the GaSb battery. However, due to black body radiation at high temperatures, ErTaO4 has high emissivity in the PV non-convertible emission power range [16]. This high emissivity not only reduces the spectral efficiency of ErTaO4 but also has a negative effect on PV cells. Specifically, it heats the PV cells, which can reduce their efficiency and lifetime. Therefore, ErTaO4 selective emitters with high selective emissivities and high spectral efficiencies are worthy of further research and exploration.

To improve the selective emissivity and spectral efficiency of the selective emitter, the following approaches may be employed: first, enhancing the emissivity in the selective band (1.40–1.60 μm) and second, suppressing the emissivity in the non-selective band. Previous studies have shown that the doping strategy is an effective means to improve the selective emissivity, which can alter the f−f transition probability of rare earth ions [19−21]. Through a doping strategy, the maximum selective emissivity of (Er*x*Ho1−*x*)3Al5O12 can reach 80% [22]. Moreover, improving the spectral efficiency can be achieved by suppressing non-selective emissivity. Moreover, doping strategies also significantly affect the non-selective emission performance of emitters [23]. Doping Ca2+ and Y3+ into CeO2 leads to a 33% decrease in the infrared emissivity [24]. Research has shown that the emission performance of α-MoO3 doped with Co is effectively suppressed [25]. Consequently, this doping strategy is expected to be an effective solution for achieving coordinated regulation with various properties, providing a method for the development of novel rare earth tantalite-based selective emitter materials.

In this work, a series of rare-earth tantalate ceramics with Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) were prepared using the high-temperature solid-state reaction and pressureless sintering method. The influence of Nb5+ doping content on the selective emissivity and spectral efficiency of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) was analyzed. Moreover, the high-temperature stability of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) was investigated. Notably, the modulation mechanisms of selective emissivity and spectral efficiency were systematically investigated. This work shows that Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) exhibits excellent selective emissivity and spectral efficiency at high temperatures and is a potential selective emitter material for RTPV applications.

### 2.1 Materials and preparation

Commercially accessible powders of Ta2O5 (99.99%), Nb2O5 (99.99%), and Er2O3 (99.99%) (Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) were employed as the fundamental materials for the experiments. Ta2O5, Nb2O5 and Er2O3 compounds were accurately weighed according to the stoichiometric ratio and ground in a planetary ball mill with ethanol as the medium for 6 h. Subsequently, the mixed powders were uniaxially pressed to form green bodies and then sintered in an air atmosphere at a heating rate of 5 °C/min from room temperature to 1600 °C for 4 h. Then, the synthesized products were subjected to wet milling for 24 h and subsequently dried at 80 °C to obtain Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) powders with uniform and fine particle sizes. The powders were pressed into a raw billet with a diameter of 30 mm and a height of 2 mm and subsequently pressed using a cold isostatic press at 300 MPa. Ultimately, the green bodies were sintered in an air atmosphere at a heating rate of 5 °C/min from room temperature to 1650 °C for 8 h to obtain dense Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) ceramics.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The phase composition was ascertained through an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Miniflex 600 instrument, Rigaku, Japan), employing Cu Kα radiation with a wavelength of 1.5406 Å, and scanning at a rate of 2 (°)/min over a range from 10° to 55°. Based on the acquired XRD patterns, the crystal structure parameters were determined through Rietveld refinement using the GSAS II software. The morphology was analyzed by a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; Apreo S LoVac Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, and Sigma 500 ZEISS, UK). The acquired SEM images were evaluated using a particle analysis tool (Nano Measurer 1.2.5, Fudan University, China) to determine the average grain sizes. The density was measured using Archimedes method. The microstructure of the powder was analyzed by a transmission electron microscope (TEM; FEI Tecnai F20, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The thermal conductivity of the bulk samples was examined by a thermal constant analyzer with a size of *ϕ*6.0 mm × 10.0 mm (TPS 2500, Hot Disk, Sweden). The vibrational absorption of the groups in the structure was characterized by a Raman spectrometer (LabRAM, Horiba, Japan). The absorbance of samples was measured by an ultraviolet–visible–near infrared (UV−Vis−NIR) spectrophotometer (Carrying 5000, Agilent, USA). The chemical state was analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) with a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha instrument (Thermo, USA). To explore the selective emission performance of rare earth tantalate emitters, the emissivity was measured using a high-temperature normal spectral emissivity measurement system self-made by the College of Physical Science and Technology of Bohai University. The testing system is shown in Fig. 1, and the calibration method of the system is the blackbody calibration method. The system consists of a high-temperature vacuum sample heater (HT2000-V, Hylax Technology, Singapore), a Fourier spectrometer with a resolution of 0.5 cm−1 (iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), a high-temperature blackbody radiation source (ISDC-IR601, Infrared Systems Development Co., USA), and an optical path conversion device.

### 3.1 Phase composition

Figure 2(a) illustrates the monoclinic crystal structure of ErTaO4. In the crystal lattice of ErTaO4, cations and anions occupy 4e and 8f Wyckoff sites, Ta ions coordinate with O ions to form distorted [TaO6] octahedrons, while Er ions coordinate with O ions to form a [ErO8] dodecahedron [26,27]. As depicted in Fig. 2(b), the inorganic crystal structure database (ICSD) standard card of ErTaO4 and the XRD patterns of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) samples are presented. ErTaO4 has a classical monoclinic structure with a space group of *I*2/*a*(15). All diffraction peaks of pure-phase ErTaO4 can be clearly distinguished in Fig. 2(b). All the diffraction peaks of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) are completely consistent with those of ErTaO4, which proves that Nb5+ has been completely dissolved into the ErTaO4 lattice and monoclinic Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) ceramics have been successfully synthesized. Notably, on the basis of the magnified peak of the (* 1¯21) lattice plane, the diffraction angles of all samples doped with Nb5+ ions shift towards higher angles than those of pure-phase ErTaO4. Doping with Nb5+ ions can decrease the cell volume, resulting in a decrease in the interplanar spacing and affecting the shift of diffraction peaks [28]. Ta5+ and Nb5+ have similar ionic radii, and the reason for the reduction in unit cell volume is that Nb5+ can change the unit cell structure, thereby reducing the unit cell volume [29]. As a result, the volume of the unit cell decreases with increasing Nb5+. According to the Bragg equation (Eq. (1)) [30,31]:

### 3.2 Microstructure

Figure 3 shows the SEM images of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2). The tight bonding between the grains forms dense Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) ceramics. Table 2 shows the theoretical density, actual density, and density of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2). The densities of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) ceramics are 96.02%, 96.29%, 95.39%, 96.37%, and 95.49%, respectively. In addition, variations in the crystal grain size can be observed among samples with different doping concentrations. As shown in Fig. 3, the average grain sizes (*D*) of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) are 3.97, 4.38, 4.57, 4.62, and 4.76 μm, respectively. This indicates that as the Nb5+ content increases, the average grain size of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) gradually increases. Owing to the Kirkendall effect, the diffusion rate of components with low melting points is fast, and the propagation rate of components with high melting points is slow [36]. During the synthesis process, Nb2O5 diffuses rapidly to the grain boundaries and forms larger grains because of the lower melting point of Nb2O5 than those of Ta2O5 and Er2O3. Figure 4 shows the high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2). The interplanar spacings of the (* 1¯21) lattice planes in Er(Ta1−x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) are 0.3194, 0.3168, 0.3150, 0.3128, and 0.3125 nm, respectively. The above discussion confirms that Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) is a homogeneous single-phase solid solution, and these variations in the lattice can be attributed to lattice contraction caused by the substitution of Ta5+ with Nb5+, which is consistent with the results of the XRD analysis.

### 3.3 Thermal conductivity and high-temperature stability

The thermal conductivities of as-prepared Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) samples are shown in Fig. 5. The effect of porosity on thermal conductivity is corrected by Eq. (2). The thermal conductivities of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) ceramics after calibration are 4.02, 3.62, 3.56, 3.35, and 3.20 W/(m∙K), respectively. Table 1 shows that the lattice constant decreases with increasing Nb5+ doping amount, indicating an increase in the disorder degree of the crystal structure. As the degree of disorder increases, the thermal conductivity of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) gradually decreases. According to Eq. (3), doping Nb5+ can lead to a decrease in the phonon relaxation time. According to Eq. (4), the phonon relaxation time is directly proportional to the lattice thermal conductivity. Hence, as the doping amount increases, the phonon relaxation time gradually decreases, resulting in a gradual decrease in the lattice thermal conductivity [37,38].

### 3.4 Thermal emission performance

Figure 7 shows the emissivity of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) ceramics at 1400 °C. The emissivity is defined as the intensity (*I*sample) of sample radiation divided by the intensity (*I*blackbody) of blackbody radiation (Eq. (6)) [40]:

All the samples exhibit excellent selective emission peaks in the range of 1.40–1.60 μm because of the emission spectrum generated by the Er3+ level transition. The selective emissivity is mainly concentrated in the maximum photovoltaic (PV) convertible emission power range, and the emitted photons can be effectively absorbed and utilized by PV cells [18]. The selective emissivity is a key factor for evaluating the ability of selective emitters. As shown in Fig. 7, the average emissivity of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) within the selective band is 87.97%, 89.50%, 88.01%, 84.63%, and 85.17%, respectively. The selective emissivity shows a parabolic trend with increasing Nb5+ doping content. The emission spectrum of ErTaO4 in the non-selective band is not within the range of PV convertible emission power, resulting in photons being unable to be effectively absorbed and utilized. However, the photons emitted by ErTaO4 in the PV non-convertible emission power range not only reduce the spectral efficiency of ErTaO4 but also heat PV cells, resulting in reduced efficiency and lifespan. Therefore, it is necessary to suppress the non-convertible PV emission power. As shown in Fig. 7, the average emissivity of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) in the non-selective band range is 36.75%, 30.34%, 31.41%, 27.67%, and 27.52%, respectively. The emissivity of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) decreases with increasing Nb5+ content in the non-selective band. Compared with ErTaO4, the emissivity of Er(Ta0.8Nb0.2)O4 in the non-selective band range is decreased by 25.12%. As shown in Fig. 7, with increasing Nb5+ doping ratio, Er(Ta1− *x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) exhibits high selective emissivity and low non-selective emissivity.

### 4.1 Influence of crystal local structural symmetry on selective emission performance

Figure 8 shows the selective emissivity of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) at 1400 °C; with increasing Nb5+ doping content, the selective emissivity shows parabolic variation. Previous studies have proven that the probability of f–f transition of rare earth atoms at high temperatures mainly depends on the static properties, that is, the local symmetry of the host crystal around the rare-earth atom [41]. As shown in Table 1, when Nb5+ enters the ErTaO4 lattice, the difference in the ionic radii of Ta5+ and Nb5+ causes a change in the cell parameters, which affects the crystal field environment around Er3+ and increases the local structural asymmetry of the crystal around Er3+ [28,32]. The emissivity of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) in the selective band is attributed to the f–f transition of Er3+, which is significantly influenced by the crystal local structure symmetry. The crystal local structure symmetry of rare earth ions can be explained through local lattice distortion and local crystal field intensity. ### 4.2 Non-selective emissivity suppression mechanism

As shown in Fig. 7, with increasing Nb5+ doping amount, the non-selective emissivity decreases gradually. According to band theory, the bandgap (*E*g) can affect the non-selective emissivity by changing the electron transition barrier [48]. Moreover, the oxygen vacancy concentration can affect the non-selective emissivity by changing the number of free carriers [49]. A narrow bandgap and high oxygen vacancy concentration favor the promotion of electron transition. The combination of electronic transition barrier and free carrier absorption affects the non-selective emissivity of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2).

The emissivity of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) in the non-selective band is significantly affected by the bandgap. In this work, the absorbance of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) was measured using BaSO4 as the baseline whiteboard. The value of *E*g is calculated using the bandgap equation (Eq. (11)) [50]:

(11) (αhv)0.5=A(hv−Eg)

where *α* is the absorption coefficient; *h* is Plank’s constant; *ν* is the phonic frequency; *A* is a constant. Figure 11(a) shows that the bandgap of ErTaO4 ceramics doped with Nb5+ is significantly greater than that of ErTaO4. As the doping amount of Nb5+ varies between 0 and 20 mol%, the bandgap of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) increases from 3.54 to 3.78 eV. Hence, adding Nb5+ effectively improves the bandgap of ErTaO4, making it difficult for electrons to transition from the valence band to the conduction band [51].

The concentration of oxygen vacancies can effectively affect the emission of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) in the non-selective band. As indicated by previous reports, the O 1s spectrum can be deconvoluted into three peaks at 529.4, 531.4, and 533.7 eV, corresponding to lattice oxygen (OL), oxygen vacancies (OVs), and chemically adsorbed oxygen (OC) [52], respectively. Figure 11(b) displays the XPS spectra of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2). Figure 11(b) shows that the oxygen vacancy (OV) concentration gradually decreases with increasing Nb5+ content. The addition of Nb5+ changes the crystal structure of ErTaO4, leading to a reduction in the oxygen vacancy concentration [26]. A decrease in the number of oxygen vacancies inhibits the free carrier absorption of ErTaO4.

The decrease in the non-selective emissivity of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) can be attributed to two factors. On one hand, the widening of the bandgap increases the potential barrier for electrons to transition from the valence band to the conduction band, which affects electron transition. On the other hand, the decrease in the oxygen vacancy concentration leads to a reduction in the number of free carriers, which suppresses the free carrier absorption of ErTaO4. The widening of the bandgap and the decrease in the oxygen vacancy concentration effectively suppress the non-selective emissivity of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2).

(13) ρ(λ,T)=2πhc2λ5(ehcKTλ−1)

where *h* is the Planck constant; *λ* is the radiation wavelength; *T* is the temperature; *c* is the speed of light in a vacuum; *K* is the Kelvin temperature. (14) η=ρeρtot

The combined effect of increasing selective emissivity and decreasing non-selective emissivity enhances the spectral efficiency of selective emitters. In this work, Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) selective emitters not only possess the advantages of a simple preparation process and low cost but also have the ability to modulate their emission properties through a doping strategy, which provides more possibilities for material development and performance optimization. ## 5 Conclusions

In this work, a series of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) selective emitters matching GaSb cells were prepared by high-temperature solid-phase reactions and pressureless sintering methods. The selective emission properties were tuned by adjusting the local crystal field strength, local lattice distortion, bandgap width, and oxygen vacancy concentration. The Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) ceramics exhibit high emissivity (49%–93%) within the selective band (1.40–1.60 μm), high spectral efficiency (59.46%–62.12%), and excellent stability at high temperatures at 1400 °C. The average selective emissivity of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) improves from 84.63% to 89.50%. The spectral efficiency of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) improves from 59.46% to 62.12%. The maximum emission power density and PV convertible emission power of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) are 153.4 W/(m2·μm) and 7.07 W/cm2, respectively. This indicates that the doping strategy can effectively improve the selective emissivity and spectral efficiency of ErTaO4 selective emitters. In addition, the thermal conductivity of as-prepared Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) is 2.98–3.78 W/(m∙K). Moreover, the grain growth rates of Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) at 1400 °C range from 0.0581 to 0.0963 μm2/h, indicating that Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) ceramics possess excellent high-temperature stability. Hence, the combination of superior selective emission performance and good high-temperature stability indicates that Er(Ta1−*x*Nb*x*)O4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) is a promising material for RTPV selective emitters.
