## 1 Introduction

B4C–TiB2 composite ceramics, which are composed of TiB2 with high hardness and low density and B4C with ultrahigh hardness and ultralow density, represent typical structural composites featuring multifunctional characteristics, including high electrical conductivity, electrical discharge machinability, and excellent specific electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding performance [1−5]. The integration of the structural and functional properties highlights the broad application prospects of B4C–TiB2 ceramics across diverse fields [6−8]. However, in practical engineering applications, ceramic materials often require fabrication into larger or more complex components. This makes the development of reliable joining techniques for B4C–TiB2 ceramics highly important.

Ceramics are typically joined using methods such as diffusion joining [9,10], transient liquid-phase diffusion joining [11], glass–ceramic joining [12], brazing [13,14], MAX-phase joining [15−17], and spark plasma sintering (SPS) [18−20]. Among these methods, SPS has gained increasing attention because materials in SPS can be rapidly heated by pulsed electric fields. This heating approach promotes mutual diffusion and accelerates migration rates, enabling samples to be obtained at low temperatures with short insulation times [21−22]. Although the feasibility of ceramic joining via SPS has been widely validated in SiC-containing ceramics [15−28], research on its applications in other ceramic systems remains scarce, particularly for B4C–TiB2 ceramics, for which no joining studies have been reported to date.

The selection of the interlayer material is also critical for achieving reliable joints. Interlayers containing active elements such as Ti foil, Ni foil, and Ti–Ni composite foil can promote reactions and have been widely used [29−32]. These metallic interlayers react partially or fully with the ceramic base material to form ceramic phases, resulting in joints that are either ceramic–metal composites or full-ceramic. Obviously, full-ceramic joints are more advantageous for the application of materials in extreme chemical and thermal environments [33].

In this study, B4C–TiB2 ceramics were joined using SPS with Ti foil as an interlayer. A B4C–TiB2 composite with 40 vol% TiB2 was deliberately selected on the basis of prior studies indicating that this composition exhibited excellent mechanical properties [1], good electrical conductivity [6−8], and electromagnetic shielding effectiveness [5]. Ti foil was used as the interlayer because Ti can react with B4C and TiB2 to form high-melting-point ceramic phases such as TiB2, TiB, and TiC. Both the B4C–TiB2 composite and Ti are highly conductive, which facilitates current passage and effectively accelerates interfacial mass transfer and bonding during the SPS joining process. The evolution and underlying mechanisms of the joint composition, microstructure, and mechanical properties at varying joining temperatures were systematically investigated by integrating thermodynamic and kinetic analyses.

### 2.1 Sintering and joining

B4C–40 vol% TiB2 (BT) composite ceramics were prepared via SPS using commercially available B4C powder (particle size *d*50: 3.12 μm, purity 99.9%, Suzhou Nutpool Materials Technology Co., Ltd., China) and TiB2 powder (particle size *d*50: 1.50 μm, purity 99.9%, Shanghai Naiou Nano Technology Co., Ltd., China) as raw materials. The specific preparation process was as follows. The B4C and TiB2 powders were weighed according to the volume ratio and ball mixed for 24 h in ethanol with ZrO2 balls as the grinding medium. The mass ratio of the ZrO2 balls to the powder was 5 : 1. After mixing, the ethanol was evaporated using a rotary evaporator. Next, the mixed powder was transferred into a vacuum drying oven and dried at 80 °C for 24 h before being sieved through a 100-mesh screen. The sieved powder was then loaded into a graphite mold for SPS (SPS-20T-10, Shanghai Chenhua Science Technology, Corp., Ltd., China) processing. The sintering temperature, pressure, and holding time were 2000 °C, 40 MPa, and 15 min, respectively. The dimensions of the samples used for testing the mechanical properties of the ceramics and those used for joining were  ϕ40 mm × 3 mm and  ϕ30 mm × 10 mm, respectively.

For the joining process, the BT composite was cut into two pieces with dimensions of  ϕ30 mm × 4 mm using an electrical discharge machine (DK77, Ningbo Zhongyuan Machine Tool Co., Ltd., China), and the surface was polished using diamond grinding discs with grades from P400 to P2000 and finally polished with a 0.5 μm diamond suspension. The disks were subsequently subjected to ultrasonic cleaning with alcohol for 10 min, followed by vacuum drying. The interlayer used in the experiment consisted of high-purity titanium foil (purity 99.99%, Beijing Trillion Nonferrous Metals Technology Development Center Co., Ltd., China) with a thickness of 50 μm that underwent thorough ultrasonic cleaning for meticulous preparation. A schematic diagram of the sample assembly is depicted in Fig. 1(a). The joining equipment was the SPS furnace previously used for ceramic sintering. During the joining process, the SPS system remained under dynamic vacuum, with the vacuum level maintained in the range of 8–20 Pa. The vacuum combined with the reducing atmosphere provided by the graphite mold ensured that Ti was not oxidized. Pre-experimental studies demonstrated that no effective joints were formed when the bonding temperature was below 1000 °C or at and above 1500 °C. The joining process was conducted at temperatures of 1000, 1100, 1200, 1300, and 1400 °C for 10 min at a pressure of 30 MPa. The heating rate was 100 °C/min, and cooling was carried out in stages. The temperature was first reduced to 1000 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, then further decreased to 600 °C at 20 °C/min, and finally cooled to room temperature. The pulse time was 20 ms, and the pause time was 2 ms.

### 2.2 Characterizations and measurement

The microstructure of the joined BT ceramics along the cross-section was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU-8010, Hitachi, Japan) coupled with an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS; Aztec X-Max50, Oxford Instruments, UK) and transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai F20, FEI, USA). The TEM samples were prepared with a focused-ion beam (FIB; Helios Nanolab 600i, FEI, USA). The phase composition of the interface was analyzed using a microarea X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/MAX RAPID II, Rigaku, Japan). Shear tests were conducted with a universal testing machine (AGS-X10kN, Shimadzu, Japan) according to the ASTM D905-08 (2021) standard, with a loading rate of 1 N/s. Five tests were conducted for each sample. The shear test diagram is shown in Fig. 1(b). The joint strength was determined using the fundamental shear stress equation: *τ* = *F*/*S*, where *τ* represents the calculated shear strength (MPa), *F* corresponds to the peak load (N) recorded at specimen failure, and *S* represents the shear cross-sectional area of the sample (4 mm × 4 mm). All mechanical property tests in this study were performed at room temperature. The reported values are the average and variance of the test results from five samples.

### 3.1 Characterizations of BT ceramics

The XRD pattern of the as-prepared BT composite, shown in Fig. 2(a), conclusively demonstrates that only B4C and TiB2 exhibit a crystalline structure. This observation suggests the absence of any chemical reaction between the raw B4C and TiB2 powder during the sintering process. The achieved bulk density of 3.31 g/cm corresponds to 99.70% of the theoretical density, indicating a high degree of consolidation. Figures 2(b)–2(f) show SEM images and EDS elemental mappings of the composite. The SEM images reveal two distinct color contrasts, consistent with the XRD findings, confirming the presence of only two phases in the ceramic. As evident from the SEM images, the distributions of these two phases are uniform. The distributions of Ti, B, and C reveal that the dark regions in the SEM images correspond to the matrix B4C phase, whereas the light regions represent the TiB2 phase. The flexural strength, Vickers hardness, and fracture toughness of the BT ceramic reached 685 MPa, 29.2 GPa, and 4.37 MPa·m¹/², respectively, demonstrating its excellent mechanical properties.

### 3.2 Microstructure and phase evolution of BT/Ti/BT joints

During the joining process, the reaction between the interlayer Ti foil and the BT ceramics is crucial for ensuring the mechanical performance of the joint. Generally, the joining temperature directly affects the reaction process, which can be investigated through the microstructural evolution and phase transformation of the joints. Figure 3 presents the SEM images and EDS elemental mappings of the joints obtained at different temperatures ranging from 1000 to 1400 °C. Figures 3(a)–3(e) clearly show that all the BT ceramics were successfully joined to themselves by using a Ti interlayer within the specified temperature range. The thickness of the reaction layer increases with increasing joining temperature, suggesting that higher temperatures facilitate the diffusion reaction between the Ti foil and BT ceramics. When the joining temperature reached 1400 °C, the Ti foil was completely consumed. Notably, significant aggregation of carbon was observed at the interfaces between the reaction layer and the Ti interlayer, particularly at joining temperatures of 1000 and 1300 °C. This observation reveals the formation of elemental C or carbides at the interface, which will be discussed in further detail in subsequent sections.

*
Fig. 3SEM images and EDS elemental mappings of joints at (a) 1000 °C, (b) 1100 °C, (c) 1200 °C, (d) 1300 °C, and (e) 1400 °C.

Figure 4 shows the micro-XRD patterns of the BT/Ti/BT joints obtained at different temperatures. The joints were primarily composed of TiB2, Ti, TiC, and TiB when joined at 1000–1300 °C. At 1400 °C, the peaks associated with metallic Ti disappeared. These peaks were detectable in the range of 1000–1300 °C but became absent at 1400 °C, indicating that the Ti foil was completely consumed by the reactions. When the temperature reached or exceeded 1200 °C, the formation of the TiB phase became apparent, and the intensity of its XRD peaks increased significantly with increasing temperature, as shown in Fig. 4(b). According to previous reports on the reaction of the Ti–B4C system, the reaction: B4C + 5Ti → 4TiB + TiC is more likely to occur than B4C + 3Ti → 2TiB2 + TiC [34,35]. The stability hierarchy between the TiB and TiB2 phases reverses with increasing titanium availability: TiB prevails under excess Ti, whereas TiB2 stabilizes under Ti-deficient conditions. However, when Ti foil was used to join B4C–SiC–TiB2 composites, no TiB was detected in the reaction product at the interface [31,36]. This can be attributed to two potential factors: (i) The Ti foil employed had a thickness of 30 μm, which is thinner than the 50 μm used in this study. Consequently, Ti can be entirely depleted at the interface by B4C, leading to the formation of TiB2 and TiC. (ii) B4C–SiC–TiB2 composites were fabricated via the reactive sintering of B4C and Ti3SiC2, with C being a byproduct of the reaction that tends to consume Ti to form TiC [31].

Additionally, carbon was not detected by XRD because of its low concentration. As analyzed in the above section, the phase evolution within the joints during heating from 1000 to 1400 °C can be summarized as follows: Ti → Ti + TiB2 + TiB + TiC→TiB2 + TiB + TiC.

Figure 5 shows the temperature-dependent microstructural evolution at the bonding interfaces from 1000 to 1400 °C. The reaction layer thickness increased 16-fold (1.63–36.91 μm) within this thermal range. Interfacial architecture evolution exhibits a strong process parameter sensitivity. SPS is a technique that uses a low-voltage direct current pulsed electric field coupled with pressure assistance, which can activate the material surface through interparticle discharge induced by a high-energy pulse current and promote diffusion through Joule heating [20,37]. Simultaneously, local overheating occurs at the interface due to contact resistance and Peltier effect, thereby facilitating material migration at the connected interface [20,38,39]. This microstructural progression arises from electrothermo–mechanical coupling effects during pulsed field-assisted sintering, where pulsed electricity (a pulse pattern with a 20 : 2 on-off ratio) and axial pressure (30 MPa) collectively modulate atomic diffusion kinetics.

*
Fig. 5Microstructural morphologies of joints at different joining temperatures: (a–a2) 1000 °C, (b–b2) 1100 °C, (c–c2) 1200 °C, (d–d2) 1300 °C, and (e–e2) 1400 °C.

At 1000–1100 °C, Kirkendall voids could be observed at the interface near the Ti side (Figs. 5(a1) and 5(b1)). This occurred because, within this temperature range, the decomposition rate of BT ceramics was extremely low, resulting in an insufficient supply of B and C atoms at the interface. Consequently, the diffusion of Ti atoms outward dominated, and Ti reacted directly with B4C to form a new phase. However, owing to the limited availability of B and C atoms, these atoms were unable to adequately fill the vacancies left by the outward diffusion of Ti atoms, leading to the formation of Kirkendall voids at the interface [31,37]. Owing to the low joining temperature, there was limited diffusion between the atoms, resulting in the formation of a thin, continuous gray reaction layer. The thickness of the reaction layer was measured to be 1.63±0.29 μm at a joining temperature of 1000 °C and 2.06±0.63 μm at 1100 °C. TiB whiskers were identified in the reaction layer of the joints bonded at 1000 and 1100 °C (Figs. 5(a1) and 5(b1)). Their anisotropic growth results from the B27 crystal structure [30,40,41]. No defects, such as voids or cracks, were observed in Figs. 5(c) and 5(d), which correspond to joining temperatures of 1200 and 1300 °C, respectively. This was attributed to the synergistic effect of the gradual decomposition of BT ceramics at the interface, the diminished disparity in diffusion rates among Ti, B, and C atoms, and the densification of the reaction layer. These processes collectively eliminate micropores and result in a dense and continuous interface. At 1200 °C, the reaction layer thickness increased notably by 9.88 μm (Fig. 5(c)). When the bonding temperature exceeded 1300 °C, the average size of the reaction layer was 13.78 μm (Fig. 5(d)). EDS mappings (Figs. 3(c3) and 3(d3)) and micro-XRD analyses revealed that a discontinuous TiC-rich layer was formed at the Ti interface (1200–1300 °C), as shown in Figs. 5(c2) and 5(d2).

At 1400 °C, the Ti foil was completely consumed, thereby achieving a nearly fully ceramic joint (Fig. 4). However, noticeable voids were observed in the joints (marked in red in Fig. 5(e)). The voids were irregular in shape, with sizes ranging from approximately 1 to 2 μm. Their formation at the interface can primarily be attributed to three key mechanisms. First, the disparity in diffusion coefficients among Ti, B, and C atoms induces an atomic imbalance and vacancy accumulation via the Kirkendall effect [31,37]. Second, during the SPS joining process, thermal expansion coefficient mismatches generate stress-driven structural instabilities that promote void nucleation at the interface (Fig. 5(e)). Third, the high electrical conductivity of TiB2 leads to local current accumulation, generating high temperatures that cause thermal ablation and the formation of microscale pores.

In addition, according to Fig. 6, C enrichment was observed at the center of the interface in the 1400 °C joint, where it reached a maximum local concentration of 82.93 at%. The C-rich region was discontinuously distributed along the central axis of the interfacial reaction layer. At 1400 °C, owing to the significant difference in the diffusion rates of B and C in Ti [31,37], B was selectively enriched at the B4C/Ti interface region because of its lower diffusion rate and preferential reaction with Ti to form TiB2 (Fig. 5(e1)). The C atoms diffused into the Ti matrix farther from the B4C/Ti interface and reacted with Ti to form the TiC phase. As the reaction proceeded, the reaction layers on both sides gradually expanded until they came into contact with each other. Owing to insufficient reactive Ti sources, the remaining C formed a discontinuous C-rich zone in the central area of the reaction layer (Fig. 6). As free C was confined to localized areas near the joint centers, its content may have been below the detection limit of XRD. Hence, no carbon peaks were observed in the XRD pattern of Fig. 4. Notably, the significant difference between the C-rich region in Figs. 5(e) and 6, which is not characteristic of a C-rich region, is attributed to the discontinuous distribution feature of the C-rich region in the 1400 °C joint. The sampling positions in Figs. 3(e) and 5(e) correspond to noncarbon-rich areas, whereas Fig. 6 specifically describes the area with a concentrated carbon-rich region.

The interfaces formed at different temperatures had symmetrical microstructures, indicating that the pulsed electric current had no influence on the phase distribution of the heterogeneous interface. Similar results have been reported in previous studies [18,19,21].

The interface microstructure was accurately revealed through TEM analysis, as shown in Fig. 7. The selected area, presented in Fig. 5(d1), corresponds to the reaction layer of the sample joined at 1300 °C. The region processed by the FIB is illustrated in Fig. 7(a). According to the bright (Fig. 7(b)) and dark (Fig. 7(c)) field images of the reaction layer, no defects, such as pores or cracks, were observed at the interface, indicating a well-bonded and integral interface. The irregularly shaped particles observed in Fig. 7(b) were identified as the TiC phase through selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern analysis (Fig. 7(d)). As depicted in Fig. 7(e), the SAED pattern of the TiB phase displays elongated diffraction spots, clearly indicating the formation of stacking faults. This phenomenon, which is rather prevalent in composites sintered via SPS, is attributed to the residual stresses generated by the rapid thermal cycling inherent to the SPS process [32]. Figures 7(f) and 7(g) display the SAED patterns of the TiB and TiB2 grains adjacent to a TiC grain, respectively. The SAED analysis confirmed the presence of TiB2, TiB, and TiC phases in the reaction layer, indicating that Ti reacted with B4C. Figure 7(h) shows the TEM bright-field phase in another region of the sample. Figure 7(i) shows an enlarged image of position 1 in Fig. 7(h), and obvious stacking faults can be clearly observed. ### 3.4 Phase evolution mechanism

Figure 10 shows a schematic diagram depicting the phase evolution mechanism and phase characteristics of electric field-assisted joining in BT ceramics at different temperatures. In-depth research on the phase evolution mechanisms reveals the laws governing the microstructural evolution of materials during the joining process, providing theoretical support for optimizing process parameters and facilitating the determination of more rational process design plans. The entire phase transition process comprises four distinct stages.

(I) Physical contact stage: Under axial pressure, the BT ceramics and Ti foil maintain only physical contact. The interdiffusion of interface atoms is restricted by the energy barrier, resulting in extremely slow diffusion (Figs. 10(a)–10(c)).

(II) Heating and diffusion stage: Elevated temperatures promote the interdiffusion of Ti atoms from the interface into the BT ceramic substrates at both ends (Figs. 10(a1)–10(c1)). Once the system enters the heating stage, thermal activation triggers plastic deformation within the Ti foil. This rheological behavior effectively compensates for the surface microdefects present in the ceramics. As a result, the interfacial contact areas are enlarged, and efficient atomic diffusion channels are established, facilitating subsequent chemical reactions at the interface.

(III) Isothermal phase transformation stage and (IV) joint formation stage: The pulsed electric field significantly promotes interfacial atomic migration and reduces the activation energy of diffusion processes. This ensures that the reaction can be initiated at lower temperatures under the kinetic conditions [43]. Simultaneously, during the isothermal stage, pulsed electric fields induce localized interfacial heating via the Joule thermal effect [15,21]. The thermodynamic analysis results (Fig. 8) reveal that within the temperature range of 1000–1400 °C, the change in Gibbs free energy ( ΔGTθ) for reactions between Ti and the B4C–TiB2 system remains negative, which thermodynamically confirms the feasibility of the solid-phase direct reactions. The non-equilibrium thermal field accelerated atomic interdiffusion across the interfaces, thereby driving the nucleation and progressive growth of new phases (TiC, TiB, and TiB2) within the interaction zone (Figs. 10(a3)–10(c3)). At relatively lower joining temperatures ranging from 1000 to 1100 °C, a thin reaction layer (1–2 μm) forms, as shown in Figs. 10(a2) and 10(a3). When the temperature increases to 1200 and 1300 °C (Fig. 10(b2)), the enhanced diffusion capability of Ti atoms enables them to penetrate through the 1–2 μm reaction layer. This allows for continuous reactions with the B4C substrate, leading to a nonlinear increase in the thickness of the reaction layer. Notably, this dense reaction layer acts as a diffusion barrier for the Ti atoms. However, experimental observations have revealed that interfacial reactions intensify at temperatures above 1200 °C. The electric-field-induced electromigration and short-range diffusion pathways at the heterogeneous TiB2/TiC/TiB interfaces jointly facilitate the migration of Ti atoms into the BT ceramic matrix [20]. C atoms at the reaction interface diffuse toward the center of the Ti foil because of their longer diffusion distance than B [43,44], react with Ti (Reaction (11)) [42,43], and form a TiC-rich layer (Figs. 5(c2) and 5(d2)).

At the higher joining temperature of 1400 °C, the interfacial reaction becomes more thorough (Figs. 10(c2) and 10(c3)). In addition to grain boundary diffusion and electric field-assisted migration, which provide atomic diffusion pathways, microcracks or pores caused by thermal stress within the reaction layer at 1400 °C also create short-range diffusion channels, as shown in Fig. 6. On the B4C side, the synergistic effect of high temperature and an electric field facilitates the reaction between B4C and the migrated Ti, enabling the epitaxial growth of the reaction layer [43]. The plastic flow behavior of Ti foils is significantly enhanced at elevated temperatures. In the later stages, as the Ti foil is reduced to a critical threshold due to extrusion-induced material loss and reaction consumption, the reaction system reverts to the diffusion-controlled regime (Figs. 10(c3) and 10(c4)). When the Ti concentration at the interface is low, Reaction (5) becomes thermodynamically and kinetically favorable [43]. This reaction-diffusion coupled mechanism persists until the Ti foil is depleted, ultimately leading to the accumulation of C at the interface center and the formation of a TiB2-rich layer on the B₄C side (Figs. 5(e) and 10(c4)). However, joints may develop pores or cracks as a result of incomplete reactions and localized thermal stress, which impairs their mechanical properties.

The above analysis confirms that direct interfacial diffusion reactions (Ti–B4C solid-phase interactions) are the dominant mechanisms. Under the synergistic influence of temperature, electric, and pressure fields, Ti atoms continuously penetrate the reaction layer via grain boundary diffusion, defect channels, and electromigration, reacting with B4C until the Ti source is exhausted.

### 3.5 Joint mechanical properties and fracture mechanism

Table 3 lists the shear strength values of the joints obtained under different joining parameters. It is clear that the shear strength first increases but then decreases with increasing joining temperature. The joint formed at 1300 °C presented the highest average shear strength of 72 MPa, whereas the shear strength was observed to be as low as 10 MPa at a joining temperature of 1000 °C. Within the temperature range of 1200–1400 °C, the average shear strength of the joints varied; however, the values remained notably high, ranging from 53 to 72 MPa. This observation demonstrates that incorporating Ti foil as an interlayer during the SPS bonding process enables the attainment of robust shear strength across a broad temperature range. Importantly, the experimental results revealed that pressure had a pronounced effect on the shear strength of the joints. Specifically, increasing the pressure from 30 to 40 MPa resulted in a notable 29.4% decrease in the shear strength of the joint at 1100 °C. This suggests that excessive pressure may negatively affect the microstructural integrity of the joint interface, leading to a reduced mechanical performance under the same temperature conditions. Conversely, bonding attempts at temperatures ≥ 1600 °C did not yield successful joint formation, which can be attributed primarily to the inherent thermal properties of Ti. Given that Ti has a melting point of 1668 °C, when the joining temperature approaches or exceeds this critical threshold, extrusion of molten Ti from the bonding interface occurs. This phenomenon resulted in material loss at the joint interface and concurrently impeded the development of a requisite reaction layer, both of which are essential for establishing robust interfacial bonding. Consequently, the inadequate interfacial bonding strength directly caused joint failure under such extreme thermal conditions. The above findings underscore the critical balance between processing pressure and temperature in optimizing joint integrity, particularly when Ti foil is used as an interlayer in SPS bonding.

## 4 Conclusions

B4C–TiB2 joints were successfully fabricated via SPS using a Ti foil interlayer within the temperature range of 1000–1400 °C. The effects of temperature on the interfacial reaction, phase composition, microstructure, and shear strength of the joint were systematically studied. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) The primary interfacial reaction between B4C and Ti yielded reaction products comprising mainly TiB2, TiC, and TiB. At 1400 °C, the complete consumption of the Ti foil formed a fully ceramic joint, featuring free C enrichment at the joint center and a TiB2-enriched layer adjacent to the ceramic substrate. The phase evolution at the interface was divided into four stages: initial physical contact, heating and diffusion, isothermal phase transformation, and joint formation.

(2) Temperature significantly influenced the reaction layer thickness. From 1000 to 1300 °C, the reaction layer thickness increased gradually from 3.26 to 13.78 μm and was directly correlated with temperature according to Arrhenius-type kinetics. *Q* for the interfacial reaction was calculated to be 263 kJ/mol, and the growth constant ( k0) was 1.98×102 mm2/s.

(3) The shear strength increased from 10 MPa at 1000 °C to 72 MPa at 1300 °C before decreasing to 53 MPa at 1400 °C, reflecting a temperature-driven, thickness-controlled strengthening mechanism. Fracture analysis of the joint with the highest shear strength at 1300 °C revealed that crack propagation occurred through the ceramic matrix, reaction layer, and residual Ti layer, exhibiting typical cross-layer fracture characteristics that confirmed strong interfacial bonding.

In summary, this work confirms that reliable bonding of B4C–TiB2 ceramics via a Ti interlayer can be achieved by optimizing the SPS temperature. These findings provide valuable guidance for broadening the practical engineering applications of this material.
