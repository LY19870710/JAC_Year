## 1 Introduction

Sunlight usually comprises approximately 5% ultraviolet (UV) rays spanning the 200–400 nm range, with further classification into ultraviolet A (UVA, 320–400 nm), ultraviolet B (UVB, 280–320 nm), and ultraviolet C (UVC, 100–280 nm) based on wavelength. UV radiation presents potential risks, including the discoloration and degradation of plastics and paper, premature aging of the skin, and carcinogenic effects. Extended exposure to UV radiation can result in noteworthy damage to both human bodies and devices [1−3]. In response, UV-shielding materials have been developed to fulfill various commercial requirements, including UV-shielding windows, food containers, contact lenses, and masks [4−6]. UV shielding solutions typically involve the incorporation of UV absorbers, which can be either inorganic (e.g., TiO2, ZnO, or CeO2) or organic (e.g., nanocellulose or lignin) [5,7], into transparent matrices such as polymers, films, coatings, or glass [8]. While existing UV shielding materials are suitable for daily use, their effectiveness diminishes in high-temperature, high-pressure, corrosive, and radioactive environments [9]. Organics fail at high temperatures, and films or coatings tend to flake under harsh conditions; glass is constrained by its inherent mechanical brittleness [10]. In response, researchers are actively engaged in the development of UV-shielding materials with high melting points, robust mechanical strength, optimal optical properties, and long-term physical and chemical stability to withstand diverse extreme conditions, including aerospace, high temperature, and acidic and alkaline environments [11].

In contrast to traditional UV-shielding materials such as resin and glass, transparent ceramics typically exhibit superior chemical stability and enhanced thermomechanical properties, including thermal-shock resistance, toughness, strength, and thermal conductivity [12]. Transparent ceramics are polycrystalline materials with optical transparency that are formed by pressing and sintering. The development of UV-shielded transparent ceramics faces challenges due to the limited availability of matrices capable of solidifying substantial amounts of UV-absorbing ions such as ZnO, CeO2, and TiO2. To maintain optical transparency in transparent ceramics, dopant ions must integrate into the crystal lattice to prevent aggregation at grain boundaries and subsequent light scattering. CeO2 is a preferable absorber for transparent ceramics over TiO2 and ZnO because its ionic radius better matches the lattice sites of the transparent ceramic matrix. While certain matrices can incorporate Ce ions, this process does not alter the UV-absorbing edge; instead, it generates absorption peaks, as seen in Ce:Y3Al5O12 and Ce:Lu3Al5O12 transparent ceramics, which do not meet the criteria for effective UV shielding [13,14]. There are also transparent ceramics with no photoluminescence after Ce doping; for example, Osipov *et al*. [15] reported 5 mol% CeO2-doped Yb:Y2O3 transparent ceramics and found that the UV absorption edge of the Y2O3 ceramics redshifted to 370 nm. In addition, Ce3+ does not emit photoluminescence in Y2O3 substrates because the 5d–4f transitions of Ce3+ are outside the bandgap of Y2O3 [16]. Liu *et al*. [17] studied the addition of 0–3% CeO2 to Y2O3 transparent ceramics with 10 at% La2O3 and 1 at% ZrO2 and found that the addition of Ce can regulate the UV absorption edge of Y2O3 transparent ceramics. While prior research has indicated that Ce doping can alter the UV absorption edge of Y2O3 transparent ceramics, and these studies have frequently involved the introduction of additional ions alongside Ce, which can readily induce color centers under UV radiation. The feasibility of employing transparent ceramics for targeted UV shielding purposes remains unverified. This involves a comprehensive evaluation of the thermal and mechanical properties of the sample, as well as the UV-shielding effect and long-term stability. Hence, there is an urgent need for a thorough investigation to systematically evaluate the practical feasibility of Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics for UV shielding.

In this work, highly transparent Y2O3 ceramics were fabricated using CeO2 as the sole sintering aid. The effect of CeO2 content on the optical, thermal, and mechanical properties of Y2O3 ceramics was studied in detail. Furthermore, the stability of the transmittance of the samples under high temperatures, acidic and alkaline conditions, and UV irradiation was tested. The findings reveal that 5 at% Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics exhibit outstanding UV shielding capabilities. Furthermore, these ceramics demonstrate excellent optical, mechanical, and thermal properties, maintaining stability across various corrosive environments, including high temperature, radiation, and acidic or alkaline conditions. Our results show that Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics are competitive UV shielding materials, especially for harsh environments such as space, radiation reactors, and high-temperature environments.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

High-purity Y2O3 (99.99%, Dingnan Dahua New Material Resources Co., Ltd., China) and CeO2 (99.99%, Yutai Qixin Chemical Co., Ltd., China) were used as the starting materials. The raw powders were blended according to the (Y1−*x*Ce*x*)2O*y* composition (*x* = 0.01, 0.03, 0.05, and 0.1). The powders were planetary ball milled in a nylon tank at 250 r·min−1 for 20 h, with zirconia balls (*φ* = 5 mm) as the grinding medium and anhydrous ethanol as the solvent. Subsequently, the resulting slurry was dried in a blower drying oven at 70 °C for 24 h. The dried powder was then sieved through a 200-mesh nylon sieve and calcined in a muffle furnace (MF-1200C-L, Anhui Beiyike Equipment Technology Co., Ltd., China) at 800 °C for 3 h. The prepared powder was shaped into 20 mm diameter tablets using a metal mold at a pressure of 20 MPa. These tablets were further subjected to isostatic pressing in a cold isostatic pressing (CIP) machine at 200 MPa for 10 min. The tablets were sintered in a carbon canister furnace (ZT-25-20, Shanghai Chenxin Electric Furnace Co., Ltd., China) at 1800 °C for 10 h under a vacuum of 1×10−3 Pa. Subsequently, the sintered samples underwent annealing in air at 1450 °C for 20 h, followed by processing and polishing on both sides in preparation for subsequent testing. The preparation process of the transparent ceramics is shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The samples doped with 1%, 3%, 5%, and 10% CeO2 were designated C1, C3, C5, and C10, respectively.

### 2.2 Crystal structure and microstructure

The ceramic phase composition was analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD) with Cu Kα radiation and a nickel filter (DX-2700, Dandong Fangyuan Instruments Co., Ltd., China). The microstructure of the ceramics was determined through a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-IT500HR, JEOL, Japan). The average grain size of the ceramics was determined by the linear intercept method using Eq. (1):

(1) G=1.56L

where *G* is the grain size and *L* is the intercept length. The distribution of elements in the samples was obtained with an energy-dispersive X-Ray spectroscopy (EDS) system (AZTEC, Oxford Instruments, UK). Raman spectra were acquired at room temperature using a Raman spectrometer (HR800, Horiba, France) equipped with a 633 nm excitation source. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; AXIS Supra, Kratos Analytical, UK) was employed to assess the valence state of the Ce element.

### 2.3 Optical properties

Transmittance measurements of the transparent ceramic samples were carried out using an ultraviolet/visible/near infrared (UV/VIS/NIR) spectrophotometer (Lambda 450, Perkin-Elmer, USA) over a wavelength range of 200–850 nm. Equations (2) and (3) express the relationship between the bandgap energy (*E*g) and temperature (*T*):

(2) α=1lln1T

(3) αhν=B(hν−Eg)1/2

where *α* represents the absorption coefficient; *hν* is the incident photon energy; *l* is the sample thickness; *B* is the absorption constant. A plot of (*αhν*)² against *hν* allows for the estimation of the band gap (*E*g) by extrapolating the linear portion of the curve to the *x*-axis (where *y* = 0).

### 2.4 Vickers hardness

Hardness (*H*) testing was performed using a Vickers hardness tester (MHVD-50AP, Shanghai Juing Precision Instrument Co., Ltd., China) with an applied load of 5 kg for 10 s (Eq. (4)):

(4) H=1.854Fd2

where *F* is the indentation load and *d* is the diagonal length of the indentation [18].

### 2.5 Thermal properties

Thermal diffusivity measurements were conducted at test temperatures of 25, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 °C, with a minimum of three independent measurements taken at each temperature point. The specimens were machined into a 12.5 mm diameter sheet to suit the measuring container. The thermal conductivities (*k*) were calculated from the values of thermal diffusivity, bulk density, and specific heat (Eq. (5)):

(5) k=αρcp

where *c**p* is the specific heat, which was calculated by the Neumann–Kopp rule, and the bulk density *ρ* was measured by the Archimedes method [19].

### 2.6 Physical and chemical stability

The ceramics underwent five 24-h cycles to assess their physical and chemical stability under various conditions, including high temperature (600 °C), UV radiation (254 and 365 nm), alkali solution (pH = 14), and citric acid (pH = 2). UV irradiation was carried out under a UV analyzer (WFH-203C, Chitang Industrial, China). The rated power of the UV lamp was 8 W, and the distance between the samples and the UV lamp was 20 cm. The solutions with pH = 2 and pH = 14 were prepared using anhydrous citric acid (AR, Kelong Chemical, China) and sodium hydroxide (AR, Kelong Chemical, China) with deionized water, respectively.

### 3.1 Crystal structure and valence state

To produce high-quality transparent ceramics, it is essential to use precursor powders with high purity, a consistent composition distribution, uniform particle size dispersion, and minimal impurity levels. Figures S2(a) and S2(b) in the ESM show the microscopic morphology of the Y2O3 and CeO2 raw materials, respectively, while Figs. S2(c)–S2(f) in the ESM show the mixed powders after 20 h of planetary ball milling. Clearly, all powders demonstrate outstanding dispersion, are devoid of significant agglomerations, and retain submicron-level particle sizes, making them well suited for use in sintered ceramics. EDS surface scanning was conducted on 5% CeO2 doped Y2O3 powders, revealing a uniform distribution of Y, O, and Ce. In the XRD pattern of the powder (Fig. S3 in the ESM). The principal diffraction peaks align with those of standard cubic Y2O3, while the remaining peaks correspond to CeO2, with their intensity increasing proportionally to the concentration of CeO2 dopant.

Digital photographs of the prepared Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics are shown in Fig. 1(a). It should be noted that after vacuum sintering, the samples had a black and opaque appearance and became transparent after air annealing. This alteration primarily results from the formation of oxygen vacancies or color centers throughout the vacuum sintering procedure. Subsequent annealing in an oxygen-enriched environment increases the number of oxygen atoms in the oxygen vacancies, leading to the complete disappearance of the black color in the samples, similar to the Y2O3 transparent ceramics using ZrO2 as a sintering aid [20]. The CeO2 content has a great influence on the transparency of Y2O3 transparent ceramics. For 1 and 3 at% CeO2 doped samples, the transmittance was slightly lower, and some pores could be seen; while no obvious pores or impurities were observed in the samples doped with 5 at% CeO2, and the transparency clearly increased. The transparency of the sample doped with 10 at% CeO2 was greatly reduced. This indicates that a moderate amount of CeO2 can produce high-quality Y2O3 transparent ceramics.

The crystal structure of cubic phase Y2O3 is shown in Fig. 1(b). The crystal structure of cubic Y2O3 features two distinct types of cation lattices: a centrosymmetric S6 lattice and a noncentrosymmetric C2 lattice. Figure 1(c) shows the XRD patterns of the Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics. All diffraction peaks exhibit sharp features, indicative of exceptional crystallinity. These peak positions align consistently with the established diffraction pattern for the cubic phase of Y2O3. No other phases were observed in any of the samples, suggesting that even up to 10 at% CeO2 is fully incorporated into the Y2O3 lattice. With increasing Ce concentration, the (440) peak gradually shifts to a lower angle, indicating an increase in the lattice constant, as shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM. The specific lattice constant values are detailed in Table S1 in the ESM. The lattice constants of the C1, C3, C5, and C10 samples are 10.60887, 10.6135 Å, 10.62445, and 10.64446 Å, respectively. In fact, the raw material used in the preparation of ceramics is CeO2, so the doped Ce should be in the form of Ce4+. If Y3+ (0.9 Å, coordination number (CN) = 6) is completely substituted by Ce4+ (0.87 Å, CN = 6), the lattice constant should decrease. However, the experimental results show the opposite trend, where the lattice constant increases with increasing Ce content. This confirms the presence of Ce3+ ions. A larger ionic radius of Ce3+ (1.01 Å, CN = 6) results in an increase in the lattice constant [21]. The disparity in ionic radii between Ce3+ and Y3+ is more substantial than that between Ce4+ and Y3+; hence, Ce3+ exerts a more pronounced influence on the Y2O3 lattice than Ce4+ does [15].

Figure 1(d) displays the Raman spectra of Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics. The most prominent peak is observed at approximately 380 cm−1, corresponding to the vibrational mode of Fg+Ag. The optical phonons in cubic Y2O3 can be categorized into two groups. The first group, featuring frequencies below 200 cm−1, relates to the square root ratio of the atomic mass between RE (RE = Y, Ce) atoms and primarily involves the translational motion of octahedral and RE ions [22]. The second group, with frequencies above 300 cm−1, is mostly attributed to the internal vibration of the REO6 octahedron. Certain phonon modes in the Raman spectrum shift to lower frequencies owing to the higher relative atomic mass of Ce than that of Y. The band at approximately 600 cm−1 was ascribed to the stretching of RE–O bonds within the REO6 octahedrons [23]. The broad peaks in the 560–670 cm−1 range become more prominent as the Ce content increases. The broad peaks in the 560–670 cm−1 range become more prominent as the Ce content increases. The broadening of the Raman peaks is usually associated with greater disorder within the oxygen sublattice. This may be related to the different crystal structures of Ce2O3 and Y2O3 as well as the charge imbalance between Ce4+ and Y3+, leading to a decrease in the stability of the Y2O3 lattice [24,25].

The presence of Ce in both the +4 and +3 valence states is well established. The XRD results indicate the presence of Ce3+. However, the presence of Ce4+ remains unclear. To address this uncertainty, XPS tests were conducted, as illustrated in Fig. 1(e). The XPS spectra reveal the coexistence of both Ce4+ and Ce3+, aligning with prior findings in studies of Ce-doped organics, glasses, and ceramics utilized for UV shielding [16,26,27].

### 3.2 Microstructure and densification mechanism

The surface morphology of the Y2O3 ceramics following thermal etching is presented in Figs. 2(a)–2(d). The respective grain sizes for these samples are 2.37, 2.89, 5.38, and 5.72 μm. Figure S5 in the ESM shows the average grain size of Ce-doped Y2O3 ceramics as a function of CeO2 content. Analysis of the grain size distribution reveals that the addition of Ce promoted the grain growth of the Y2O3 ceramics. SEM images of the Y2O3 ceramic fracture surface (Figs. 2(e)–2(h)) indicate that all the fracture modes exhibit transgranular fracture characteristics [28]. Notably, numerous pores are observed in the C1 and C3 samples, marked by arrows in Figs. 2(e) and 2(f). It is well established that pores represent a major source of scattering in transparent ceramics, potentially contributing to the decrease in transmittance [29]. The C5 sample demonstrates high transmittance, which is consistent with the absence of pores. Notably, almost no pores were observed in the C10 sample (Fig. 2(h)); however, the transmittance of the C10 sample was much lower, suggesting that the pores were not the main factor contributing to the lower transmittance of the C10 sample. The primary cause of the decrease in transmittance is linked to the emergence of the second phase (fluorite type), resulting in a decrease in the relative density of the C10 sample. CeO2 and Y2O3 have different crystal structures because they cannot form an infinite solid solution. CeO2 in Y2O3 has a solid solubility limit, and the inclusion of 10 at% CeO2 might have already surpassed this limit. Chavan *et al*. [30] reported that the coexistence of fluorite and cubic phases was detected after sintering 10 at% CeO2 in Y2O3 at 1400 °C. In this work, signs of the emergence of a second phase are also observed in the XRD and Raman data, such as the consistently enhanced (440) diffraction peaks in the XRD pattern and the peak at approximately 600 cm−1 in the Raman spectrum of the C10 sample, both of which are associated with the gradual emergence of the fluorite phase. This trend is also reflected in the relative densities of the samples, as shown in Table S1 and Fig. S6 in the ESM.

Y2O3 transparent ceramics commonly utilize sintering aids such as La2O3 and ZrO2, whose sintering mechanisms have been thoroughly investigated and substantiated [20,31]. The ability of CeO2 to promote the densification sintering of Y2O3, thus serving as a viable sintering aid for transparent Y2O3 ceramics, has been established. However, the intricate mechanisms underlying its action, particularly in terms of grain growth behavior, remain elusive. Consequently, we undertook an in-depth analysis of the sintering mechanism of CeO2 to refine the preparation process of this ceramic material. In Y2O3, Ce is present in both the Ce3+ and Ce4+ valence states, as evidenced by the XRD, XPS, and transmittance spectra, each of which plays a distinct role in the densification process of Y2O3. Ce3+ acts as a trivalent dopant in the Y2O3 lattice, and its introduction does not lead to an increase in defects due to charge balance. The variance in ionic radii (Ce3+: 1.01 Å, Y3+: 0.9 Å) can induce lattice distortion of Y2O3, facilitating rapid diffusion of Y3+ interstitial sites and consequentially enhancing grain boundary mobility. Furthermore, Ce3+ can somewhat decrease the grain mobility activation energy of Y2O3 grain boundary [32]. The promotion of grain boundary migration contributes to densification by facilitating the elimination of pores at grain boundaries, thereby reducing sintering temperatures and enhancing the overall sintering performance of Y2O3 ceramics. Various studies have shown that doping Y2O3 with trivalent rare earth ions such as La3+, Sc3+, Gd3+, Yb3+, and Lu3+ effectively promotes grain boundary migration [33−35]. Therefore, Ce3+ promoted grain boundary migration in Y2O3, facilitating grain growth during the sintering process.

On the other hand, there is a certain amount of Ce4+, which has an impact on the sintering process of Y2O3. The charge imbalance between Ce4+ and Y3+ induces defect formation. Ce4+ doping in Y2O3 can be considered donor doping [36]. Through an interstitial mechanism, the diffusion of Y3+ can be hindered by the presence of interstitial oxygen  Oi″. When CeO2 is doped into Y2O3, the defect reaction is as Reaction (6):

(6) 2CeO2→Y2O32CeY+3OOX+Oi″

Reaction (6) illustrates the substitution of Y3+ by Ce4+ in the lattice, with a ratio of one Ce4+ for every two Y3+ ions. The introduction of additional  Oi″ serves to impede the diffusion of Y3+, thereby diminishing grain boundary mobility and impeding grain growth. The operational mechanism of Ce4+ mirrors that of other tetravalent dopants, such as Zr4+, given their shared capacity to induce  Oi″. Zr4+ is the most extensively utilized sintering aid for Y2O3 transparent ceramics, as its pronounced inhibitory effect on Y2O3 grain growth has been empirically demonstrated [37]. Chen et al*. [32] reported that Ce4+ doping had a smaller suppressive effect on Y2O3 grain boundary mobility than Zr4+ doping, probably because Ce4+ tends to decrease to Ce3+ at high temperatures.

In conclusion, Ce3+ and Ce4+ have opposite effects on Y2O3 grain growth. The gradual increase in grain size with Ce addition, as shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM, suggested that the promotion of grain growth by Ce3+ was more pronounced than the inhibition by Ce4+ under the specific preparation conditions of this study. EDS analysis (Figs. 2(i)–2(l)) of the C5 sample revealed a homogeneous distribution of Y and Ce.

### 3.3 Optical properties

Figure 3(a) displays the transmittance spectra of the Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics across wavelengths ranging from 200 to 850 nm. The transmittances of Y2O3 transparent ceramics doped with 1%, 3%, 5%, and 10% CeO2 at 800 nm are 42.3%, 54.4%, 77.1%, and 15.1%, respectively. It is worth noting that Y2O3 has an exceptionally high melting point of 2430 °C, posing challenges to achieving densification through conventional pressureless sintering. Studies have consistently shown that Y2O3 ceramics without sintering aids do not attain the desired high-quality transparency. Therefore, the production of transparent Y2O3 ceramics typically involves the addition of sintering additives, commonly ZrO2 or La2O3. In this work, even samples doped with 1% CeO2 demonstrated some transparency. This suggests that CeO2 can also be an effective sintering additive, helping to enhance the densification of Y2O3 ceramics and hence the transmittance. However, when the transmittance of the C5 sample was close to the theoretical maximum, the transmittance of the C10 sample decreased significantly. The reason for the reduced transmittance of the C10 sample is discussed in detail in Section 3.2. The transmittance results indicate that high-quality Y2O3 transparent ceramics can be prepared by using CeO2 as a sintering aid through appropriate doping adjustments.

The UV absorption edge experiences a gradual redshift with the introduction of CeO2. The extent of this redshift intensifies with increasing Ce concentration. Notably, the UV cutoff edges of the C1, C3, C5, and C10 samples are 369, 378, 382, and 388 nm, respectively. The UV cutoff edge of the ZrO2-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics is approximately 220 nm. The 5 at% CeO2-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics not only exhibit good transparency in the visible and infrared ranges but also show excellent blocking ability for UV light below 375 nm, including all of UVC (100–280 nm), UVB (280–320 nm), and 90% of UVA (320–400 nm) ranges. Therefore, the 5 at% Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics have excellent potential for serving as a UV shielding window. The band gap is obtained by conversion of the transmittance spectra. Based on the extrapolation shown in Fig. 3(b), the band gap energies of the Y2O3 ceramics are 3.36, 3.28, 3.24, and 3.19 eV, corresponding to 1%, 3%, 5%, and 10% CeO2 doping, respectively. In accordance with Ref. [17], the E*g of the current Ce-doped Y2O3 ceramics is considerably lower than that of pure Y2O3 (i.e., 5.6 eV) or Y2O3 ceramics doped with other elements (e.g., ~4.9 eV for Zr4+ and ~4.4 eV for Gd3+/Eu3+). In this work, even a small amount of doped Ce has a significant effect on the Y2O3 band gap. This is due to the large discrepancy in the bandgap between Y2O3 (5.6 eV) and CeO2 (3.2 eV) or Ce2O3 (2.6 eV) [38]. In contrast, other dopants, such as ZrO2 (5.0 eV), Gd2O3 (5.4 eV), La2O3 (5.5 eV), and Lu2O3 (5.5 eV), have relatively small effects on the bandgap of Y2O3 due to their similar bandgap values [39]. The relatively small change in the bandgap between the 5% and 10% samples is attributed to the close to saturated modulation of the Y2O3 band gap by Ce4+ [40]. Ce4+ plays a major role in band gap narrowing in Y2O3 transparent ceramics. However, despite the addition of more Ce, the lattice structure of Y2O3 can accommodate a limited amount of Ce4+, and most of Ce is transformed to Ce3+; therefore, the increase in the amount of Ce4+ is not significant (as evidenced by the XPS data, Fig. 1(e)), resulting in insignificant changes in the band gap [41]. This suggests that CeO2 doping represents an effective method for customizing the optical bandgap of yttria, particularly for applications requiring UV-shielding windows. The 4f–5d absorption of Ce3+, which is crucial for photoluminescence but not conducive to UV shielding, occurs outside the Y2O3 bandgap, resulting in the absence of absorption peaks within the visible spectrum [42]. This is different from Ce3+:YAG or Ce3+:LuAG, which have strong absorption in the visible range, causing their samples to be yellow, which is unfavorable for use in optical windows. Additionally, the Y2O3 lattice can accommodate some amount of Ce4+, and the absorption band of Ce4+ in Y2O3 is approximately 380–400 nm, precisely in the ultraviolet region [43]. In Ce:Y2O3, the absorption band of Ce3+ in the visible range, which is detrimental for UV shielding, is absent. This property makes Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics ideal for UV shielding, blocking harmful UV radiation while permitting visible light transmission. Figure 3(c) shows images of 5 at% Ce-doped Y2O3 ceramics. With respect to the transparent ceramics, the distant scenery is still clearly visible, proving its excellent window function. Figure S7 in the ESM shows the transmittance of 5 at% Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics in the infrared region, and the samples exhibit ~80% transmittance in the range of 2–6 μm. The extensive range of infrared transmittance exhibited by Y2O3 transparent ceramics is a notable advantage, enabling their utilization as infrared windows.

### 3.4 Vickers hardness

Good mechanical properties are critical to the reliability and processability of UV shielding materials for applications in the form of windows or lenses. As a bulk material, transparent ceramics unequivocally exhibit superior mechanical properties compared to those of UV shielding materials such as organics or films. Figure 4 shows the Vickers hardness of the Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics. The Vickers hardness values of C1, C3, C5, and C10 samples were approximately 7.05±0.11, 7.41±0.31, 7.97±0.17, and 8.21±0.10 GPa, respectively. Yang *et al*. [44] reported the mechanical properties of (Y1−*x*Sc*x*)2O3 transparent ceramics, revealing enhanced mechanical characteristics compared to those of individual Y2O3 or Sc2O3 components. The enhanced mechanical properties can be primarily attributed to solid solution strengthening induced by lattice distortion. In the context of an ideal crystalline structure where atoms occupy precise lattice sites, the introduction of solid solutions, exemplified by CeO2–Y2O3, involves the substitution of Ce ions, thereby creating supplementary space to accommodate the mobility of Y atoms [33]. The difference in ionic radius between Y and Ce induces atomic fluctuations, hindering dislocation motion, amplifying lattice strain, and contributing to additional strength [45].

### 3.5 Thermal properties

Exceptional thermal properties are crucial for material performance in extreme environments characterized by elevated temperatures, with conventional UV shielding materials such as organics or thin films exhibiting shortcomings in this regard. High-temperature resistance has always been one of the strengths of ceramic-based materials, and Y2O3 ceramics have melting points of up to 2400 °C. Furthermore, Y2O3 transparent ceramics demonstrate exceptional thermal conductivity, facilitating efficient heat transfer, a pivotal factor in their application as high-power solid-state lasers. The superior thermal conductivity not only aids in effective heat dissipation and enhancing laser stability but also contributes to sustaining a high-power output. Clearly, the service temperature of Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics is much higher than that of other current UV shielding materials, such as glass and films. A high thermal conductivity ensures rapid heat dissipation at high temperatures, effectively preventing heat buildup to maintain stable performance. Table S2 in the ESM displays the measured values of thermal diffusivity and thermal conductivity for the fabricated Y2O3 ceramics at room temperature, acquired through the laser flash method. The thermal conductivity of pure Y2O3 ceramics is approximately 14 W/(m·K), which is higher than those of YAG and MgAlO4 transparent ceramics, but the preparation process requires the addition of sintering aids, both of which lead to a decrease in thermal conductivity [46]. As the CeO2 concentration increases from 0 to 10 at%, the thermal conductivity gradually decreases from 7.734 to 3.814 W/(m·K), which is in line with expectations for solid-solution compositions. The thermal conductivity of pure CeO2 is lower than that of Y2O3 and theoretically decreases sequentially in transparent ceramics with increasing Ce content [30]. However, the thermal conductivity results in this paper do not simply decrease monotonically with increasing Ce content, which may be related to another factor affecting the thermal conductivity—relative density. Different CeO2 additions lead to different degrees of densification, as discussed in the previous section on the densification behavior of CeO2 on Y2O3 transparent ceramics, with the C5 sample having the highest densification (higher densification of transparent ceramics leading to higher optical transmittance). Typically, high densification implies fewer defects and stronger atomic or molecular interactions, which in turn facilitates more efficient heat transfer. Additionally, based on the temperature dependencies illustrated in Fig. 5, a temperature increase of up to 600 °C results in an approximately 45.5% reduction in the thermal conductivity of the samples.

The similar atomic masses and ionic radii of Ce3+ and Y3+ suggest an expected level of disorder and influence on thermal properties when Ce3+ partially replaces Y3+ cations in the Y2O3 host matrix. The introduction of tetravalent Ce4+ into Y3+ sites may lead to oxygen vacancies for charge compensation, contributing to an additional decrease in thermal conductivity. Phonon propagation, which governs heat transport in dielectric materials, is primarily determined by the mean phonon free path, a crucial parameter for thermal conductivity [47]. The incorporation of Ce in Y2O3 induces structural distortion due to a significant ionic radius mismatch between Ce4+ and Y3+, with the introduced Ce4+ cations acting as substitutional defects, leading to additional phonon scattering, a shortened mean free path, and adverse effects on thermal properties [48]. Table S3 in the ESM shows the thermal conductivity of the Y2O3 transparent ceramics prepared with various sintering aids. Notably, Y2O3 without added sintering aids exhibits the highest thermal conductivity, while the incorporation of other types of sintering aids results in a decrease in thermal conductivity. The thermal conductivity of Y2O3 with 5% Ce, as presented in this study, surpasses that of Y2O3 ceramics employing La2O3 or Sc2O3 as sintering additives.

### 3.6 UV-shielding capacity of Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics

To demonstrate the effectiveness of UV shielding, we utilized UV intensity indicator paper, which changes color from white to purple upon UV exposure. In the experiment, C5 transparent ceramics were covered on a UV intensity indicator paper and exposed to typical UV wavelengths of 254 nm (UVB) and 305 nm (UVA), while the other half served as a control group with no shielding, as illustrated in Fig. 6. After 5 min of irradiation, the observed results revealed no color change in the paper covered with the C5 sample, confirming the efficient shielding of the C5 sample across the full UV wavelength spectrum. This result visually demonstrates the UV shielding capability of C5 transparent ceramics.

### 3.7 Photo, physical, and chemical stability of Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics

Stability is a critical parameter for ensuring the prolonged efficacy of UV-resistant materials, especially in challenging environments [17,26,49]. The stability analysis encompasses evaluating the photostability, thermal stability, and resistance to corrosion under alkaline and acidic conditions for C5 samples. In the thermal stability test, the C5 sample underwent cycles of heating at 600 °C for 24 h, followed by cooling to room temperature. Figure 7(a) shows the transmittance of C5 samples under these conditions every 24 h. Photostability is a critical consideration for UV shielding devices exposed to prolonged radiation. Figure 7(b) shows that following exposure to a UV lamp (254 and 365 nm), the C5 samples maintained their UV-shielding properties and transparency after UV radiation. The C5 sample did not show significant changes after long-term UV irradiation, and no color centers were generated in the sample. This excellent photostability can be attributed to the fact that the energy from UV radiation is not sufficient to break ionic bonds in Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics [49]. As anticipated, the C5 sample exhibited no changes after immersion in a NaOH solution (pH = 14) for 120 h, as shown in Fig. 7(c). C5 samples were submerged in citric acid to evaluate the stability of the material under acidic conditions. Figure 7(d) indicates no notable changes in the UV-shielding capacity or visible light transmittance, with naked-eye observations from the inset images revealing no discernible differences. In addition, the long-term durability of transparent ceramics ensures that they do not require frequent replacement.

### 3.8 Comparison with other UV shielding materials

In various application scenarios, ultraviolet shielding materials such as thin films, coatings, and glass are available to meet diverse needs [8]. This work introduces Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramic as an innovative option for UV shielding. Figure 8 outlines a comprehensive comparison of the mechanical, thermal, and stability properties between transparent ceramics and glass in contrast to films and coatings. Bulk materials such as transparent ceramics and glass outperform films, coatings, and organics in terms of mechanical strength, thermal characteristics, and stability [7]. Regarding stability, both transparent ceramics and glasses surpass coatings and films in chemical stability, as the latter are significantly impacted by chemical exposure. For high-temperature scenarios, while glass already surpasses films and coatings, transparent ceramics have emerged as a superior alternative due to their preparation at extremely high temperatures (approximately 1800 °C) and significantly higher melting points than traditional glass [50]. In terms of processability, transparent ceramics can also utilize various wet forming techniques, such as slip casting, gel casting, and three-dimensional (3D) printing, to achieve net-size forming, thereby enabling the preparation of large-sized, thin sheets and complex-shaped samples [51]. This approach is feasible under the prerequisite conditions for industrial production. In conclusion, after comparing the properties, it can be seen that transparent ceramics are very comprehensive transparent materials for UV shielding. It embodies the advantages of UV shielding glass and offers additional benefits. In contexts where UV shielding glass, such as in windows or lenses, is employed, transparent ceramics may present a superior alternative.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics were fabricated through vacuum sintering at 1800 °C. The effect of a CeO2 solid solution on the optical, mechanical, and thermal properties of Y2O3 transparent ceramics was investigated for the first time. The results indicate that CeO2 serves not only as a UV absorber but also as an effective sintering aid for Y2O3 transparent ceramics. The 5 at% Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics exhibit the optimal optical quality, with an in-line transmittance of ~77% at 800 nm. Due to the absorption in the visible region of Ce4+, the UV cutoff edge of the Y2O3 ceramic shifted from 250 nm to 375 nm. This shift grants UV shielding capabilities to Y2O3 transparent ceramics, resulting in 100% shielding for UVC and UVB and approximately 95% shielding for UVA. The stability of the samples under various corrosive conditions (acid and alkali, UV irradiation, and high temperature) was investigated, and the results proved that the transparent ceramics had excellent stability. The obtained Ce-doped Y2O3 transparent ceramics proved to be better suited for extreme conditions than traditional UV shielding materials such as glass and films. Our work introduces a competitive UV-shielding material and provides comprehensive guidance on its design, preparation, and application as UV-shielding transparent ceramics.
