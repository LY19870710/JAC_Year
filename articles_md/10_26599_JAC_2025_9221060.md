## 1 Introduction

The generation of electricity that is affordable and environmentally friendly represents a pivotal challenge in steering our society toward a sustainable future. Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) have garnered significant attention as promising solutions because of their remarkable efficiency and low environmental footprint [1−3]. In particular, protonic ceramic fuel cells (PCFCs) have attracted considerable interest for their capacity to function at low temperatures (LTs; ≤ 600 °C), uphold sufficient electrolyte conductivity (≥ 0.01 S·cm−1), and exhibit low activation energy, thereby establishing them as viable contenders in the commercial arena [4−8]. Nevertheless, as the operating temperature decreases, a significant hurdle arises from the constrained electrocatalytic activity of the cathode, predominantly due to the sluggish kinetics of the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) [4,6,9,10]. The development of highly active and durable cathode materials is crucial for PCFC applications.

The morphology of cathode particles significantly influences microstructural parameters such as triple phase boundary (TPB) density, surface area density, connectivity, and tortuosity factors, which in turn affect transport coefficients, such as chemical‒species diffusivity, electronic conductivity, and ionic conductivity, ultimately determining cathode performance [11,12]. Hence, the particle configuration, which encompasses several variables, including size, particle size distribution (PSD), shape, and packing conditions, is crucial for establishing the electrode microstructure and the total number of active sites [11]. These variables are intricately linked with processing parameters such as the sintering temperature and duration [13]. However, current research is predominantly focused on reducing the particle size and employing surface modification techniques such as infiltration [14], atomic layer deposition [14], and exsolution [15] to obtain nanoparticles. Smaller or nanosized particles typically offer increased surface area, increasing the availability of active sites and facilitating improved diffusion of fuel and oxygen, thereby enhancing electrode reactions [14,16]. Moreover, maintaining a relatively narrow PSD ensures uniform contact, minimizing areas of potential high resistance or poor connectivity, thereby promoting more efficient charge transfer [17,18]. Despite these advancements, cathode particle shapes in PCFC cathodes mostly describe spherical or quasi-spherical geometries in the literature. The rarely explored shapes, such as octahedrons or cubes, could reveal impressive electrochemical performance because of their ability to expose special lattice planes with high electrocatalytic activity. Accordingly, designing and tailoring microstructures by controlling particle geometry shapes represents a promising avenue for future research in enhancing PCFC performance.

Layered Pr1.5Ba1.5Cu3O7−*δ* (PBC) [19,20], recognized as the stacking of one Pr*x*Ba1−*x*CuO*y* perovskite with a complex arrangement including a PrO layer sandwiched in two CuO5 pyramids (Fig. 1), has promising electrocatalytic potential because of its ability to accommodate diverse material properties through different structural configurations. Hence, in this assignment, we investigate perovskite-related PBC as a standalone cathode for BaZr0.1Ce0.7Y0.2O3−*δ* (BZCY)-based PCFC, which enhances cathode performance by controlling the growth of cubic PBC particles. Notably, by increasing the calcination temperature from 900 to 950 °C, PBC particles transition from spherical to cubic shapes, with further growth at 1000 °C. Consequently, the particle-cubic PBC cathode obtained at 950 °C (PBC-950) showed enhanced electrochemical performance with good durability, underscoring the importance of exploring materials with specific particle geometries to activate specialized properties at particular lattice planes and thereby meeting specific requirements in related electrocatalytic fields.

## 2 Experimental

PBC and BZCY electrolyte powders were synthesized via a citric acid‒nitrate gel combustion method described elsewhere in detail [21]. The raw materials used are Pr6O11, Cu(NO3)2·3H2O, BaCO3, Zr(NO2)4·5H2O, Ce(NO3)3·6H2O, and Y(NO4)3·6H2O. The resulting ash-like PBC powders were calcined at 900, 950, and 1000 °C for 3 h to produce powder materials denoted PBC-900, PBC-950, and PBC-1000, respectively, while the BZCY powders were obtained by calcination at 1000 °C for 3 h in air. All powders were identified via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; DX-2700BH, Haoyuan Instrument, China) to analyze the phase structure parameters. The morphology and crystallinity of the as-prepared powders were examined via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2 F20, FEI, USA). The surface chemical states of PBC-900, PBC-950, and PBC-1000 were analyzed via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific, USA), and the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller analyzer (BET; ASAP 2460, Micromeritics, USA) was used to assess the specific surface area of these materials. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Phenom XL G2, Thermo Scientific, USA) was used to investigate particle geometry variations among PBC-900, PBC-950, and PBC-1000. Furthermore, to explore the impact of particle geometry variations within the cathode on fuel cell performance, BZCY-based cells were fabricated, in which BZCY and NiO-BZCY (65 : 35 weight ratio) served as the electrolyte and anode, respectively. Green BZCY-based half cells were prepared via co-pressing and subsequently calcined at 1350 °C for 6 h. PBC-900 cathode ink obtained by mixing thoroughly with a 10 wt% ethylcellulose-terpineol binder was applied onto dense BZCY surfaces via the screen-printing technique and then fired at 900, 950, and 1000 °C for 3 h in an air atmosphere to achieve porous PBC-900, PBC-950, and PBC-1000 cathode layers. As a result, single-cell NiO-BZCY|BZCY|PBC-900 (PBC-950/PBC-1000) was obtained. The assembled cells were evaluated in a four-probe configuration via an electrochemical workstation (Squidstat Plus, Admiral Instruments, USA) at 550–700 °C. Under an atmosphere with static ambient air and wet hydrogen (~3% H2O, 30 mL·min−1) as the oxidant and fuel, the *V*–*I*/*P*–*I* curves of the cell were collected in linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) mode, and the electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were recorded under open circuit conditions from high to low frequencies in the range of 1 MHz–0.1 Hz with an AC amplitude of 5 mV. The microstructures of the cells were analyzed via SEM.

## 3 Results and discussion

The particle morphology is influenced by the calcination temperature and duration, which involves the heating of particles to the point where they adhere and bond together. At elevated temperatures, surface atom diffusion accelerates, lowering the surface energy and promoting particle rearrangement. This can lead to an Ostwald ripening effect where smaller particles dissolve and redeposit onto larger ones, potentially causing particle coalescence [22,23]. Then, the evolution of the particle geometry is systematically analyzed. As depicted in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b), increasing the calcination temperature from 900 to 950 °C causes smaller spherical/quasi-spherical particles to coalesce into larger cubic particles, and these cubes continuously grow as the calcination temperature further increases to 1000 °C (Fig. 2(c)). The average sizes of the spherical/quasi-spherical and cubic particles obtained at 900, 950, and 1000 °C for 3 h were 298 nm (Fig. 2(d)), 385 nm (Fig. 2(e)), and 571 nm (Fig. 2(f)), respectively. This phenomenon might be interrelated with the exposure of specific crystal facets, which is likely linked to slower crystal growth kinetics and better thermodynamic stability [24,25]. Subsequently, TEM was used to identify the exposed crystal facets of the cubes. As shown in Fig. 2(g), the well-formed crystallized PBC cubes offer promising opportunities for creating a finely porous cathode, facilitating rapid gaseous diffusion and effective electrochemical reactions. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) analysis (Fig. 2(h)) confirmed the specific crystal facets of the PBC cubes, with a lattice spacing of 3.90 Å for both the equivalent (100) and (010) lattice planes, indicating the exposure of the {100} facets on the surface of the PBC cubes. Moreover, the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) results (Fig. 2(i)) further corroborate the exposure of the {100} facets. Notably, the TEM energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping results (Figs. 2(j)–2(m)) confirmed the uniform distribution of all the metal elements containing Pr, Ba, and Cu without any element segregation, suggesting a homogeneous phase.

To analyze the formation process and influencing factors for the cubes, the as-prepared PBC powders were also treated under various calcination durations. When treated at 950 °C for 1 h, most of the PBC particles are cubic/quasicubic (Fig. 3(a)), confirming that this calcination temperature could offer sufficient energy to coalesce smaller spherical/quasi-spherical particles into cubes. However, many spherical PBC particles still exist, which should possess a slightly larger particle size than the coalesced particles, demanding slightly higher energy to fuse. Interestingly, almost all the PBC particles changed into cubes after treatment at 950 °C for 2 h (Fig. 3(b)), and the corresponding average size was 360 nm (Fig. 3(c)). This phenomenon might be mainly derived from the further coalescence of the remaining spherical particles. Even so, the cubes are not evenly distributed, with relatively large differences in cube size. Successively, the PBC cubes continue to grow with increasing calcination duration to 3 h (Fig. 2(b)), achieving a more uniform cube size distribution (Fig. 2(e)), thus indicating the occurrence of cube coalescence. Then, the possible cube formation and growth mechanism are analyzed. As illustrated in Fig. 3(d), cube formation occurs in the process of particle coalescence [26,27]. First, the smaller PBC particles begin to thaw on larger particles (process d1–d2) when calcined at 950 °C, which could provide sufficient energy to activate atomic motion. However, to achieve complete particle fusion, heat should be transferred from the outside of the particles to the inside by heat conduction. When the calcination duration is sufficient, fusion (process d2–d3–d4) can occur gradually, and the melted PBC molecules can spread out due to surface tension. When the calcination duration increases continuously until complete fusion, the smaller PBC particles collapse (process d4–d5). The collapsed melting PBC molecules could recrystallize on the larger particle surface, causing particle growth and forming quasi-cubes (process d5–d6), which might be potentially related to the much slower growth rate of the {100} facets. As a result, in the continuation of the recrystallization process, the quasi-cubic particles are gently regularized (process d6–d7) due to the existence of surface tension and the tendency to decrease surface energy, ultimately generating regular cubes during the further spread of PBC molecules.

The recrystallization and probably slower growth rate of the {100} facets determine the formation of PBC cubes, which is correlated with the crystal structure. As illustrated in Fig. 3(e), owing to the 8-fold coordination with oxygens of Pr cations, in addition to the formation of a PrO layer between two pyramids, the Pr cations located on Pr(Ba) sites could lead to the generation of oxygen vacancies on the (001) facet. Fascinatingly, the oxygen vacancies originating from the coordination deficiency of Pr(Ba) sites could only occur at the O2 sites, which can be attributed to the need for CuO5 pyramids (Cu2 uniting 4 O1 atoms in the horizontal direction and 1 O3 atom in the vertical direction) and linear O‒Cu‒O bonding styles (Cu1 uniting at least 2 O3 atoms) to maintain the PBC crystal structure. This could also result in the existence of oxygen vacancies in the two equivalent (100) and (010) facets, both of which are perpendicular to the (001) facet and share oxygen vacancies. As a result, the (001) facet has significantly more oxygen vacancies than the (100) and (010) facets. Because oxygen vacancies can interact with the surrounding atmosphere during calcination in air, various oxygen species containing molecular oxygen (O2), atomic oxygen (O), superoxide (O2−), peroxide (O22−), and oxygen ions (O2−) can be adsorbed onto the {100} facets, which leads to relatively slow growth kinetics along the  direction (Figs. 3(f) and 3(g)) compared with other crystal orientations in the PBC crystal system under highly active atomic motion. This could lead to the retention of {100} crystal facets in the particle growth and coalescing processes, and the (001) facet plays a leading role in determining the cube size. Nevertheless, under actual calcination conditions, the particle geometry is spherical below 900 °C, which is likely related to the properties of the molten PBC molecules, including their adhesion and surface tension. When the particle is relatively small, the {100} crystal facets could not be exposed because the surface tension could play the leading role (*E*0 S, Fig. 3(h)), forming spherical particles with lower surface energy. In contrast, as the particle increase in coalescing procsses at 950 °C, as depicted in Fig. 3(i), the enlarged {100} facets with adequate oxygen vacancies can adsorb sufficient oxygen species to form a relatively stable state (*E*1 > *E*S, Fig. 3(h)). At this moment, the surface tension and adhesion cannot destroy this stable state because it does not possess enough high energy. Then, the {100} facets are retained in the crystal growth process, and finally, the molton PBC molecules can be recrystallized to extend the {100} facets, thus exposing the {100} facets with larger areas to form PBC cubes. In addition, the vertical view shown in Figs. 3(j) and 3(k) also show a visual expansion process of the (001) facet, which can grow and expand in the [100], [010], [* 1¯00], and [0 1¯0] directions equally (Figs. 3(j) and 3(k) severally corresponding to Figs. 3(d5) and 3(d6)), ultimately evolving from the collapsed melting state to the quasi-cube state.

However, the cubes also grow with increasing calcination temperature to 1000 °C, and the average sizes of the cubes obtained at 1000 °C for 1 and 2 h are 422 nm (Figs. S1(a) and S1(b) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) and 467 nm (Figs. S1(c) and S1(d) in the ESM), respectively. This finding indicates that increasing the calcination temperature and duration could provide considerable energy for cube growth, which is potentially derived from cube/quasi-cube coalescence. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the smaller cubes/quasi-cubes begin to thaw (processes I and II) when more energy is acquired, thus forming semi-melted spherical/quasi-spherical particles (processes II and III). As the melting process progresses, the semi-melting spherical particles decrease (processes III–V), and the melting PBC molecules flow along the {100} crystal facets. Owing to the complete fusion of the particles (processes V–VII), the melted PBC molecules clad the original cube, thus producing larger cubes via the co-action of surface tension and surface energy (processes VII and VIII).

The BZCY-PBC XRD pattern reveals two distinct phases (Fig. 5(a)), indicating excellent chemical compatibility between BZCY and PBC at 1000 °C. The peak shifts toward lower angles with increasing calcination temperature from 900 to 1000 °C (Figs. 5(a) and 5(b)), which corresponds to lattice expansion according to the Bragg equation (2d*sin*θ* = *nλ*, where *d* is the interplanar spacing, *θ* is the diffraction angle, *n* is the diffraction order, and *λ* is the wavelength). In addition, the XRD Rietveld refinement profiles (Figs. 5(c)–5(e)) reveal that PBC-900, PBC-950, and PBC-1000 exhibit tetragonal layered perovskite-related structures with *P*4/*mmm* space group symmetry, verifying the lattice expansion (Table S1 in ESM), and the expanded lattice planes could be affirmed by the increased lattice spacings derived from the Rietveld refinement results (Table S2 in the ESM). This phenomenon could be attributed to the increased atomic vibration and the relaxation of internal stresses with increasing temperature. XPS was subsequently employed to analyze the surface chemical states to provide further evidence. Owing to the overlap of the Cu 2p and Pr 3d peaks (Fig. 5(f)), both the Cu 2p1/2 + Pr 3d3/2 and Cu 2p3/2 + Pr 3d5/2 energy regions could be fitted to four components with decreasing binding energy (BE): Cu3+ 2p1/2, Cu2+ 2p1/2, Pr4+ 3d3/2, Pr3+ 3d3/2, Cu3+ 2p3/2, Cu2+ 2p3/2, Pr4+ 3d5/2, and Pr3+ 3d5/2 [28−32]. As shown in Table 1, the ratios of Pr4+/Pr3+ for these three powders have almost no variation, with ratios of 0.67:0.33, 0.68:0.32, and 0.67:0.33 for PBC-900, PBC-950, and PBC-1000, respectively, indicating a weak relationship with changes in particle geometry. Interestingly, the ratios of Cu2+/Cu3+ for PBC-900, PBC-950, and PBC-1000 were 0.73:0.27, 0.55:0.45, and 0.56:0.44, respectively. This phenomenon could be attributed to the exposure of {100} facets, which could expose more Cu3+ cations on the surface because the formation of CuO6 octahedra in the PBC structure determines the location of Cu3+ on the {100} facets. Hence, more effective Cu2+–O–Cu3+ electron hopping transition paths appeared on the cube surface, providing higher electrocatalytic activity, accelerating the ORR process, and thereby promoting the cathode reaction. Interestingly, Fig. 5(g) shows XPS spectra of O 1s for PBC-900, PBC-950, and PBC-1000, indicating different oxygen species on the surface: lattice oxygen (Olat), highly oxidative oxygen species (O−/O22−), surface hydroxyl groups and/or carbonate (OH−/CO32−), and adsorbed molecular water (H2O) [33]. The ratio between O−/O22− associated with the surface oxygen vacancy and Olat reflects the oxygen vacancy content [34−36]. Surprisingly, as listed in Table 2, the calculated ratio increases from 0.28 for PBC-900 to 1.19 for PBC-950, indicating that the exposure of {100} facets enhances the surface oxygen vacancy concentration, facilitating the ORR by promoting oxygen adsorption and activation at active sites [37,38]. Conversely, the ratio for PBC-1000 decreases to 0.44, likely due to reduced exposure of {100} facets with cube growth.

To evaluate the effect of particle geometry variation inside the cathode on fuel cell performance, the performance of PBC-900, PBC-950, and PBC-1000 cathodes for PCFC applications was comparatively assessed. The PBC-900 cell outputs peak power densities (PPDs) of 1073, 759, 384, and 168 mW·cm−2 at 700, 650, 600, 550 and 500 °C, respectively (Fig. 6(a)). In contrast, the particle-cubic PBC-950 cell has PPD outputs of 1982, 1281, 797, and 477 mW·cm−2 at the corresponding temperatures (Fig. 6(b)), whereas the corresponding PPD values for the PBC-1000 cell are 1203, 873, 525, and 252 mW·cm−2 (Fig. 6(c)). Specifically, the cell power performance significantly improved when the materials transitioned from the PBC-900 cathode to the PBC-950 cathode (Fig. 6(d)), demonstrating that compared with the spherical/quasi-spherical PBC particles, the PBC cubes could offer increased electrocatalytic activity. Nevertheless, the PBC-1000 cathode reveals substantial power deterioration (Fig. 6(d)), being closely interrelated with much larger PBC cubes, which provide fewer TPBs. Since the three cells are identical except for the cathode, the disparity in cell performance could be attributed to the cathode variation. As a result, the PBC-950 cell demonstrates a much higher power output than the reported Co-based and Cu-based single-phase cathodes at both 700 °C (Fig. 6(e)) and 600 °C (Fig. S2 in the ESM), which is engaging. The power output of the single-phase PBC-950 cathode is also much greater than that of the previously reported PDC–BCC–SBCC–CuO quaternary cathode [20], which was obtained via an in* *situ* Pr diffusion reaction between Sm0.2Ce0.8O2−*δ* and PBC, highlighting the superiority of the particle-cubic PBC-950 cathode. Furthermore, the operational durability is crucial. As shown in Fig. 6(f), both the PBC-950 and PBC-1000 cells possess excellent stability with almost no degradation over 200 h at 600 °C, whereas the PBC-900 cathode only works stably for approximately 130 h with a subsequent apparent attenuation, indicating that the architecture of the cathode with cubes could provide a favorable combination of operational durability and high performance.

The enhanced electrocatalysis of the particle-cubic PBC-950 cathode is further evidenced by typical EIS. As depicted in Figs. 7(a) and 7(b), the high-frequency intercept denotes the cell ohmic resistance (*R*O), whereas the difference between the two intercepts corresponds to the interfacial polarization resistance (*R*P). The minimal polarization loss with the lowest *R*P (Table 3) in the PBC-950 cell reveals the most rapid cathode reaction according to the Butler‒Volmer equation (* i≈(i0/RP)η, where i* is current density (proportional to the reaction rate), * i0 is the exchange current density (a measure of reaction kinetics), and  η is the overpotential (driving force for the reaction)), indicating the fastest electrochemical reaction kinetics, which is related to easier charge transfer, mass transport, and surface reactions [46]. Similarly, R*O for the PBC-950 cell exhibited a similar trend, with the lowest *R*O values occurring at 550–700 °C (Table 3). Owing to the identical electrolyte and structure in the three cells, the ionic resistance of the electrolyte should theoretically possess a weak discrepancy. The noticeable decrease in ohmic loss can be attributed primarily to the formation of particular cubes in the PBC-950 cathode, which exposes specific crystal facets, leading to an improvement in the number of efficient electron hopping transition paths of Cu2+–O–Cu3+ on the surface of the cubes, as evidenced by the Cu 2p XPS results (Fig. 5(f)). This increases the efficiency of electron transfer, thus reducing the cathode resistance and its contribution to *R*O [47]. Additionally, the improved charge transfer provides a highly efficient ORR, producing abundant oxygen ions at the electrolyte‒cathode interface and accelerating proton transfer, thereby reducing ohmic loss. The above two factors influence the ohmic resistance. Both *R*O and *R*P decrease with increasing temperature, indicating that the electrochemical reaction processes are thermally activated. As shown in the Arrhenius relationships in Figs. 7(c) and 7(d), the activation energies (*E*a) of *R*O for the PBC-900, PBC-950, and PBC-1000 samples are 0.35, 0.36, and 0.38 eV, respectively, whereas the corresponding *E*a values of *R*P are 1.44, 1.19, and 1.13 eV, respectively. The slightly faster *R*O deterioration in the PBC-1000 cell than in the PBC-950 cell reveals the slightly quicker attenuation of the proton-transfer efficiency, whereas the *E*a value for *R*P is closely connected with the decay of the ORR process of the cathode.

Distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis, a tool for deconvoluting a complicated EIS to isolated electrochemical processes, was also performed. As shown in Fig. 7(e) and Fig. S3 in the ESM (DRT results at 600 °C in the ESM), on the basis of the different frequency ranges, at least three distinctive peaks can be observed, denoted as high frequency (HF), medium frequency (MF), and low frequency (LF). These peaks correspond to different key reaction steps involved in the electrode [48]. The narrower LF peaks for the PBC-950 sample than for the other two samples suggest easier gas diffusion, oxygen adsorption, and dissociation processes. The difference in MF peaks related to surface exchange (oxygen reduction) and ionic diffusion processes illustrates that the ionic diffusion of oxygen ions and protons significantly affects the superior performance of PBC-950. In addition, the difference in the HF peaks corresponding to charge transfer at the interface clearly influences the cathode polarization reaction processes. The EIS plots were subsequently fitted with an LR(*R*H*Q*H)(*R*M*Q*M)(*R*L*Q*L) equivalent circuit for further in-depth analysis of these three electrode processes [49−52]. For much easier proton (H+) generation and migration than oxygen ion (O2−), and because the anodes in the three different cells are identical, the polarization discrepancy could be attributed to the cathode variance. All resistances, including resistances in the LF range (*R*L), resistances in the MF range (*R*M), and resistances in the HF range (*R*H), for the PBC-950 cell have the lowest values (Table 4), revealing the fastest electrode processes. The lower *R*L with a clearly decreased *E*a for both PBC-950 and PBC-1000 cells (Fig. 7(f)) is correlated with the formation of cubes, which expose {100} crystal facets with abundant oxygen vacancies, benefiting oxygen adsorption/dissociation. However, the higher *R*L with a significantly lower *E*a for PBC-1000 cell than for the PBC-950 cell could be attributed to the larger cubes, which can create larger pores that are conducive to O2 transportation. Even so, fewer cubes with discounted TPBs work against O2 adsorption, thus increasing the *R*L values. Moreover, the decreased *R*M with a lower *E*a in particle-cubic PBC-950 and PBC-1000 cells (Table 4 and Fig. 7(g)) could indicate an improved charge transfer ability for the production of oxygen ions (* Oad+e−→Oad−,  Oad−+e−→Oad2−). The lower R*M reveals faster oxygen diffusion, which is closely related to improved efficient electronic transport for more efficient Cu2+–O–Cu3+ electron hopping transition paths on the cube surface. In addition, the larger reaction surface area at an even larger cube in the PBC-1000 cathode leads to a lower *E*a for *R*M, whereas fewer TPBs derived from fewer cubes restrict oxygen ionic diffusion and transfer. As expected, both *R*H and the corresponding *E*a reveal a profound reduction in the particle‒cubic cathode (Table 4 and Fig. 7(h)), demonstrating a much faster reaction rate and a greater reaction potential between protons and oxygen ions at the cathode‒electrolyte interface. Interestingly, the higher *E*a value of *R*H in the PBC-1000 cathode may stem from the lower interface contact area, providing fewer reaction sites for proton transfer. Hence, although the specific surface areas for PBC cubes are significantly lower than those for spherical particles (Table 5), the exposed {100} facets on the cube surface could also lead to faster electrochemical reactions for improved surface oxygen vacancies and efficient electronic transportation.

Figures 8(a)–8(f) present the microstructures of the three cell types after electrochemical performance testing. In all the cathode layers, the particles interconnect well with each other, facilitating gas diffusion and electrochemical reactions. Notably, the particle geometry of the PBC-900 cathode is spherical/quasi-spherical (Fig. 8(a)), whereas the cubes in the PBC-950 and PBC-1000 cathodes could be maintained after cell testing (Figs. 8(c) and 8(e)), and the corresponding average sizes are 360 nm (Fig. S4(a) in the ESM), 454 nm (Fig. S4(b) in the ESM), and 627 nm (Fig. S4(c) in the ESM), respectively. Both types of particles could grow further after cell testing, as evidenced by the particle analysis results before testing (Figs. S5(a)–S5(f) in the ESM). Closer interlinkages with smaller cores in all three cathodes are observed (Figs. S5(a), S5(c), and S5(e) in the ESM), and the average sizes are 345 nm (Figs. S5(a) and S5(b) in the ESM), 390 nm (Figs. S5(c) and S5(d) in the ESM), and 579 nm (Figs. S5(e) and S5(f) in the ESM). The similar morphology with good adherence in the three cells suggests that the cell performance discrepancy should be ascribed to the variation in the cathode particle geometry (Figs. 8(b), 8(d), and 8(f)). Furthermore, a reaction mechanism at the cathode interface is proposed for both particle-spherical and particle-cubic cathodes (Figs. 9(a)–9(d)). Although the overall ORR and incorporation into PBC spherical particles or cubes are similar (Figs. 9(a) and 9(c)), oxygen dissociative adsorption (process I: * O2→2Oad) could be promoted because of the remarkably improved surface oxygen vacancies at the cube surface derived from the exposure of the {100} facets. The adsorbent oxygen atoms ( Oad) diffuse along the surface to the PBC-BZCY-gas TPBs or are injected into the bulk phase of PBC spherical particles to occupy the oxygen vacancies (process II:  Oad+VO,inner⋅⋅+2e−→OO×; Fig. 9(b)), slowing process I. In contrast,  Oad could easily occupy the surface oxygen vacancies (process III:  Oad+2e−+VO,cube−surface⋅⋅→OO,cube−surface×,  Oad−+e−+VO,cube−surface⋅⋅→OO,cube−surface×, and  Oad2−+VO,cube−surface⋅⋅→OO,cube−surface×; Fig. 9(d)) with no injection process for PBC cubes, advancing the process I. Additionally, the improved efficiency of electron transfer on the cube surface increases the generation of oxygen ions (process IV:  Oad+e−→Oad−,  Oad−+e−→Oad2−,  Oad+2e−→Oad2−), benefiting oxygen transfer to the BZCY-PBC cube interface (refer to process III). Thus, the PBC cubes admit much quicker oxygen ion generation and transfer, accelerating the overall fuel cell reaction 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O. However, with relatively abundant oxygen ions at the cathode‒electrolyte interface under working conditions, the proton migration distance affects the effective reaction area, contributing to the reaction rate between protons and oxygen ions (charge transfer, 2H+ + O2− → H2O). As illustrated in Fig. 9(d),  Oad could capture the surface oxygen vacancies at the cube surface quickly, forming a channel for proton transfer via proton rotation and jumping, ultimately enhancing proton migration. This is evidenced by the lower R*H results obtained by the EIS analysis, which could reveal the proton properties of PBC with changing microstructures. Although the cubes may possess slightly worse particle content and cathode interface contact than the spherical particles, the similar proton-transfer reaction potential and much more rapid charge transfer between H+ and O2− could further reveal the superiority of employing PBC cubes as cathodes. This is evidenced by the similar *R*O deterioration with a small difference in *E*a values for the three cells (Fig. 7(c)) and the significantly lower *R*H values with much slower deterioration (Fig. 7(h) and Table 4). In addition, with obvious cube growth when the PBC-950 cathode (average size of 390 nm/454 nm for cubes before/after testing) was switched to the PBC-1000 cathode (average size of 579 nm/627 nm for cubes before/after testing), the cathode interface contact area between the PBC cube and BZCY clearly decreased (Figs. 9(e) and 9(f)), decreasing the number of electrochemical reaction sites by a wide margin, resulting in much inferior electrochemical performance outputs in PBC-1000 cells. Overall, the exposure of {100} crystal facets by controlling the formation of PBC cubes could result in integrated preeminent cell electrochemical performance with good durability.

## 4 Conclusions

Advancing cathodes with exceptional activity is crucial for improving PCFC performance, impelling researchers to investigate novel materials, approaches, and configurations. Configuring electrode particles with specific particle geometries may reveal great potential for the exposure of special lattice planes with high electrocatalytic activity and specialized properties, providing more opportunities for designing highly catalytically active electrode materials. In this assignment, perovskite-related PBC is successfully applied as a stand-alone cathode for PCFC, and the amelioration of PBC is attempted via the controllable growth of PBC cubes. Consequently, the PBC particle geometry changes from spherical to cubic at 950 °C, resulting in the exposure of {100} crystal facets on the cube surface. This creates more efficient Cu2+–O–Cu3+ electron hopping transition paths and much higher surface oxygen vacancy concentrations, leading to higher electrocatalytic activity with promoted oxygen adsorption and activation at active sites and thus facilitating the ORR process. As a result, the particle-cubic PBC-950 cathode shows markedly enhanced cell performance, with power outputs of 1982, 1281, 797, and 477 mW·cm−2, along with *R*P of 0.028, 0.069, 0.164, and 0.376 Ω·cm2 at 700, 650, 600, and 550 °C, respectively. In comparison, the PBC-950 cells significantly outperform both the PBC-900 and the PBC-1000 cells. Although PBC is first improved by configuring a specific particle geometry, its performance surpasses that of PCFC, which employs Co-based and Cu-based single-phase cathodes reported in the literature. Crucially, the favorable electrochemical performance does not compromise its durability, indicating the high potential for the utilization of particle‒cubic cathodes in PCFCs. This attempt to configure materials with specific particle geometries could be significant for the possible exposure of special crystal facets with specialized properties, which could meet particular needs and provide a path toward new material designs in PCFC and related electrocatalytic fields.
