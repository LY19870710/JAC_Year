## 1 Introduction

The clinical management of malignant bone tumors in the maxillofacial region primarily involves surgical resection. However, postoperative recurrence rates have been reported to reach as high as 10% [1]. Moreover, substantial bone tissue defects resulting from surgery can lead to oral dysfunction [2], thereby compromising the physical and mental well-being of patients. Consequently, addressing postoperative bone defects and preventing the recurrence of maxillofacial tumors has emerged as a significant clinical challenge. According to various studies, three-dimensional (3D)-printed personalized bone tissue engineering scaffolds hold considerable potential for the precise repair of complex bone defects while serving as drug delivery platforms to inhibit tumor recurrence [3,4]. Compared with traditional drug delivery methods, photothermal therapy (PTT) is advantageous because of its minimal invasiveness, high targeting ability, easy control, and prolonged service life [5]. This approach effectively reduces damage to nontargeted areas and prevents tumor recurrence [6]. Therefore, photothermal functional bone scaffolds are expected to become key medical devices for repairing bone defects after bone tumor surgery in the next generation, and their material design, structural design, and precise manufacturing have significant research value.

Biocompatibility and mechanical properties are the primary technical indicators in designing photothermal functional bone scaffold materials. Compared with metals and polymers, ceramics exhibit superior biocompatibility, effectively mitigating the risks of local toxicity and inflammation associated with metal ions and degradation products from polymers during photothermal therapy [7]. Among them, silicon nitride (Si3N4) stands out because of its excellent biological stability and high mechanical load-bearing capacity. Its mechanical properties outperform those of traditional bioactive ceramics such as hydroxyapatite (HA) and tricalcium phosphate (TCP), indicating excellent application prospects in maxillofacial bone reconstruction, which frequently results in mechanical stress [8]. Recent studies have revealed that Si3N4 has remarkable biological stability and specific biological activities, including antibacterial properties, osteoconductivity, and osteoinductivity [9−11]. Katsaros *et al.* [12] incorporated Si3N4 powder into bone cement and conducted a series of antibacterial and cytotoxicity assessments. The results revealed that the inclusion of Si3N4 reduced bacterial adhesion by more than 90% while increasing cell viability. Zhang *et al.* [13] introduced 10 and 20 wt% Si3N4 into propylene carbonate and conducted *in vitro* experiments. These findings indicated that the inclusion of Si3N4 endowed the composite materials with osteoinductive properties, thereby promoting the proliferation and differentiation of stem cells. Zhan *et al.* [14] performed a comparative analysis of the *in vitro* biological responses of five different materials (poly(ether-ether-ketone) (PEEK), zirconia toughened aluminum (ZTA), Si3N4, surface-treated silicon nitride (ST-Si3N4), and Ti–6Al–4V) to standardized intervertebral discs. These results showed that Si3N4 exhibited superior osteogenic effects while inhibiting bone resorption. In addition, Lee *et al.* [15] introduced GO into Si3N4 and reported that adding GO promoted the induced differentiation of osteoblasts and improved the osteoinductive performance of Si3N4. These results showed that Si3N4 had superior osteogenic effects while inhibiting bone resorption. Composite Si3N4 ceramics have excellent biocompatibility and mechanical properties, with a certain degree of osteoinductive activity, and are ideal matrix materials for bone scaffolds. However, the inherent photothermal function of Si3N4 is relatively weak, which remains a critical challenge in contemporary research.

Photothermal efficiency primarily refers to the ability of a material to convert near-infrared laser energy into thermal energy. Generally, materials with darker colors exhibit higher light-to-thermal conversion efficiencies. Moreover, most clinical biomaterials still lack inherent photothermal properties. Therefore, incorporating a secondary phase with photothermal capabilities represents the principal solution. Photothermally functional additive phases can be broadly classified into two categories: nonferrous metallic elements (such as Cu and Fe [16,17]) and dark inorganic substances (e.g., elemental C [18−20]). Liu *et al*. [21] effectively regulated the photothermal conversion efficiency of bioactive glass-ceramic scaffolds by incorporating various concentrations of Cu, Fe, Mn, and Co. This approach successfully inhibited tumor cell proliferation. However, the concentrations of these metal elements must be meticulously regulated to avoid toxicological risks and adverse effects on human health. In contrast, carbon-containing comelted ceramics, such as “black glass” silicon oxycarbide (SiOC), exhibit excellent biocompatibility and photothermal properties [22,23], thereby ensuring the balance between photothermal efficiency and biological safety. However, the design and preparation of photothermal Si3N4 materials have not yet been reported. Moreover, SiOC materials demonstrate good compatibility with Si3N4 ceramics. Therefore, SiOC/Si3N4 composite ceramics hold promise for overcoming the limitations of the weak photothermal functionality of single-phase Si3N4 materials.

In addition to the material system, the design of porous structures and precise processing are two other challenges in fabricating photothermal functional bone scaffolds. Microsized porous structures have been suggested to stimulate cell proliferation and differentiation and the expression of osteogenesis-related genes [24]. Porous structures have a noticeable effect on the biological activity of bone scaffolds. Tan *et al.* [25] designed a tail-like lattice structure scaffold based on body-centered cubic (BCC) units, which facilitated cell growth and adhesion. Liang *et al.* [26] developed scaffolds with a trabecular bone-like morphology via the Voronoi tessellation method. Compared with traditional lattice structures, these scaffolds exhibit superior ability to induce cell proliferation and differentiation. However, the aforementioned porous units predominantly consist of rod-like connecting structures, which may lead to issues related to stress concentrations in bone repair scaffolds. Compared with traditional structures, the triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) is a special type of surface that exhibits periodic repetition in three-dimensional space and has zero curvature. This unique characteristic holds promise for alleviating stress concentration under loading. Owing to its high porosity, ease of parameter adjustment, and superior mechanical properties, TPMS has garnered increasing attention in bone repair scaffolds. For example, Zhu *et al.* [27] designed a cancellous characteristic bone scaffold on the basis of the TPMS algorithm. According to the experimental results, the scaffold promoted cell proliferation and adhesion and possessed strong bone and vascular formation capabilities. Moreover, the brittle and hard nature of ceramics is not conducive to their processing [28]. In this respect, 3D printing is an effective technology for precisely forming complex porous scaffolds [29]. Currently, various 3D printing methods, such as stereolithography (SLA) [30,31], digital light processing (DLP) [32,33], powder bed fusion (SLS/SLM) [34,35], binder jetting (BJ) [36], and extrusion freeform fabrication (EFF) [37], have been applied in ceramic forming. Among them, SLA-3D printing combined with a downdrafting technique ensures high manufacturing precision, excellent mechanical properties, and large forming dimensions, making it particularly suitable for the precise preparation of personalized porous ceramic bone scaffolds [38]. Huang *et al.* [39] SLA-3D printed Si3N4 honeycomb and cubic lattice structures with a compressive strength of 290.7 MPa for a cubic lattice with 50% porosity under sintering conditions at 1700 °C. Dong *et al.* [40] synthesized SLA-3D printed complex rod-like porous BCP scaffolds with a compressive strength of 8.14 MPa for a 54.9% porosity scaffold under sintering conditions at 1250 °C. Arab *et al.* [41] synthesized SLA-3D-printed Voronoi HA scaffolds, and the compressive strength of 60% porosity scaffolds under sintering conditions at 1250 °C was 27.2±0.3 MPa. Zhang *et al.* [42] synthesized SLA-3D-printed G unit TPMS-structured β-TCP scaffolds with a compressive strength of 43.6 MPa for 40% porosity scaffolds under sintering conditions at 1200 °C. The above achievements have confirmed the precise preparation ability of the SLA process for porous structures. However, current research on SLA-3D printing of photothermal functional ceramic scaffolds is still scarce.

Ceramic SLA-3D printing technology consists of a two-step process. First, the ceramic slurry is layer-by-layer cured to form a ceramic green body. The green body subsequently undergoes high-temperature debinding and sintering treatments to obtain the final dense ceramic component. In the aforementioned process, the ceramic slurry’s photosensitive properties and rheological characteristics determine the layer-by-layer stacking effect of SLA-3D printed green bodies and their postprocessing quality [43]. The design and preparation of slurries are critical factors in ceramic additive manufacturing. However, dark additives such as GO and SiOC can easily absorb ultraviolet light, significantly weakening the photosensitivity and single-layer thickness of the ceramic slurry and even hindering SLA-3D printing. For example, Zhang *et al.* [44] reported that at the same laser power, the curing line width and layer thickness of a GO/3Y-ZrO2 ceramic slurry were 45% and 70% lower than those of a blank group. Xing *et al.* [45] reported that the curing line width and layer thickness of a 10 wt% SiCw/Al2O3 ceramic slurry decreased by 20% and 40%, respectively, relative to those of a blank group. Therefore, the photosensitive Si3N4 slurry design and preparation with a low-scattering effect are the key prerequisites for SLA-3D printing of photothermal porous Si3N4 bone scaffolds. Carnieri *et al.* [46] prepared SiOC ceramics through the pyrolysis of polymer precursors. The resulting material was nontoxic to adult stem cells, allowing them to adhere, grow, and expand. Su *et al.* [47] obtained polymer-derived SiOC ceramic scaffolds through 3D printing technology, whose compressive strength was 31 MPa, indicating excellent mechanical properties and high stability. Therefore, the present study intends to select a polymer rich in Si and C that can serve as both the photosensitive binder of the slurry and the SiOC precursor component to overcome the difficulty in curing the directly mixed SiOC/Si3N4 slurry.

In summary, this study addresses the issues of weak photothermal function, low osteoinductive performance, and difficulty in precisely forming porous structures of Si3N4 bone scaffold materials. Research on the performance of SLA-3D printed GO-enhanced SiOC/Si3N4 photothermal functional black bioceramic scaffolds is proposed. The role of GO consists of enhancing the osteoinductive properties and mechanical strength of Si3N4 ceramics. Additionally, the photosensitive silane coupling agent KH570, which is rich in silicon and carbon, served as both a binder for the Si3N4 slurry and a component of the SiOC precursor. Through the combination of SLA-3D printing and postprocessing techniques, a photothermal functional GO/SiOC/Si3N4 porous structure bone scaffold was fabricated. A composite Si3N4 ceramic slurry was initially formulated, and the effects of varying GO concentrations on the photosensitive and rheological properties of the slurry were systematically investigated. On the basis of these findings, the SLA-3D printing parameters were optimized. Thereafter, multiple groups of GO/SiOC/Si3N4 porous structures were produced by debinding and sintering processes. The microstructural evolution of GO, SiOC, and Si3N4 was monitored at different temperatures. Finally, an in-depth analysis of the mechanical properties, infrared thermal behavior, and biological performance of the GO/SiOC/Si3N4 ceramic scaffolds was performed. This study promotes the development of a new generation of photothermal functional bone scaffolds for postoperative defect reconstruction after bone tumor resection.

### 2.1 Materials

The α-Si3N4 powder (Shanghai Chaoqin, China) had a bimodal particle size distribution with a 6 : 4 ratio of particles measuring 2 μm and 500 nm. GO (Angxing, China) has a sheet diameter of 0.2–10 μm and a layer thickness of 1 nm. The silane coupling agent (KH570, Shanghai Yuanye Bio, China) was modified and treated as a photosensitive resin. Thermoplastic polyolefin (TPO) (BASF, Germany) served as the photoinitiator. Rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (rBMSCs) were extracted from the femurs of 3-week-old SD rats and cultured for 3–5 generations for experimental studies.

### 2.2 Design and preparation of a photothermal functional Si<sub>3</sub>N<sub>4</sub> slurry for SLA-3D printing

First, a photosensitive precursor polymer was designed using silane polymers rich in Si, C, and O. The silane coupling agent KH570 was subsequently mixed with methanol at a ratio of 4 : 1. Concurrently, an 8 vol% hydrochloric acid solution was added dropwise to the mixture for 40 min. The mixture was then left standing at room temperature for 6 h to facilitate hydrolysis and condensation reactions under acidic conditions for the construction of a siloxane framework that generated photosensitive functional C=C double bonds. The reaction temperature was subsequently increased to 70 °C and maintained for 2 h. After that, the temperature was increased to 90 °C under reduced pressure for the removal of methanol for another 2 h. A rotary evaporator was employed to eliminate residual methanol, resulting in KH570-H with a viscosity of 450 mPa·s at room temperature. Finally, the addition of 2 wt% TPO photoinitiator yielded a photosensitive polysiloxane resin that also served as a precursor for SiOC (Fig. 1(a)). Second, GO with a concentration range of 0.2–0.8 wt% was selected to enhance the osteoinductivity and mechanical properties of Si3N4. The GO was incorporated into the resin and subjected to ultrasonic stirring under light-shielded conditions until a homogeneous dispersion was achieved (Fig. 1(b)). Third, α-Si3N4 powders with particle sizes of 2 μm and 500 nm were mixed at a weight ratio of 6 : 4 and combined with the resin prepared in the previous step. Ethanol and grinding media were then added to the mixture, which was subsequently subjected to ball milling for 6–8 h (Fig. 1(c)). Finally, heat was applied upon stirring to evaporate the ethanol, resulting in a homogeneous slurry with a solid content of 50 wt%, which comprised a GO/KH570-H/Si3N4 mixture (Fig. 1(d)). For simplicity of presentation, the 0–0.8GO/KH570-H/Si3N4 slurry and the composite formed by sintering are uniformly abbreviated as 0–0.8GO in this paper.

### 2.3 SLA-3D printing and postprocessing optimization

The steps for SLA-3D printing of the photothermal functional black bioceramics are illustrated in Fig. 2. They include SLA-3D printing of GO/KH570-H/Si3N4 ceramic green bodies and preparation of GO/SiOC/Si3N4 dense ceramic components through high-temperature sintering. A rheometer (MCR302, Anton Paar, China) was used to test the viscosity variation of slurries with different GO contents under shear rates from 0.1 to 1000 s−1, and a mathematical model was developed between the GO content and viscosity. The influence of 0–0.8 wt% GO content on the photosensitive performance of the Si3N4 slurry was quantified, and the SLA-3D printing parameters (laser power and scanning speed) were optimized. The widths and thicknesses of the curing lines from each group under different laser powers (440–660 mW) and scanning speeds (500–2500 mm/s) were tested via an SLA equipment (3D Printer 300, CeraMultic, China). This allowed characterization of the critical exposure energy and curing depth along with other photosensitive parameters of the slurries with varying GO contents. A mathematical model was derived between the slurry properties and the printing parameters to optimize the 3D printing process. A 15 mm × 15 mm × 15 mm G unit TPMS structure with a wall thickness of 0.9 mm and a porosity of 60% was subsequently fabricated via 3D printing. Additionally, an identical TPMS structure with dimensions of 12 mm × 12 mm × 4 mm and a wall thickness of 0.9 mm was prepared to evaluate the biocompatibility of the scaffold. Finally, post-sintering treatment and SiOC conversion were performed. Thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA; STA200, HITACHI, Japan) was conducted on the KH570-H cured samples. The elemental composition and chemical bond states of the samples were analyzed via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Escalab 250Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA). The material composition and microstructure were characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Gemini SEM 300, Zeiss, Germany) and an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; SmartLab SE, Rigaku, Japan). Furthermore, the conversion behavior of the polymer precursor within a temperature range of 400–1200 °C was examined in detail. The sintering densities of the samples were determined according to the Archimedes drainage method. On the basis of these findings, the sintering process for the composite Si3N4 ceramics was optimized under an argon atmosphere at temperatures ranging from 1000 to 1300 °C.

#### 2.4.1 Mechanical properties and photothermal function characterization

The compressive strength and elastic moduli of the scaffolds were assessed via a universal mechanical testing machine (LD23.104, Shanghai Lishi, China). The hardness of each sample was measured via a Vickers hardness tester (HVS-30, Shanghai Yanrun, China). The optimal absorption wavelength of the samples was determined via an ultraviolet–visible near-infrared diffuse reflectance spectrometer (UV-3600i Plus, Shimadzu, Japan). The bioceramic scaffolds were irradiated with 808 nm near-infrared light at a power intensity of 1 W/cm2 for 10 min, and the temperature was measured every 2 min via a photothermal tester (HM300, Guangzhou Satehong, China).

#### 2.4.2 Biological performance testing

The cytotoxicity of the samples was assessed as follows. The sterilized ceramic scaffolds from each group were incubated in complete culture medium at a surface area-to-extract ratio of 3 cm2/mL for 72 h to obtain a 100% extract solution. After being subcultured into 96-well plates at a density of 1.5×104 cells per well, bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were subsequently seeded and cultured for 24 h to ensure cell adhesion. The complete culture medium was then replaced with 100 μL of the scaffold extract mixture in each well and further incubated for 24 h. The complete culture medium served as the negative control, while 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) served as the positive control. The plates were subsequently incubated in the dark for 1 h in complete culture medium containing 10% cell-counting-kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent. A blank control without cells was also prepared using complete culture medium. The optical density (OD) values were measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader. To evaluate cell viability, the relative growth rate (RGR) of the cells in each group was calculated by dividing the difference between the average OD values of the experimental group and the blank control by the difference between the average OD values of the negative control and the blank control.

A cell proliferation assay was conducted as follows. Sterilized Si3N4 samples from each group were placed in 24-well plates, and BMSCs were seeded at a density of 1.5×104 cells per well. The culture medium was changed every two days. On days 1, 3, and 5, the cells were incubated in the dark with complete culture medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent for 1 h. A blank control without cells was prepared from complete culture medium. The OD values at 450 nm were measured via a microplate reader to analyze and evaluate the proliferative capacity of the BMSCs cocultured with each experimental group.

#### 3.1.1 Influence of GO content on the rheological properties of slurry

The solid content has a significant effect on the performance of the slurry. When the solid content exceeds 50 wt%, the addition of Si3N4 increases light scattering, sharply decreases the curing performance, and results in a poor levelling effect in the slurry. Therefore, rheological tests were conducted on slurries with different GO contents at 50 wt% solid content. The results are depicted in Fig. 3(a). ### 3.3 Postsintering treatment and mechanical properties of SLA-3D printed scaffolds

On the basis of the thermogravimetric analysis and GO thermal decomposition characteristics, the sintering temperature in this study was determined to be 1000–1300 °C (the holding time and heating rate were 60 min and 2 °C/min, respectively). Figure 9(a) displays the TPMS-structured GO/SiOC/Si3N4 scaffolds (0–0.8 wt% GO) without crack defects. The linear shrinkage of the scaffolds stabilized at 10%, and the deviation of the characteristic wall thickness was controlled at ±100 μm (design value of 0.9 mm). Figure 9(b) confirms that the process parameters can be precisely tuned to successfully obtain a three-dimensional structure. According to the mechanical property analysis (Fig. 9(c)), there is a positive correlation between the sintering temperature and the compressive strength. In particular, the 0.2GO group sintered at 1300 °C reached a compressive strength of 41.88 MPa, which was 64.2% greater than that at 1000 °C. Notably, there was a significant decrease in strength after the degree of GO doping exceeded 0.4%. For example, the strength of the 0.8 wt% GO sample decreased to 30.5 MPa (by 26%) at 1300 °C, revealing that the optimal strengthening window for GO was 0.2–0.4 wt%. The elastic modulus of human cancellous bone is between 0.1 and 5 GPa, and that of cortical bone is between 7 and 30 GPa. Therefore, the elastic modulus of the bone scaffolds prepared in this study (7.62–13.31 GPa) matches that of human cortical bone (Fig. 9(d)). These findings indicate that the prepared scaffolds can effectively circumvent the stress-shielding effect, which provides a reliable material for bone implants to achieve efficient osseointegration.

This is attributed to the high GO content, which causes layer stacking due to van der Waals forces. On the basis of the microstructure‒performance correlation analysis of the 0.2GO/SiOC/Si3N4 composite ceramics (Fig. 11), the mechanical strengthening mechanism can be interpreted as follows. The surface step effect resulting from the layer-by-layer stacking characteristic of SLA-3D printing (Fig. 11(a)) had a small effect on the material density. The corresponding SEM image (Fig. 11(c)) revealed that the fracture surface possessed low and uniformly distributed porosity, confirming that the SiOC/Si3N4 matrix achieved full densification during sintering at 1300 °C. The key factor contributing to the enhancement in mechanical properties stems from nanostructural regulation by GO. The high-magnification SEM image (Fig. 11(b)) and fracture morphology (Fig. 11(d)) confirmed that the GO sheets were embedded within the matrix, forming strong interfacial coupling through the Si‒O‒C bonds. The even GO sheet thickness resulted in no thermal decomposition, which was attributed to the effective inhibition of GO reduction upon sintering in the argon atmosphere. In addition, under the action of loading, the GO sheets consumed the fracture energy through toughening mechanisms such as crack deflection (indicated by the arrow in Fig. 11(d)) and interfacial debonding. Moreover, the uniformly dispersed GO network prevented crack propagation, enhancing both the material’s strength and toughness. Studies have shown that in situ* C can increase ceramic strength on a specific basis, providing a basis for the ability of GO to increase ceramic strength [50]. This result indicated that the optimal balance between structural stability and strengthening efficiency was achieved in a 0.2 wt% GO sample.

#### 3.4.1 Assessment of photothermal function effectiveness

To determine the optimal absorption wavelength of the SiOC material, ultraviolet/visible/near-infrared diffuse reflectance tests were conducted. As seen from the absorption spectrum in Fig. 12, the baseline fluctuated in a nonmonotonic manner within the near-infrared region II (1100–2500 nm). More stable absorbance was observed in the near-infrared region (780–1100 nm). Finally, the optimal absorption of SiOC was attained between ~500 and 1000 nm. Therefore, near-infrared light with a wavelength of 808 nm was chosen for further experiments.

Fig. 12Absorption spectrum of SiOC.

The photothermal performance evaluation revealed that the GO/SiOC/Si3N4 composite scaffold possessed precise temperature control ability (Fig. 13). To verify the stability and reliability of the bone scaffold in terms of its photothermal performance and to observe whether it can maintain a good thermal response speed during multiple irradiation cycles, five cycles of tests were conducted. The results revealed that the temperature rise of the scaffold was nearly consistent during each cycle, indicating good stability (Fig. 13(a)). Figure 13(b) shows that the pure SiOC material reaches a temperature of 134 °C within 3 min under 808 nm laser irradiation because of the high heating rate, which stabilizes thereafter (heating rate of 45 °C/min). Although the introduction of Si3N4 led to a decrease in the photothermal performance (the heating rate of the 0GO group was 22.5 °C/min), the temperature increase could be restored to 120 °C (the heating rate of the 0.8GO group was 24 °C/min) after the incorporation of GO. For example, compared with the 0GO group, the 0.8GO group composite scaffold exhibited a temperature increase of 5 °C (Figs. 13(c) and 13(d)). This was attributed to the π‒π conjugated structure of GO, which enhances the local surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) effect through nonradiative relaxation while effectively compensating for the dilution effect of Si3N4. Moreover, the cycle stability coefficient (R*2 = 0.986) indicated no significant attenuation of the photothermal performance. These results demonstrate that the GO/SiOC/Si3N4 composite scaffold can convert light energy into heat while possessing excellent photothermal capability.

When the bone scaffold is embedded in the human body, the near-infrared light must penetrate several millimeters of skin tissue to reach the target scaffold. To verify the photothermal effect of the in vivo scaffold under near-infrared light irradiation, the ceramic scaffold was covered with 4–5 mm thick pig skin for testing. After irradiation, the image changed from a precise contour to a blurred contour. After irradiation for 3 min, the temperature of the SiOC scaffold stabilized at 54 °C (heating rate of 12 °C/min), whereas the temperature of the 0GO group after being combined with silicon nitride was 47 °C (heating rate of 5.5 °C/min). After adding 0.8 wt% GO, the temperature increased to 49 °C (heating rate of 6 °C/min), as shown in Fig. 14. These results indicate that near-infrared light can overcome the specific thickness of skin tissue and heat ceramic scaffolds inside the skin. Some researchers have coated the scaffold surface with HA to simulate the effect of bone tissue growth on the photothermal properties of the scaffold. The results indicate minimal temperature changes before and after coating [51]. Notably, each component’s temperature increase rate and temperature control range (40–50 °C) precisely match the biomedical requirements, whereby the range of 40–45 °C promotes osteoblast proliferation through thermal stimulation (the ALP activity increases by 2.1 times). When the temperature approaches 50 °C, it can kill bone tumor cells [52]. In combination with the outstanding mechanical properties (compressive strength of 41.88 MPa), this controllable photothermal behavior has a synergistic effect, breaking through the technical bottleneck of the imbalance between function and performance in traditional bioceramics.

#### 3.4.2 Biological safety

To evaluate the biological safety of the GO/SiOC/Si3N4 composite ceramic scaffolds, a quantitative analysis of their cytotoxicity and metabolic activity was conducted using rBMSCs as a model. The assessment involved coculture experiments (Fig. 15) combined with the CCK-8 assay according to the ISO 10993-5 standard. WST-8 reagent was employed to measure the dehydrogenase activity of viable cells, whereby RGR served as the primary indicator of biocompatibility. The results demonstrated that after 7 days of coculture, the RGR values for the rBMSCs in all the groups exceeded the internationally recognized threshold for “no cytotoxicity” (RGR ≥ 70%) (Table 3). These findings indicate that under argon sintering conditions at 1300 °C, the GO/SiOC/Si3N4 composite system released no cytotoxic substances that inhibited cell metabolism. Moreover, the surface chemical properties of the samples matched those of the cellular microenvironment. Morphological observations further confirmed that there were no signs of apoptosis or necrosis in rBMSCs. These findings validate the biological safety of the composite scaffold, meeting the fundamental requirements for the clinical application of biomedical materials and establishing a reliable toxicological foundation for its use in bone tissue engineering and tumor therapy.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, we manufactured 0–0.8 wt% GO/SiOC/Si3N4 composite ceramic scaffolds via SLA-3D printing and investigated their mechanical, photothermal, and biological properties. The research conclusions are as follows:

1) In this work, the photosensitive polyorganosiloxane resin KH570-H was synthesized as the SiOC precursor, and 0–0.8GO groups of ceramic slurries for SLA-3D printing were prepared. Mapping models of the photosensitive parameters (*C*d and *E*c) and SLA process parameters (*P*, *v*) for slurries with different GO contents were constructed. The dimensional accuracy of the printed green bodies improved by 12.5% compared with that of their nonoptimized counterparts.

2) The GO/KH570-H/Si3N4 slurry was subjected to SLA-3D printing, debinding, and sintering, and the resulting GO/SiOC/Si3N4 composite ceramics were transformed. Under sintering conditions at 1300 °C, the composite ceramic with 0.2GO group exhibited optimal comprehensive performance. The compressive strength of the gyroid unit TPMS scaffold with 60% porosity reached 41.88 MPa.

3) The GO/SiOC/Si3N4 composite scaffold has excellent photothermal properties and biocompatibility. Under irradiation with 808 nm 1 W/cm2 near-infrared light, the 0.2GO ceramic scaffold covered with pig skin reached 47.8 °C within 10 min. The 0–0.6GO group of scaffolds had good biocompatibility, and the results of the cell proliferation experiments indicated that the 0.2GO group had the greatest degree of cell proliferation.
