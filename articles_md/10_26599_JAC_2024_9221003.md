## 1 Introduction

High-entropy borides (HEBs), a class of novel ultra-high temperature ceramics (UHTCs), have attracted significant interest because of their unique properties, including high strength and hardness, low thermal conductivity, and excellent high-temperature mechanical properties [1−5]. These properties indicate that HEBs are potential high-temperature structural and thermal protection materials for extreme environments, such as aviation, aerospace, and nuclear reactors [6,7].

High-entropy ultra-high temperature ceramics initially presented advantages in high-temperature oxidation resistance. In 2016, Gild *et al*. [8] prepared seven kinds of HEBs (metal elements: Zr, Hf, Ta, Nb, Ti, Mo, W, etc.) by spark plasma sintering (SPS) for the first time and tested their oxidation resistance (1000–1200 °C, 1 h). The results show that the oxidation weight gain of all HEBs is lower than that of individual diborides (ZrB2, TaB2, CrB2, and TiB2), but the specific oxidation resistance mechanism is not explained. Yudin *et al*. [9] believed that the improvement in the oxidation resistance of high-entropy carbides (HECs, metal elements: Zr, Hf, Ta, Nb, and Ti) was related to the formation of a dense protective layer on the surface consisting of TiNb2O7 and Nb2Zr6O17/Ta2Hf6O17. Zhang *et al*. [10] investigated the ablation behavior (~2200 °C, 60 s) of (Zr1/4Hf1/4Cr1/4Ti1/4)B2–SiC–Si composite coatings and found that the combination of various oxides formed by the oxidation of HEBs could provide a protective effect over a wide temperature range (1700–3000 °C). Wang *et al*. [11] prepared (Zr1/4Hf1/4Ta1/4Nb1/4)C by SPS and studied its ablation behavior at 2100 °C (60 and 120 s), and the results indicated that there were significant compositional differences in the oxides from the center to the edge of the ablated samples. Zr–Hf oxides with high melting points were enriched in the center of the sample, while Nb–Ta oxides were enriched at the edge of the sample. The microstructure and phase composition of (Zr1/5Hf1/5Ta1/5Nb1/5Ti1/5)C oxide layer after oxyacetylene flame ablation (2000 °C, 60 s) were analyzed in detail by Ni *et al*. [12]. The special dense layer composed of independent (Zr,Hf)6(Ta,Nb)O17 grains embedded in continuous Ti(Ta,Nb)2O7 was considered beneficial for obtaining better ablation resistance. Although the mechanism is still ambiguous, HEBs consistently exhibit better oxidation resistance than individual borides at lower temperatures (8,13,14]. However, the antioxidant performance and mechanisms of HEBs in extreme environments (> 1800 °C) are not clear.

As a novel material, there have been no reports on the element selection and design principles of HEBs. Recently, metal element selection in the research of oxidation/ablation resistance of high-entropy ultra-high temperature ceramics has focused mainly on the Zr–Hf–Ta–Nb–Ti system, and the composition ratio is equal to the molar ratio [7,9,12,15]. Compared with that of carbides, the liquid oxidation product B2O3 of diborides hinders oxygen diffusion. Therefore, the oxidation resistance of diborides is generally better than that of carbides [16,17]. Among individual diborides, zirconium and hafnium diborides have good ablation resistance [18,19]. Compounds of Ta and Ti are considered additive phases capable of improving the oxidation resistance of HfB2- or ZrB2-based materials [20−23]. In addition, typical Nb oxide (Nb2O5) has the lowest melting point (1520 °C) among the transition metal oxides in this system, so the contribution of Nb element to the oxidation resistance may be negative, especially above 1800 °C [24]. Thus, (Zr–Hf–Ta–Ti)B2 may have better antioxidant properties than many equimolar HEBs. The antioxidant performance of equimolar (Zr–Hf–Ta–Ti)B2 may not be the best because of the variation in the nature of different elements. Carrying out the design and optimization of non-equimolar components is a key step to promote the application of HEBs, but there are few reports on this topic.

In this work, a series of (Zr–Hf–Ta–Ti)B2 bulk materials with different element ratios were prepared by boron thermal reduction combined with SPS, including ZrB2, (Zr4/5Hf1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2, (Zr1/2Hf1/6Ta1/6Ti1/6)B2, (Zr1/4Hf1/4Ta1/4Ti1/4)B2, (Ta4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ti1/15)B2, (Ti4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ta1/15)B2, HfB2, (Hf4/5Zr1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2, and (Hf13/30Zr13/30Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2 (Fig. 1(a)). The oxidation resistance of these samples at 1800–2400 °C was evaluated by laser heating. The oxidation product compositions and microstructural features of the different component materials were characterized in detail; the roles of the various elements are analyzed; and the advantages of non-equimolar high-entropy borides in terms of oxidation resistance are proposed. This study is expected to provide some guidance for the material design of HEBs.

### 2.1 Powder synthesis and sintering

Commercially available monoclinic ZrO2, monoclinic HfO2, Ta2O5, anatase TiO2 and B4C (purity ≥ 99.5%, ~1.0 µm, Shanghai Titan Technology Co., Ltd., China) were selected as row powders. The designed components include ZrB2, (Zr4/5Hf1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2, (Zr1/2Hf1/6Ta1/6Ti1/6)B2, (Zr1/4Hf1/4Ta1/4Ti1/4)B2, (Ta4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ti1/15)B2, (Ti4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ta1/15)B2, HfB2, (Hf4/5Zr1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2, and (Hf13/30Zr13/30Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2, where ZrB2 and HfB2 are control samples. The four oxide powders corresponding to the metal elements in the borides were mixed with an appropriate amount of B4C powder in ethanol. After drying, the mixed powders were put into a graphite heating furnace (ReBorn, Shanghai Chen Hua Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China) and then heated to 1650 °C for 1 h to obtain high-entropy powders. At high temperatures, B4C, as a reducing agent, reacts with metal oxidation to form metal borides, B2O3, and CO. Taking (Zr1/4Hf1/4Ta1/4Ti1/4)B2 as an example, the chemical equation can be expressed by Eq. (1):

(1) ZrO2(s)+HfO2(s)+1/2Ta2O5(s)+TiO2(s)+41/14B4C(s)→4(Zr1/4Hf1/4Ta1/4Ti1/4)B2(s)+13/7B2O3(g)+41/14CO(g)

Subsequently, the high-entropy powders were densified by an SPS apparatus (HPD 250, FCT, Germany) to prepare high-entropy ceramic blocks. The process conditions were 30 MPa, 10 min of holding time at 1900 °C, and a sample size of *ϕ*10 mm.

### 2.2 Oxidation tests

To simulate the response of the material in an ultra-high temperature environment, the samples were subjected to anti-oxidant testing using a CO2 laser ablation test system (Changzhou Replac Optoelectronics Technology Co., Ltd., China). A picture of the device is shown in Fig. 1(b). The samples were divided into two groups for laser ablation experiments with power densities of 57.3 and 71.6 MW/m2, with the aim of ensuring that the sample surface temperatures were maintained above 1800 °C. During the experiment, the laser power output was increased to a set value within 60 s and then held for 300 s. The surface temperature of the samples during ablation was measured via a two-color infrared thermometer (E1RL-F2-V-0-0, Fluke, Germany) with a measurement range of 1000–3000 °C.

### 2.3 Characterization

The density of sintered ceramics was tested by the Archimedes method. Bulk samples before and after ablation were analyzed by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany) and a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Verios G4, USA) equipped with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The microstructures of the samples after ablation were characterized by a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-2100F, JEOL, Japan). The TEM samples of the ablative coating were prepared using a focused ion beam (FIB; Versa 3D, FEI, USA). The depth of the ablation pits of the samples after ablation was tested using an optical super depth of field microscope (OSM; RH-2000, HIROX). To predict the possible oxidation products, phase stability diagrams of ZrB2, HfB2, TaB2 and TiB2 were calculated by Factsage 8.0. The calculation details were specified in our previous work [25,26].

### 3.1 Phase compositions and microstructures of sintered ceramics

Figure 2 shows the XRD patterns of nine sintered ceramics. The independent diffraction peaks for all sintered ceramics indicate a good solid solution. As the Zr content decreased, the unit cell became smaller and the position of the diffraction peak moved to the right. The diffraction peaks of single-phase ZrB2, TaB2, and TiB2 are not found in the diffraction pattern of (Hf4/5Zr1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2, indicating that the solid solution of each metal element is good. Compared with that of HfB2, the diffraction peak of (Hf4/5Zr1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2 shifted only slightly to the right. The density and relative density of the sintered ceramics are statistically presented in Table 1. The densities of all the prepared ceramic blocks were higher than 85%. The individual ZrB2 and HfB2 had more micropores inside, while the non-equal-ratio HEBs had higher densities. These findings indicate that high-entropy ceramics have better sintering properties than individual ZrB2/HfB2 ceramics under the same sintering conditions [27]. ### 3.2 Laser ablation behavior and oxide layer microstructure of high-entropy diborides with different components

To analyze the effects of different metal elements (Zr, Ta, and Ti) and their proportions on the oxidation resistance of HEB, the oxidation resistance of ZrB2, (Zr4/5Hf1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2, (Zr1/2Hf1/6Ta1/6Ti1/6)B2, (Zr1/4Hf1/4Ta1/4Ti1/4)B2, (Ta4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ti1/15)B2, and (Ti4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ta1/15)B2 were tested at the same power density (57.3 MW/m2). Figure 4 shows the macroscopic morphologies of the six samples after oxidation. ZrB2 and (Zr4/5Hf1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2 had relatively flat surfaces, whereas the remaining samples presented obvious ablation craters, and (Ta4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ti1/15)B2 exhibited spherical bulges in the craters. The dimensions of the ablation craters or oxide skin protrusions on the surface of the samples were measured by an optical super depth of the field microscope and are marked in Fig. 4 (red word). The depth of the ablation pit of ZrB2 was approximately 47 μm, indicating that its oxidation product, zirconia, could be easily detached from the surface. The height of the oxide skin protrusion of (Zr4/5Hf1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2 was approximately 107 μm. The remaining four samples showed large ablation craters with depths greater than 200 μm, which indicated severe volatilization of the oxidation products.

To analyze the differences in the oxidation behavior of different component diborides, the phase composition and microstructure of the samples after ablation were characterized in detail. Figure 5 depicts the XRD patterns of the six ceramics after laser ablation. The XRD patterns of the oxides were labeled by comparing the published data [12,28−30] and Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) cards. The oxidation products were more complex because of the large number of elements involved. The phases of the different samples after oxidation are summarized in the table at the bottom of Fig. 5. The ablated ZrB2 phase consists only of m-ZrO2. The generation of (Zr,Hf)TiO4, (Zr,Hf)6Ta2O17, and TiTa2O7 with low melting points increased with decreasing proportion of Zr. The solid solution behavior of oxides containing the elements Zr, Hf, Ta, and Ti at the same time in a high-temperature environment was very complex and could be defined as (Zr,Hf,Ta,Ti)Oχ, which has been similarly expressed by many researchers [29,31,32]. Chen et al*. [29] concluded that the diffraction peak positions of (Zr,Hf,Ta,Nb,Ti)Oχ were close to those of HfTiO4. It can be inferred from the phase diagram of oxides that (Zr,Hf,Ta,Ti)Oχ has a low melting point (33,34]. The oxidation products of (Ta4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ti1/15)B2 and (Ti4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ta1/15)B2 were dominated by Ta2O5 and TiO2 with low melting points, which could not play a supporting role, and thus the depth of the ablation craters was greater.

In the HfO2–Ta2O5 system, a Ta2O5 solid solution with a small amount of Hf atoms is called a homologous compound, denoted by Ta2O5(H) [34]. In this work, Ta2O5(H) was extended to a solid solution with small amounts of Zr, Hf, and Ti atoms. Similarly, TiTaO4(H) was used herein to denote the solid solution of a few Zr and Hf atoms. It is hypothesized that the oxidation of high-entropy borides produces high-entropy oxides (Zr1/4Hf1/4Ta1/4Ti1/4)Oχ. Due to the difference between the bond energy of each metal element and oxygen ion, high-entropy oxides are unstable in high-temperature environments and precipitate to form solid solutions dominated by two or more metal elements, e.g., TiTaO7(H), (Hf,Zr)6Ta2O17(H), (Zr,Hf)TiO4(H), etc.

It is guessed that due to the high viscosity of the liquid oxidation product dominated by elemental Ta, the oxide layer of (Ta4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ti1/15)B2 could not be spread on the sample surface, resulting in a spherical shape.

Based on these observations, the following phenomena are summarized:

1) The oxide layer morphologies of Zr-, Hf-, Ta- and Ti-based multi-component solid solutions are significantly different. The oxide layer of Zr- and Hf-based borides presented a double-layer structure with liquid covering the solid layer. The oxide layers with Ta and Ti as the main borides were dominated by liquid products, which exhibited spherical droplets and liquid spreading morphology, respectively.

2) The depth of the boride ablation crater is related to the content of the high melting point phase in its oxidation products. With decreasing Zr content, the number of ablation craters on the multi-component boride solid solution gradually increased, indicating that the volatilization phenomenon was aggravated. The oxidation products of Ti-based borides had the least amount of high melting point material and the greatest depth of ablation crater.

3) The oxidation of multi-component boride solid solutions can lead to the formation of an oxide solid solution (high-entropy oxide), which can effectively improve the high-temperature phase stability. The oxide precipitates into a solid solution dominated by one element, such as (Hf0.8,Zr,Ta,Ti)O2, Ta2O5(H), or TiO2(H).

## 4 Discussion

The oxidation behavior and oxidation product characteristics of (Zr–Hf–Ta–Ti)B2 bulk samples with different elemental ratios were analyzed to illustrate the roles played by different elements in the oxidation process and the effects of elemental ratios on the oxidation resistance. Then, the advantages of non-equimolar high-entropy borides in terms of their antioxidant performance are proposed.

### 4.1 Oxidation procedures

The possible products of the oxidation process were determined by minimizing the Gibbs free energy of the system. Phase stability diagrams of ZrB2, HfB2, TaB2, and TiB2 at 2000 °C were calculated using Factsage 8.0 software, and all the thermodynamic data were obtained from NIST-JANAF tables. Phase stability diagrams of individual borides ZrB2, HfB2, TaB2, and TiB2 at 2000 °C as a function of B2O3 and O2 partial pressures, as shown in Fig. 14, are intended to be used to predict the oxidation process of high-entropy borides. The calculations indicate that ZrB2, HfB2, and TaB2 oxidation processes may generate Zr, Hf, and Ta metal elements. Silvestroni *et al*. [35] proved that Ta metal was formed during the oxidation of (Zr,Ta)B2 solid solution by TEM. The oxygen partial pressure corresponding to the formation of Zr and Hf metal elements is low and may be relatively more difficult to observe. Metastable oxides (TiO, Ti2O3, and Ti3O5) are formed during the oxidation of TiB2, and stable TiO2 is formed under high oxygen partial pressure. Complex phase transitions are not conducive to the stability of the oxide layer.

The phase stability diagrams of TiB2 and TaB2 are combined to obtain Fig. 14(e), and the phase stability diagrams of ZrB2, HfB2, TaB2, and TiB2 are combined to achieve Fig. 14(f). Owing to the different oxygen partial pressures corresponding to the oxidation of each boride, the oxidation sequence is different. For example, TiB2 is oxidized to Ti2O3 in the red dotted region of Fig. 14(e), while TaB2 is still not oxidized. Based on the calculations (Fig. 14(f)), the order of oxidation in HEBs can be inferred to be HfB2 > ZrB2 > TiB2 > TaB2. Wen *et al*. [28] calculated the oxygen adsorption energy charge transfer between oxygen atoms, and different metal adsorption sites through the First-Principles calculations, which confirmed that Hf and Zr elements would preferentially oxidize. Under low oxygen partial pressure, the oxidation process of (Hf4/5Zr1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2 can be expressed by Eq. (2):

(2) (Hf4/5Zr1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2→t−(Hf,Zr,Ta,Ti)O2(Hf−rich)+(Hf,Zr,Ta,Ti)Bχ→t−(Hf,Zr,Ta,Ti)O2(Hf−rich)+(Hf,Zr)6(Ta,Ti)2O17

The detailed process is described as follows: First, a large amount of Hf and a small amount of Zr, Ta, and Ti atoms were preferentially oxidized to form Hf-rich t-(Hf,Zr,Ta,Ti)O2 (Figs. 12(c3) and 12(d3)). Second, (Zr,Hf,Ta,Ti)Bχ containing certain Ta and Ti atoms was oxidized to (Hf,Zr)6(Ta,Ti)2O17 (Fig. 12(e3)). Remarkably, the ratio of each atom in (Zr,Hf)6(Ta,Ti)2O17 was variable, rather than a fixed stoichiometric ratio. The amorphous phase observed by TEM might be formed by cooling a small amount of liquid decomposed from (Zr,Hf)6(Ta,Ti)2O17 at high temperatures. The oxidation processes of (Hf13/30Zr13/30Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2 and (Zr4/5Hf1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2 at low oxygen partial pressures were similar because of the similar properties of Hf and Zr elements.

Since (Zr1/2Hf1/6Ta1/6Ti1/6)B2, (Zr1/4Hf1/4Ta1/4Ti1/4)B2, (Ta4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ti1/15)B2, and (Ti4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ta1/15)B2 did not form a dense oxide layer, the oxygen partial pressure inside the material was still high. Hence, preferential oxidation did not easily occur. In an oxygen-rich environment, all the metal atoms in the boride solid solution would be oxidized together to form an oxide solid solution. For example, (Hf4/5Zr1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2 was directly oxidized to t-(Hf0.8,Zr,Ta,Ti)O2, and (Zr1/4Hf1/4Ta1/4Ti1/4)B2 was oxidized to (Zr1/4Hf1/4Ta1/4Ti1/4)Oχ. At ultra-high temperatures of 2000 °C, (Zr1/4Hf1/4Ta1/4Ti1/4)Oχ decomposed into Hf–Zr-rich solid and Ta–Ti element-rich liquid (Fig. 7(d2)). The oxidation product (Zr1/4Hf1/4Ta1/4Ti1/4)Oχ contained approximately half of Ta and Ti atoms, leading to its low melting point (2–TiO2 [36], HfO2–TiO2 [37], and HfO2–Ta2O5 [38].

### 4.2 The role of different elements

The different roles played by the elements in the multi-component boride solid solutions are illustrated by significant differences in the morphology of oxide layers of the multi-component boride solid solutions, which are dominated by Zr, Hf, Ta, and Ti. The melting point of Zr and Hf oxides is more than 2500 °C, which is the main skeleton used to support the oxide layer. Compared with ZrO2, HfO2 has a lower oxygen diffusion coefficient, higher phase transition temperature, and smaller volume change when the tetragonal phase transforms into the monoclinic phase. More importantly, HfO2 has a lower thermal expansion coefficient, which is closer to that of borides, so fewer stresses or defects are generated during the oxidation process [39,40]. Consequently, the high proportion of Hf element in boride solid solution is more conducive to oxidation resistance. The oxidation products of Ta and Ti have a low melting point (41]. The liquid viscosity of Ta2O5(H) is high, and it cannot wet the solid well. Thus, its ability to promote sintering is poor. The liquid dominated by TiO2 has good fluidity to promote solid sintering, but the oxygen resistance is insufficient because of its relatively low viscosity. The mixed liquid oxidation products of Ti and Ta can combine the characteristics of both materials, including high viscosity, good oxygen resistance, strong flow and mass transfer ability, and better promotion of solid sintering.

The ratio of each element in the multi-component boride solid solution plays a decisive role in the oxidation resistance. As the content of Zr element decreases, the amount of solid oxidation products decreases; meanwhile the content of liquid oxidation products increases, and the depth of the ablation crater gradually increases. It is assumed that at 2000 °C, the gaseous B2O3 is completely volatilized, and Ta and Ti elements are all in liquid after oxidation; while Zr and Hf elements are all in solid, and the volatilization of liquid is ignored. Therefore, the ratio of the liquid products is directly related to the composition of liquid and service temperature.

### 4.3 Advantages of high-entropy borides

Combined with the results of the present experiments, an attempt is made to propose the advantages of non-equimolar high-entropy borides in oxidation resistance:

1) In contrast to individual borides, high-entropy borides are richer in oxidation products and can synthesize different oxidation products, providing superior protection over a wide temperature range. Taking (Zr–Hf–Ta–Ti)B2 as an example, the melting point range of its oxidation products is large (1600–2800 °C). Through the appropriate proportion of component design, the content of liquid and solid oxidation products can be effectively controlled, and thus, the structure of the oxide layer can be regulated.

2) Compared with a variety of boride mixed materials, high-entropy borides produce corresponding high-entropy oxides during oxidation. This can avoid thermal mismatches between different oxides and reduce cracks and thermal stresses caused by phase transformation or grain growth, which is conducive to the formation of a stable and defect-free oxide layer.

## 5 Conclusions

Seven kinds of non-equimolar high-entropy boride ceramics were prepared by boron thermal reduction combined with spark plasma sintering technology. The oxidation behavior of these materials in ultra-high temperature environments (> 1800 °C) was studied by laser heating and compared with that of traditional individual ZrB2 and HfB2. The roles of different metal elements in the oxidation process were analyzed, and the effects of the ratio of liquid oxidation products on the oxidation resistance were clarified. The advantages of non-equimolar high-entropy borides in terms of oxidation resistance were proposed. The specific conclusions are as follows:

1) In the studied material systems, the oxide layer structure of individual ZrB2 and HfB2 after oxidation was loose and easily dislodged. The four materials, (Zr1/2Hf1/6Ta1/6Ti1/6)B2, (Zr1/4Hf1/4Ta1/4Ti1/4)B2, (Ta4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ti1/15)B2, and (Ti4/5Zr1/15Hf1/15Ta1/15)B2, had craters after ablation due to excessive high-temperature liquid oxidation products. The crater depth increased with decreasing high-melting point substances in the oxidation products. The oxide layers of (Hf4/5Zr1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2, (Hf13/30Zr13/30Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2, and (Zr4/5Hf1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2 had a small amount of liquid covering the columnar particles, indicating good oxidation resistance. Among them, (Hf4/5Zr1/15Ta1/15Ti1/15)B2 had the best oxidation resistance and the thinnest oxide layer.

2) Among the four elements of HEBs, Zr and Hf elements mainly played a skeleton support role in the oxidation process, while Ti and Ta elements mainly filled in the solid skeleton in the form of liquid to heal the pores and promote solid sintering. The mixed liquid oxidation products of Ti and Ta elements can combine the characteristics of both materials, including high viscosity, good oxygen resistance, and a good ability to promote solid sintering. The high proportion of Hf element in the boride solid solution was more favorable to the oxidation resistance.

3) The advantages of high-entropy borides in terms of oxidation resistance are as follows: (i) Compared with single-component borides, high-entropy borides have more abundant oxidation products that can integrate the characteristics of different oxidation products and provide better protection over a wide temperature range; (ii) compared with various boride composites, the high-entropy oxides formed by the oxidation of high-entropy borides avoid the thermal mismatch between different oxides, reduce the defects caused by phase transformation or grain growth, and are more conducive to the formation of a stable oxide layer.

4) The proportion of each element in the non-equimolar boride solid solution should cause the oxidation product to have a suitable solid‒liquid ratio. Facing the extreme environment above 1800 °C, the oxidation resistance is better when the liquid phase ratio is 10–27 mol%.
