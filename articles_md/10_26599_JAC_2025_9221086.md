## 1 Introduction

Bone defects caused by trauma, infection, and congenital diseases have great demands for bone repair materials in the clinic [1]. Autologous and allogeneic bone transplantation are common methods for the clinical treatment of bone defects. However, challenges often arise owing to the limited availability of donors and the potential risk of disease transmission. In recent years, there have been significant advancements in synthetic bone repair materials, particularly bioactive glasses, which are attributed to their ability to release bioactive ions and induce excellent osteogenic activity [2−4].

Bone is a highly dynamic tissue that undergoes continuous remodeling [5]. Bone-forming osteoblasts and bone-resorbing osteoclasts are tightly regulated in the bone remodeling process through a complex communication network in the bone microenvironment [6]. Once bone tissue is damaged, bone homeostasis is disrupted. Therefore, rebuilding the bone microenvironment to achieve a balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts is the key to designing bone repair materials. The pH value of the bone microenvironment plays an important role in modulating the activity of osteoblasts and osteoclasts [7]. The osteogenic differentiation ability of human bone marrow stromal cells is significantly influenced by slight variations in the extracellular pH, with alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and collagen synthesis increasing 2–3-fold when the pH increases from 6.6 to 7.8 [8]. The proliferation and matrix mineralization of osteoblasts increases as the pH increases from 7.4 to 7.8 [9]. Moreover, appropriately increasing the intracellular pH during the preosteoclast stage could effectively suppress the secretion of H+ ions by osteoclasts, thereby attenuating the differentiation of osteoclast precursors into mature osteoclasts [10]. The expression of cathepsin K, which is crucial for the functionality of activated osteoclasts, increased approximately 300-fold as the pH decreased from 7.4 to 6.8 [11]. Therefore, a weakly alkaline microenvironment favors the promotion of bone formation and mineralization while preventing the inhibition of the differentiation of osteoclast precursors into osteoclasts.

45S5-Glass is the first and most studied bioactive glass and has been approved as a bone graft filler in various orthopedic applications clinically. 45S5-Glass is able to form stable bonds with living bone and promote osteogenesis through the release of bioactive Ca and Si ions [12]. However, 45S5-Glass has high reactivity upon contact with water-based solutions, resulting in rapid ion exchange and an undesired increase in pH [13]. This increase may have detrimental effects on cellular metabolism and function, subsequently impacting cell respiration through enzyme alterations and affecting the diffusion of nutrients and gases to cells [14]. Moreover, high alkalinity may induce inflammatory infiltration, resulting in the accumulation of inflammatory cells such as lymphocytes and macrophages in fibrous tissue, thereby causing bone damage and osteolysis [15].

The sol‒gel methodology is another important technique for the fabrication of bioactive glasses, and it has been demonstrated that 58S bioactive glasses have comparable efficacy in bone regeneration to the widely used 45S5-Glass. However, rapid degradation is also prevalent in sol–gel bioactive glass. Sol–gel bioactive glasses have inherent porous structures with high specific surface areas, which facilitate fast ion exchange in aqueous solutions, resulting in rapid increases in pH values and concentrations of bioactive ions in the culture medium, thereby influencing cytocompatibility [16]. Therefore, the simultaneous achievement of biocompatibility and osteogenic activity is challenging for bioactive glass prepared through melting or sol‒gel methods.

The degradation of bioactive glasses is determined by network connectivity, which subsequently impacts the bioactivity and biocompatibility of the material [17]. The incorporation of highly charged metal oxides (MO*X*), such as ZrO2 and TiO2, into silicate glass systems can enhance the structural connectivity of the glass network, which can reduce the solubility and improve the biocompatibility of the glass due to the high bonding energy of the M–O bond [18]. The biocompatibility of TiO2 has been well established, making it widely utilized in various orthopedic and dental applications [19]. Previous studies have shown that the incorporation of TiO2 into silicate bioactive glasses not only results in a slower ion release rate but also enhances the proliferation of osteoblasts [20]. However, TiO2 has an inherent tendency toward heterogeneity and phase separation, resulting in poor glass-forming abilities, which is a great challenge for the preparation of high-purity and homogeneous TiO2-containing glasses through melting and sol‒gel techniques [21]. Therefore, it is crucial to find an appropriate method for the fabrication of novel bioactive glasses with mild alkalinity that possesses both biocompatibility and the ability to modulate the balance of osteogenic and osteoclastic activities to promote bone regeneration.

The containerless melting process is an emerging technology that can levitate raw materials via external forces such as airflow buoyancy and electrostatic, ultrasonic, and magnetic forces. Then, the raw materials are melted with the help of laser irradiation, which effectively suppresses the occurrence of inhomogeneous nucleation at the contact interface [22]. Aerodynamic levitation is based on the utilization of a gas flow to position the sample in a stable manner, which offers enhanced ease of implementation compared with other levitation methods. The levitated sample is subsequently melted via a CO2 laser, and the homogeneous melt can be quenched into a solid sphere without any physical contact. As a result, aerodynamic levitation has remarkable versatility and wide applicability, which facilitates the achievement of various types of high-purity glasses with a strong tendency to crystallize because of the benefits of a fast cooling rate, high melting temperature, and especially the absence of a contact interface with the crucible. For example, black CaO−SiO2−TiO2 glasses can be fabricated through a containerless melting approach with discs composed of CaSiO3 and TiO2 as levitational materials [23]. Thus, bioactive glasses with mild alkalinity could be achieved via a containerless melting process, which can effectively prevent the interaction between the molten materials and the container walls.

In this study, a containerless melting process is proposed to fabricate bioactive glass with mild alkalinity (SP-Glass) via SP-Ceram (CaTiSiO5 ceramic, sphene) as a model material in a custom-made aerodynamic levitation furnace. The SP-Ceram is initially placed in a funnel and suspended via an oxygen flow. Subsequently, the SP-Ceram is melted with laser irradiation to form spheres and further solidified under a cooling process. In this process, SP-Ceram with a stable crystalline phase is transformed into SP-Glass with a metastable amorphous phase, improving the reactivity of the system. During the degradation process after implantation, SP-Glass shows sustained release of bioactive Ca and Si ions to create a microenvironment with mild alkalinity. As a result, SP-Glass shows biocompatibility by addressing the issue associated with rapid ion exchange-induced significant increases in the pH of 45S5-Glass. Furthermore, it effectively regulates osteogenic and osteoclast activity to promote bone regeneration (Fig. 1).

### 2.1 Preparation of SP-Ceram and SP-Glass

CaTiSiO5 powders were synthesized through a sol‒gel method according to a previous study [24]. In brief, tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS; Si(OC2H5)4), Ca(NO3)2·4H2O, and titanium butoxide (Ti(Bu)4; Ti(O(CH2)3CH3)4) (mol ratio of Si : Ca : Ti set to 1 : 1 : 1) were added to HNO3 solution with continuous stirring, which resulted in the formation of a white gel after complete hydrolysis of TEOS and Ti(Bu)4. The white gel was aged and subsequently dried to obtain a dry gel. The obtained dry gel was then ground and calcined to obtain a white powder. SP-Ceram discs were prepared by pressing CaTiSiO5 powders into compacts and then sintering at 1300 °C for 2 h.

SP-Glass was prepared from the above obtained SP-Ceram discs through the containerless melting process and traditional melting method. The SP-Ceram disc (0.1 g) was placed in a custom-made aerodynamic levitation furnace, floated with O2 gas, rapidly heated to 1450 °C via CO2 lasers, and melted for 1 min. Subsequently, it solidified during the cooling process. The temperature curve for the preparation of SP-Glass in the containerless melting process is shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). For the conventional melting method, SP-Ceram discs (5 g) were melted in a platinum crucible via a muffle furnace (VSF 1750, Nanjing Boyuntong Instrument, China) at 1450 °C, after which the sample was poured on a brass plate at room temperature.

The SP-Ceram dicks and SP-Glass spheres were characterized with an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 advance, Bruker, Germany), a Raman spectrometer (LabRam HR Evolution, Thermo Fischer, Japan), a 29Si nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscope (NMR; 400 M, Bruker, Germany), and an X-ray fluorescence spectrometer (XRF; PW-2404, Philips, the Netherlands). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) were used to analyze the microstructures of the SP-Ceram and SP-Glass via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-2100F, Japan Electronics Co., Ltd., Japan). Additionally, the SP-Glass spheres were polished into a 2 mm thick wafer for observation by a scanning electron microscope and energy-dispersive spectroscope (SEM-EDS; SU 8220, Hitachi, Japan). 45S5 glass was purchased from Kunshan Chinese Technology New Materials Co., Ltd., China. The phase, composition, and particle size of 45S5-Glass were characterized through XRD, XRF, and laser diffraction particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). The thermal transformations of SP-Glass and 45S5-Glass were determined via a differential thermal analyzer (DTA; STA449F3, Netzsch, Germany), and the samples were heated to 1000 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min in an air atmosphere. For kinetic analysis, the SP-Glass was heated to 1000 °C at rates of 5, 10, 20, and 40 °C/min in an air atmosphere via a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC; STA449C, Netzsch, Germany).

### 2.2 Degradation properties and mineralization ability

To investigate the degradation properties of the three samples, SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass powders with particle sizes ranging from 300 to 500 μm were immersed in Tris-HCl buffer solution (pH 7.4) at 37.0 °C, and the ratio of the tris-HCl buffer volume to the powder volume was 200 mL/g. The supernatant fluid was collected at different soaking times (1, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 d). The concentrations of Ca, Si, and Ti ions in the collected solutions were tested via an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES; 715-ES, Varian, USA). An electrolyte-type pH meter (E-201F, Leici Co., Ltd., China) was used to measure the pH values of the solutions.

The mineralization properties of SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass were evaluated by soaking SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass powders in simulated body fluid (SBF) with an SBF/powder ratio of 10 mL/g in a shaking water bath at 37.0 °C. The SBF solution was refreshed daily. After a period of 14 days, the SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass powders were removed from the soaking medium, rinsed, and dried in an oven. The collected powders were characterized via an XRD (D8 advance, Bruker, Germany), an SEM (SU 8220, Hitachi, Japan), and a Fourier transform infrared spectroscope (FTIR; Spotlight400, PerkinElmer, USA).

### 2.3 Cell culture experiments with osteoblasts and osteoclasts

Human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs) were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences and cultured in hBMSC basal medium supplemented with 10% hBMSC-qualified fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin/streptomycin (p/s) solution (1%, v/v) in a humidified, 5% CO2-containing incubator at 37.0 °C. All the aforementioned reagents were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences, USA.

Osteoclast-like cells were generated from RAW264.7 cells according to a previous study [25]. RAW264.7 cells (ATCC, USA) were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 6.25×103 cells/cm2 and cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% p/s solution for 12 h. RAW 264.7 cells were subsequently cultured in α-MEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA), 1% p/s solution (Gibco, USA), receptor activator for nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) (50 ng/mL), and M-CSF (30 ng/mL) for 5 days to induce osteoclastic differentiation.

### 2.4 Cell proliferation assay

Steam sterilization was carried out on the SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass wafers, which were 2 mm thick and 5 mm in diameter. hBMSCs and osteoclasts were cultured on wafers at a density of 5×103 cells per wafer. After 7 days, the morphology and attachment of the cells on the SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass wafers were evaluated via an SEM (SU 8220, Hitachi, Japan). Briefly, the cellular samples were fixed with glutaraldehyde before they were dehydrated with graded ethanol of 30%, 40%, 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%, volume concentration (v/v)), followed by hexamethyldisilazane treatment (Aladdin Scientific, China). Furthermore, the proliferation of hBMSCs and osteoclasts on SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass wafers was evaluated with a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8; Dojindo Laboratories, Japan). The cells were cultured on the wafers at a density of 5×103 cells per wafer. After 1, 3, and 7 days, the cells were incubated with CCK8 solution at 37.0 °C for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm via a microplate reader (Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer, BioTek Instruments, USA).

### 2.5 Typical marker expression in hBMSCs and osteoclasts

After 7 day of culture, gene expression in hBMSCs and osteoclasts was detected. The cells were subjected to a 30-min treatment with Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Total RNA was obtained via chloroform extraction and isopropanol precipitation. cDNA was synthesized, and qPCR was carried out on an instrument (StepOnePlus, ABI, USA). GAPDH was used as a housekeeping gene. The 2−ΔΔCT method was used to analyze relative gene expression, where CT refers to the fractional cycle number at which the amount of amplified target reaches a fixed threshold. All the experiments were conducted in triplicate. The sequences of primers used are listed in Table S5 in the ESM.

Sterile wafers were placed on a 96-well plate. hBMSCs were seeded with 5×103 cells per wafer. After 7 days, the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde (4%, v/v) and permeated with Triton X-100 (0.1%, v/v). The cells were blocked with bovine serum albumin (BSA) (1%, v/v) and incubated with an osteopontin (OPN) antibody overnight. The cells were subjected to treatment with 647-conjugated IgG (Molecular Probes, USA) after being washed with PBS. The actin filaments were labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated phalloidin (Molecular Probes, USA). Finally, the cell nuclei were stained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). A confocal microscope (SP8, Leica Microsystems, Germany) was used to observe the cells.

### 2.6 ALP activity and alizarin red-S assay of hBMSCs

hBMSCs were cultured on SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass wafers with osteogenic differentiation medium supplemented with β-glycerophosphate (10 mM) and ascorbic acid (50 mg/mL) at a density of 2×103 cells per wafer. For the ALP activity experiment, after 7 days, the cells were lysed with Triton X-100 and centrifuged at 14,000 r/min. The supernatant was used to test ALP activity via an ALP kit (Beyotime, China). For ALP staining, the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde after 7 day of cultivation. A BCIP/NBT kit (Beyotime, China) was used for ALP staining. The cells were viewed under an optical microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Alizarin red staining was used to detect osteoblastic mineralization. In brief, the cells were fixed with ethanol (70%, v/v) for 15 min after 14 d of cultivation. The cells were incubated with 1% Alizarin red solution for 60 min. The mineralized nodules were stained bright red by alizarin red. After being observed by an optical microscope (DMi8, Leica Microsystems, Germany), the mineralized nodules were eluted with cetylpyridinium chloride (10%, v/v) and quantified at an absorbance of 550 nm. To establish a positive control, hBMSCs were cultured with pure osteogenic induction medium on blank culture plates. Three samples were included in each group for these experiments.

### 2.7 <i>In vivo</i> evaluation of subcutaneous implantation

The animal models for this study were established via Sprague Dawley (SD) rats (male, 12 weeks old), and all the animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Six People’s Hospital (2022-0151). The rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital in advance, and SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass wafers with thicknesses of 2 mm and diameters of 5 mm were implanted subcutaneously into the backs of the SD rats. The rats were euthanized at 7 and 14 days postimplantation. After the wafers were removed, the tissue samples were dehydrated and then embedded in paraffin. The tissue samples were sectioned for hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, and protein expression related to the immune response surrounding the implanted wafers was detected via immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining, including CD68, and immunofluorescence (IF) staining, including CD86 and CD206.

For IHC staining, the sections were dewaxed, subjected to high-pressure heat antigen retrieval and incubated with 3% H2O2 solution to block endogenous peroxidase activity. The sections were then blocked with 5% BSA for 20 min at room temperature. After the samples were washed, they were incubated overnight with a primary antibody against CD68 (97778, CST, USA). Then, the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ab6721, Abcam, UK) for 30 min at 37.0 °C. The sections were subsequently visualized with diaminobenzidine (DAB), counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and imaged with a microscope equipped with a digital camera (Leica Microsystems, Germany). For double IF staining, the primary antibodies used included CD86 (19589, CST, USA) and CD206 (ab64693, Abcam, UK). IF staining was performed by incubating all the sections with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with an HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ab6721, Abcam, UK) for 30 min at 37.0 °C. The sections were incubated at room temperature for 10 min after adding 594 Tyramide and washed three times with PBS in a microwave. The sections were subsequently incubated with the other primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After being washed with PBS three times, the sections were incubated with HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ab6721, Abcam, UK) for 30 min at 37.0 °C and then incubated with 488 Tyramide for 10 min. Finally, the sections were incubated with DAPI, and fluorescence images were taken via a confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany).

### 2.8 <i>In vivo</i> evaluation of osteogenesis

SD rats (male, 12 weeks old) were used to establish femoral defect models to investigate the osteogenesis of SP-Glass. Cylindrical defects (*ϕ*3.5 × 4 mm) were surgically created in the femoral condyles of the hind limbs of the SD rats, followed by the implantation of SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass particles (300–500 μm) into the defects. Two weeks postimplantation, the femur samples were collected and immersed in formalin solution for 72 h, followed by decalcification for one month. After the sample particles were removed, the tissue samples were dehydrated and paraffinized before being sectioned for IHC staining, which included tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) and OPN. The TRAP+ osteoclast-like cells were stained with a TRAP kit (ZC-G10052, Zcibio, China). For IHC staining, the sections were incubated with primary antibody against OPN (25715-1-ap; Proteintech, USA) overnight at 4 °C and then with an HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ab6721; Abcam, UK) for 30 min at 37.0 °C. Finally, the color was developed with DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin, and images were taken with a microscope equipped with a digital camera (Leica Microsystems, Germany). At four and eight weeks postimplantation, the rats were euthanized, and the femurs containing the samples were extracted for analysis. The samples were examined via a 3D reconstructed microcomputed tomograph (micro-CT; skyscan1172, Bruker, Germany). The samples were subsequently dehydrated and embedded in poly(methyl methacrylate) for histological examination. To detect newly formed bone tissues, van Gieson’s picrofuchsin stain was used for histological staining. The organ tissues of the rats in the different groups, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were collected eight weeks postimplantation. The collected organ tissues were subsequently prepared into slices, and HE staining was performed for analysis.

### 2.9 Statistical analysis

All the data are expressed as the means±standard deviations, and the significance of the experimental data was analyzed via *t*-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post hoc tests. **P* < 0.05, ***P* < 0.01, and ****P* < 0.001.

### 3.1 Microstructure characterization of SP-Glass

In this study, pure-phase SP-Ceram was successfully achieved through the pressing of sol–gel-derived CaTiSiO5 (sphene) powders into compacts followed by sintering at 1300 °C. The XRD pattern of the SP-Ceram sample shows only diffraction peaks corresponding to CaTiSiO5 (Standard Card No. JCPDS 25-0177) (Fig. 2(a)). The SP-Ceram particles are sintered with dense structures and clear grain boundaries in terms of surface morphology (Fig. 2(b)). HRTEM demonstrated that the lattice spacings of SP-Ceram are 4.67, 4.83, and 6.36 Å (Fig. 2(c)), which correspond to the lattice spacings of the (* 1¯11), (011), and (100) planes, respectively. Moreover, the SAED pattern confirmed the high crystallinity of the SP-Ceram (Fig. 2(d)).

Sphene is a common mineral with excellent chemical and thermal stability. Owing to its excellent structural stability and consequently high cytocompatibility, SP-Ceram, as a novel bioceramic, has great potential for application in orthopedic and dental implants [24]. SP-Ceram is a nesosilicate with monoclinic symmetry that consists of chains of Ti–O octahedra connected by Si–O tetrahedra with interstitial Ca ions. Within this structure, Si and Ca ions are encapsulated in isolated SiO44− tetrahedra and crystal interstitials; consequently, the release of bioactive Ca and Si ions from the crystal lattice is hindered. In contrast to CaSiO3, SP-Ceram minimally altered the pH value and ion concentration of the medium, which could support human bone-derived cell proliferation and differentiation [24].

The SP-Glass wafer polished with the SP-Glass spheres is colorless and has high transparency. A weak and broad diffraction band is observed in the XRD pattern, confirming the amorphous feature of SP-Glass (Fig. 2(e)). The surface morphology of SP-Glass is highly homogeneous (Fig. 2(f)), and representative EDS elemental maps of SP-Glass show a uniform distribution of all the constituent elements at the micrometer scale (Fig. S2(a) in the ESM). The amorphous structure of SP-Glass was further confirmed via TEM. No discernible contrast indicative of a crystalline phase can be observed in the HRTEM image of SP-Glass, and the corresponding SAED pattern exhibits a ring-shaped hollow feature (Fig. 2(h)). Moreover, the elemental compositions of both SP-Ceram and SP-Glass are highly consistent with the theoretical composition (Fig. S2(b) in the ESM). This finding indicates that the elemental compositions of SP-Glass are not influenced by a containerless melting process with laser irradiation.

From the viewpoint of glass-forming principles, TiO2 can be categorized as an intermediate oxide that has poor glass-forming ability [26]. TiO2 promotes phase separation in silicate glasses and is commonly used as a nucleating agent in glass-ceramic materials [27]. Therefore, the preparation of SP-Glass via the melting method presents significant challenges and has seldom been reported. Through the traditional melting method, the compound in a TiO2–SiO2 system does not undergo vitrification when the TiO2 content exceeds 10 wt% [28]. For example, glasses in the CaO‒TiO2‒SiO2 system with a TiO2 content of 30 mol% prepared via the traditional melting method show strong phase separation with high inhomogeneity [29]. The traditional melting method in a crucible unavoidably causes heterogeneous nucleation and contamination at the interface between the crucible wall and the molten material [30]. In this study, SP-Glass is also prepared via a traditional melting method via a crucible as a container, which results in fractured blocks with distinctive brownish hues attributed to the presence of structural and chemical defects (such as oxygen vacancies and Ti3+) induced by the cooling process in ambient air (Figs. S3(a) and S3(b) in the ESM). The front side of the sample appears to be in a transparent glassy state, whereas the reverse side of the sample, which is in contact with a brass mold, displays a partially white crystalline state (Fig. S3(c) in the ESM). The glassy nature of the front side of the sample is confirmed by XRD analysis (Fig. S3(d) in the ESM). However, the glass sample exhibited phase separation with a heterogeneous distribution of Ti and Si (Fig. S3(e) in the ESM). Ti4+ is a quadrivalent ion with a high ionic potential that can form a more stable octahedral network with O, and the SiO2‒TiO2 binary system has a broad temperature range, which is conducive to stable liquid‒liquid separation. The reverse side of the sample has square and rodlike particles at the micron scale, and XRD analysis reveals that the precipitated crystals consisted of CaTiO3, CaTiSiO5, SiO2, and TiO2 (Fig. S3(f) in the ESM). The contact between the container and the melting liquid typically results in extrinsic heterogeneous nucleation of the liquid at a temperature proximate to the equilibrium melting point. Overall, the strong crystallization tendency makes it challenging to achieve high-quality SP-Glass with a uniform composition distribution via a traditional melting method with a container.

45S5-Glass is a typical silicate bioactive glass synthesized via a melting process. Here, thermal analysis is introduced to evaluate the thermal stability and glass-forming ability of 45S5-Glass and SP-Glass. The XRD pattern shows a broad diffraction band, confirming that 45S5-Glass is in an amorphous glassy state (Fig. S4(a) in the ESM) and that the oxide composition of 45S5-Glass is in agreement with the theoretical composition (Fig. S5 in the ESM). The DTA curves provide information on the glass transition temperature (T*g), onset crystallization temperature (*T*c), and crystallization peak temperature (*T*p). The Δ*T* value is an important parameter for characterizing the thermal stability of a material and its glass-forming ability and represents the difference between *T*c and *T*g. A larger Δ*T* value typically indicates a stronger glass-forming ability. Δ*T* of SP-Glass is 72.3 °C, which is much lower than that of 45S5-Glass (127.5 °C). Therefore, the vitrification ability of SP-Glass is lower than that of silicate bioactive glass 45S5-Glass. Figure S6(c) in the ESM shows the differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curves of SP-Glass measured at heating rates of 5, 10, 20, and 40 °C/min. The crystallization activation energy (Δ*Ec*) is calculated via the following modified Johnson‒Mehl‒Avrami (JMA) equation (Eq. (1)) [31]:

(1) ln⁡αTp2=−ΔEcRTp+C

where C is the constant term, *T*p is the crystallization peak temperature, *α* is the heating rate, Δ*E*c is the crystallization activation energy, and *R* is the gas constant.

The fitting yield Δ*Ec* of SP-Glass is 97 kJ/mol, which is significantly lower than that of 45S5-Glass (approximately 350 kJ/mol) [32]. The decreased crystallization activation energy leads to a decrease in nucleation barriers, thereby facilitating the devitrification of glass. Therefore, in comparison with 45S5-Glass, SP-Glass has a greater propensity for crystallization and significantly diminished glass-forming ability. According to the aforementioned results, the CaTiSiO5 system has a limited ability to form glass, and the SP-Glass prepared via a containerless melting process has a significantly greater level of element distribution uniformity. Therefore, compared with the traditional melting method, the containerless melting approach for preparing SP-Glass offers distinct advantages, including the elimination of contact between materials and container walls during the melting and solidification process, as well as precise temperature control during preparation, which facilitates the generation of high-purity SP-Glass with homogeneity.

In contrast to crystalline solids, glasses are amorphous and lack long-range order [17]. NMR and Raman spectroscopy are often used to characterize structural units within glass. The basic structural unit in silicate glass is the SiO4 tetrahedron, with silicon at the center and oxygen at each corner [33]. The host silica network is characterized by *Q**n*, where *Q* represents a tetrahedron bonded to four oxygen atoms, and *n* denotes the number of bridging oxygen (BO) atoms per tetrahedron. Solid-state 29Si NMR spectroscopy was conducted for qualitative analysis of BO atoms and nonbridging oxygen (NBO) atoms around Si in SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass. The quantitative determination of Si coordination units can be achieved through signal deconvolution. As expected, the 29Si NMR signals from SP-Ceram exhibit a prominent and narrow peak at −79.5 ppm, which is consistent with the calculated shift of −79.6 ppm for nesosilicate containing four NBO atoms in titanite [34] (Fig. 3(a)). The 29Si NMR spectra of both SP-Glass and 45S5-Glass show broad peaks (Figs. 3(b) and 3(c)), and the relative fractions of *Q**n* determined by the deconvolution of the spectra for both SP-Glass and 45S5-Glass are presented in Table S1. SP-Glass is found to be within the *Q*1–*Q*4 region at 17% (*Q*1), 29% (*Q*2), 50% (*Q*3) and 4% (*Q*4). For 45S5-Glass, a distribution of 17% (*Q*1), 49% (*Q*2), and 34% (*Q*3) is evident. The removal of Na2O and the addition of TiO2 increase the distribution of the *Q*3 and *Q*4 species, and the *Q*3 content of SP-Glass is much higher than that of 45S5-Glass, suggesting that the silicate network connectivity of SP-Glass is greater than that of 45S5-Glass. 45S5-Glass is characterized by a highly open silicate network, primarily composed of *Q*1 and *Q*2 species interspersed with isolated orthophosphate groups [35]. Multiple coordination modes of Ti, including TiO4, TiO5, and TiO6 structural forms, have been observed in the amorphous structures of silicate glasses [36]. The presence of Ti coordination units can strengthen the silicate glass framework where Si–O–Si bonds are substituted by Si–O–Ti bonds, thereby forming an interconnected network of TiO4 and SiO4 tetrahedra [37].

### 3.2 Degradation and mineralization properties of SP-Glass

The degradation behaviors of SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass in tri-HCl solution at a pH of 7.4 were evaluated. For 45S5-Glass, the pH initially reached a maximum value of 9.5 and then gradually decreased to approximately 8.6 with increasing soaking time. After 14 d, the pH value of 45S5 glass stabilized at approximately 8.2. In contrast, for SP-Glass, the pH value reached a peak of 7.6 and remained relatively constant at approximately 7.5 over time. SP-Ceram has a minimal impact on the solution and does not cause any significant changes in the pH due to its excellent chemical stability (Fig. 3(g)). The cumulative release amounts of Ca, Si, and Ti ions from SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass in tris-HCl solutions at various soaking times are shown in Figs. 3(h)–3(j). For the release of bioactive Ca and Si ions, 45S5-Glass presents distinctly sustained release of Ca and Si bioactive ions, and the cumulative release of Ca and Si ions in 28 days reached 250 and 143 ppm, respectively. The values are considerably greater than the cumulative releases of Ca (22.2 ppm) and Si (7.8 ppm) ions from SP-Glass. The cumulative releases of Ti ions from SP-Glass and SP-Ceram at 28 days are 0.024 and 0.019 ppm, respectively. TiO2 can act as a glass former and increase the interconnectivity of the glass network, whereas Ti ions are less likely to be released from the silicate network [20]. Compared with 45S5-Glass, the enhanced stability of the glass network structure of SP-Glass led to a significant decrease in both the pH value and the release rate of bioactive ions.

The phase significantly influences the release rate of bioactive ions. Crystallization generally results in a slower rate of degradation of the corresponding glasses [42]. Compared with the crystalline phase, the amorphous phase is more favorable for the release of bioactive ions because of the faster rate of ion exchange [43]. Therefore, the transformation of highly crystalline SP-Ceram into SP-Glass with a metastable amorphous phase can alter the microstructure of the material, resulting in an appropriate release rate of bioactive ions and a moderate pH microenvironment. The degradation of silicate glass is dependent on the connectivity of the glass network. For 45S5-Glass, Na and Ca cations as network modifiers entered the silicate network, forming NBO and disrupting the connectivity of the silicate network, which resulted in a high proportion of active *Q*1 and *Q*2 in the microstructure of 45S5-Glass. Consequently, once 45S5-Glass contacts water, cations (Ca2+ or Na+) are exchanged for protons from the surrounding solution, especially Na+ ions, resulting in a significant increase in the local pH value and substantial release of bioactive ions [44]. It has been reported that the smaller cation Na+ migrates more rapidly from the network than the larger cation Ca2+ ions do [45]. SP-Glass is sodium-free, and the formation of Si–O–Ti linkages further enhances the bonding of NBOs, thereby improving the connectivity of the silicate network. On the other hand, the proportions of highly active *Q*1 and *Q*2 in the silicate network structure of SP-Glass decrease, whereas the proportions of *Q*3 and *Q*4 increase compared with those of 45S5-Glass, suggesting an increase in the connectivity of the silicate network. Titanosilicate has considerable structural complexity, as Ti4+ species can be present on tetrahedral and octahedral sites, as well as in 5-fold coordination to oxygen [36]. According to a previous study, TiO2 mainly acts as a network former with [TiO4] tetrahedral units and participates in the network chain in the form of [TiO6] and [TiO5] depending on the concentration of TiO2 in the titanosilicate glass [46]. The presence of TiO2 is found to strengthen the silicate glass skeleton. Thermodynamically, the *T*g of SP-Glass (747.1 °C) is much higher than that of 45S5-Glass (555.1 °C), which indicates an increase in the number of connections in the glass network. The strong binding of Ti–O-connected Ca and Si in the glass framework effectively inhibits the migration of Ca2+, which leads to the enhanced chemical stability of silicate glass, and thereby less prone to leaching bioactive ions. SP-Glass addresses the issue associated with the rapid release of cations that cause a significant increase in the local pH of 45S5-Glass, thereby promoting bone regeneration.

The ability to induce mineralization is crucial for bone repair materials, as it facilitates enhanced chemical bonding between the materials and bone tissue. After being immersed in the SBF solution for 14 days, the surface of the SP-Ceram is relatively smooth, with little change compared with the original surface before immersion. In contrast, the surfaces of SP-Glass and 45S5-Glass are completely covered by mineralization layers (Fig. 4(a)). XRD analysis confirms that the diffraction peaks of hydroxyapatite are obvious for SP-Glass after immersion in SBF for 14 days, and the 45S5-Glass group is mainly composed of hydroxyapatite and CaCO3 (Fig. 4(b)). The increased peak spitting at wavelengths of 500–600 cm−1 in the FTIR spectra indicates the formation of hydroxyapatite. After soaking in SBF for 14 days, clear peak splitting at 500–600 cm−1 is observed for both SP-Glass and 45S5-Glass (Fig. 4(c)). Therefore, SP-Glass has the ability to stimulate the formation of a hydroxyapatite layer on the surface when immersed in an SBF solution, similar to 45S5-Glass. The hydroxyapatite layer facilitates the formation of a chemical bond between the bone and the material, which can be favorable for good bone-material integration.

### 3.3 <i>In vitro</i> osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis of SP-Glass

To evaluate the *in vitro* osteogenic properties of SP-Glass, cell proliferation, cell attachment, and osteogenic gene expression assays were performed. The proliferation level of hBMSCs increased with culture time in all the groups, and the number of cells in the SP-Ceram and SP-Glass groups is clearly greater than that in the 45S5-Glass group (Fig. 5(a)). With respect to cell attachment and morphology, hBMSCs cultured on the surface of 45S5 glass exhibits a rounded morphology and limited spread. In contrast, hBMSCs grown on the surfaces of SP-Ceram and SP-Glass spread well with numerous pseudopodia after 7 day of culture (Fig. 5(b)). The expression of important osteogenic markers for hBMSC differentiation is subsequently determined (Fig. 5(c)). Compared with 45S5-Glass, SP-Glass induces the upregulation of the expression of bone morphogenetic protein (BMP2), a pivotal regulator of bone formation. Additionally, SP-Glass stimulates the expression of osteogenic genes, such as the early marker runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), ALP and the late differentiation marker OPN. Furthermore, IF staining of proteins reveals similar results. A much stronger red fluorescence signal for OPN protein is detected in the SP-Glass group than in the SP-Ceram and 45S5-Glass groups (Fig. 5(d)), indicating that OPN expression in hBMSCs in the SP-Glass group is markedly upregulated compared with that in the SP-Ceram and 45S5-Glass groups. Figures 5(e) and 5(f) demonstrate a significant increase in ALP activity and expression in hBMSCs cultured on SP-Glass compared with those in hBMSCs cultured with SP-Ceram and 45S5-Glass over 7 days. Additionally, alizarin red analysis confirms that, compared with SP-Ceram and 45S5-Glass, SP-Glass significantly enhances the terminal mineralization of osteogenic differentiation in hBMSCs after being cultured for 14 d (Figs. 5(g) and 5(h)). As a result, SP-Glass facilitates the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs in comparison with 45S5-Glass and SP-Ceram.

The pH of the microenvironment plays a pivotal role in bone remodeling, as it directly impacts bone homeostasis by influencing the activity of osteoblasts and osteoclasts [48]. The alkaline environment effectively neutralizes H+ ions, thereby impeding the fusion process of preosteoclasts into multinucleated osteoclasts and diminishing the release of β-glucuronidase from osteoclasts, consequently leading to the inhibition of osteoclast activity [49]. Moreover, alkaline conditions increase the proliferation of osteoblasts and accelerate terminal differentiation and calcification by upregulating critical regulators of terminal differentiation and function [8]. However, severe alkalinity (pH > 8.5) has negative effects on the proliferation and viability of hMSCs through triggering senescence [47]. Therefore, a mildly alkaline microenvironment is advantageous for both osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Moreover, a previous study showed that Si ions at a concentration of 1.97 ppm enhance the ALP activity and osteogenic expression of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells derived from ovariectomized rats while simultaneously suppressing the expression of osteoclastic factors in RANKL-induced osteoclasts [50]. In comparison with 45S5-Glass, SP-Glass can provide a weakly alkaline environment and exhibit a moderate release rate of bioactive ions, thereby establishing a favorable microenvironment to improve the activity of osteoblasts and inhibit the activity of osteoclasts.

### 3.4 <i>In vivo</i> bone regeneration of SP-Glass

Bioactive glasses are often used as surgical grafts in particulate forms, as they can be filled easily into the defect [51]. SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass particles with particle size distributions ranging from 300 to 500 μm were implanted into the rat femoral defect model to evaluate the *in vivo* bone regeneration of different samples. The morphology and bone repair effects of the samples in the three groups were analyzed via 3D reconstructed micro-CT after implantation for 4 and 8 weeks (Fig. 6(a)). Micro-CT images reveal newly formed bone (red) within the particles (green) in both transverse and longitudinal sections. The formation of new bone tissue is more pronounced in the SP-Glass and 45S5-Glass groups than in the SP-Ceram group at both 4 and 8 weeks postimplantation. The newly formed bone (red) infiltrates the interstices of the SP-Glass particles (green) at the edge of the defect at 4 weeks after implantation. After 8 weeks, the new bone tissue grows into the interstitial spaces between the particles in the SP-Glass and 45S5-Glass groups, whereas the interface between the material and the original bone tissue in the SP-Ceram group shows only a limited amount of new bone formation. Furthermore, the statistical analysis reveal significantly greater values of bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), bone surface density/total volume (BS/TV) and trabecular number (Tb.N) in both the SP-Glass and 45S5-Glass groups than in the SP-Ceram group, and the trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) values show opposite trends, indicating that, similar to 45S5-Glass, SP-Glass has favorable *in vivo* bone formation ability. Histological analysis via van Gieson’s picrofuchsin staining further reveal that the peripheral host bone grow in close proximity to the SP-Glass and 45S5-Glass particles after implantation for 8 weeks (Fig. 6(f)). In the 45S5-Glass group, many spaces remain around the particles because of the rapid degradation of 45S5-Glass. In contrast, in the SP-Ceram group, only a small amount of new bone forms at the periphery of the defect, which is consistent with the findings from micro-CT. As shown in the TRAP-stained and OPN-stained tissue sections, TRAP+ osteoclast-like cells and OPN+ osteoblasts are observed on the surface of the material particles or new bones in the regions of the defect (Figs. 6(g) and 6(h)). Compared with the SP-Glass group, the 45S5-Glass group presents a significantly greater number of TRAP+ osteoclast-like cells at the sites of implantation, whereas the number of OPN+ osteoblast-like cells display the opposite trend, which is consistent with the findings from the *in vitro* results.

### 3.5 <i>In vivo</i> biocompatibility of SP-Glass

The histocompatibility and immune response of SP-Ceram, SP-Glass, and 45S5-Glass were evaluated through a subcutaneous implantation assay. Subcutaneous tissues attached to the samples at 1 and 2 weeks postimplantation were harvested and subjected to HE staining. As shown in Fig. 7(a), SP-Ceram and SP-Glass are wrapped by neointimas and exhibit a negligible immunoinflammatory response, with the thickness of the neointimas progressively increasing over time. Conversely, 45S5-Glass can elicit a local tissue inflammatory response with significant aggregation of inflammatory cells, concomitant with the formation of numerous fibrous vesicles after 1 week of implantation. Two weeks later, the tissue inflammatory response of 45S5-Glass became slightly milder, with abundant inflammatory cells and fibrous vesicles remaining. It has been reported that 45S5 glass causes external alkalinization, thereby influencing cellular metabolism and resulting in delayed bone mineral formation, dystrophic ossification, and exacerbation of the soft tissue reaction [52].

Macrophages play a pivotal role in the immune system, actively participating in inflammatory responses. In the early inflammatory phase, macrophages induce an inflammatory response through the expression of proinflammatory cytokines. CD68 is a heavily glycosylated glycoprotein that is highly expressed in macrophages [53]. As shown in Fig. 7(b), many macrophages were recruited around the samples after 1 week of implantation. In the 45S5-Glass group, accumulation of CD68+ macrophages surrounding fibrous vesicles are observed after implantation for 1 week. Two weeks later, continued accumulation of CD68+ macrophages are evident around the tissue in contact with 45S5 glass, as well as the remaining fibrous vesicles. For the SP-Ceram and SP-Glass groups, the number of CD68+ macrophages in the surrounding tissue in contact with the samples 2 weeks after implantation are significantly lower than that observed in the groups after implantation for 1 week. In response to tissue injury or inflammation, macrophages undergo polarization toward the M1 phenotype, thereby inducing the production of proinflammatory chemokines and cytokines. Alleviation of inflammation is crucial for the modulation of bone homeostasis. During the process of healing, macrophages undergo a transformation into an M2 phenotype with anti-inflammatory properties, subsequently producing anti-inflammatory chemokines and cytokines to relieve inflammation and facilitate tissue repair and remodeling [54]. IF staining of the M1 marker CD86 (red) and the M2 marker CD206 (green) was used to investigate the changes in the number of macrophages of different phenotypes (Fig. 7(c)). The M1 phenotype of the macrophages in the SP-Ceram and SP-Glass groups predominantly converted to the M2 phenotype after 2 weeks of implantation, while a significant number of M1 macrophages remained in the 45S5-Glass group, and the quantitative statistical results revealed a greater proportion of M1 macrophages in the 45S5-Glass group. The accelerated degradation of 45S5-Glass may result in a significant quantity of ions and alkaline compounds entering the surrounding tissues, eliciting a severe inflammatory response and subsequent recruitment of inflammatory cells. These results indicate that SP-Glass can induce a milder inflammatory microenvironment after implantation *in vivo* and has the potential to facilitate the shift of macrophages from a proinflammatory M1 state to an anti-inflammatory M2 state, which offers significant benefits for tissue regeneration.

Moreover, the histological images of the organs collected from the sacrificed rats after they were implanted with the femoral defects for 8 weeks are presented in Fig. 7(e). Compared with those in the 45S5-Glass group, no noticeable abnormalities or evident organ damage were observed in the SP-Glass group, indicating the excellent *in vivo* biocompatibility of SP-Glass.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, pure SP-Glass with high uniformity was fabricated via a containerless melting approach, which has not been fabricated via the traditional melting method. In this process, the stable BOs in the Si‒O tetrahedra of SP-Ceram are transformed into mainly NBOs in SP-Glass. Furthermore, the proportions of highly active *Q*1 and *Q*2 in the silicate network structure of SP-Glass decrease, whereas the proportions of *Q*3 and *Q*4 increase, resulting in increased connectivity of the silicate network compared with that of 45S5-Glass. Interestingly, SP-Glass shows excellent biocompatibility and can provide a moderate ion release rate during the degradation process to create a mildly alkaline microenvironment for facilitating the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of osteoblasts and inhibiting osteoclast activity, thereby promoting bone regeneration *in vivo*. Hence, SP-Glass, which combines biocompatibility and osteogenic activity, represents a new class of bioactive glasses for bone regeneration.
