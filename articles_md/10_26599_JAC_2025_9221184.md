## 1 Introduction

Processing science and technology are central to ceramics. Powder synthesis, green body forming, sintering, as well as postsintering treatment and machining altogether determine the properties, performance, and reliability of ceramic materials and products [1]. This differs substantially from some other fields where the chemical composition more or less determines the overall performance matrix of the materials. The fundamental reason is that ceramics have strong ionic and covalent bonds, lack active plastic deformation mechanisms under in-service conditions, thus remain brittle and sensitive to macroscopic flaws left or produced during processing. One major task is thus to reduce the flaw sensitivity via toughening. In ceramics, (flexural) strength and (fracture) toughness are often synergistic, which again differs from the case of metals and metallic alloys. This is why tetragonal zirconia is practically stronger than alumina despite having a lower hardness (a measurement of materials “intrinsic” strength).

It then comes to the question why one should care about the strength of very brittle ceramics without effective toughening mechanisms. In addition to the detailed technological requirements of specific materials, we argue that the realization and exploration of ultra-strength brittle ceramics are the best way to calibrate and improve processing in a scientific manner. Kingery [1] pointed out that sintering by itself is a proof test. The The ability to sinter ceramics to high density, fine grain size, and at low temperatures is the testimony of good processing. The same argument holds for flaw-sensitive mechanical properties such as three-point bending, and it best applies to brittle ceramics, as their low toughness leads to extreme sensitivity to microstructural features (e.g., grain size, density, microstructural uniformity) and processing flaws—the factors that collectively govern the fracture behaviors. This is the logic of the present work, proof-testing the processing of alumina ceramics using flexural strength.

Below, we first review the state-of-the-art on the flexural strength of alumina ceramics reported in the literature. Table 1 summarizes the flexural strength (*σ*3pb) of three-point bending tests reported in the literature (selectively listed for the best data we are aware of) [3−12], as well as the powders, forming and sintering methods, sintering temperature (*T*), average grain size (*G*avg of the sintered samples, and span length (*L*) of the three-point bending tests. Their *σ*3pb values typically fall into the range of 500–900 MPa. The highest value reported to date is 1330±150 MPa from Koike *et al*. [3] in 1996 using TM-DAR powders, centrifugal casting of a special equipment, and pressureless sintering at 1230 °C. They tried hot isostatic press (HIP) post-treatment, but the strength degraded significantly after HIP at 1250–1300 °C [3]. Except for the work of Koike *et al*. [3], there are no reports on alumina ceramics with *σ*3pb above 1 GPa in the past 30 years. In 2018, Michalek *et al*. [13] reported a biaxial strength of 1205±93 MPa via ball-on-three-ball tests using “unknown but commercially available powders” with high purity, injection molding, pressureless sintering, and HIP post-treatment. The unknown powders and different types of flexural strength measurements make this work difficult to compare. Ikesue and Aung recently reported a flexural strength of 921 MPa via four-point bending tests following JIS R1601 with an unknown span length [14], which is very high and challenging to reproduce, as is the case of Koike *et al*. Some other notes include the following: (i) TM-DAR is the most commonly used alumina powder to obtain high flexural strength. (ii) Colloidal forming methods are able to provide fewer processing flaws and higher strength than dry forming methods such as cold isostatic pressing (CIP). (iii) Low sintering temperatures and fine grain sizes are beneficial, as the data in Table 1 exclude the common sintering temperature of ≥ 1500 °C for alumina and coarsened grain sizes over a few microns. This also excludes possible abnormal grain growth that is especially detrimental to alumina.

Considering the three decades’ gap of producing 1 GPa alumina ceramics (here referring to *σ*3pb ≥ 1 GPa following ASTM C1161-13), it is non-trivial to revisit the processing and verify the credibility. This revisiting is especially meaningful given alumina’s central role in the fundamental science and industrial applications of advanced ceramics in conventional and new fields. This sets the task of the present work. Referring to Table 1, TM-DAR powders (high purity and high sinterability) and centrifugal casting (high density and uniform microstructure of the formed green bodies) were chosen as the raw materials and forming technique. For sintering temperature reduction and grain size refinement, pressureless two-step sintering was chosen [15,16]. We showed that fully dense alumina ceramics can be obtained by two-step sintering at 1275 °C for 1 min and 1175 °C for 20 h, reaching 99.2% relative density, 0.52 μm grain size, 21.6 GPa Vickers hardness, 3.81 MPa m1/2 indentation fracture toughness, and 1036±32 MPa flexural strength by three-point bending over a 20 mm span length. The calculated critical flaw size responsible for fracture is 3.4 µm based on Griffith’s fracture theory of brittle materials [17]. Implications from this study shall be discussed.

## 2 Experimental

Commercial high-purity *α*-Al2O3 powder (TM-DAR; Taimei Chemicals Co. Ltd., Japan) was used as the starting material. The powders were first deagglomerated by ball milling at 300 r/min using deionized water as the solvent. Concentrated HNO3 was added to the slurry and obtain a total acid concentration of 1 mol/L. This step was intended to destabilize the particle dispersion and facilitate sedimentation under moderate centrifugal conditions [18]. The slurry was then transferred to a custom-made plastic container and centrifugal casting was conducted at 3000 r/min (equivalent to a g force of 1800 g) for 20 min using a desktop lab-size centrifuge (TG20.5; BIORIDGE, China). The obtained wet cake was dried at 50 °C to obtain disk-shaped green bodies with 43 mm in diameter. The green bodies were heat-treated at 600 °C for 1 h (1 °C/min heating rate and natural cooling) to remove residual water and HNO3. The 600 °C-treated green bodies did not show any shrinkage before and after the heat-treatment and were used for microstructure characterizations.

Two types of pressureless sintering schedules were conducted in air inside a muffle furnace. (i) Constant-heating-rate sintering: Green bodies were heated to target temperatures without being held and then cooled to room temperature. (ii) Two-step sintering: Green bodies were heated to a higher first-step sintering temperature (*T*1) for 1 min, then immediately cooled to the second-step sintering temperature (*T*2) for 20 h, and finally cooled to room temperature. For all the experiments, the heating rate was set to 5 °C/min. The cooling rate from *T*1 to *T*2 in two-step sintering was set as 10 °C/min to preserve the microstructure achieved at *T*1 upon reaching *T*2. For other cooling stages above 600 °C, the rate was set to 5 °C/min; below 600 °C, furnace cooling was used.

The pore size distribution and average pore size were characterized via a mercury porosimeter (AutoPore V 9600, Micromeritics, USA). Densities were measured via Archimedes’ method. The theoretical density of Al2O3 was set as 3.99 g/cm3 to calculate the relative density (*ρ*). The microstructure was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Merlin, Zeiss, Germany). For samples with *ρ* avg) was determined by statistically counting more than 500 grains from SEM images of the fracture surfaces. No correction factor was applied. For samples with *ρ* > 75%, *G*avg was calculated via the linear intercept method with > 200 intercepts and a correction factor of 1.56 from SEM images of the polished and thermally etched surfaces. The thermal etching temperature was set to 150 °C below the sintering temperature for the constant-heating-rate sintered samples and 150 °C below *T*2 for the two-step sintered samples, with the holding time of 2 h in all the cases.

The test bars for flexural strength measurements were cut with a diamond wheel and polished with boron carbide suspensions of W14 and W7 grades. They were then fine-polished with a 50 nm silica suspension to obtain a scratch-free, mirror-like surface. After polishing, the long edges of the samples were chamfered to reduce the stress concentration. The flexural strength was measured via three-point bending tests according to ASTM C1161-13 on three 25 mm × 2 mm × 1.5 mm bars via a 100 kN universal tester (AGIC, Shimadzu, Japan). The test conditions included a span length of 20 mm and a loading rate of 0.2 mm/min. The Vickers hardness (*H*V) was measured via an indenter (Tukon 2500B, Wilson, USA) under a 9.8 N load for 10 s over 10 successful indents. The indentation fracture toughness (* KICindent) was calculated from the Vickers indentation cracks via the Anstis equation (Eq. (1)) [19]:

(1) KICindent=0.016(EHV)0.5Pc1.5

where E* denotes the Young’s modulus (taken as 380 GPa for Al2O3 [20]), *P* is the indentation load (N), and *c* represents the radial crack length (μm). Ten indices were used to calculate the mean and standard deviation of *K*Icindent. The critical flaw size (*a*) was calculated via Griffith’s fracture theory for brittle materials (Eq. (2)) [17]:

(2) σ3pb=KICindentYπa

where *Y* denotes the geometry factor taken as 1.12 [21].

## 3 Results and discussion

A key technique of Koike *et al*. [3] is a special equipment allowing high-speed centrifuge with a very large *g*-force of 10,000–20,000 g over a heavy metal mold containing ceramic slurries. It is expected to produce high-quality green bodies that can be readily densified to full density at temperatures down to 1230 °C. With the same design principle for centrifugal casting and recognizing the technical difficulties in setting up a specialized centrifuge, we added acid to the slurry containing TM-DAR alumina to destabilize the particle dispersion and assist sedimentation under modest centrifuge conditions. With a total acid concentration of 1 mol/L, we were able to obtain a sedimented wet cake within a few minutes at 3000 r/min (equivalent to 1800 g force, much smaller than Koike *et al*.’s setup) in a desktop lab centrifuge. The cake can be slowly dried in an oven without cracking and remains a complete piece with good surface finishing.

Acid-assisted forming did not alter the fundamental properties of the starting powders. The powders remained highly crystalline and pure *α*-alumina (Fig. 1), with no detectable transitional alumina phases or other impurities. The dried green body has a diameter of approximately 43 mm. The powders are well packed, resulting in a high relative density of 63%. This is close to the relative density of 64% in the case of random close packing of spheres in three dimensions [22]. SEM inspection confirmed that the microstructure is free of observable macroscopic defects at low magnification (Fig. 2(a)) and that the particles are well packed uniformly at high magnification (Fig. 2(b)). The measured particle size distribution (Fig. 2(c)) is unimodal, and the average particle size is 145 nm. The pore size distribution (Fig. 2(d)) is very narrow, and the average pore size is 53 nm, which equals 37% of the average particle size. These microstructural characterizations prove that the employed acid-assisted centrifugal casting produces high-quality green bodies suitable for follow-up sintering.

Green bodies have exceptional sinterability. To demonstrate this, we first conducted constant-heating-rate sintering experiments, i.e., heating the green bodies to set temperatures without holding, followed by cooling to room temperature. The data in Fig. 3(a) show the rapid onset of sintering between 1100 and 1200 °C, and densification to full density can be finished below 1400 °C even without holding. With respect to the grain size, pronounced grain growth can be suppressed up to 93% relative density at 1275 °C without holding (Figs. 3(a) and 3(b)). This is very close to the theoretical prediction of the highest relative density of 92%, which marks the transition from interconnected pore channels in the intermediate sintering stage to isolated pores in the final sintering stage from Rayleigh instability analysis [23]. This suggests that a uniform microstructure (the size distributions and spatial distributions of grains and pores) can retain uniform pore attachment to grain boundaries and best utilize the Zenner pinning effect of diminishing porosity. The grain growth above 1300 °C is very fast, rapidly coarsening the average grain size (G*avg) to 1.36 μm when the close-to-full density is reached at 1400 °C.

The microstructural characteristics shown in Fig. 4 support the above results. Pronounced sintering and the formation of inter-particle sintering necks, grain boundaries and continuous pore channels are clear when the sample is treated at 1200 °C, as shown in Fig. 4(b), representing the microstructural features of the intermediate sintering stage. The breakup of the continuous pore channels into isolated pores is clear when the samples are treated at 1250–1300 °C, as shown in Figs. 4(c) and 4(d), representing the transition from the intermediate stage to the final sintering stage. The elimination of porosity to full density and rapid grain growth are clear when the samples are treated at 1350–1400 °C, as shown in Figs. 4(e)–4(g), representing the microstructural features of the final sintering stage. In all the cases, the microstructure is rather uniform without abnormal grain growth.

To suppress grain growth in the final sintering stage (i.e., the region with a relative density > 93% in Fig. 3(b)) and achieve dense ceramics with finer microstructures, a two-step sintering strategy was subsequently employed. Two-step sintering operates on the principle of exploiting the differential kinetics between grain boundary diffusion and grain boundary migration [15,16]. By first sintering at a higher temperature of T*1 to achieve an intermediate density and then lowering and holding at a lower temperature of *T*2 for an extended period, densification proceeds via grain boundary diffusion, while grain coarsening is effectively suppressed. This mechanism allows the sintered sample to retain an unusually high density-to-grain-size ratio [15,16]. In our previous work [24], two-step sintering was practiced in gel-casted samples using TM-DAR powders. The work identified a high critical relative density of ~94% after the first sintering step for successful two-step sintering to fully densify the samples. With such information and the sintering data in Fig. 3, we set *T*1 as 1250 °C (leading to 89% relative density in constant-heat-rate sintering) and 1275 °C (leading to 93% relative density in constant-heat-rate sintering) and conducted two-step sintering trials with varying *T*2 values. For simplicity, the holding time (*t*1) at *T*1 was set as 1 min for better thermal equilibrium for large samples (because we target for the measurement of the flexural strength), and the holding time (*t*2) at *T*2 was set as 20 h (typical for two-step sintering). As shown in Table 2, setting *T*1 as 1250 °C cannot enable successful two-step sintering to reach full density, despite the high *T*2 up to 1200 °C. In contrast, setting *T*1 as 1275 °C can enable successful two-step sintering to reach > 99% relative density when *T*2 is set as 1175 and 1200 °C. Because the latter two offer similar densities while *T*2 at 1175 °C results in a relatively fine grain size, we consider *T*1 = 1275 °C and *T*2 = 1175 °C as the optimal two-step sintering conditions for the centrifugally cast green bodies. This corresponds to a temperature difference (Δ*T*) of 100 °C between the first and second sintering steps and a critical density of 93%, which is similar to our previous work in gel-casted TM-DAR. The final average grain size is 0.52 μm according to the linear intercept method, which is the finest in Table 1. This demonstrates the effectiveness of two-step sintering in achieving high density with suppressed grain growth in centrifugally casted TM-DAR green bodies. Moreover, we checked the microstructure after different two-step sintering experiments, and no abnormal grain growth was identified. Selected examples of the SEM images are shown in Fig. 5 for *T*1 = 1275 °C and *T*2 = 1150–1200 °C.

We now proceed to test the mechanical properties of the dense samples prepared via TM-DAR powders, acid-assisted moderate-condition centrifugal casting, and pressureless two-step sintering. As explained at the beginning of the manuscript, this is the best proof-test of the processing techniques. The samples have a Vickers hardness of 21.6±0.4 GPa, an indentation fracture toughness of 3.81±0.32 MPa∙m1/2, and a flexural strength of 1036±32 MPa by three-point bending (span length: 20 mm; a larger span length was limited by the capability of our centrifuge and the corresponding size of the centrifuge container). The Vickers hardness is greater than that reported for coarse-grained micron-scale alumina (4,10,25] and aligns with values reported for submicron-grained alumina with similar grain sizes (~21–22 GPa) [14,25], suggesting a typical Hall–Petch relationship. The indentation fracture toughness is consistent with values reported in the literature [20,26,27] and shows no clear microstructural dependence, which is understandable given the absence of additional toughening mechanisms. The > 1 GPa flexural strength is especially encouraging, as it not only far surpasses most values reported for alumina ceramics [4−12], bridging a thirty-year gap in reproducing such high-performance alumina but also confirms the great potential of colloidal processing and two-step low-temperature sintering in producing high-density, fine-grained, and flaw-free ceramics. According to Griffith’s fracture theory of brittle materials in Eq. (2), a critical flaw size of 3.4 µm is responsible for fracture at such stress, which is 6.5 times the average grain size. Figure 6 shows the fracture surfaces of the samples. The fracture clearly exhibited mixed intra- and inter-granular modes, suggesting strong grain boundary cohesion despite the low sintering temperature of 1175 °C for full densification (weak grain boundaries typically lead predominantly to intergranular fracture). At least in such a temperature range, two-step sintering does not produce weak grain boundaries for pre-mature crack opening.

Before ending, we would like to comment on the implications of the present study. First, 1 GPa alumina ceramics have been reproduced. In our opinion, the reported value of 1330±150 MPa in Koike *et al*.’s 1996 paper [3] is credible. Second, centrifugal casting does not necessarily require a complex, expensive setup and can be designed simpler if one can work out the proper colloidal formulation. Acid-assisted centrifugal casting at moderate speed is a practical, cost-effective way to achieve similar high-quality forming. Third, two-step sintering at lowered sintering temperatures can produce very high-strength technical ceramics. At least for alumina, a temperature 28] and ceria [29]) and spinel. Composites such as zirconia toughened alumina (ZTA) and alumina “toughened” zirconia (ATZ) can be used if phase separation can be suppressed during centrifugal casting. Fifth, ultra-strength ceramics can be produced with scalable and cost-effective processing techniques. 1 GPa alumina is doable. This can be done better if further cautions are taken to further shrink the critical flaw size. With some toughening mechanisms, such as phase transformation toughening, the flexural strength can go to > 2–3 GPa levels. Sixth, machining and surface finishing are important to showcase the intrinsic high strength of those ceramics. In our case, the flexural strength improved from ~800 MPa to > 1000 MPa when more careful surface polishing was employed. The corresponding optical micrographs of the polished bar surfaces are presented in Fig. 7. In fact, we think the main difference between our 1036±32 MPa strength over 20 mm span length and Koike *et al*.’s [3] 1330±150 MPa over 30 mm span length is likely not attributable—or at least not predominantly—to intrinsic microstructures but rather stems from variations in machining and polishing procedures (detailed machining and polishing methods were not reported by Koike *et al*.). Lastly, for those ultra-strength samples, a large sound was produced when the bars for bending tests were fractured. The samples then suddenly broke into several pieces and burst out, some with high kinetic energy and cannot be found anymore. This, in our opinion, can be used as a simple measurement for ultrastrength ceramic samples, regardless of the exact value of the calculated strength.

## 4 Conclusions

(1) The course of the 1 GPa alumina ceramics was revisited. The work of Koike *et al*. [3] has been reproduced. A flexural strength of 1036±32 MPa by three-point bending tests over 20 mm span length has been obtained.

(2) The acid-assisted centrifugal casting method was able to form fine powders such as TM-DAR into high-quality green bodies at moderate centrifuge speeds and equivalent *g*-forces. It largely lowers the equipment requirements and can be scalable more easily. The produced green bodies have a high density of 63%, a uniform microstructure, narrowly distributed nanopores, and minimal macroscopic defects.

(3) Pressureless two-step sintering can effectively densify the above green bodies to > 99% relative density at low sintering temperatures down to 1175 °C in air. The sintered samples have fine grain sizes and very high flexural strengths. Grain boundary cohesion is strong enough to enable mixed intra- and inter-granular cracking under high stress in bending tests.

(4) Ultra-strength ceramics, even for brittle ones, are valuable to calibrate processing techniques. The documented properties would stimulate more thinkings on the practical applications of ceramic materials and products and enable new opportunities.
