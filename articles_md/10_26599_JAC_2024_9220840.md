## 1 Introduction

Nowadays, solid-state lighting sources have been recognized due to their long lifetime, high luminous efficiency (LE), and eco-friendliness. However, LEDs suffer from severe luminescent decline at high temperatures, which limits their application in high-power lighting [1−3]. Laser diode (LD) lighting source, as a new generation of technology, is compact with high LE and high brightness. Consequently, it has various applications, including high-brightness projection, searchlights, and automotive lighting [4−7]. In general, LD lighting system is achieved by exciting phosphor converters using blue LDs [8−10]. The common phosphor converters includes phosphor in epoxy-resin [11−13], phosphor in glass [14−18], single crystal phosphor [19−23], and transparent ceramic (TC) [24−28]. Among them, TC is considered a promising candidate for a phosphor converter because of the high thermal conductivity, high luminescent, and high saturated power density.

In a typical LD lighting system, TC is usually expected to be high transmittance as a light-conversion phosphor [24−26]. However, this high transmittance of TC reduces the absorption of incident blue light and then leads to a lower light conversion efficiency [29−31]. In addition, owing to the doping of rare-earth ions, the thermal conductivity of ceramics is always reduced, thus inducing a thermal-focusing effect, which limits their application in high power lighting [17,32]. Researchers have prepared the CCP to solve the above luminous and thermal problems, and the secondary phase often has the higher thermal conductivity, such as Al2O3 (32–35 W·m−1·K−1), MgO (47–54 W·m−1·K−1), and AlN (~220 W·m−1·K−1) [26,27,29]. Zhao *et al.* [33] also found that using Al2O3 in CCPs could improve the thermal stability, and the CCPs exhibited high energy efficiency (84.6%). The thermal conductivity of MgO–YAG:Ce CCPs prepared by hot pressing method was also effectively improved, and the luminescence saturation threshold could reach 32 W/mm2 [26]. However, the CCPs were often designed with a large size (*φ* ＞ 10 mm) as limited by the forming mold of green body and the strong heat-release requirement, and the luminous area became larger, which led to an increase in the volume of encapsulation system, as well as the higher manufacturing costs.

The current mainstream method of LD lighting encapsulation is divided into the reflective type and transmissive type [34−36]. For the reflection structure, the white light is obtained by residual blue light mixed with yellow fluorescence through a remote-exciting phosphor converter with a blue LD [37]. However, it has a bulky volume and brings a greater difficulty in the second-step optical system design. On the contrary, the chip-level packaging is adopted in the transmission configuration which effectively reduces the etendue and significantly improve the integration of lighting system [38]. However, in reflective and transmissive configuration, a yellow ring phenomenon caused by mismatched spatial intensity distribution of emitted blue and yellow light appears, which leads to visual discomfort in some lighting applications. For instance, we prepared small-sized CCPs using YAG as the wrapping material [39]. The fluorescence generated by the ceramic escaped from the sides of transparent YAG, resulting in an obvious yellow ring. The non-uniform mixing phenomenon by blue light and yellow light was difficult to eliminate, though this phenomenon could enhance the luminous flux (LF). In response to the yellow ring phenomenon, Li *et al.* [40] optimized the light energy mapping relationship by designing a freeform lens structure, achieving high-quality white light illumination without yellow ring. In addition, a parabolic reflector coupled with a freeform lens was used by Ma *et al.* [41] to form a secondary optical system, which matched the intensity distribution of blue light and yellow light to achieve uniform illumination with small divergent angle. However, they both adopted packaging design and then increased the difficulty in optical design and processing. Some researchers have also extensively designed composite structure ceramic to improve the CRI and LE in laser lighting recently [42−44]. Therefore, exploring a new composite structure ceramic to achieve high color spatial uniformity without secondary optical design is a promising direction.

In this study, the ceramic with a novel Al2O3/Al2O3–YAG:Ce/YAG composite structure was fabricated using the two-step sintering method. In order to solve the light non-uniformity phenomenon using YAG as the wrapping material, the Al2O3 ceramic is emerging to become a well wrapping material, owing to its low cost, outstanding thermal stability and opacity after sintering (~1450 ℃). The Al2O3–YAG:Ce were wrapped with the Al2O3 and YAG TC, which effectively solved the yellow ring phenomenon in laser lighting applications. Meanwhile, the thermal behavior of ceramic could also be alleviated due to the high thermal conductivity of the wrapping material. The microstructures, thermal behaviors, and luminescence behaviors of ceramics were systematically studied, including photoluminescence spectra (PL), photoluminescence excitation spectra (PLE), electroluminescence spectra (EL), and spatial CCT distribution. By adjusting the thickness of the CCP, doping concentration of Ce3+ ion and the contents of Al2O3 in the ceramics, an optimized high-brightness white light source with an LF of 618 lm, an LE of 126 lm/W, a CCT of 6615 K, a CRI of 69.9, and an operating temperature as low as 74.1 ℃ was obtained. These results indicate that the Al2O3/Al2O3–YAG:Ce/YAG composite structure ceramic is a promising candidate material for the high-power laser lighting applications.

### 2.1 Material preparation

High purity commercial Y2O3 (99.99%, Alfa Aesar, Ward Hill, USA), α-Al2O3 (99.99%, Alfa Aesar, Ward Hill, USA), and CeO2 (99.99%, Alfa Aesar, Ward Hill, USA) powders were used as the initial raw material powders. In the first step, the powders of Al2O3–YAG:Ce and YAG were prepared. The content of Al2O3 was 10–40 wt%, and the concentrations of the Ce3+ were set to 0.2 and 1.0 at%. PEI, anhydrous ethanol, and Al2O3 balls were used as dispersant, solvent, and ball milling medium, respectively. The powders were dried in a drying oven at 55 ℃ for 10 h. The dried powders were crushed and sieved three times with a 100-mesh screen. Subsequently, they were calcined in a muffle furnace at 800 ℃ for 300 min. The calcined powders were pressed into flakes using a *φ*22 mm mold and then cold isostatic pressing at 200 MPa for 10 min. The green bodies were vacuum sintered at 1770 ℃ for 8 h and then annealed at 1450 ℃ for 10 h under air. The prepared ceramics were polished with both sides and cut to 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm to await further shaping. In the second step, the Al2O3 powders were molded under a pressure of 3 MPa, and then the ceramics with a size of 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm was pressed into the Al2O3 green bodies. After cold isostatic pressure of 200 MPa for 10 min, the green bodies were placed into a muffle furnace and sintered at 1500 ℃ for 10 h to obtain the composite structure ceramics. After polishing, the total thickness of Al2O3–YAG:Ce/YAG ceramic was 0.7 mm. The thickness of Al2O3–YAG:Ce ceramics changed from 0.2 to 0.4 mm, corresponding to the thickness of YAG transparent ceramics in the range of 0.3−0.5 mm. ### 2.2 Characterization

The macroscopic connection of the sample was characterized with an optical microscope (FXD-30 MW, Shandong, China) and the microscopic morphology of the sample was measured by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-6510, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). An energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS, Inca X-Max, Oxford Instruments, Oxford, UK) connected with the SEM was utilized to recognize the element distribution of ceramics. PL and PLE were detected with a spectrophotometer (OmniFluo 900, Beijing, China). The maximum power of the blue fiber laser is 4.6 W (4 A). The diameter of fiber core is 200 μm and the beam angle is 8.8°. Therefore, the laser irradiation point at a distance of 0.5 mm can be approximated as a circle with a diameter of 0.354 mm. The input power of the LD is divided by the area of the laser irradiation spot to determine the input power density. The LF, CCT, and CRI values at different power densities were investigated with the integrating sphere and a spectrometer (HAS-2000, Hangzhou, China). The luminescence intensity of the ceramics-based device at different angles was characterized with a spatial spectroradiometer (GO-SPEX 500, Hangzhou, China). Surface temperature distribution of ceramics was measured by an infrared camera with the spatial resolution was 17 μm (Fotric 225s, Fotric, USA). A heat-conduction coefficient measurement device was used to test the thermal conductivity (Hotdisk 2500S, Goteborg, Sweden).

## 3 Results and discussion

An illustration shown in Fig. 2(a) displays the interaction between laser source and a conventional parallel CCP. In the transmissive configuration model, the blue laser is a collimated beam, but the yellow light excited by laser is emitted from all surface instead of the light output surface. The blue and yellow light exhibits different spatial intensity distributions, which results in a problem with the angular color uniformity (yellow ring phenomenon) [41]. Two solutions are mainly used to address the aforementioned problem. One is expanding the blue light distribution, and the other is narrowing the yellow light distribution. However, the first method would decrease the luminance. Therefore, an improved scheme was propose and the Al2O3/Al2O3–YAG:Ce/YAG composite structure ceramic with a size of 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm was designed (Fig. 2(b)), matching the size of the laser irradiation point (the diameter of the circular spot is 0.354 mm). In this transmission configuration, the Al2O3–YAG:Ce/YAG ceramic was excited by the blue LD from the bottom. Importantly, the transparent YAG ceramic did not affect the incident of blue LD due to its high transmittance, and it could also serve as a heat sink for the fluorescent ceramic, providing a pathway for thermal conduction generated during the laser excitation. As known, the emitted light from the fluorescent ceramic exhibited the typical Lambertian emission. The opaque Al2O3 ceramic in this structure could constrain the yellow lateral fluorescence emission and redirect the scattered blue light, to improve their spatial intensity distribution. Additionally, the optimized structure would be beneficial for thermal management. In general, the composite ceramics are usually in the reflective configuration model. However, in this work, the composite ceramics were used in the transmissive configuration. In fact, a secondary phase was introduced into the ceramic to scatter the blue light to enhance the absorption of blue light and achieve more uniform illumination, and more importantly, most light could be passed through the ceramics due to the thin thicknesses (below 0.4 mm) in Fig. 2(b), although their inline transmittance was not very high (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)).

The yellow CCPs covered with Al2O3 could be detected in Fig. 3(a). Figure 3(b) depicts a photograph of their optical microscope. The boundary of the Al2O3 (right) part and junction part of Al2O3–YAG:Ce (left) were clearly visible and tightly connected. To evaluate the bonding strength between two ceramic phases, the multiple fracture tests on them were conducted and the fracture positions not only occurred at the interface between the two phases but also within the Al2O3–YAG:Ce ceramics. This observation remarkably indicates that a strong bonding state was achieved for this composite structure ceramics via two-step sintering. In the meanwhile, the transparent YAG ceramics and luminescent layered Al2O3–YAG:Ce ceramics also exhibited the good bonding strength due to their same garnet category. The heat generated during the laser excitation by the Al2O3–YAG:Ce could be quickly transferred to the Al2O3 due to the strong structural connections between the ceramics, resulting in a reduction in the temperature of the luminescent centers. The structural design uses Al2O3 as the wrapping material, which has little effect on the photometry and chromaticity of the ceramic. The advantage of this structural design is to match the intensity distribution of blue light and yellow light, to achieve more uniform illumination.

Figure 6 shows the PLE and PL spectra of Al2O3/Al2O3–YAG:Ce/YAG ceramics with different Ce3+ concentrations and Al2O3 contents under the excitation of xenon lamp. The PLE of ceramic contained two broad absorption bands peaking at about 340 and 460 nm. The PL at 460 nm excitation was a broadband centered at 530 nm, which was attributed to the Ce3+ ion 5d–4f energy level transition [45]. The excitation and emission intensities of ceramics with the same Ce3+ ions were reduced with increasing the contents of Al2O3. More Al2O3 in ceramics indirectly reduces the amount of Ce3+ ions on the ceramic, which decreases the excitation and emission intensities [29,45−47]. The intensity of the ceramics with 1.0 at% Ce concentration was lower than that of low Ce3+ ions (0.2 at%) of ceramics with the same Al2O3 content owing to the concentration quenching [32].

LF, LE, CRI, and CCT were meticulously characterized for the ceramic encapsulated devices to further explore the luminescence performance under high-power density excitation. Figure 7(a) depicts the value of the LF variation with the laser power density. In the transmissive configuration, the LF of CP02.10.04, CP02.20.04, CP02.10.03, and CP02.20.03 ceramics exceeded 650 lm at 4.675 W (47.51 W/mm2) laser excitation. No luminous saturation occurred, and the LF enhanced linearly with the increase in power density for all ceramics. These results indicate that the new design of ceramic structure could meet the requirements of high-power laser lighting. Figure 7(b) illustrates the LE of the ceramics as a function of the power density. In comparison to the typical LE of commercial phosphors encapsulated in epoxy-resin (usually around 100 lm/W in the transmissive configuration [48]), our ceramics exhibit a significant improvement, ranging from 110 to 150 lm/W. The ceramics with the Ce concentration of 1.0 at% was also considered. The details are shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. The LF of all ceramics exceeded 600 lm and even reached 800 lm. Owing to the large amount of Ce3+ ions in ceramics, most of the incident blue laser was absorbed and converted into yellow green light, which resulted in a significant improvement in the LE of ceramics, with a maximum of nearly 170 lm/W. Figures 7(c) and 7(d) depict the CRI and CCT of ceramics under different power density excitations of LDs, respectively. The CRI of all ceramics with Ce3+ concentration of 0.2 at% was over 60, and the CRI remained stable with the increase in power density. In fact, the high Ce concentration of 1% Ce value had little effect on the CRI change (Fig. S2 in the ESM) due to that it could absorb most blue light and surely less residual blue light did not improve the CRI effectively. In addition, the CCT of all ceramics was between 4500 and 7000 K, which met the color temperature requirements in practical lighting applications [49]. This finding underscores the outstanding luminescence stability of our designed structural ceramics, suggesting their potential for highly effective practical applications.

Figure 8 displays the surface temperatures of ceramics when subjected to an incident blue laser with a power density of 47.51 W/mm2. The temperatures were all below 100 ℃, which indicates that the ceramics had excellent thermal performance. The insets in Fig. 8 showcase the temperature distribution of CP1.10.04 under blue LD excitation, revealing a maximum temperature at the center and a uniform temperature distribution in the surrounding region. At the same thickness and the Ce concentration of 0.2 at%, the surface temperatures of the ceramics with increased the content of Al2O3 were declined from 76.1 to 69.2 ℃ for the thickness of 0.2 mm, from 84.2 to 74.1 ℃ for the thickness of 0.3 mm, and from 89.2 to 70.1 ℃ for the thickness of 0.4 mm. The thicker ceramics produced more fluorescence due to the increased absorption of blue laser, which generated more energy loss and led to an increase in the surface temperature of the ceramic. The ceramics with 1.0 at% Ce resulted in higher surface temperatures, which was similar to the above mentioned reason. In the case of sample CP02.40.04, where the thickness of fluorescent layer was 0.4 mm, and the contacting area between the ceramic and Al2O3 wrapping material was larger, resulting in a stronger heat dissipation effect and lower surface temperature compared to CP02.40.03 (thickness of 0.3 mm). Furthermore, the ceramic temperature was influenced by the synergistic effect between heat generation and heat dissipation. When the Ce concentration was 0.2 at%, and the thickness of fluorescent layer was less than 0.3 mm, the heat generation capability was stronger than the heat dissipation capability, resulting in an increase in temperature with increasing thickness. When the Ce concentration reached 1 at%, the heat generation effect was also dominated, resulting in an increase in temperature with increasing thickness.

Under high-power laser excitation, the temperature of the phosphor materials rapidly increases as working time progresses and it leads to a thermal quenching or a rapid decrease in LF and limits the application of laser lighting [50−53]. Therefore, the phosphor materials are often encapsulated by heat sinks, which markedly increases the volume of the lighting system [53−55]. By contrast, the Al2O3–YAG:Ce had a high thermal conductivity (≥ 11 W·m−1·K−1). Most importantly, the opaque Al2O3 had a higher thermal conductivity of 23.85 W·m−1·K−1 compared to Al2O3–YAG:Ce. The details are shown in Table S1 in the ESM. By sintering to tightly connect the above mentioned materials, the heat generated during the luminescent process could be rapidly released. It was also the reason that the ceramics did not reach the luminescence saturation. In addition, the temperature dependent spectra of CP02.40.04 are displayed in Fig. S3 in the ESM. They were recorded from 298 to 473 K with the step of 25 K. Compared to the luminous intensity at room temperature (298 K), the CP02.40.04 maintained 88.2% and 80.7% at 423 and 473 K, respectively. The ceramics exhibited the good thermal stability. Combined the CP02.40.04 with a 4.675 W blue laser, a high brightness light source with the LF exceeding 600 lm, the LE of 126 lm/W, the CCT of 6615 K, the CRI of 69.9, and the operating temperature of 74.1 ℃ was obtained. This result indicates that the Al2O3/Al2O3–YAG:Ce/YAG composite structure ceramic had good thermal management performance under excitation of high-power density.

Figures 9(a)–9(c) display the CIE and photographs of the ceramics in different light colors under a 4.675 W power excitation. The CIE chart illustrates the color information for different ceramics, aiding our understanding of their positions and color representations in the spectrum. The ceramics with the Ce3+ concentration of 1.0 at% exhibited strong yellow light emission, which is displayed in the circled area of the CIE chart. For the ceramics with the Ce concentration of 0.2 at%, the blue component of the emitted light from the ceramic gradually increased with the rise in Al2O3 content, and the CCT changed from 5332 to 6796 K for 0.2 mm, from 5297 to 6774 K for 0.3 mm, and from 4915 to 6615 K for 0.4 mm. When the thickness was 0.4 mm, the lighting effect was significantly improved, such as for CP0.2.30.04 and CP0.2.40.04 ceramics with an emission of positive white light (~6500 K). In addition, the size of spot was about 6 mm (in Figs. 9(a)–9(c)) while the size of luminescent ceramic was only 1 mm × 1 mm. Thus, the impact of spot limitation was not pronounced in this composite structured ceramic. The EL spectra of ceramics excited by a 4.675 W laser (47.51 W/mm2) in the transmissive configuration is shown in Figs. 9(d) and 9(e). The changes in lighting color could be explained in the EL spectrum. As the Al2O3 contents increased from 10 to 40 wt%, the residual blue light of the ceramics with 0.2 at% of Ce3+ was continuously raised due to the less available Ce3+ ions at the same volume [56]. Compared with Al2O3 content, the Ce3+ concentration showed more obvious factors for residual blue light, which resulted in a higher blue content in ceramics with 0.2 at% of Ce3+ than ceramics with 1.0 at% of Ce3+. However, it caused a negative phenomenon that most blue light was absorbed and induced a large amount of yellow emission, which made it difficult to apply in white LD lighting. In fact, the warm light is suitable for living rooms with CCT in the range of 3000–5000 K. While the CCT is over 5000 K, the light as cool white light is suitable for in high illumination situation, like offices and workshops. Hence, adjusting the doping concentration of Ce3+ and the thickness of ceramics allows for the creation of cool and warm light sources tailored to specific applications.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, the Al2O3/Al2O3–YAG:Ce/YAG composite structure ceramics for high-power laser lighting were fabricated via the two-step sintering process. The composite structure ceramics exhibited clear connection interface and good optical quality. The prepared ceramic successfully solved the problem of yellow ring in laser lighting applications, and this structure exhibited excellent thermal performance. In this case, we achieved the match of the intensity distribution of the blue and yellow lights and obtained a high angular color uniformity of 0.9 with a view angle of ±80°. In addition to with the aforementioned optical and thermal performances, the CP02.40.04 (0.4 mm in thickness, 0.2 at% Ce3+ concentration, and 40 wt% Al2O3 content) displayed a high-brightness white light source in the transmissive configuration with the best LF of 618 lm, LE of 126 lm/W, CCT of 6615 K, and CRI of 69.9, and the surface temperature of the ceramic was only 74 ℃. These results indicate that Al2O3/Al2O3–YAG:Ce/YAG composite structure ceramics have high color spatial uniformity and thermal conductivity, and they can play an important role in high-power lighting applications.
