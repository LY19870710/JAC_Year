## 1 Introduction

Solids that are built up of van der Waals (vdW)-bonded layers exhibit fascinating and technologically useful properties [1,2]. They are playing increasingly essential roles in industrial production. Among these vdW solids, graphite, hexagonal boron nitride (*h*-BN), and molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) are the most notable examples [3]. Graphite powders are widely utilized as anode materials in lithium-ion batteries because of their unique intercalation properties and electrically conducting nature, whereas *h*-BN and MoS2 in powder form are widely used as solid lubricants [4−6].

As an analog of typical vdW solids, a large family of MXenes whose interlayer coupling is 2−6 times stronger than that of typical vdW solids has emerged [7]. MXenes are derived from layered ternary carbides/nitrides (MAX Phases). Traditional synthesis is generally based on chemical etching of A atomic layers from MAX phases and spontaneous surface functionalization of MX layers by terminations, including –OH, –O, and –F [8−10]. Through etching MAX with different Lewis acid molten salts at elevated temperatures, MXenes with different functional groups (–Cl, –S, –Se, –Te, and –NH) can be obtained [11,12]. The rich chemistry of MXenes has led to a variety of potential applications. These applications include but are not limited to energy storage [13,14], electromagnetic interference shielding [15,16], water treatment [17], optoelectronics, and more [18]. Nonetheless, MXenes encounter critical issues of lattice defects and degradation due to harsh etching environments. To stabilize MXenes, several efficient strategies, such as optimizing the synthesis of the MAX phase, changing the preparation of MXenes, and altering the storage conditions, have been proposed, whereas direct synthesis is the ultimate solution for addressing this issue [19−21]. In 2023, direct synthesis of Ti2CCl2 was achieved via chemical vapor deposition and traditional solid-state synthesis [22]. Newly layered vdW solids are constantly being discovered by the solid-state community. Recently, direct synthesis of Nb2CSe2 was achieved by using elemental powders and C6Cl6 as a triggering agent. The synthesized Nb2CSe2 had excellent electrocatalytic properties [23]. To date, theoretical calculations of Nb2CSe2 have been based on monolayers and lack experimental support [24−26]. In contrast to calculations based on monolayer Nb2CSe2 MXenes, the as-prepared MXenes are almost in a stacked state with interlayer interactions. Therefore, a detailed understanding of the stacking nature of Nb2CSe2 from both theoretical calculations and experimental works is crucial for the exploration of the physicochemical properties of newly synthesized MXenes.

In this work, we first disclose the structural features of Nb2CSe2 synthesized via a molten salt-assisted method. By combining experimental efforts with density functional theory (DFT) calculations, it was demonstrated that Nb2CSe2 features Se–Nb–C–Nb–Se quintuple atomic layers and that the layers stacked in Bernal mode are energetically favorable. Next, the electronic structure and elastic, vibrational, and friction properties are studied. The electronic structure shows that Nb2CSe2 is an electronic conductor, which is experimentally evidenced by the high electrical conductivity of 6.6×105 S·m−1 at room temperature. The elastic, vibrational, and friction properties are all in line with the nature of vdW interlayer coupling in Nb2CSe2. Moreover, vibrational properties may offer an optical identification standard for new ternary vdW solids in spectroscopic studies, including Raman scattering and infrared (IR) absorption.

### 2.1 Materials

Nb powders (−325 mesh, 99.5%, Beijing Goodwill Metal, China), acetylene black (99.9%, Denka, Japan), Se powders (100 mesh, 99.99%, Aladdin, China), and analytical-grade NaCl and KCl (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) were used as received without further purification. The raw powders included micrometer-scale Nb powders, Se powders, and nanosized acetylene black (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). MoS2 (3−5 μm, 99.5%, Macklin, China) and graphite (99.99%, SGL Carbon, Germany) were used to determine the friction coefficients.

### 2.2 Direct synthesis of Nb<sub>2</sub>CSe<sub>2</sub>

Nb2CSe2 was synthesized via a molten salt strategy using stoichiometric elemental powders of Nb, acetylene black, and Se. Briefly, preweighed reactants were admixed in ethanol and subjected to planetary ball milling (150 r/min for 1 h, ball-to-powder ratio of 5 : 1). After drying, the mixture was compacted into tablets with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 4 mm under a pressure of 10 kN. The tablets were then embedded in powders of equimolar NaCl and KCl and pressed in a steel mold with an inner diameter of 15 mm under a pressure of 10 kN. As a result, the tablets were encapsulated with a shell of NaCl/KCl. After that, the encapsulated samples consisting of elemental powders were placed into an alumina crucible and hermetically sealed within quartz ampules. The sealed samples were then heated to 1250 °C at a rate of 3 °C·min−1 in a horizontal alumina tube furnace (GSL-1600X, Weida, China) with an inner diameter of 70 mm. After being allowed to dry for 20 h at that temperature, the samples were naturally cooled to room temperature. Throughout the entire heating and cooling process, the sealed samples were protected by an argon flow. The resulting powders were collected by crushing the ampules and carefully removing the salts by washing with ultrapure water with a resistance over 18 MΩ·cm and vacuum-assisted filtration with a porous membrane (pore size of 0.45 µm). After drying at 60 °C in an oven (WGL-125D, Taisite, China) overnight, grayish black powders were collected for further use.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The phase constitution and crystal structure were analyzed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 advance, Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 0.15418 nm). The surface morphology, microstructure, and chemical composition were characterized via a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, SU-70, Hitachi, Japan) coupled with an energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS; Quanta 600, FEI, USA). The chemical composition of Nb2CSe2 was further analyzed via a high-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Model Axis Supra, UK) system equipped with an Al Kα source (1486.6 eV). The raw XPS data were calibrated with reference to adventitious carbon (C 1s,284.6 eV). Morphological characterization and chemical compositional mapping of Nb2CSe2 were carried out via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200X, Thermo Fisher, Czech Republic) equipped with a Super-X four-probe energy spectrum system operating at 200 kV. For scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) cross-sectional observations, the sample was prepared on a double-beam xenon ion microscope system (Helios G4 PFIB CXe, Thermo Scientific, USA). To minimize damage from the ion beam and preserve the integrity of the sample surface during the focused ion beam (FIB) milling process, platinum deposition was employed prior to milling. Atomic-resolution STEM images of Nb2CSe2 were collected via a 300 kV TEM (FEI spectra 300, Thermo Fisher, the Netherlands) equipped with a high-brightness field-emission gun, a double spherical aberration corrector, and a monochromator. We simulated the high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) and annular bright field (ABF) images via the Dr. probe package developed by Dr. Barthel J., which is based on the multislice method [27]. The simulation parameters for the STEM experiment settings are available in the ESM. Electrical transport properties were measured in the temperature range of 2−300 K in a quantum design’s physical properties measurement system (PPMS). Raman spectra were collected with a confocal laser Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR800, Horiba, Japan) equipped with a HeNe laser (633 nm) as the excitation source. A 50× long working distance objective with a numerical aperture of 0.50 was employed for the Raman measurements. To prevent damage to the samples from laser-induced irradiation, the laser power was maintained below 4 mW on the sample surface. Friction coefficient determination was carried out with a pin-on-disc friction and wear tester (HT-1000, Zhongkekaihua, China) at ambient temperature. K465 superalloy pins were used as counterparts at a normal load of 2.55 N.

### 2.4 Computational methods

For the computational study, two stacking forms of bulk Nb2CSe2, namely, simple hexagonal (SH) and Bernal stacks, were used to investigate the electronic properties via the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) [28,29]. The exchange-correlation potential was described via the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation [30]. A plane wave basis set with a 500 eV cutoff was employed, and Brillouin zone integrations were performed via a 10 × 10 × 1 Monkhorst-pack *k*-point mesh for geometry optimizations. The convergence threshold was set to 10−6 eV for energy and 0.01 eV·Å−1 for force [31]. vdW interactions were accounted for via the use of Grimme’s DFT-D method [32]. The crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) was calculated via the Lobster program [33,34]. Phonon spectra and Raman-active modes were analyzed via the PHONOPY package (Version 2.24.1) with the finite displacement method [35], where the force constant matrix was constructed via slight displacement of atoms in a primitive cell.

### 3.1 Synthesis of Nb<sub>2</sub>CSe<sub>2</sub>

The synthesis strategy of the layered metal carbo-selenide of Nb2CSe2 is schematically illustrated in Fig. 1(a). The synthesis of Nb2CSe2 was achieved through the reactions of elemental powders in a medium composed of an alkaline metal melt. At elevated temperatures (for example, 700 °C) beyond the eutectic point of NaCl and KCl [36], Nb first reacts with Se to form nonstoichiometric Nb−Se intermetallics, whereas NbC*x* is detected as a product of the reaction between carbon black and Nb−Se intermetallics and/or unreacted Nb. Nb2CSe2 was identified at temperatures above 1200 °C. Due to insufficient reaction time and low temperatures, phase-pure Nb2CSe2 was not obtained. With increasing temperature and incubation time, the amount of Nb2CSe2 increased monotonically at the expense of Nb−Se intermetallics and NbC*x*. Dwelling at 1250 °C for 20 h gives rise to phase-pure Nb2CSe2 (Fig. S2 in the ESM). The synthesis of Nb2CSe2 in this work involved primarily the chemical fusion of the intermetallic compound and NbC*x* in a medium composed of alkaline metal chloride melt. The diffraction peaks of NbO2 that appear at a low temperature may be due to reactions that occur during the water-washing process (Fig. S3 in the ESM). Notably, the alkaline metal chloride melt played a positive role in the direct synthesis of Nb2CSe2. This claim was supported by control experiments. In the absence of the alkaline metal chloride melt, phase-pure Nb2CSe2 was not obtained (Fig. S4 in the ESM). The use of molten salts as a liquid medium facilitates the mass transport of active species dissolved therein, consequently accelerating the formation of the target phase [37,38]. Notably, the salt shell acts as a protective barrier that effectively prevents the volatilization of elemental powders (Se in this work) with a low boiling point during the sealing of quartz ampules by a H2/O2 flame, maintaining the preset molar ratio of elemental powders for the synthesis of Nb2CSe2.

### 3.2 Characterizations

The synthesized powders are grayish black (Fig. 1(b)). They consist of aggregated platelets of hundreds of nanometers in the lateral direction (Fig. 1(c)). As measured by nitrogen adsorption at 77 K, the specific surface area of the as-grown Nb2CSe2 platelets is 5.33 m2·g−1 (Fig. S5 in the ESM). XPS analysis is particularly useful for studying surface properties, as the generated electrons have low kinetic energy and remain confined within a range of 2–5 nm from the surface [39]. XPS spectra were collected to understand the elemental compositions and chemical bonding states of Nb2CSe2. The survey spectrum shows that the Nb2CSe2 platelets are composed of Nb, C, Se, and O (Fig. S6(a) in the ESM). Because Se predominates on the surface of the Nb2CSe2 crystals, the Auger Se LMM, which has an extensive structure, interferes with the spectra of other elements (Fig. S7 in the ESM). In the core-level C 1s spectra, C–C, C–O, and C=O peaks are most likely from adventitious carbon (Fig. 1(f)). The energy position of the C–C peak was used for the calibration of the XPS spectra. The peak at approximately 283.2 eV is attributed to C–Nb in Nb2CSe2. Notably, the core-level Nb 3d spectrum was well deconvoluted into two doublets corresponding to 3d5/2 and 3d3/2. The doublets centered at approximately 203.9 eV (Nb 3d5/2) and 206.7 eV (Nb 3d3/2) can be attributed to C–Nb–Se [40,41]. The doublet near 204.8 eV (207.6 eV) corresponds to C–Nb–O [10], which is related to the terminal group formed on the surface of the Nb2C layers (Fig. 1(e) and Table S1 in the ESM). However, the disappearance of the Nb–O signal suggests slight oxidation of the outermost layer of the Nb2CSe2 platelets (Fig. S6(b) in the ESM) [39]. The core-level Se 3d spectrum was deconvoluted into Se 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 peaks at approximately 54.3 and 55.1 eV (Fig. 1(g) and Table S2 in the ESM), respectively, with a fixed area ratio of 3 : 2. No significant changes were observed before or after sputtering, indicating the stable presence of Se in Nb2CSe2 (Fig. S6(d) in the ESM). In addition, there is a broad band at a relatively high binding energy in the Se 3d spectrum, which most likely originates from Se atoms with unsaturated coordination at the outermost surface of the Nb2CSe2 nanoplates [42,43].

Next, we focused on the morphological and structural features of the Nb2CSe2 platelets. Figure 2(c) shows a typical STEM image of the synthesized platelets with a well-defined hexagonal profile, which are 200−500 nm in lateral size. EDS analysis indicates that the atomic percentages of Nb and Se are equivalent (Fig. S8 in the ESM) and that Nb, Se, and C are homogeneously distributed throughout the platelets (Fig. 2(d) and Fig. S9 in the ESM). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) analysis of the Nb2CSe2 platelets revealed that the lattice spacing of 0.29 nm between adjacent planes matches well with the *d*-spacings of Nb2CSe2(010) and (100) planes (Fig. 2(e)) [23,44]. The angle between (010) and (100) planes is 60°, as measured along the [001] zone axis. HAADF-STEM characterization reveals that the Nb2CSe2 material has a hexagonal ring structure along the [001] zone axis, indicating the trigonal symmetry of Nb2CSe2 (Fig. 2(f)). Note that the carbon atom column is invisible, as the intensity of an atomic column in the HAADF-STEM image is proportional to *Z**α* (*Z* is the atomic number, *α* = 1.6–1.9) [45].

The performance of materials in practical applications is primarily determined by their stacking manner. The stacking structure of traditional MXenes includes SH stacking and Bernal stacking [7,46]. Figures 2(a) and 2(b) illustrate the projections of two stacking structures. Compared with SH stacking, Bernal stacking is more energetically favorable, as evidenced by DFT calculations. Moreover, the simulated XRD pattern of the Bernal stacking closely matches the experimental XRD results, further confirming its validity (Fig. S11 and Table S3 in the ESM). To investigate the stacking form of the Nb2CSe2 platelets further, they were immobilized with Ti3C2Tx* MXene sheets and then cross sectioned via FIB. By doing so, they can be viewed parallel to the platelets. Typical low-magnification TEM images of the sliced sample are shown in Fig. S10 in the ESM, revealing Nb2CSe2 nanoplates with a mean thickness of approximately 12 nm. Atomic-resolution STEM images (Figs. 2(g) and 2(i)) show that two Nb atomic layers construct the skeleton of the Nb2CSe2 monolayer and stack into nanoplates following a sequence of ABAB, where A and B stand for the adjacent two Nb2CSe2 monolayers. Figures 2(h) and 2(j) present simulated HAADF and ABF images of the Bernal stacking structure of Nb2CSe2 along the [110] zone axis, respectively. Atomic configurations are built on the HAADF and ABF observations. The simulated HAADF and ABF images agree with the experimental images, indicating that the stacking form of the Nb2CSe2 unit in the Nb2CSe2 platelets is of the Bernal type. Nb2CSe2 is hereafter referred to as Bernal stacking unless specified otherwise.

### 3.3 Electronic properties

Figure 3(a) shows the band structure of Nb2CSe2, which has a Fermi energy at the H/K points and points between the Γ and H/K points where the bands touch, resulting in a zero gap. The bands near the Fermi energy form a cylindrical Fermi surface, indicating better in-plane electronic conduction (Fig. 3(b)). In detail, the Nb 4d and Se 4p states make the main contributions, combined with the C 2p states around Fermi level (*E*F) from the projected density of states (PDOS), as shown in Fig. 3(c) and Fig. S12 in the ESM. To understand the bonding features in Nb2CSe2, COHP calculations were carried out. Figure 3(d) shows the COHP diagram and the corresponding integration values. The bonding strength of Nb–C (−3.11 eV) is greater than that of Nb–Se (−2.91 eV), especially Se–Se (−0.06 eV), which is consistent with the in-plane *σ* bonding states for Nb–C and Nb–Se, and the interactions between Se atoms show weaker van der Waals forces. The effective mass of carriers is a measure used to evaluate the mobility of carriers, which determines their applicability to electronic devices [46]. To gain insight into the transport properties of carriers, the effective masses of electrons (*m*e*) and holes (*m*h*) were evaluated along the parabolas at the specific points indicated in Figs. S13 and S14 in the ESM. *m*h* at the K-point in the M−K direction is comparable to the *m*e* at the Λ-point in the K−Г direction. This is also the case for *m*e* at the Λ-point in the K−Г direction and *m*h* at the K point in the K−Г direction (Table S4 in the ESM).

Experimentally, the electrical resistivity of Nb2CSe2 is linearly dependent on temperature in the range of ~50–300 K, which corresponds to the residual resistivity, indicating metal-like electron transport properties (Fig. 4(a)). This is in agreement with DFT results, i.e., Nb2CSe2 has a Fermi energy at H/K points and points between Γ and H/K points where the bands touch, resulting in a zero gap. As shown in Fig. 4(b), Hall voltage curve measurements were conducted at various temperatures, revealing a minimal slope on the *R*H−*B* curve for Nb2CSe2. This observation implies that both holes and electrons as charge carriers contribute comparably to Nb2CSe2. This assertion is consistent with the comparable values of *m*e* and *m*h* calculated from the band structure. Moreover, a survey of the electrical conductivities of the MXene materials indicates that Nb2CSe2 reaches a remarkable level of high conductivity, surpassing its counterparts including graphite [50], 1T-MoS2 [51], graphene [52], Ti3C2*Tx* [15], Mo2C*Tx* [53], Ti2C*Tx* [54], Nb4C3*Tx* [55], Nb2C*Tx* [56], V2C*Tx* [57], Mo2TiC2*Tx*, and Mo2Ti2C3*Tx* [15] (Fig. 4(c)). The high electrical conductivity of Nb2CSe2 far surpasses that of 1T−MoS2, indicating that the metal carbo-selenide is an electrically conductive lubricating solid.

### 3.4 Elastic properties

Following the above structural and electronic property exploration, the elastic properties are studied, as the elastic constants of crystals are crucial for comprehending their mechanical stability, bonding characteristics, and other physical properties [58,59]. To facilitate the study and application of materials subjected to external loads, it is necessary to study the degree of anisotropy of various properties determined by the elastic constants of solid materials.

In the trigonal (I) system, the elastic constants *C**ij* of materials must satisfy the Born stability criterion: *C*11 > |*C*12|, 2*C*213 33(*C*11 + *C*12), *C*44 > 0 and *C*66 > 0 [60]. The calculated elastic constants of Nb2CSe2, along with typical vdW solids such as graphite and MoS2, are consistent with the elastic moduli reported in previous studies [61,62]. This indicates that all of them satisfy the Born stability criterion (Table 3 and Table S5 in the ESM) [63−65]. With respect to the obtained elastic properties, their bulk modulus (*B*), Young’s modulus (*E*), and shear modulus (*G*) were compared in three-dimensional (3D) diagrams, as shown in Fig. 5. Both the bulk modulus and Young’s modulus show significant elastic anisotropy. The smallest bulk modulus is in the *c*-direction, whereas the smallest Young’s modulus is in the *c*-direction. The bulk modulus is a measure of the resistance of a material to hydrostatic deformation. In the 3D diagrams, blue color indicates more linear compressibility in that direction. The ellipsoid shape of the three materials investigated in this work indicates the anisotropy in the linear compression coefficients. The volume resistance to elastic deformation in the intralayers is significantly greater than that in the interlayers. This is in line with the fact that interlayer bonding is weaker than intralayer bonding, as evidenced by electronic structure calculations. Similarly, Young’s modulus and shear modulus also show strong anisotropy in Nb2CSe2. We know that graphite and MoS2 are well-recognized vdW solids with weak interlayer coupling. The elastic properties demonstrated here clearly show that the anisotropy in Nb2CSe2 closely resembles that in graphite and MoS2. This similarity is supported by the highly comparable values of the Poisson’s ratio, sound velocity, and Debye temperature.

### 3.5 Vibrational properties

The phonon dispersion of Nb2CSe2 was calculated along the direction that contains the highest number of high-symmetry points in the Brillouin zone, along with the corresponding one-PDOS. The primitive unit cell of Nb2CSe2 contains five atoms. Hence, 15 phonon branches produce a complicated dispersion. The 15 phonon branches are divided into three acoustic phonon branches (the three lower energy curves) and 12 optical phonon branches. ### 3.6 Friction properties

vdW solids usually have low friction coefficients because of their weak interlayer coupling [66]. To determine whether this is applicable to Nb2CSe2, we measured its friction coefficient against the K465 superalloy at the ambient. The friction coefficient against the K465 superalloy is as low as 0.14. Under identical evaluation conditions, the friction coefficients of MoS2 and graphite are approximately 0.17 and 0.21, respectively (Fig. 7(a) and Fig. S16 in the ESM). To understand the low friction coefficient of Nb2CSe2, the interlayer binding energies of graphite, MoS2, and Nb2CSe2 were calculated. As shown in Fig. 7(b), the binding energy of Nb2CSe2 is comparable to that of well-investigated vdW solids with weak interlayer bonding, e.g., graphite and MoS2. The weak interlayer bonding in Nb2CSe2 makes it a promising candidate for solid lubrication.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, layered metal carboselenide Nb2CSe2 stacked in Bernal mode has been identified as a vdW solid. This material is analogous to MoS2 in terms of elastic properties, interlayer coupling, and friction coefficients. Unlike semiconducting MoS2, Nb2CSe2 is a conductive material that achieves a remarkable level of high conductivity, surpassing other materials in the MXene family. The rich chemistry of the layered metal carbo-selenide Nb2CSe2 provides opportunities for scientists to study its potential in diverse fields. These include but are not limited to lubrication, energy storage materials, electromagnetic shielding, and catalysis.
