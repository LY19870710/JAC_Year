## 1 Introduction

Flash sintering (FS) is a novel ceramic sintering technology that significantly reduces the sintering temperature and shortens the sintering time by applying various types of electric fields during the sintering process. It also results in ceramics with smaller grain sizes, achieving superior electrical, thermal, and mechanical properties [1]. The furnace temperature required for flash sintering can be reduced by increasing the electric field strength [2]. Room-temperature flash sintering goes a step further by completely eliminating the need for external heating sources, thereby further reducing ceramic production costs [3].

In recent years, various ceramics have been reported to achieve room-temperature flash sintering. Liu *et al.* [4] achieved room-temperature flash sintering of dog bone-shaped ZnO ceramics (14.5 mm × 3.3 mm × 1.7 mm) with 99% density under an alternating electric field of 3530 V·cm−1. Nie *et al*. [5] used water vapor to increase the electrical conductivity of green bodies, achieving room-temperature sintering of ZnO discs (6.4 mm in diameter and 1 mm thick). Wu *et al.* [6] utilized arc-induced flash sintering technology to achieve room-temperature sintering of similarly sized dog bone-shaped ZnO varistor ceramics with a density of 99.1%. Li *et al.* [7], using arc-induced flash sintering technology, produced dense dog bone-shaped 3 mol% yttrium-doped zirconia (3YSZ) ceramics (14.5 mm × 3.3 mm × 1.7 mm) at room temperature. Liu *et al.* [8] successfully synthesized a five-component entropy-stabilized oxide ceramic (Mg0.2Ni0.2Co0.2Cu0.2Zn0.2O) via reaction-assisted flash sintering at room temperature.

However, there are still many challenges with current room-temperature flash sintering technology. First, the shape of the green ceramic body is limited. Most existing studies use dog bone-shaped [9−11] or strip-shaped [12] green bodies, which are conducive to the success of room temperature (RT) flash sintering experiments but lack practical application scenarios. Second, the successfully sintered ceramics are small in size, with the diameter of the disc-shaped samples typically not exceeding 20 mm [5,13,14]. Third, the power supply requirements are high, requiring either high-voltage or complex power supply designs. Furthermore, some studies impose additional requirements on the flash sintering atmosphere, further increasing production costs.

A well-designed flash sintering device can effectively reduce the sintering temperature and the required initial electric field strength, increasing the power delivered to the green body. This is critical for achieving room-temperature flash sintering of ceramics and addressing the aforementioned problems. In previous studies, flash sintering devices often required additional heating or pressurizing modules, or they implemented structures where wires were directly wrapped around the sample’s sides. These designs are poorly suited for automated production and are not cost-effective [15−17]. Xie *et al.* [18]*,* Dong *et al.* [19], and Mao *et al.* [20] proposed a novel plasma device using a pair of carbon fiber electrodes with an applied voltage to generate ultrahigh-temperature plasma under atmospheric pressure. The main principle is that the high-density short carbon fibers and the sharp tips of long carbon fibers broken by Joule heating jointly enhance the electric field, enabling the plasma to be initiated and stabilized at low voltage. This device is characterized by stability, uniformity, high temperature, and low required voltage and current conditions, making it highly suitable for room-temperature flash sintering of ceramics. However, employing high currents (30–90 mA·mm−2) to achieve rapid sintering under a nonequilibrium state at ultrahigh temperatures (8000 K) requires tremendous instantaneous power, making it unsuitable for preparing large samples. Wang *et al.* [21] sintered YSZ ceramics at room temperature via plasma assistance and a relatively small current, but the sample size was relatively small.

In this study, a novel flash sintering device based on a composite layered carbon electrode structure was constructed. Using this device, large-diameter ZnO ceramic discs and ZnO varistor ceramic discs, as well as 3YSZ ceramic discs, were successfully flash sintered at room temperature in an air atmosphere under a direct voltage of less than 100 V.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

The particle sizes and sources of the powders used are listed in Table A1 in the Appendix. The powders were mixed with a 5 wt% polyvinyl alcohol aqueous solution at a mass ratio of 10:1. A certain amount of powders was uniaxially pressed into green discs at 400 MPa. The sizes of various green bodies are shown in Table 1, with green body relative densities less than 68%. The ZnO varistor ceramic powders were formulated by mixing ZnO, Sb2O3, Bi2O3, and Co2O3 at a molar ratio of 90 : 5 : 2.5 : 2.5, whereas the ZnO ceramics and 3YSZ ceramics were prepared from the ZnO and 3YSZ powders, respectively. ### 2.2 Experimental description

The flash sintering device is shown in Fig. 1, and the electrode structure consisted of a composite laminated carbon electrode: from the outside to the inside, copper electrodes, graphite felt, graphite paper, and green bodies were arranged symmetrically up and down. The graphite paper is 0.1 mm thick, and the graphite felt is 8.0 mm thick with a porosity of approximately 90%. The graphite paper was precut to match the dimensions of the green bodies, whereas the graphite felt was cut slightly larger to fully cover the green body.

The flash sintering described in this study was conducted in an air atmosphere. The top and bottom copper electrodes were connected to a high-power direct current (DC) power supply. The initial output voltage was preset to 100 V. Once the power supply was turned on, the sample immediately exhibited intense flashes, marking the start of flash sintering. The power supply then switched from voltage control mode to current control mode. The current limit was gradually increased from 0 A at a rate of 1 A·s−1 until reaching 5 or 6 A, maintained for 180 s, and then decreased back to 0 A at the same rate, completing the flash sintering process.

### 2.3 Characterization

During the flash sintering experiments, the voltage and current data were recorded through the built-in measurement module of the power supply. An infrared thermal imager (FLIR A615, FLIR Systems Inc., USA) was used to record the temperature distribution at various positions in the device during the process. A camera was used to capture phenomena at different times during the process.

The thicknesses of the sintered ceramic discs were measured via a thickness gauge, and their densities were determined via the Archimedes water displacement method. The microstructures of the samples and their grain size distributions were analyzed via a cold/hot field scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU8010, Hitachi, Japan; Apreo 2S HiVac, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The elemental distribution in the samples was analyzed via an energy-dispersive spectroscope (EDS; XFlash 6|60, Bruker, USA). The Vickers hardness was measured via a Vickers hardness tester (XHVT-30Z, Shanghai Shangcai Testermachine Co., Ltd., China). The fracture toughness of the samples was also determined via the indentation method.

The *E*–*J* curves of the ZnO varistor ceramics were measured via a digital source meter, and the relevant electrical parameters of the samples were calculated: the voltage gradient (*E*1mA) corresponds to the electric field strength at a current density of 1 mA·cm−2; the nonlinearity coefficient *α*, defined as 1/log(*E*1mA/*E*0.1mA); and the leakage current *J*L, indicated by the current density at 0.75 *E*1mA.

### 3.1 ZnO ceramics

For the sintered sample of ZnO discs with a green body diameter of 40.0 mm and a thickness of 1.80 mm (hereinafter referred to as ZnO-d40 discs), upon powering the flash sintering device, intense light was emitted from one edge of the sample. The voltage quickly decreased from 100 to 50–60 V, and the current increased to the first-level limit of 1 A. Subsequently, the voltage decreased further, and at 6 s, the current reached its final stable value, with the voltage then decreasing to approximately 20 V. The initial intense glow lasted only a few seconds before fading rapidly, leaving only the main body of the sample to exhibit high temperature and dimmer red light.

The typical voltage and current variations during the flash sintering period are shown in Fig. 2. An analysis of the voltage changes between 6 and 175 s (the period during which the current remained at the experimental setting of 6 A) revealed that the voltage changes represented the power delivered to the sample. In Fig. 2, between 6 and 100 s period, both the voltage and power fell, initially at a fast rate and later at a slower rate. Between 100 and 175 s, the voltage and power stabilized, with the voltage remaining near 12 V.

Fig. 2Voltage and current of ZnO-d40 samples as a function of time during flash sintering process.

Images captured at different moments during the sintering process are shown in Fig. 3, and the typical temperature distribution of the device over time is shown in Fig. 4. Analysis of these three sets of data revealed three distinct stages in the sintering process. (1) Initial stage: Within seconds of powering the device, surface discharge and breakdown occurred at one edge of the sample. The longitudinal temperature distribution revealed extreme heat in the green body, graphite paper, and part of the graphite felt. This heat generated high-temperature plasma, resulting in intense light emission. In this process, the sudden voltage drop was due to the rapid increase in the sample conductivity, indicating that flash sintering had been initiated [21,22]. During this stage, the sample received relatively high power, resulting in a rapid temperature increase in the edge region. (2) Transition stage: The initial intense glow rapidly decreased, with red-hot light confined to the initial discharge zone. Over the 6–100 s period, the red-hot region expanded laterally across the sample, whereas in the longitudinal direction, the temperatures decreased further from the central region of the green body. The expansion of the red-hot region was attributed to the fact that the low-temperature region was heated both by the Joule heating power from the electrical current and by heat transfer from the already red-hot region. This expansion reduced the sample’s overall electrical resistance, leading to a voltage drop under constant current. Early in this phase, the expansion rate was faster, leading to a more rapid voltage drop. (3) Final stage: By 100 s, the red-hot region had fully engulfed the sample. During the subsequent 100–175 s period, all parts of the sample received a stable and uniform power input, resulting in a further temperature increase and increased densification of the sample.

For the other ceramic discs in this study, the sintering process can also be divided into the three stages described above, with only slight differences in the duration and phenomena of each stage, which will be detailed later.

The voltage and current curves over time during the flash sintering process of the ZnO varistor ceramic discs (hereinafter referred to as ZnO-V discs) are shown in Fig. 5, while the photos and temperature distributions are presented in Figs. 6 and 7. Unlike that of the pure ZnO discs, the surface discharge duration was approximately 5 s for pure ZnO but extended to approximately 20 s for the ZnO varistors. This is because the presence of doped Sb, Bi, and Co phases increases the number of grain boundaries and defects in ZnO varistor ceramics, hindering heat conduction, and thus resulting in a slower increase in the conductivity of the green body. Furthermore, the slower expansion of the red-hot region meant that it took approximately 150 s to completely cover the sample, with the expansion phase lasting approximately 130 s. For a pure ZnO sample of the same size, this process takes 95 s. This is also due to the impeded heat transfer caused by the doped elements, resulting in a slower expansion of the red-hot region.

### 3.2 3YSZ ceramics

The variations in voltage and current over time during the flash sintering of 3YSZ discs are shown in Fig. 14. Photos and temperature distributions during the process are displayed in Figs. 15 and 16. Compared with the red-hot phenomenon that occurs during flash sintering of ZnO ceramic discs, the luminous and thermal intensities of 3YSZ discs are greater following surface discharge. Infrared thermography indicated that their maximum temperature exceeded 1500 °C. This is attributed to the markedly lower thermal conductivity of the 3YSZ ceramics than that of the ZnO ceramics, which restricts heat dissipation. Between 50 and 175 s, the light and heat region continuously covered the entire disc, with the central zone's intensity increasing as sintering proceeded. This is because heat in the central zone, compared with the edges, accumulates, as it is less likely to dissipate. During the initial phase, the central zone's stronger light and heat intensity resulted in more heat retention than dissipation, increasing the temperature and intensity further. On the other hand, the edge region consistently dissipated heat to the environment while receiving Joule heating from electric current and heat transfer from the central zone, maintaining stable temperature and intensity levels overall.

## 4 Discussion

The main mechanism for preparing ceramics through flash sintering in this study is as follows: In the early stage, the plasma preheats the sample and may also cause the formation of numerous crystal defects. Once the sample temperature reached the critical point, flash sintering was triggered. In the later stage, it is further completed through the sustained action of Joule heating from the electric current. In addition, the nucleation of Frenkel pairs and dielectric breakdown are also possible densification mechanisms [17,21,25−27].

Compared with the traditional electrode-green body-electrode structure, the novel carbon electrode structure used in this work played an essential role in the flash sintering process by incorporating graphite felt and graphite paper. These materials perform the following functions:

(1) The graphite felt and graphite paper effectively limit heat loss, facilitating the flash sintering process.

(2) The fiber structure of graphite felt, with varying surface lengths, can generate high-temperature plasma and trigger microscale electrical arc discharges across various parts of the sample. Microscale electrical arcs can generate localized high temperatures and strong electric fields within a sample. The elevated temperature facilitates neck growth between particles and local melting during the initial stage of sintering and promotes the expansion of the red-hot region. In addition, arcs increase the number of defects on the green body, such as oxygen vacancies, which enhance ion mobility and the rate of mass diffusion. Through these effects, the flash sintering process is effectively promoted.

(3) As flexible electrodes, graphite felt and graphite paper ensures effective and sufficient contact with the sample without causing it to fracture, thereby reducing the unevenness of the current distribution and sintering.

The flash sintering device used in this work was inspired by the ultrafast high-temperature sintering (UHS) process, which employs a carbon electrode structure to improve the heating efficiency and uniformity during flash sintering. However, the current still passes through the sample during flash sintering and generates Joule heat within the green body, whereas in the UHS process, the current does not flow through the sample. This represents a fundamental difference between the two methods [28].

High voltage and large current, associated with high costs and stringent equipment requirements, are major obstacles limiting the industrial-scale application of flash sintering for ceramics. During the stable stage of flash sintering, the current density for the ZnO-d40 disc was 4.8 mA·mm−2, and the electric field strength was 85.6 V·cm−1, both of which are comparable to those recorded for the ZnO varistor disc and the 3YSZ disc. Compared with previous flash sintering studies, the carbon-based electrode structure in this work significantly reduced the required current density and electric field strength [29]. Hence, the flash sintering technique described here holds significant promise for industrial applications.

## 5 Conclusions

In this study, by applying a carbon electrode structure to the flash sinter, room-temperature flash sintering in the air was achieved for large-sized ZnO ceramic discs and ZnO varistor ceramic discs, as well as for 3YSZ discs. The relative densities of the flash-sintered samples reached 96.02%, 99.27%, and 98.48%, respectively, all of which exhibited excellent electrical and mechanical properties. During flash sintering, the carbon electrode structure can generate high-temperature plasma at relatively low voltages, causing microarcing discharges across the sample. This creates localized regions of high temperature and strong electric fields and leads to an increase in green body defects. Additionally, the carbon electrode structure restricts heat loss during the flash sintering process and ensures good electrical contact between the green body and the electrodes. With these effects, the previously reported issue of inhomogeneous current distribution within the green body during flash sintering is optimized, facilitating the full densification of large samples. Compared with existing flash sintering processes, the method proposed in this work has broader applicability and provides possibilities for the large-scale industrial production of ceramics via flash sintering technology.
