## 1 Introduction

Sapphire, a single-crystal form of Al2O3 [1−3], is recognized for its excellent optical transmission, mechanical properties, high-temperature resistance, and wear resistance. It is often connected with metal substrates such as stainless steel, copper alloys, and titanium alloys [4,5], which facilitates its extensive use as an optical window across various sectors, including aerospace, electronics, new energy, and military applications [6−8]. However, the ongoing technology advancements necessitate alternatives to traditional metal substrates, as they are no longer sufficient to satisfy the requirements of optical windows in extreme environments such as high temperatures, corrosion conditions, and radiation exposure [9,10]. Yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) ceramics exhibit remarkable properties, such as superior oxidation resistance, high-temperature stability, corrosion resistance, and low thermal conductivity [11,12]. These characteristics render them suitable for use as alternatives or coatings for metallic substrates. Therefore, the integration of Yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) ceramics with sapphire has the potential to expand the range of applications for optical windows [13].

Owing to the brittleness of ceramics, the thermal expansion difference between different ceramics is an important factor limiting the success of welding. Traditional welding methods, such as brazing and diffusion bonding, require the application of high temperature and pressure across the entire ceramic substrate during the welding process [14,15]. This approach results in a substantial heat-affected zone, which increases the likelihood of joint fracture. In contrast to traditional welding techniques, femtosecond laser pulses, characterized by their exceptionally high peak power, can facilitate nonlinear absorption melting and welding of materials. The ability to significantly reduce the thermal stress at joints, increase the joint strength, and achieve welding beyond the capabilities of traditional welding methods renders them promising solutions for addressing the challenges associated with welding dissimilar ceramics [16,17]. Compared with continuous lasers and long pulse lasers, femtosecond laser pulses present several advantages [16,18,19]: (1) They generate a highly localized interaction area around the focal point, ensuring precision and selectivity; (2) the nonlinear absorption effect allows for significantly reduced interaction time between the femtosecond laser and the material, which minimizes the heat-affected zone and thermal stress during the welding process; (3) they enable room-temperature welding, eliminating the necessity for comprehensive heating and cooling.

The first application of femtosecond laser welding for transparent materials was to weld two SiO2 glass components [20]. This technique has subsequently demonstrated efficacy in welding dissimilar transparent materials, including quartz glass and borosilicate glass [21]. Notably, the process of femtosecond laser pulse welding exhibits minimal sensitivity to a specific type of ceramics, facilitating the successful welding of various ceramics, such as polycrystalline alumina and YSZ, in subsequent applications [16,22,23]. However, two primary challenges arise when utilizing femtosecond laser pulses for the direct welding of ceramics: (1) To effectively prevent surface ablation and ensure adequate welding strength, femtosecond laser welding necessitates stringent conditions regarding the surface of samples. Specifically, most laser welding research has been carried out under optical contact conditions, which require that the surface gap must be less than 1/4 of the laser wavelength [24,25]. For the dissimilar ceramic laser welding of YSZ and sapphire in this study, optical contact is a necessary prerequisite. This requirement often requires mechanical restraint of the materials before welding, thereby increasing the welding stress. (2) Furthermore, rigorous pre-welding conditions often result in joint strengths that are both unstable and relatively low. Therefore, it is essential to enhance the existing direct welding methods to overcome the challenges associated with the application of femtosecond laser welding in ceramics.

The advantageous plasticity and deformation properties of metals, along with their capacity to flow and occupy interfacial voids upon melting, facilitate a reduction in the pre-processing requirements before welding. Furthermore, the energy threshold necessary for melting metals is lower than that for ceramics, allowing the welding process to be executed at a diminished energy density. This reduction in energy input contributes to minimizing residual stress and enhancing joint strength [26,27]. Ti is frequently employed as an active element in brazing because of its superior weldability, as it can establish robust chemical bonds with ceramics, thereby improving the welding strength [4,28]. Consequently, we propose the deposition of a Ti layer onto a ceramic surface, followed by femtosecond laser welding, as a viable alternative to direct welding.

In this study, we used nanoscale Ti as an interlayer and applied femtosecond laser pulse welding technology to successfully establish high-strength and reliable welding between YSZ and sapphire. This is the first time that the metal interlayer method has been successfully applied to femtosecond laser welding of dissimilar ceramics, and very interesting new interfacial morphology with a honeycomb transition structure has been found in welded joints. We studied the phase composition of the YSZ/sapphire joint and revealed the formation mechanism of the joint from both energetic and crystallographic perspectives. We found that the presence of Ti metal layer optimized the temperature distribution after the interface absorbed laser energy, increased the interface fluidity during the micro-welding process, and thus promoted the formation of transitional honeycomb structure. Additionally, we explored the impact of different laser power levels on the performance and strength of the joint. We believe that the observed honeycomb structure enhances the interfacial transition between the two types of ceramics. These findings provide valuable insights into the effective and high-quality connection of dissimilar ceramics.

### 2.1 Material processing

The YSZ ceramics and sapphires used in this study were provided by Hefei Yuanjing Crystal Co., Ltd. The YSZ ceramics are ZrO2 ceramics doped with 5 wt% Y2O3 as a stabilizer, exhibiting a tetragonal polycrystalline structure. Sapphire is a single crystal of α-Al2O3. The sapphire welding surface selected in this study is oriented in the (0001) direction. Before welding, the YSZ and sapphire ceramics were cut into blocks of 8 mm × 8 mm × 2 mm and 6 mm × 6 mm × 2 mm, respectively, and the surfaces to be welded were polished to achieve a surface roughness of *R*a a Fig. 1. The stacked samples were positioned at the center of the fixture and secured using mechanical constraints. A miniature pressure sensor (SBY761D, SIMBATOUCH) was placed under the samples to regulate the pressure on the samples through a digital display. In this study, the interface pressure between YSZ and sapphire was maintained at approximately 0.2 MPa.

### 2.2 Femtosecond pulse laser welding processing

In this study, a CARBIDE (CB3-40W) femtosecond laser, produced by Light Conversion, was used to perform femtosecond laser welding of YSZ and sapphire. The equipment comprises a femtosecond laser, an optical path system, a galvanometer mirror system, a motion workbench, a software operation control system, etc. The numerical aperture of the galvanometer mirror system is approximately 0.027, and the beam waist diameter is approximately 23 μm. The welding experimental process is shown in Fig. 2. Before welding, the sample was installed on the *xyz* displacement platform. The *z*-axis was adjusted to ensure that the laser focal plane coincided with the welding surface, whereas the *x-* and *y*-axes were aligned to match the laser scanning path with the welding surface. Based on some existing research [22] and in combination with our previous research [19,29], the laser welding path is set as a 5 mm × 5 mm grid matrix, composed of two sets of parallel lines with a spacing of 80 μm. The scanning process involves completing the scanning path in the *x* direction first, followed by the *y* direction. The scanning directions of the adjacent two lines were opposite to each other. In addition to configuring the scanning path, other laser parameters, such as pulse width and scanning speed, were also set. The specific parameters for the femtosecond laser welding process employed in this study are detailed in Table 1.

### 2.3 Characterizations and analysis of joint microstructure and mechanical properties

The joint morphology was initially observed by a confocal laser scanning microscope (OLS4100, Olympus). Then the microstructure and element distribution characteristics of the joint and fracture were analyzed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (SU5000, Hitachi) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The samples were prepared by a focused ion beam (FIB, Helios G4 PFIB), and the properties and distribution of the joints were thoroughly investigated with a transmission electron microscope (TEM, FEI Talos F200X) and transmission Kikuchi diffraction (TKD, Helios G4 PFIB). The shear test was carried out with an electronic universal testing machine (AgXplus 20 kN, Shimadzu) at room temperature, with the loading speed set at 0.5 mm/min. A minimum of 3 samples were tested for each welding parameter. The shear strength was calculated by dividing the maximum force recorded during the shear test by the area of the laser scanning area.

### 3.1 Typical microstructure of YSZ/sapphire joint

Figures 3(a) and 3(b) show the macroscopic optical morphology of the YSZ/sapphire joint welded by a femtosecond laser with the Ti layer at 8 W power, a repetition frequency of 200 kHz, a pulse width of 800 fs, and a scanning speed of 50 mm/s. The joint appears black in the area affected by the laser, while the unaffected area retains a white color, which is characteristic of the Ti layer. YSZ and sapphire form a joint structure with a regular distribution under the action of the femtosecond laser, without any macroscopic cracking or deformation. The optical interface morphology provides preliminary evidence for the feasibility of femtosecond laser welding of YSZ and sapphire with the assistance of a Ti layer. The typical microstructure of the YSZ/sapphire joint is shown in Figs. 3(c)–3(f). Figure 3(c) demonstrates a continuous, regularly distributed, and defect-free joint structure. Compared with the unaffected region, the laser-affected region is wider, with the two regions alternating to form the welded structure of the YSZ/sapphire joint. The femtosecond laser operating region (zone A in Fig. 3(c)), as shown in Fig. 3(d), is ~50 μm long and ~10 μm wide. It can be divided into two sections according to their distinctive characteristics: The zone I features a continuous and dense structure with a small amount of dark phase, while zone II exhibits a honeycomb structure formed by interconnected polygons. ### 3.2 Characterizations of typical interfacial products

The crystal spacing measured by HRTEM is 3.12 Å, which aligns with the (011) plane of ZrO2, indicating that the phase is YSZ substrate.

The measured crystal plane spacing of 1.81 Å corresponds to the (11 5¯) plane of Al2O3, and the SAED results from FFT match the (00 4¯), (11 1¯), and (11 5¯) crystal orientations of Al2O3. These findings indicate that the continuous structure in the joint comprises a matrix phase that is gray‒white intermixed, with a small amount of a dark phase embedded within it. The dark phase consists of Tix*O*y*, formed by residual Ti after femtosecond laser treatment. The gray‒white intermixed matrix phase is binary Al2O3–ZrO2 eutectic ceramic composed of white ZrO2 and gray Al2O3. Moreover, the crystallinity of this phase is not obvious, with the crystal sizes of Al2O3 and ZrO2 being very small and their boundaries being indistinct. Therefore, we believe that in the gray‒white intermixed matrix phase, there are still some amorphous ceramic phases that have not crystallized because of the extremely fast cooling rate. Both the ZrO2 and Al2O3 phases contain a certain amount of Ti, which is due to the dispersion of Ti elements into the joint during femtosecond laser treatment. This process participates in the crystallization process and dissolves into the oxides of Zr and Al, forming eutectic ceramics. Owing to the better mutual solubility between Ti and Zr, the Ti content in ZrO2 (4.67 at%) is higher than that in Al2O3 (2.47 at%) [30,31].

### 3.3 Effects of femtosecond laser power on the interface and strength of joint

The power of femtosecond laser pulses significantly influences the micro-welding effect, as variations in the laser power directly affect the microstructure and distribution characteristics. Figure 10 shows the microstructure of the YSZ/sapphire interface under different laser power levels, with a scanning speed of 50 mm/s, pulse frequency of 200 kHz, pulse width of 800 fs, and scanning spacing of 80 μm. The lengths and widths of both the micro-welding region and the unaffected region in the joint clearly vary at different power levels. When the laser power is set to 6 W, the length of the unaffected region is considerably greater than that of the micro-welding region, and the width of the micro-welding region is narrower. This observation suggests that at lower power levels, the reaction and formation processes at the interface are insufficient. The continuous structure within the joint is narrow, and only a limited amount of honeycomb structure can be observed. These findings indicate that both the Al2O3–ZrO2 eutectic ceramic and ZrO2 that participates in sapphire recrystallization are present in minimal quantities within the joint. When the laser power increases to 8 W, both the length and width of the micro-welding region significantly expand, while the unaffected region remains only approximately 20 μm in length. In the enlarged microstructure of the joint (Fig. 10(b2)), continuous structures and honeycomb patterns are clearly visible, with the honeycomb structure exhibiting a relatively regular shape. As the laser power reaches 10 W, the unaffected region in the joint disappears, indicating that during the micro-welding process, certain areas experience overlapping laser effects. The width of the micro-welding region further increases compared with that at 8 W, with both continuous and honeycomb structures displaying greater widths. Additionally, at higher energy levels, the morphology of the honeycomb structure transitions from polygonal to more elongated shapes. This change is inferred to result from the increased initial velocity of the molten liquid entering sapphire at higher energy levels, causing the honeycomb structure formed by recrystallization to become more elongated and less regular in shape.

Shear strength tests were conducted on the YSZ/sapphire joint to characterize its mechanical properties. Figure 11 shows the shear strength of the joints at different laser power levels, with the average value for each power setting calculated after conducting at least three separate tests. Overall, the application of femtosecond laser micro-welding, combined with the addition of a Ti layer results in a significant increase in the shear strength of the YSZ/sapphire joint compared with the direct welding method using a nanosecond laser in our previous experiments [29]. This value is significantly greater than those observed at both 6 and 10 W. In our previous nanosecond laser direct welding experiment, the optimal shear strength of the joint was 32 MPa. In this work, the optimal shear strength of femtosecond laser micro-welding with Ti layer is increased by approximately 147% compared with that of nanosecond laser direct welding. These findings preliminarily indicate that the change in the welding method and the addition of a Ti metal layer play an active role in improving the welding effect of the joint.

From the above analysis of shear strength and fracture morphology of the YSZ/sapphire joint, it is evident that excessively high or low laser power is clearly detrimental to the formation of a joint with a regular shape and high shear strength. When the laser power is low, the continuous structure and honeycomb structure are narrow, and the fracture surface is relatively flat, indicating that the welding effect of the joint is weak and the joint strength is the lowest at this time. ### 3.4 Pulse–material interaction

Based on the research findings presented above, we have clearly identified the composition of the YSZ/sapphire joint, including the specific compositions of the continuous and honeycomb structures. To elucidate the formation process and mechanism of the interface, we further analyze it from the perspectives of energy transfer and crystallography.

The interaction process between femtosecond lasers and materials differs from that of other pulse lasers because of the ultra-short duration of femtosecond laser pulses, which leads to a nonlinear and non-equilibrium process. While femtosecond laser pulses operate on a femtosecond timescale, the picosecond timescale of lattice relaxation means that only electron–electron heat transfer occurs during the action of the femtosecond laser pulse. As time progresses to the picosecond scale, electrons transfer the absorbed photon energy to the lattice. Therefore, the traditional Fourier heat transfer equation is not suitable for analyzing the interaction between femtosecond laser pulses and materials. It is necessary to establish distinct differential equations (Eqs. (1) and (2)) for the temperature variations in electrons and lattices, commonly referred to as the dual-temperature model [32]:

(1) Ce∂Te∂t=∇(ke⋅∇Te)−G(Te−Tl)+S

(2) Cl∂Tl∂t=∇(kl⋅∇Tl)−G(Te−Tl)

### 3.5 Formation mechanism of honeycomb structure

According to previous studies, a honeycomb structure forms within the sapphire substrate, and TEM analysis confirms that the main phase of this honeycomb structure is ZrO2, which is produced by the recrystallization of Zr4+ and O2− ions at the interface. As shown in Figs. 3(e) and 3(f), the joint structure gradually transitions from a continuous form to a honeycomb configuration. By combining the TEM results from Fig. 7 and the TKD images in Fig. 9, we observe that the crystal orientation of the sapphire substrate surrounding the honeycomb remains consistent, indicating that the sapphire around the honeycomb did not melt. Therefore, the transition of the interface structure from a continuous form to a honeycomb structure suggests that the sapphire at the interface shifts from a fully melted state to a partially melted state. This phenomenon occurs because as the distance from the center of the interface increases, the amount of heat received by the sapphire substrate gradually decreases, becoming insufficient to induce complete melting of the sapphire. Therefore, we hypothesize that under these conditions, the molten liquid exhibits a certain degree of directional selectivity as it penetrates the sapphire substrate.

The surface energy of a crystal reflects the ease with which atoms can escape from the solid surface. Therefore, surface energy directly influences the melting behavior of a crystal [33]. In general, directions with lower surface energy experience easier atomic detachment, meaning that these directions melt more readily. As a result, crystals tend to begin melting from crystal planes with lower surface energies. Thus, we believe that the formation of the honeycomb structure occurs because the molten liquid penetrates into the sapphire more readily in directions with lower surface energy. To verify this hypothesis, we conducted further analysis of the honeycomb structure and the surrounding sapphire, as shown in Fig. 15. The liquid phase of the honeycomb structure, while infiltrating the sapphire, primarily follows the three directions indicated in Fig. 15(a). These directions correspond to the three crystal planes (* 2¯10), ( 1¯20), and ( 1¯ 1¯0) of the surrounding sapphire matrix, as illustrated in Figs. 15(b1) and 15(b2). These three planes represent the main crystal planes of sapphire within the current plane, with interplanar angles of approximately 60° between them, as depicted in Figs. 15(c) and 15(d).

### 3.6 Formation process and mechanism of YSZ/sapphire joint

Based on the energy analysis of femtosecond laser pulse interactions with materials and the crystallographic characteristics of different crystal orientations of sapphire, we derived the formation process and mechanism of the joint.

#### 3.6.1 Melting of interface under laser pulse interaction

As shown in Figs. 16(a) and 16(b), under femtosecond laser pulses, the Ti layer at the interface first undergoes nonlinear absorption of photon energy, followed by the thermal processes described in Section 3.4. As the absorbed energy is transferred to the lattice through electron‒phonon coupling, the Ti layer melts first. Subsequently, through thermal diffusion, this melting induces partial melting of the sapphire interface in contact with it, resulting in the formation of a small “micro-melt pool” at the interface.

#### 3.6.2 Expansion and mixing of the interfacial melted region

As shown in Fig. 16(c), the thinness of the Ti layer at the interface allows the melted region to continue expanding as energy diffuses. Both the sapphire and YSZ in contact with the Ti layer undergo heating and melting. Once the base materials have melted, a large number of Al3+, Zr4+, and O2− ions from the substrates mix with the molten Ti ions at the interface. Due to the high temperature, the various ions in the molten state are thoroughly mixed within the melt pool. During this process, most Ti elements disperse throughout the molten region, but because the melting process is maintained for only a short duration, a small number of Ti-enriched regions remain. These regions subsequently form Ti-rich phases during the crystallization process.

#### 3.6.3 Eutectic phase precipitation and formation of honeycomb structure

The heat-affected zone of femtosecond laser welding is extremely small, and the energy gradient is highly distinct. As shown in Figs. 16(d) and 16(e), when heat diffuses from the melted region into the sapphire substrate, the sapphire close to the interface melts directly and enters the molten region, while the sapphire farther from the interface receives insufficient thermal diffusion energy to undergo complete melting. According to the calculations presented in Section 3.5, the varying surface energies of sapphire along different crystal orientations cause the molten liquid to exhibit a specific directionality as it penetrates the sapphire. Specifically, it preferentially moves in directions with lower surface energy. This directionality ultimately results in the formation of a honeycomb structure within the sapphire. In summary, the formation of a honeycomb structure is mainly determined by three factors: (1) Ti metal has a lower melting point and specific heat than ceramics. It is the first material to absorb laser energy and melt, preventing the ceramic substrate from cracking due to an excessive energy gradient. After absorbing femtosecond laser energy, molten Ti increases the fluidity of the micro-melt pool at the welding interface, expands the heat-affected zone, participates in the interfacial reaction, and lays the foundation for the formation of a honeycomb structure. (2) The temperature gradient distribution within the welding interface causes the sapphire to transition from complete melting to incomplete melting. (3) The crystallographic characteristics of sapphire itself make the high-temperature liquid phase from the welding interface more likely to penetrate into sapphire along the crystal direction with lower surface energy.

At the same time, as the temperature decreases, compounds formed by ions in this molten region begin to precipitate. Since the majority of the atoms in the molten region originate from the two base materials, with Ti atoms being in the minority, the precipitated compounds primarily consist of Al2O3–ZrO2 eutectic ceramics and some amorphous ceramics. The Ti atoms are dispersed and dissolved within the Al2O3–ZrO2 eutectic phase, while a small portion of undispersed Ti atoms form Ti-rich phases, which include various Ti-containing compounds. Thus, through the development of the Al2O3–ZrO2 eutectic ceramic phase, the precipitation of Ti-rich phases, and the formation of the honeycomb structure in sapphire, the final YSZ‒sapphire joint is established.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, micro-welding of YSZ to sapphire was successfully achieved using femtosecond laser pulses in conjunction with a Ti metal layer. Femtosecond laser pulses induce a honeycomb structure at the interface, which helps to improve the interface transition. By examining the microstructure, mechanical properties, and forming mechanisms of the joint, the relevant conclusions are as follows:

(1) The YSZ/sapphire joint features a continuous structure alongside a honeycomb structure, with a well-formed micro-welding interface that is free of defects such as cracks and holes. YSZ and sapphire can be successfully micro-welded using a femtosecond laser pulse combined with a Ti layer. The continuous structure within the joint is composed of Al2O3–ZrO2 eutectic ceramic and a Ti-rich phase, which consists of various Ti compounds, Ti*x*O*y*. The honeycomb structure is formed through the recrystallization of ZrO2 after Zr4+ and O2−- from the molten liquid penetrate the sapphire substrate. Additionally, a small amount of AlO3 transitional phase exists in the transition region between the honeycomb structure and the sapphire.

(2) The power of the femtosecond laser pulse significantly affects the quality of micro-welding and the shear strength of the joint. Both excessively high and low power levels can hinder the formation of a joint with well-defined characteristics and optimal shear strength. By adjusting the laser power to 8 W, a micro-welded region with a uniform distribution and a well-defined cellular structure can be achieved in the YSZ/sapphire joint. At this power setting, the maximum shear strength of the joint reaches 79 MPa, with the fracture surface exhibiting brittle failure accompanied by a few step-like features.

(3) The formation of the honeycomb structure is closely related to the energy distribution at the interface and the surface energy of sapphire crystal planes. Initially, the energy from the laser pulse induces nonlinear absorption and melting of the Ti metal, followed by thermal diffusion, which transfers energy to adjacent substrates. Calculations of the surface energy for different sapphire crystal planes reveal that sapphire tends to melt more readily along directions with lower surface energy. Consequently, the direction in which the molten liquid phase penetrates the sapphire is influenced by the surface energy of different crystal planes, ultimately leading to the formation of a polygonal honeycomb structure within the sapphire. This honeycomb structure, induced by the femtosecond laser pulse, facilitates a smoother transition between YSZ and sapphire, thereby aiding in the formation of the YSZ/sapphire joint.
