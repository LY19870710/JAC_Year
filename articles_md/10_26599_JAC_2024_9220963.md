## 1 Introduction

The fossil energy crisis and environmental pollution have led to an increased focus on the development and utilization of green and clean energy sources [1−4]. In this context, the development of efficient and cost-effective energy storage and conversion systems has become crucial [5,6]. Rechargeable lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have been widely utilized because of their high energy density, long cycle life, and environmental friendliness [7−12]. Despite the significant advancements in LIBs, the widely used commercial graphite anode experiences significant capacity decay during rapid charging and discharging, is prone to the growth of lithium dendrites, and lacks safety measures [13,14]. Therefore, developing anode materials with excellent fast-charging performance and high specific capacity is highly important [15−18].

Transition metal oxides (TMOs), such as Fe*x*O*y* [19,20], Co*x*O*y* [21−23], and Mn*x*O*y* [24,25], have become promising LIB anode materials because of their multiple chemical valence states, better safety, and higher theoretical capacity [26−28]. However, TMOs also encounter challenges such as significant volume changes during cycling and low electronic conductivity, which results in poor cycle performance and rate performance, thereby limiting their commercial application. To address these issues, researchers typically employ two strategies. The first strategy involves the rational design and control of the size and morphology of TMOs, including the use of nano- or porous structures [29]. The second strategy is to combine TMOs with other materials that possess superior performance to compensate for their own defects or to create a synergistic effect. For example, TMOs can be combined with carbon materials that exhibit good conductivity to improve the rate performance [30,31]. Alternatively, TMOs can be combined with other metal materials, combining the advantages of each component to improve their electrochemical performance through synergistic effects [32].

Among the various TMOs, manganese oxide has received significant attention because of its high theoretical capacity (> 700 mAh·g−1), low thermodynamic equilibrium potential (approximately 1.0 V vs. Li+/Li), and abundant reserves [33]. The valence electron configuration of manganese is 3d54s2. It has multiple valences and can form binary manganese oxides with different valence states, including MnO [34,35], MnO2 [36−38], Mn3O4 [39,40], and Mn5O8 [41,42].

Various manganese oxides, such as MnO and MnO2, have been used as anode materials for LIBs. MnO2, with a high manganese valence of +4, has a remarkably high specific capacity. However, it also undergoes significant volume expansion during cycling, leading to poor cycle performance. To address this issue, Mao *et al*. [43] employed carbon nanotubes as inner coating materials to synthesize MnO2@CNT secondary microspheres, aiming to increase the cycle stability of MnO2. Although this approach improved the cycle performance, it resulted in poor fast-charging performance under high-current conditions. In contrast, MnO, with a manganese valence of +2, possesses a lower specific capacity than MnO2 but has better cycling performance. To increase the conductivity and electrochemical performance of MnO, Wang *et al*. [44] prepared MnO/C solid microspheres by incorporating carbon materials. This modification significantly improved the cycle performance of LIBs. However, the preparation process of this material is relatively complex, and the raw material, polyaniline, is not only expensive but also highly toxic. Therefore, it is not suitable for commercial LIBs as an anode material.

Recently, an oxide with the chemical formula Mn5O8 was discovered. Metastable Mn5O8 is the only bivalent manganese oxide with a layered structure and can be represented as [Mn22+Mn34+O8]. In this state, both Mn2+ and Mn4+ ions are stable. The Mn4+ sites are not fully occupied, and the Mn2+ ion sites are distributed above and below the vacant sites [21]. These abundant interlayer and intralayer defects can also provide favorable conditions for accelerating ion transport. On the basis of the above analysis, it is speculated that Mn5O8 may have a relatively high specific capacity and improved cycling performance.

However, there are few reports on the application of Mn5O8 in batteries. Sun *et al*. [45] used hydrothermal and annealing methods to deposit Mn5O8 nanoparticles on the surface of graphene to form a layered structure and applied it to the cathode of aqueous zinc-ion batteries, but the Coulombic efficiency of the battery was low. In 2020, Zlatanova *et al*. [42] reported on layered Mn5O8 as a structural matrix for Li+ intercalation. This study decomposed the newly precipitated γ-MnOOH in air at 500 °C to produce Mn5O8, investigated the intercalation performance of Mn5O8 in nonaqueous electrolytes, analyzed its electrochemical reaction mechanism, and explored its lithium storage capacity. However, the characterization and testing methods used in these studies were not comprehensive. Our study aims to further explore the application of Mn5O8 in batteries. A simple hydrothermal and annealing method was used to synthesize a porous hexagonal nanosheet structure of Mn5O8 with a new morphology through careful design and morphology control, which was subsequently applied to LIB anode materials. The rich valence of manganese in Mn5O8 contributes to its higher specific capacity and improved cycle performance. Additionally, Mn5O8 nanosheets have abundant pores, which facilitate the penetration of the electrolyte and increase the rate of ion transport. As a result, Mn5O8 anode material demonstrates excellent electrochemical performance. Even after 100 cycles at a current density of 100 mA·g−1, a specific capacity of 938.7 mAh·g−1 is still retained. Furthermore, Mn5O8 also exhibits excellent fast charging performance, with a capacity of 675.7 mAh·g−1 after 1000 cycles at 3000 mA·g−1. These excellent electrochemical properties make Mn5O8 a highly promising fast-charging anode for LIBs.

### 2.1 Synthesis of Mn<sub>5</sub>O<sub>8</sub>

Two aqueous solutions were prepared, specifically a 0.2 M Mn(Ac)2 solution and a 2 M NaOH solution. NaOH solution was added dropwise to Mn(Ac)2 solution at a slow rate, and the resulting mixture was stirred for 1 h. Next, 2 mmol of ascorbic acid was added. The resulting mixed solution was then transferred into a reaction vessel made of polytetrafluoroethylene and subjected to a hydrothermal reaction at 180 °C for 21 h. Once the hydrothermal reaction was complete, vacuum filtration was performed, followed by three washes with a solution of distilled water and ethanol. The obtained material was then placed in a vacuum oven (DZG-6050, SenXin, China) and dried for 12 h. The dried precursor was subsequently annealed at 500 °C for 3 h in a muffle furnace (SGM M6/10, Sigma, China), resulting in the formation of Mn5O8.

### 2.2 Characterization

The structure of Mn5O8 was characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; AXS D8 Advance, Bruker, USA). The elemental and valence analyses of Mn5O8 were performed via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCA Lab 250Xi, Thermo, USA, scanning speed of 10 (°)·min−1). The microscopic morphology of Mn5O8 was characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-7800F, JEOL, Japan), an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS; JSM-7100F, JEOL, Japan), a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM 1011, JEOL, Japan), and a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; JSM-6700F, JEOL, Japan). Autosorb-iQ (Quantachrome) was used to determine the N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms.

### 2.3 Electrochemical measurements

To obtain a uniformly mixed Mn5O8 slurry, the first step involved dissolving 10 mg of sodium alginate in 2000 µL of deionized (DI) water. Subsequently, 70 mg of Mn5O8 powder and 20 mg of super P were thoroughly ground for 30 min. Once evenly mixed, they were added to the aqueous solution of sodium alginate and stirred for 8 h after ultrasonication to form a uniform slurry. To ensure consistency among samples, a coater was used to coat the evenly dispersed slurry onto copper foil, which was then dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C overnight. After the solvent was completely evaporated, it was cut into discs with a diameter of 10 mm using a slicer to prepare the working electrode. The mass of the active substance was maintained at 0.8–1.0 mg·cm−2. Li foil was utilized as the counter electrode, Celgard 2500 was employed as the separator, and a 1 M LiPF6 electrolyte was used to assemble the CR-2025 coin cell. A LAND Battery testing system (CT2001A, LAND, China) was utilized to conduct constant current charge/discharge cycles and electrochemical performance tests at various current densities. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were performed using a CHI660e electrochemical workstation (CHI660e, CH Instruments, China). To facilitate direct comparison, commercial graphite was chosen as the reference material and subjected to electrochemical performance testing via the LAND battery testing system. A mixture of commercial graphite (70 wt%), Super P (20 wt%), and poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF, 10 wt%) was dispersed in 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone, and the resulting mixture was uniformly coated onto copper foil to obtain the reference working electrode. The remaining preparation and testing methods were identical to those employed for the Mn5O8 electrode.

To investigate the morphology and structure of Mn5O8, SEM and TEM were employed for characterization. As shown in the SEM image (Fig. 2(a)), Mn5O8 exhibited an irregular hexagonal plate-like structure with a thickness ranging from approximately 50 to 100 nm. Figure S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) shows SEM images of Mn5O8 and its precursor at different magnifications. Compared with the Mn5O8 precursor obtained through hydrothermal treatment, Mn5O8 material has a more uniform distribution and a more regular structure. TEM images (Figs. 2(b) and 2(c)) of Mn5O8 reveal a nonsmooth, plate-like structure with abundant pores, approximately 14.4 nm in size, providing ample active sites for Mn5O8. Combined with the N2 adsorption/desorption measurements (Fig. S2 in the ESM), the pore size distribution of the Mn5O8 nanosheets is mainly concentrated in the range of 2.8–15.1 nm, indicating a mesoporous structure. The existence of these abundant mesoporous structures not only accelerates the infiltration of electrolytes during the cycling process but also decreases the transmission distance of ions/electrons, thus increasing the efficiency of ion transmission [46]. The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image (Fig. 2(d)) reveals the regular atomic arrangement and clear lattice fringes. The lattice spacings of Mn5O8 are 0.307, 0.406, and 0.486 nm, corresponding to the (111), ( 2¯01), and (200) crystal planes of standard Mn5O8 (JCPDS card No. 39-1218), respectively [47]. EDS mappings (Fig. 2(f)) reveal that Mn and O are evenly distributed in Mn5O8.

Further analysis of the elemental composition and oxidation states of the sample was conducted via XPS. As shown in Fig. 3(c), the total XPS spectrum reveals that the obtained sample consists of two elements, Mn and O, confirming that its elemental composition is consistent with that of the expected Mn5O8. Figure 3(d) displays the 2P spectra of Mn. The characteristic valence states of Mn 2p clearly correspond to pairs of binding energy values for the two spin–orbit peaks (2p3/2, 2p1/2) of Mn2+ (641.18 and 652.30 eV) and Mn4+ (639.10 and 653.70 eV), which corresponds to the presence of Mn2+ and Mn4+ in the theoretical Mn5O8 [47,49]. In addition, the molar ratio of Mn2+ to Mn4+ is 1:1.46 (Table S2 in the ESM), which closely approximates the theoretical molar ratio of Mn5O8 of 1:1.5, and its chemical formula is [Mn22+Mn34+O82−]. The presence of very small Mn0 values may be attributed to incomplete oxidation. The 1s spectrum of O exhibits two distinct oxygen peaks (Fig. 3(e)). The peak at 529.18 eV corresponds to lattice oxygen, indicating the Mn–O bond, whereas the peak at 531.28 eV, with a higher binding energy, signifies the presence of adsorbed oxygen, corresponding to OH–.

Electrochemical performance tests were conducted on Mn5O8 as a LIB anode material via CR-2025 button cells. Initially, the cyclic voltammetry (CV) was employed to obtain the voltammogram of Mn5O8 at a scan rate of 0.2 mV·s−1. As shown in Fig. 4(a), the electrochemical behavior of the first cycle differs from that of the subsequent cycles. The emergence of the reduction peak in the first cycle is linked to the development of a solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) film, which is a complex process. The reduction peak observed at 0.23 V in the subsequent cycles corresponds to the reduction reaction involving high-valent manganese. The oxidation peaks observed at 1.3 and 2.2 V are related to the oxidation process of low-valent manganese. The cycling performance test was conducted at a current density of 100 mA·g−1 (Fig. 4(b)). After 100 cycles, the specific capacity of the Mn5O8 anode reached 938.7 mAh·g−1.

Furthermore, the constant current charge/discharge curves at 100 mA·g−1 almost overlapped from the second cycle to the fiftieth cycle (Fig. 4(c)), indicating the excellent cycling stability and reversibility of the anode material. During the initial cycling process, the battery demonstrated a remarkable discharge-specific capacity of 1293.9 mAh·g−1, along with a notable charge-specific capacity of 828.5 mAh·g−1. Owing to the formation of an SEI film and the occurrence of some side reactions during the discharge process, a portion of Li+ is consumed. Therefore, the battery has a first discharge-specific capacity that is higher than the charge-specific capacity. Additionally, it not only exhibits excellent performance under low-current conditions but also delivers a high discharge capacity at high rates of 5 and 10 A·g−1 (Fig. 4(d)), surpassing the capacity of commercial graphite (Fig. S5 in the ESM). When the current density is decreased from 10 to 0.1 A·g−1, a rapid increase in specific capacity is observed. The capacity retention at different current densities during the rate performance test is shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM, indicating the excellent rate capability of Mn5O8.

The charge/discharge curves at different current densities are presented in Fig. 4(e), revealing a capacity of 271.9 mAh·g−1 even at 10 A·g−1. Additionally, cycling performance tests were conducted at other current densities. As shown in Fig. S7 in the ESM, a specific capacity of 592.2 mAh·g−1 was still retained after 150 cycles at a current density of 1000 mA·g−1. In contrast, commercial graphite exhibited a specific capacity of only 345.54 mAh·g−1 under the same conditions. Compared with other previously reported manganese-based materials, Mn5O8 anode material not only has superior charging performance (Fig. 4(f)) but also exceptional specific capacity and high cycling stability (Table S1 in the ESM) [50−57]. In addition, compared with other anode oxide materials for fast-charging LIBs, Mn5O8 material exhibits excellent fast-charging performance (Fig. S8 in the ESM) [58−62].

Furthermore, long-term cycling performance testing was conducted at a current density of 3000 mA·g−1 (Fig. 4(g)), and the specific capacity remained at 675.7 mAh·g−1 even after 1000 cycles. This indicates a high level of cycle stability, which is significantly greater than that of graphite, with a specific capacity of only 276.76 mAh·g−1 (Fig. S9 in the ESM). However, during the cycling process, the capacity tends to decrease slightly in the first 30 cycles and then increases and maintains a high level of stability. This fluctuation in capacity during the cycling process is a common phenomenon observed in transition metal-based batteries, and it has been reported in Refs. [63−65]. The decrease in capacity during the initial cycle of the Mn5O8 anode material may be attributed to the irreversible formation of the SEI film and the volume expansion during cycling. As the number of cycles increases, the increase in capacity may be attributed to the kinetic activation process in the electrode. After hundreds of cycles, the battery capacity fluctuates due to the instability of the SEI film. In addition, the SEM image (Fig. S10 in the ESM) reveals that the electrode remains relatively flat after cycling. Figure S11 in the ESM shows the XRD pattern after 1000 cycles, which is basically consistent with the characteristic peaks of the crystal structure observed after the first cycle. This further demonstrates the excellent stability of the Mn5O8 material. Therefore, Mn5O8 demonstrates excellent electrochemical properties when used as an anode material for LIBs.

At a scan rate of 1 mV·s−1, 60.1% of the total capacity of the Mn5O8 electrode is attributed to the pseudocapacitive response, and the control of the surface capacitance gradually increases with increasing scan rate (Fig. 5(d)). The porous structure of the Mn5O8 nanosheets plays a crucial role in the pseudocapacitive contribution and results in excellent charge transfer kinetics for Li+ storage [71]. EIS measurements were conducted on the Mn5O8 electrode before and after 100 cycles at a current density of 1000 mA·g−1 to analyze its kinetic behavior [72,73]. Figure 5(e) shows that the diameter in the high-frequency region significantly decreases after 100 cycles, indicating a considerable reduction in the impedance of the Mn5O8 electrode during the reaction. This decrease is attributed to the increased rates of ion/electron transport during the reaction process, enabling the electrode to achieve excellent rate performance. Despite undergoing numerous cycles, charge transfer resistance (*R*ct) of the Mn5O8 electrode remains significantly smaller than its initial value [74]. This further confirms the excellent cycle stability of the Mn5O8 material.

Furthermore, the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) was used to evaluate the Li+ diffusion coefficient (* DLi+) (Fig. S13 in the ESM). After a series of assumptions and simplifications, the Li+ diffusion coefficient can be calculated via Eq. (4):

(4) DLi+=4πτ(nmVmS)2(ΔEsΔEτ)2

where τ* represents the relaxation time (s); *n*m (mol) and *V*m (cm3·mol−1) represent the number of moles and molar volume of the compound, respectively; *S* (cm2) represents the contact area of the electrode/electrolyte; Δ*E*s represents the voltage change caused by the pulse; Δ*E**τ* indicates the voltage change during constant current charging/discharging. According to this equation, during the charge/discharge process, Mn5O8 has a high Li+ diffusion value (Fig. 5(f)), which is approximately 10−10–10−9 cm·s−1. These findings suggest that Mn5O8 has fast reaction kinetics due to its abundant pores and many defects.

To further explore the Li+ storage mechanism of Mn5O8, *ex situ* XRD tests were conducted on the Mn5O8 electrode at different charge/discharge states during the initial cycle. As shown in Fig. S14 in the ESM, when the electrode is discharged from the initial state to 0.01 V, the characteristic peaks of Mn5O8 at 18° and 36° gradually weaken and disappear, suggesting that Mn5O8 undergoes a conversion reaction with Li+ as the discharge process progresses. Although the corresponding Li*x*Mn*y*O*z* is produced, it was not detected due to its low crystallinity. During the charging process, the characteristic peak of Mn5O8 reappears and returns to the initial state after charging is completed, indicating that Li*x*Mn*y*O*z* is converted back into Mn5O8, making the reaction process highly reversible. The diffraction peaks at 30° and 33° correspond to the SEI films. These SEI films are typically formed during discharge to low voltage. The most obvious peaks at 43° and 50° [75] are the copper peaks generated by the copper foil current collector.

On the basis of the aforementioned characterization and testing results, the exceptional electrochemical performance of the material can be attributed to the following factors: (1) The plate-like morphology and mesoporous structure of Mn5O8 facilitate the shortening of ion/electron transport pathways, thereby increasing electrolyte penetration and accelerating ion diffusion rates. (2) The presence of multiple valence states of Mn and its conversion reactions contribute to a higher theoretical capacity. (3) The porous hexagonal structure of Mn5O8, along with its uniform and abundant porosity, enables effective accommodation of volume changes, thereby leading to improved cycling stability.

## 4 Conclusions

In conclusion, porous hexagonal Mn5O8 nanosheets were successfully synthesized via a simple hydrothermal method followed by annealing. The electrochemical performance of the synthesized material as an anode material for rechargeable LIBs was further investigated. The porous structure significantly increased the contact area between the anode material and the electrolyte, shortening the transmission distance of ions/electrons and thus accelerating the rate of charge transfer for ions/electrons. Consequently, the Mn5O8 electrode exhibited excellent electrochemical performance. Even after 100 cycles at a current density of 100 mA·g−1, it still achieved a specific capacity of 938.7 mAh·g−1. This demonstrates the significant potential of Mn5O8 with a layered structure as an anode material for LIBs.
