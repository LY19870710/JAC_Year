## 1 Introduction

High-entropy materials have attracted extensive attention in recent years since the invention of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) in 2004 [1,2]. Thereafter, the concept of high-entropy ceramics was proposed when entropy-stabilized five-component oxides were fabricated by Rost *et al*. in 2015 [3]. With respect to HEAs, high-entropy ceramics consist of equimolar or near-equimolar multicomponent elements (no less than four types of cations or anions) in a single phase. Although the development of high-entropy ceramics is still prosperous, the materials have been largely extended to the categories of borides [4], carbides [5], nitrides [6], phosphates [7], silicates [8,9], etc., wherein transition metal borides and carbides high-entropy ceramics have been the main focus owing to their high-temperature stability.

The transition metal borides and carbides are known as ultrahigh-temperature ceramics (UHTCs) with excellent physical and chemical properties, including a superhigh melting point (> 3000 °C), high hardness, high elastic modulus and good resistance to thermal shock, oxidation and corrosion [10,11]. Therefore, the investigations of these materials can guide the discovery of suitable materials for extreme environments at high temperatures. Moreover, the core effects of high-entropy materials, such as lattice distortion and cocktail effects, could further improve the overall performance of UHTCs [12]. Consequently, diverse high-entropy UHTCs with selected components have been reported, and indeed, they possess higher hardness, higher elastic modulus, lower thermal conductivity [13,14] or better oxidation resistance [15] than do single transition metal component UHTCs. Moreover, owing to their tailorable properties, these new materials have broad application prospects.

At present, most works have focused on the fabrication of powders [5,16−30] and dense bulk materials [4,5,13,14,31−37] of high-entropy UHTCs. Recently, porous high-entropy UHTCs with tailored microstructures have received increasing attention because of their unique properties, such as excellent thermal stability, light weight and low thermal conductivity [38−40]. These properties are derived from the integration of porous structure and high-entropy UHTCs materials. As is well known, high porosity can significantly decrease both weight and thermal conductivity [41], whereas the high-entropy UHTCs have low thermal conductivity, good thermal stability and good oxidation resistance. To date, the reported porous high-entropy ceramic (Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Ti0.2)B2 clearly has a lower thermal conductivity (0.51 W/(m·K)) [38] than does dense high-entropy boride (HEB) (24.6 W/(m·K)) [14]. Similarly, porous (Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Ti0.2)C also has a lower thermal conductivity (0.39 W/(m·K) and 0.164 W/(m·K)) [39,40] than dense high-entropy carbide (HEC) (13.2 W/(m·K)) [42]. The merits of extremely low thermal conductivity and low density highlight porous high-entropy UHTCs as a new class of ultrahigh-temperature thermal insulation materials.

Both ultrahigh-temperature borides and carbides have their own superiorities and insufficiencies as ultrahigh-temperature thermal insulation materials [43]. Specifically, ultrahigh-temperature borides have lower density and better oxidation resistance than carbides do, whereas ultrahigh-temperature carbides have much lower thermal conductivity and higher melting points than borides do. Inspired by the above points, the design of novel dual-phase UHTCs could combine the advantages of both ultrahigh-temperature borides and carbides [42,44]. Compared with single-phase samples, dual-phase samples generally inhibit each other’s growth, resulting in smaller grains and more grain boundaries. Moreover, the abundant grain boundaries could increase the interfacial thermal resistance and thus obviously reduce the high thermal conductivity of the boride component. In addition, the introduction of both additives and borides components effectively improved the antioxidation capacity of the carbides.

Hence, we designed novel porous dual-phase high-entropy (DPHE) UHTCs (high-entropy boride (HEB) and high-entropy carbide (HEC)) by tailoring their microstructures and components, which would be endowed with the expected low density, high strength, low thermal conductivity and good oxidation resistance. In the present work, we first investigated the synthesis of porous dual-phase high-entropy UHTCs and further characterized their microstructure and compositional homogeneity, as well as their compressive strength, thermal conductivity, and oxidation resistance. The present findings show that dual-phase high-entropy UHTCs are promising ultrahigh-temperature thermal insulation materials.

### 2.1 Starting materials and preparation procedure

TiB2, ZrB2, HfB2, NbB2, TaB2 (borides, ≥ 99% purity, ~0.5 μm), TiC (≥ 99.5% purity, 1–2 μm), ZrC (≥ 99% purity, ~1 μm), HfC (≥ 99% purity, ~0.8 μm), NbC (≥ 99%, purity 1–3 μm), and TaC (≥ 99.6% purity, 1–3 μm) were selected as the starting materials (Forsman Scientific (Beijing) Co., Ltd.). B4C (≥ 99% purity, ~0.5 μm, Qinhuangdao ENO High-Tech Material Development Co., Ltd.) and SiC (≥ 99% purity, ~0.5 μm, Shanghai Chao Wei Nanotechnology Co., Ltd.) were used as sintering additives. The long-chain surfactants sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and gelatin were chosen as the foaming agent and the gelation agent, respectively. The five transition metal borides and carbides with equal molar ratios were mixed in Si3N4 jars with Si3N4 balls by a planetary ball mill using absolute ethyl alcohol as the solvent. After ball milling, the slurries were dried and sieved to obtain mixed powder borides and carbides (denoted as mixed powders B and C, respectively). The detailed preparation procedure for the porous sample was described in our previous works [40,45]. The molar ratio of the mixed powders B and C in the slurry was set to 1 : 1. All the as-prepared samples were supplemented with 2.0 wt% SiC according to previous methods [40]. Moreover, the additive amount of B4C was based on the quality of mixed powder B, and the addition of SiC was based on the quality of mixed powder C. A vacuum freeze dryer (SCIENTZ-10 N, Scientz, China) was used to eliminate the moisture of the green body. The samples were fabricated after high-temperature pressureless sintering at 2000 °C for 2 h in a vacuum graphite tube furnace (MT-18-22, Chen Hua, China). In addition, the porous dual-phase high-entropy samples fabricated from different solids contents (7–20 vol%) were named DPHE-7, DPHE-10, DPHE-15, and DPHE-20.

### 2.2 Characterizations

Phase composition was conducted on an X-ray diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.54178 Å) over a 2*θ* range of 20°–80° in steps of 0.02°. Rietveld refinements of X-ray diffraction (XRD) data were performed via the general structure analysis system (GSAS) through the EXPGUI interface. The theoretical density of dual-phase high-entropy UHTC (*ρ*t) was obtained via Eq. (1):

(1) ρt=VHEB×ρt−HEB+VHEC×ρt−HEC

where *V*HEB and *V*HEC are the volume fractions of HEB and HEC in dual-phase high-entropy UHTC (54.9% and 45.1%, respectively). *ρ*t-HEB and *ρ*t-HEC are the theoretical densities of HEB and HEC, respectively, which are obtained from the XRD Rietveld refinement. After the abovementioned calculation, the theoretical density (*ρ*t) of the dual-phase high-entropy UHTC is 8.78 g/cm3. The porosity (*P*) was calculated via Eq. (2):

(2) P=(1−ρρt)×100%

where *ρ* is the apparent density of the porous dual-phase sample, which is calculated by the ratio of the measured mass to the volume. The microstructures of the samples were analyzed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; SUPRA 55, Zeiss, Germany) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2 F20, FEI, USA). The chemical element distribution was characterized via energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) via SEM and TEM. The pore size distributions of the porous samples were analyzed with Nano Measurer (1.2) software on the basis of several SEM images.

Samples with dimensions of approximately  ϕ20 mm × 20 mm were employed to determine the compressive strength via a universal testing machine (CMT 4204, SANS, China) with a loading speed of 0.5 mm/min. Each value is an average of five samples. The thermal conductivity of each sample ( ϕ50 mm × 3 mm) was measured via the guarded heat flow test method (DTC-300, TA Instruments Co., USA) in air and within a temperature range of 25–300 °C. Thermogravimetric analysis combined with differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC, STA449F3, Netzsch, Germany) was used to estimate the oxidation resistance of mixed powder B, mixed powder C, single-phase HEB, single-phase HEC, and dual-phase high-entropy UHTC powder, and the test temperature ranged from 30 to 1400 °C under airflow at a heating rate of 10 °C/min.

### 3.1 Phase compositions

The XRD patterns of the raw materials and as-sintered porous samples are presented in Fig. 1. The phase compositions of mixed powders B and C together with the corresponding five components of borides and carbides are shown in Fig. 1(a). It obviously demonstrates that all the five binary borides have hexagonal close packed (HCP) structure and all the five binary carbides have face centered cubic (FCC) structure. Figure 1(a) shows that the mixed powders B and C are still mixtures of borides and carbides, respectively, after ball milling. Figure 1(b) shows the XRD patterns of the as-sintered porous samples with different sintering additive contents. The main phases are the HEC phase and HEB phase, but a small amount of (Zr,Hf)B2 still does not completely diffuse into the HEB phase when the additive amount of B4C is less than 0.5 wt%. As the content of B4C reached 1.0 wt%, the diffraction peak of (Zr,Hf)B2 disappeared. This indicates that the solid solution is complete and that a dual-phase solid solution with two different phase compositions (HEB & HEC) is formed. Moreover, the diffraction peaks of the HEB phase become narrower in the dual-phase high-entropy sample when B4C is added at 2.0 wt%. Notably, the DPHE solid solution materials were successfully fabricated at 2000 °C under the combined action of two sintering additives, SiC and B4C. In this study, the optimal amounts of 2.0 wt% B4C and 2.0 wt% SiC in this porous dual-phase high-entropy UHTC system were selected.

### 3.2 Microstructure and compositional homogeneity

Figure 3 shows micrographs of DPHE-7, DPHE-10, DPHE-15, and DPHE-20 with different solid contents. All the as-prepared porous samples have uniformly distributed isotropic pores (almost Figs. 3(a)–3(j). The pore size distributions of DPHE-7, DPHE-10, DPHE-15, and DPHE-20 are 47–202, 46–198, 43–194, and 44–187 μm, respectively. Some windows also exist on the pore wall of the spherical pore. The corresponding window sizes are 5.7–99.4, 4.1–85.4, 5.7–75.3, and 5.4–65.9 μm, respectively. The mean sizes of the pores and windows are 120 and 36.4 μm for DPHE-7, 117 and 35.6 μm for DPHE-10, 115 and 30.1 μm for DPHE-15, and 109 and 24.7 μm for DPHE-20. The SEM images reveal that the skeleton is much thicker and that the window area is much smaller for porous samples with lower porosities (in Figs. 3(g)–3(k)), whereas the pores are composed of thinner pore struts and larger windows for samples with higher porosities (Figs. 3(a)–3(e)).

*
Fig. 3SEM micrographs of the pore structure, cell skeleton and BSE images of dual-phase particles: (a–c) DPHE-7, (d–f) DPHE-10, (g–i) DPHE-15, and (j–l) DPHE-20.

For the formation of the pores, the spherical pores are generated by the foaming process. Specifically, vigorous mechanical stirring formed many mutually contacted bubbles in the ceramic slurry supplemented with the foaming agent. Then, the ceramic particles are squeezed together by the bubbles and form the ceramic skeleton. After gelation, drying and sintering, the bubbles become spherical pores. Windows are the areas where adjacent bubbles squeeze most severely. There are very few ceramic particles left, and they become the window between two adjacent bubbles after sintering. In addition, with increasing solid content (7–20 vol%), the amount of ceramic particles per volume increases, which means that more ceramic particles wrap the bubbles. Thus, the spherical pore wall becomes more integral, and the window size decreases as the solid content increases.

The backscattered electron (BSE) images of the samples are also shown in Fig. 3(c), 3(f), 3(i), and 3(l). An obvious contrast between the dual-phase UHTC grains is apparent in the BSE images. The gray contrast phase is the low-density HEB, whereas the bright contrast phase is the high-density HEC. In addition, HEB particles present a plate-like shape, and HEC particles tend to have an equiaxed shape. Both high-entropy UHTC grains are uniformly distributed inside the skeleton. Visually, the area proportion of HEB in the dual-phase sample is greater than the proportion of HEC according to the BSE images. In addition, all the samples exhibit a firmly sintered skeleton, which benefits from the improvement in sinterability with additives. Moreover, a firmly sintered skeleton and strong cell wall are highly important for achieving good mechanical properties [46].

Figure 4 shows bright field images, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images, and corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) images of DPHE-20. A dual-phase particle is shown in Fig. 4(a), which is coincidentally shaped like a cherry. Both of the HRTEM images in Figs. 4(b) and 4(c) present a periodic symmetric atomic lattice, in which the set fringes are approximately 2.65 and 2.26 Å, corresponding to the d*-space of the (-1100) plane for HCP and the *d*-space of the (200) plane for FCC. The inserted FFT images reveal the [0001] zone axis of the HCP and the [001] zone axis of the FCC. Furthermore, the *d*-spaces analyzed from the HRTEM images are in accordance with the XRD results (HEB: *d*(-1100) = 2.69 Å, HEC: *d*(200) = 2.27 Å). After analysis, the above lathy particle (“peduncle of the cherry”) in Fig. 4 is verified to be the HEB phase with an HCP structure, and the below quasirectangular particle (“fruit of the cherry”) is confirmed to be the HEC phase with an FCC structure.

DPHE-20 was selected to characterize the compositional homogeneity of the HEB phase and HEC phase in dual-phase sample. The associated EDS elemental maps for C, B, Ti, Zr, Hf, Nb, and Ta at both the micro- and nanoscales are shown in Fig. 5. At the microscale, the intensity of C in the bright region is stronger, whereas the distribution of B in the gray region is more intensive in Fig. 5(a). It is difficult to distinguish light elements from each other completely by EDS, but the present evidence still supports the conclusion that the sample is a dual-phase material. Despite slight differences in Ti and Ta, the distribution of each transition metal element is uniform in the respective phases.

At the nanoscale, Fig. 5(b) suggests that B element is enriched in the long-strip ‘peduncle’ region, which again verifies that this region is the HEB phase. As presented in the EDS mappings more clearly, there are also differences in the distributions of some transition metal elements for the two phases at the nanoscale. The content of Ti in the HEB phase is slightly greater than that in the HEC phase, and the content of Ta presents the opposite result. However, the distribution of each metal element is also uniform in the separate HEB and HEC phases, which indicates that the solid solution reaction is sufficient in porous dual-phase high-entropy samples. Similar phenomena are also found in dense dual-phase high-entropy bulk samples [42]. In general, the metal cation percentages in the two high-entropy phases are different in some DPHE ceramic systems with two kinds of raw materials equimolar because some metal elements are inclined to stabilize at different concentrations in different phases when thermodynamic equilibrium between the two high-entropy solution phases is achieved. The detailed reason for this difference needs specialized thermodynamic theoretical calculations for verification in future works. In brief, the sample has uniform element distributions, especially in the respective two-phase particles (HEB and HEC).

In addition, the EDS results explain why some differences exist in the lattice parameters of HEB and HEC in the dual-phase high-entropy sample compared with those of the single-phase HEB and HEC of other works. Differences in the Ti and Ta concentrations in the two phases cause the experimental lattice parameters of HEB and HEC in the DPHE sample to deviate from the ideal lattice parameters of equal-molar solid solutions ((Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Ti0.2)C and (Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Ti0.2)B2). Specifically, Ti has a small atomic radius, whereas Ta has a large atomic radius [5]. For the HEC phase in the DPHE sample, there is more Ta in its crystal lattice. Concurrently, there is very little solid solution of oxygen (lattice oxygen, which has a smaller atomic radius than C) (in Fig. 1(b)) due to the addition of sintering aids, which would also increase the lattice parameter. Thus, the HEC phase has a larger lattice parameter than the other phases do. For the HEB phase, although the content of lattice oxygen is also very small in the HEB lattice (the lattice parameters should also be increased), the existence of more Ti (smallest atomic radius among the five transition metal elements) results in a decrease in the lattice parameters. Consequently, the lattice parameters of the HEB phase in the DPHE sample do not increase significantly and are similar to those reported in other works.

### 3.3 Compressive strength and thermal conductivity

The compressive strength and thermal conductivity are crucial properties of thermal insulation materials, and these properties are closely related to the porosity of the materials. The samples with solid contents ranging from 7–20 vol% have high porosities ranging from 96.4%–90.1% and low densities ranging from 0.31–0.87 g/cm3. Notably, the porous UHTC fabricated via the foam-gelcasting-freeze drying method has the advantages of superhigh porosity and light weight. Figure 6(a) presents the compressive strength as a function of the porosity of porous dual-phase high-entropy UHTC. The compressive strengths of the porous DPHE samples increase from 0.45 to 4.17 MPa as the porosity decreases from 96.4% to 90.1% in this work. Figure 6(b) exhibits the thermal conductivity of dual-phase samples with different porosities as a function of the test temperature (room temperature to 300 °C). At room temperature, the thermal conductivity decreases from 0.281 to 0.202 W/(m·K) as the porosity increases from 90.1% to 96.4%. Similarly, the DPHE sample with higher porosity also has lower thermal conductivity at 300 °C, which ranges from 0.510 to 0.365 W/(m·K). For the highly porous DPHE sample (> 90%), the pore volume fraction is evidently greater than that of the ceramic skeleton. The thermal conductivity (0.026 W/(m·K)) of air is obviously lower than that of ceramics. Hence, the porous DPHE sample in this work has a relatively low thermal conductivity, and the sample with a relatively high porosity has a relatively low thermal conductivity at room temperature. Notably, the thermal conductivity of the porous sample increases with increasing temperature. This phenomenon can be explained by the fact that as the temperature increases, solid heat conduction, gas heat conduction and heat radiation all increase simultaneously, causing the total thermal conductivity to increase.

### 3.4 Qualitative oxidation resistance

The oxidation resistance is also an important index for nonoxide thermal insulation materials. Hence, thermogravimetric (TG) analysis combined with differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was used to qualitatively evaluate the oxidation behavior of the porous UHTCs samples. Figure 7 shows the TG‒DSC curves of mixed powder B, mixed powder C, single-phase HEB, single-phase HEC and DPHE-20 powder in air to compare their oxidation behavior. As shown in Fig. 7(a), there are multiple exothermic peaks in mixed powders B and C, which indicate the individual oxidations of the corresponding components. For the raw materials, the maximum exothermic peaks of oxidation are at ~467.5 and ~656.5 °C for mixed powders C and B, respectively. This suggests that mixed powder B has better oxidation resistance than mixed powder C. This is because the oxidation products of borides are liquid-phase B2O3, which can inhibit the diffusion of oxygen and delay the progression of oxidation. Moreover, the rapid oxidation temperatures are ~586.7 and ~702.9 °C for single-phase HEC powder and single-phase HEB powder, respectively. This suggests that the high-entropy powders have much better oxidation resistance than the corresponding mixed powders do, among which single-phase HEB powder has the highest values. This demonstrates that single-phase high-entropy powder has better oxidation resistance than mixtures of corresponding components do, which benefits from the cocktail effects of high entropy. As expected, the single-phase HEB has better oxidation resistance than does the single-phase HEC. In good accordance with other investigations, ultrahigh-temperature borides have better oxidation resistance than ultrahigh-temperature carbides do, which motivated the present work to fabricate dual-phase samples.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, we report a novel porous dual-phase high-entropy UHTC material with ultrahigh porosity (96.4%) fabricated via foam-gelcasting-freeze drying and an *in situ* pressureless reaction sintering technique. When the B4C and SiC additives are both incorporated, pure DPHE UHTC can be obtained. The as-prepared DPHE samples consisted of two phases: HEB with an HCP structure and HEC with an FCC structure. Specifically, a dual-phase high-entropy solid solution is formed by the interdiffuse and *in situ* react sintering of mixed single transition metal boride and carbide powders at high temperatures. The porous samples have uniform pore sizes and random alternating distributions of plate-like HEB particles and equiaxed HEC particles. Two-phase components help to inhibit the growth of grains and form abundant grain boundaries. The generated small grains are good for obtaining high strength and low thermal conductivity. Moreover, abundant grain boundaries could bring in interfacial thermal resistance and further decrease the thermal conductivity of the sample. In addition, incorporating a second component of boride and additive into the carbide system can effectively improve its oxidation resistance. In this study, the porous DPHE samples have excellent overall properties, including a high porosity of 90.1%–96.4%, a low density of 0.31–0.87 g/cm3, a high strength of 0.45–4.17 MPa, a low thermal conductivity of 0.202–0.281 W/(m·K), and good oxidation resistance. These research results indicate that highly porous dual-phase high-entropy UHTCs have broad application prospects in ultrahigh-temperature thermal insulation.
