## 1 Introduction

With the rapid expansion of electromagnetic wave (EMW) applications in fields such as wireless communication, military operations, and consumer electronics, electromagnetic interference has become a pervasive challenge, affecting public health and the functionality of critical electronic systems. Consequently, the design, development, and application of EMW-absorbing materials have emerged as effective solutions for mitigating these issues [1,2]. Ideal EMW absorbing materials should possess strong absorption, a wide effective absorption bandwidth (EAB), and a thin profile [3−5]. Additionally, it should meet demanding requirements for lightweight materials, robust mechanical strength, excellent thermal stability, and efficient thermal insulation, particularly for absorbers used in aerospace and military applications [6,7]. Consequently, designing microwave absorbers for lightweight, multifunctional applications has become a prominent focus and significant challenge within the EMW absorption research community.

EMW absorbers are generally classified into magnetic loss- and dielectric loss-type materials. Magnetic loss-type materials tend to lose their magnetism above the Curie temperature, leading to a significant reduction in microwave absorption efficiency [8,9]. In contrast, dielectric-type absorbers can remain effective under extreme environmental conditions such as high temperatures, oxidative atmospheres, and mechanical stress, making them more suitable for versatile applications [8]. Among dielectric absorbers, carbon-based materials, especially three-dimensional (3D) carbon fiber (Cf) networks, have attracted special attention owing to their lightweight structure, good stability, and high dielectric loss [10−12]. Unfortunately, the inadequate impedance matching, weak mechanical properties, and poor oxidation resistance of pure Cf networks hinder both their microwave absorption performance and their multifunctional applications [13−18]. Additionally, carbon fibers are easily damaged during composite preparation.

Combining carbon fibers with precursor-derived ceramics (PDCs), such as SiOC, effectively improves the properties of pure Cf by enhancing microwave absorption through optimized impedance matching and the creation of heterogeneous interfaces, with increased thermal stability and mechanical strength [6,14,19−23]. For example, Zhao *et al.* [24] reported a Cf/SiOC composite with a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −62.9 dB and an EAB of 3.52 GHz, compared with −8.4 dB for pure Cf, and it exhibited a weight retention of over 60% in an air atmosphere at approximately 850 °C. Qian *et al.* [25] utilized a chemical vapor infiltration (CVI) process to fabricate a Cf/SiOC–SiCnw (nanowire) composite, achieving RLmin of −48.2 dB and EAB of 3.06 GHz. The improved microwave absorption capability of those composites is believed to be due mainly to the introduction of SiOC and SiCnws to optimize the impedance matching of Cf. Sun *et al*. [19] synthesized a Cf/SiOC composite via a precursor infiltration and pyrolysis (PIP) process, which improved the compressive strength by over 50.00% compared with that of pure Cf. After 1 h of static oxidation at 600 °C, the composite exhibited a mass loss of less than 0.6 wt%. Moreover, Yang *et al.* [8] incorporated SiOC in the form of aerogel into Cf via sol‒gel technology, producing a Cf/C-SiOC composite with a density of 0.33 g·cm−3. The composite achieved RLmin of −58.88 dB, EAB of 4.28 GHz, and a compressive strength of 4.08 MPa in the *z*-direction. These enhancements in microwave absorption, mechanical strength, and thermal stability highlight the potential of PDCs in enhancing the multifunctional performance of Cf-based composites [26].

Among all PDCs, SiBCN ceramics offer advantages such as low density, excellent resistance to oxidation, and outstanding high-temperature stability [27−32]. Luo *et al*. [33] reported a graphene*@*Fe3O4/SiBCN nanocomposite with EAB of 3.4 GHz and RLmin of −43.78 dB. Xia *et al*. [34] prepared SiBCN*@*Cf/CNT composites, achieving RLmin of −44.3 dB. In our previous work [35], we synthesized a Cf/hydrothermal carbon (HC)–SiBCN composite via hydrothermal transformation and the PIP process, which significantly enhanced the mechanical strength, thermal stability, and EMW absorption capability, achieving RLmin of −52.08 dB and EAB of up to 7.64 GHz. However, the traditional PIP method tends to introduce excessive SiBCN, which hinders the maintenance of the composite’s lightweight nature and low thermal conductivity while ensuring multifunctionality [19,25].

Through multiscale and compositional design, the issues of fiber damage in Cf composite preparation, inadequate impedance matching in carbon materials, and the challenge of achieving synergy between light weight, mechanical strength, and oxidation resistance in Cf composites were resolved. A hydrothermal method was used to prepare HC coatings on carbon fibers to suppress fiber damage [35], and SiBCN was introduced to enhance the composite’s mechanical properties, thermal stability, and impedance matching. By combining the PIP and impregnation‒filtration processes and adjusting the precursor concentration and number of impregnation cycles, a novel lightweight (~0.32 g·cm−3) carbon fiber-reinforced silicon–boron carbonitride (Cf/HC–SiBCN) composite was developed, achieving a synergistic combination of lightweight and enhanced properties. Depending on the impregnation–filtration conditions, the composites demonstrated flexural and compressive strengths of up to 14.75**±**0.43 and 9.36**±**0.20 MPa, respectively, while retaining more than 64.78% of their mass above 1200 °C in air. In addition, the low thermal conductivity of approximately 0.145 W·m−1·K−1 and superior EMW absorption performance were achieved. The optimal EMW absorption performance was observed in a composite processed through two impregnation‒filtration cycles with a 20 wt% SiBCN precursor solution, with RLmin of −58.13 dB and EAB of 7.38 GHz. These results demonstrated that the Cf/HC–SiBCN composite exhibited a combination of light weight, good mechanical properties, high-temperature stability, low thermal conductivity, and superior EMW absorption capabilities, making it a promising candidate for advanced multifunctional applications.

### 2.1 Materials

HC-coated Cf preforms, as described in our previous study [35], were used as the skeleton and reinforcement for the EMW absorbing composites. The Cf preforms (T700, 0.25 g·cm−3) were supplied by Jiangsu Tianniao High Technology Co., Ltd., China. Polyborosilazane (PBSZ), a liquid precursor for SiBCN with a molecular weight of 600–800, was sourced from the Institute of Chemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China. Toluene (PhMe) obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Co., Ltd., China, was used as the solvent. All the reagents used in this study were of analytical grade.

### 2.2 Preparation of Cf/HC–SiBCN composites

The preparation process of the Cf/HC**–**SiBCN composites is illustrated in Fig. 1. First, PBSZ was mixed with PhMe to prepare SiBCN precursor solutions with SiBCN concentrations of 5, 10, 20, and 30 wt%. The solutions were stirred at room temperature for 30 min to ensure homogeneity. The HC-coated Cf (Cf/HC) preforms were then immersed in the SiBCN precursor solution. To ensure thorough impregnation and uniform distribution of the SiBCN precursor, the system was evacuated for 1 h. After immersion, the samples were vacuum-filtered for 120 s and cured at 180 °C for 2 h in an argon atmosphere. The Cf/HC**–**SiBCN composites were obtained by pyrolyzing at 1000 °C for 1 h at a heating rate of 3 °C·min−1 under high-purity argon (this pyrolysis temperature was selected on the basis of the work of Wang *et al*. [36], who demonstrated that the organic–inorganic transition process in PBSZ predominantly occurs between 450 and 800 °C, ensuring complete inorganic conversion at 1000 °C). This process was repeated by varying the precursor concentrations to achieve similar densities across the samples. The choice of precursor concentration and number of cycles aimed to optimize the mechanical and thermal properties of the composites while balancing material uniformity and preparation efficiency. Low concentrations (e.g., 5 and 10 wt%) require more cycles to ensure sufficient SiBCN loading and uniform distribution. Higher concentrations (e.g., 20 and 30 wt%) favor achieving similar densities with fewer cycles but face risks of uneven deposition during curing and pyrolysis. On the basis of this trade-off, four cycles of 5 wt%, three cycles of 10 wt%, two cycles of 20 wt%, and one cycle of 30 wt% SiBCN precursor cycling/solution concentrations were selected. For simplicity, these composites are labeled S5P4, S10P3, S20P2, and S30P1 hereafter, where the first number represents the SiBCN precursor concentration and the second one indicates the number of impregnation‒filtration cycles.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The densities and porosities of the samples after different numbers of cycles were measured via the Archimedes method via an electronic balance with an accuracy of 0.0001 g. The density (*ρ*) was calculated via Eq. (1), and the porosity (*P*) was calculated via Eq. (2) [19]:

(1) ρ=m1−m2m1×ρH2O

(2) P=m1−m3m1×100%

where *m*1 is the weight of the sample in air (g), *m*2 is the weight of the sample in deionized water (g), *m*3 is the weight of the sample filled with water (g), and * ρH2O is the density of the deionized water (g·cm−3).

The crystalline phases and compositions of the samples were analyzed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X'Pert Pro MPD, Panalytical, Holland) via Cu Kα radiation (2θ*: 10°–90°) at a scanning rate of 0.05°·s−1. Raman spectroscopy was performed via a confocal Raman microspectrophotometer (via-Reflex, Renishaw, UK) equipped with a near-infrared laser (633 nm). X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Escalab 250, Panalytical, USA) was used to examine the elemental composition and chemical bonding states. The microstructures of the samples were observed via a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; NanoLab 600i, FEI, USA). The pore diameter distribution was measured via an automatic mercury porosimeter (AMP; AutoPore IV 9500, Horiba, Japan). A detailed microstructure analysis was conducted via a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM; Tecnai G2-F30, FEI, USA). The thermal conductivities of the composites were measured via a thermal constant analyzer (TPS 2500 S, Hot Disk AB, Sweden). The thermal insulation properties were assessed by monitoring the surface temperatures via an infrared thermal imager with a 300 °C heat source. The thermal stability was evaluated via a synchronous thermogravimetric analyzer (TG; Netzsch STA 449 F5, Netzsch, Germany) at a heating rate of 3 °C·min−1 in air. The flexural strength was measured on three-point bending samples with dimensions of 3 mm × 4 mm × 36 mm, a span of 25 mm, and a cross-head speed of 0.5 mm·min−1 on a testing machine (Instron Model 5569, Instron, USA). The compressive strength of samples with dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm was evaluated at a displacement rate of 0.2 mm·min−1. The average values and standard deviations of the flexural and compressive strengths were calculated for at least four samples. The electromagnetic parameters in the 2–18 GHz frequency range were characterized via a vector network analyzer (Agilent N5230A, Agilent Technologies, USA) via the coaxial method. The Cf/HC–SiBCN composites were ground into powders, and 20 wt% powder was mixed with 80 wt% paraffin wax. Test samples with dimensions of outer diameter (*φ*out): 7.00 mm and inner diameter (*φ*in): 3.00 mm were prepared for analysis.

### 3.1 Characterizations of Cf/HC–SiBCN composites

The densities and porosities of S5P4, S10P3, S20P2, and S30P1 are shown in Fig. S1(a) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The bulk densities of the composites are 0.31, 0.32, 0.32, and 0.33 g·cm−3. By adjusting the concentration of the SiBCN precursor solution and the number of impregnation‒filtration cycles, the density of the Cf/HC**–**SiBCN composites remained at approximately 0.32 g·cm−3, while the porosity remained above 80%. Mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) analysis, as shown in Fig. S1(b) and Table S1 in the ESM, revealed that the pore structure was dominated by pores with diameters greater than 1 μm, with a significant peak centered at approximately 50 μm. This unique pore structure allows most incident EMWs to penetrate deeply into the materials, resulting in multiple reflections within the channels and thereby enhancing EMW absorption [25].

The XRD patterns of the four Cf/HC–SiBCN composites are shown in Fig. 2(a). A distinct peak at approximately 26.3°, corresponding to the (002) plane of graphite, was observed, primarily originating from the graphitized structure of the carbon fibers [32]. This peak broadened owing to the presence of disordered carbon phases, including amorphous carbon and turbostratic graphite, which formed during the pyrolysis of SiBCN and HC [32]. Additionally, a weaker peak at 44.4°, attributed to the (100) plane of graphite, indicates partial graphitization of the carbon fibers [35]. After pyrolysis at 1000 °C, the SiBCN ceramic remained amorphous because no crystalline features of SiC were detected in the XRD patterns [37,38]. This amorphous structure has been proven to effectively prevent phase transitions and grain growth at high temperatures, ensuring the superior thermal stability of the composite [39].

Raman spectroscopy was conducted to investigate the carbon states within the composites, as shown in Fig. 2(b). The D-band at 1350 cm−1 and the G-band at 1580 cm−1 are characteristic peaks observed in all the spectra. The D-band corresponds to disordered carbon, whereas the G-band is associated with the in-plane stretching of sp*² bonds in the ordered carbon [30,40−42]. The ratio of the integrated area of the D-band to that of the G-band (*I*D/*I*G) is commonly used to assess the degree of graphitization [43]. A higher ratio of the *I*D/*I*G value indicates more disorder in the carbon structure, with increased defects and a lower degree of graphitization [29,31,36,44]. For samples S5P4, S10P3, S20P2, and S30P1, the *I*D/*I*G value initially increases and then decreases, measuring 1.22 for S5P4, 1.28 for S10P3, 1.42 for S20P2, and 1.21 for S30P1. The fluctuations may arise from the synergistic effects of the amorphous carbon in SiBCN and the turbostratic carbon in HC. Consequently, the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites exhibit relatively high *I*D/*I*G values. It is expected that a significant number of defects in the composites could create polarization centers, which might act as active sites to dissipate EMW energy through dipole polarization, thereby enhancing the EMW absorption ability of the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites [6,24,34].

XPS analysis was conducted to further explore the surface chemical composition and bonding states of the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites, as shown in Figs. 2(c)–2(h). The spectra reveal characteristic XPS peaks for N 1*s*, C 1*s*, B 1*s*, and Si 2*p* at binding energies of 398.2, 285.5, 192.1, and 102.8 eV, respectively [35]. In the C 1*s* spectrum, the peaks at 285.5 eV (C‒C bonds) and 284.5 eV (C‒Si bonds) indicate strong carbon‒silicon interactions between HC and SiBCN, suggesting hybridization between the HC-coated carbon fibers and the SiBCN ceramic [29]. Additionally, the Si 2*p* spectrum shows peaks corresponding to Si‒N bonds at 102.8 eV, and the N 1*s* spectrum identifies N‒Si bonds at 398.2 eV, whereas the B 1*s* spectrum confirms the presence of B‒N bonds [35]. These features confirm the formation of an amorphous structure within the composites after the pyrolysis of the SiBCN precursor [45]. Furthermore, the O 1*s* spectrum exhibits signals for oxide bonds at 532.9 and 533.3 eV, suggesting a certain degree of surface oxidation [46]. Overall, the XPS results indicate that the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites are composed of multiple phases, which are expected to enhance EMW dissipation through increased scattering, reflection, improved impedance matching, and dielectric loss mechanisms.

Further details regarding the microstructure of the Cf/HC–SiBCN composite (S20P2) were obtained via TEM and HRTEM, as shown in Figs. 2(i)–2(p). The HRTEM image in Fig. 2(j) reveals the amorphous structure of SiBCN, as confirmed by the absence of diffraction rings in the selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern, in agreement with the XRD results [47]. The magnified image in Fig. 2(k) shows disordered graphite layers. Diffuse diffraction patterns of the carbon phase indicate the presence of turbostratic graphite and amorphous carbon. This observation supports the broad peak at 26.3° observed in the XRD pattern. The (002) plane of the graphitized carbon fibers reflects contributions from turbostratic graphite and amorphous carbon formed during the pyrolysis of HC and SiBCN [37]. The elemental maps in Figs. 2(l)–2(p) confirm the uniform distribution of Si, B, C, N, and O, which is consistent with the XPS results. The presence of oxygen, coupled with the O 1s peak observed via XPS, suggests that the surface underwent mild oxidation.

To visualize the microstructure, SEM images of the pristine 3D needle-punched Cf and the Cf/HC composites are presented in Fig. S2 in the ESM. As shown in Figs. S2(a)–S2(d) in the ESM, the carbon fibers are uniformly distributed in the *xy-*direction and are needle-punched along the *z*-axis, resulting in an anisotropic microstructure and thus mechanical properties of the composites [24]. Figures S2(e) and S2(f) in the ESM show that the carbon fibers are uniformly coated with HC, with each fiber encased in a smooth and consistent HC coating resembling a matchstick. This HC coating protects the carbon fibers from high-temperature damage in an oxygen-rich environment by preventing direct contact with air [35]. The as-prepared Cf/HC exhibited large pores, which provided channels for the incorporation of SiBCN ceramic precursors.

The SEM images in Fig. 3 clearly show that the precursor concentration and number of impregnation‒filtration cycles significantly influence the distribution of the ceramic components in the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites. Figures 3(a) and 3(b) show the effects of a single cycle with 5 and 30 wt% SiBCN precursor solutions. The composite prepared with 5 wt% solution exhibited an uneven ceramic distribution with significant fiber exposure. In contrast, the 30 wt% solution resulted in ceramic particle agglomeration and localized SiBCN accumulation on the fiber surface. Figures 3(c) and 3(d) compare the results of the individual fibers prepared with a 10 wt% SiBCN solution after one and three cycles. After one cycle, the ceramic distribution remains uneven, leaving the fibers partially exposed. However, after three cycles, the ceramic distribution becomes uniform with full fiber coverage and without visible agglomeration, demonstrating that the uniformity improves with the increasing number of cycles. Similarly, Figs. 3(e) and 3(f) show composites prepared with 5 wt% SiBCN precursor solution after 4 cycles and 20 wt% SiBCN solution after 2 cycles. Despite multiple cycles, the composite prepared with the 5 wt% SiBCN solution produced a thin, uneven ceramic distribution, whereas the composite prepared with the 20 wt% SiBCN solution achieved uniform coverage after just 2 cycles without any signs of agglomeration. Figures 3(g)–3(o) show the detailed microstructures of S5P4, S10P3, S20P2, and S30P1, illustrating the effects of varying concentrations and cycles. For S5P4, Figs. 3(g) and 3(h) show that a low precursor concentration (5 wt% SiBCN) combined with multiple cycles results in incomplete fiber coverage, with SiBCN primarily accumulating at the fiber junctions. This phenomenon suggests that a uniform ceramic distribution is difficult to form when low-concentration solutions are used even after several cycles. In contrast, Figs. 3(i) and 3(j) show the microstructures of S10P3, in which a moderate precursor concentration (10 wt% SiBCN) and three cycles yield an even ceramic distribution on the fiber surfaces, especially effective coverage at the fiber junctions. As a result, a dual-layer structure consisting of HC and SiBCN layers enhances the protective performance of the composite. Figures 3(l) and 3(m) show the microstructure of S20P2, which has a uniform ceramic distribution and excellent ceramic coverage at the fiber junctions, similar to S10P3. Conversely, Figs. 3(n) and 3(o) show that the S30P1 composite was prepared with a high precursor concentration (30 wt% SiBCN) after a single cycle. Particle agglomeration leads to uneven ceramic distribution. In summary, optimizing the precursor concentration and the number of impregnation‒filtration cycles is essential for achieving uniform ceramic distribution on fiber surfaces and ensuring effective coverage at fiber junctions. A moderate concentration (10 and 20 wt% SiBCN) combined with two to three cycles provides the most uniform distribution and optimal morphology.

### 3.2 Mechanical properties, thermal stability, and thermal conductivity

The mechanical properties of the original Cf perform and the Cf/HC composites were evaluated, as shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. The original Cf performance results in low compressive and flexural strengths in both the *xy-* and *z-*directions. In contrast, the Cf/HC composite demonstrates significant strength improvements, with compressive strength of 1.53 MPa in the *z*-direction, flexural strengths of 2.13 MPa in the *xy*-direction, and 3.96 MPa in the *z*-direction. These improvements could be attributed to the enhanced interfacial bonding provided by the HC coating. The mechanical properties of the composites were further enhanced by the introduction of the SiBCN ceramic, as shown in Figs. 4 and S4 in the ESM. For the S5P4, S10P3, S20P2, and S30P1 composites, the flexural strengths in the *xy-*direction are 11.33±0.72, 14.75±0.43, 13.92±0.71, and 10.66±0.49 MPa, respectively, whereas, in the *z-*direction, they are 10.86±0.37, 14.45±0.66, 13.20±0.47, and 10.30±0.18 MPa, respectively. In addition, compressive properties are crucial, but in real service environments, more attention is given to the compressive strength in the *z*-direction. A similar trend was observed for the compressive strength in the *z* direction, with values of 7.46±0.18, 9.36±0.20, 8.71±0.28, and 6.17±0.13 MPa for S5P4, S10P3, S20P2, and S30P1, respectively. Among them, S10P3, prepared with 10 wt% precursor solution and three impregnation‒filtration cycles, achieves the highest flexural and compressive strengths. This highlights the importance of using a moderate precursor concentration and an appropriate number of impregnation‒filtration cycles to achieve superior mechanical properties. Figure 3 confirms that a more uniform SiBCN distribution and effective encapsulation of fiber junctions contribute to these higher strengths because they suppress interfacial defects and enhance the load transfer between the fibers. As shown in Fig. 4(d), after compression along the *xy*-direction, the composites nearly returned to their original dimensions upon unloading, demonstrating the resilience of the carbon fiber felt structure. As shown in Fig. 4(e) and Table S2 in the ESM, the compressive strengths of these samples are compared with those reported in recent studies [8,19,24,48−54]. Compared with other similar composites, the Cf/HC**–**SiBCN composite has a significantly greater specific compressive strength. Generally, the specific strength of other modified carbon fiber composites is less than 15.00 MPa·g−1·cm3, whereas that of the Cf/HC**–**SiBCN composite reaches 29.35 MPa·g−1·cm3 (S10P3).

Thermal stability is a critical factor in evaluating the high-temperature performance and oxidation resistance of materials. The thermal stabilities of Cf, Cf/HC, S5P4, S10P3, S20P2, and S30P1 were assessed via TG in an air atmosphere. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the untreated Cf begins to lose weight significantly at approximately 550 °C and is almost fully oxidized at 800 °C without residual mass. In contrast, Cf/HC results in a two-step mass loss. The initial oxidation occurs at approximately 300 °C due to the high chemical activity of the disordered graphite structure in the HC coating, which has more reactive sites. As this carbon is consumed, oxygen penetrates the more graphitized and chemically inert carbon fibers, resulting in a slower second oxidation stage [55]. Although the HC coating does not significantly improve the oxidation resistance of Cf, it usually serves as an effective transition layer between Cf and SiBCN by protecting the carbon fibers from reacting with the silicon-based ceramic and mitigating the mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients between the ceramic and the carbon fibers. With the incorporation of the SiBCN ceramic, the initial oxidation temperature of the composites further increased, indicating enhanced oxidation resistance. A careful examination of the TG curves revealed that S30P1 exhibited the highest degree of oxidation, with a loss of 42.41% at an initial oxidation temperature of approximately 700 °C. This suggests that the nonuniform SiBCN distribution, formed by a high precursor concentration and a single filtration cycle, can only partially protect Cf from oxidation [56]. Similarly, the S5P4 sample shows an oxidation loss of 41.37% with an initial oxidation temperature of 700 °C, indicating that despite multiple cycles, the low SiBCN solution concentration provides limited protection. In contrast, the S20P2 and S10P3 samples exhibit lower oxidation losses of 35.41% and 35.22%, respectively, with initial oxidation temperatures of 750 °C, indicating better oxidation resistance. On the basis of the TG results obtained in air, the Cf and Cf/HC samples are almost fully oxidized at 800 °C, whereas S20P2 and S10P3 exhibit superior oxidation resistance. SiBCN effectively acts as a barrier to oxygen diffusion, thereby slowing the overall oxidation rate. These findings demonstrate that a moderate precursor concentration combined with appropriate impregnation‒filtration cycles results in a more uniform ceramic distribution, thereby enhancing the oxidation resistance of the materials.

In addition to their oxidation resistance and mechanical properties, their enhanced thermal insulation performance further broadens the potential applications of microwave absorbers in aerospace environments. To evaluate the thermal insulation, 10-mm-thick samples of S5P4, S10P3, S20P2, and S30P1 were placed on a 300 °C heat source, and their upper surface temperature variations were tracked over time via infrared thermal imaging, as shown in Figs. 6(a)–6(d). Within 2 min, all the samples were gradually warmed, and after 3 min, their upper surfaces reached 98.70, 90.40, 89.10, and 97.20 °C. This results in a substantial temperature difference of approximately 200 °C from the heat source, clearly demonstrating the excellent thermal insulation capability of the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites. To further understand this insulation behavior, thermal conductivity measurements were conducted for the Cf, Cf/HC, and Cf/HC–SiBCN composites (Fig. 6(e)). The untreated carbon fibers exhibited the initial thermal conductivity of 0.138 W·m−1·K−1, which decreased slightly to 0.130 W·m−1·K−1 after the HC coating was added. The introduction of the SiBCN layer results in minor variations, with values of 0.156 W·m−1·K−1 for S5P4, 0.149 W·m−1·K−1 for S10P3, 0.145 W·m−1·K−1 for S20P2, and 0.155 W·m−1·K−1 for S30P1, showing a decreasing trend followed by a slight increase. The low thermal conductivity of the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites results from the combination of the porous structure of the carbon fibers and the inherently low thermal conductivity of the amorphous SiBCN ceramic [33]. Compared with other lightweight carbon fiber composites [19,25,51,54,57,58], the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites exhibit significantly lower densities and thermal conductivities, as shown in Fig. 6(f) and Table S3 in the ESM. Although some aerogel-modified carbon fibers exhibit lower densities (−3) and thermal conductivities (−1·K−1), their compressive specific strength in the z* direction is lower than 13.00 MPa·g−1·cm3, which is far smaller than the value of 29.35 MPa·g−1·cm3 observed in this study (Table S2 in the ESM). Overall, the composite maintains a low density while exhibiting enhanced mechanical properties, improved oxidation resistance compared with that of the original carbon fibers, and low thermal conductivity.

### 3.3 EMW absorption performance

The microwave absorption performance of an absorber is primarily determined by its critical electromagnetic parameters, specifically the complex permittivity (*ε*r = *ε*′ − j*ε′*′) and complex permeability (*μ*r = *μ*′ − j*μ*′′) [8,36,45]. The real parts of these parameters (*ε*′ and *μ*′) represent the capacity to store electric and magnetic energy, respectively, whereas the imaginary parts (*ε′*′ and *μ′*′) correspond to the capacity to dissipate electric and magnetic energy [30]. In this study, because the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites contain no ferromagnetic components, their complex permeability was assumed to be unity (*μ*r = 1) [35]. Figure 7 shows the complex permittivity of the composites prepared with different numbers of impregnation‒filtration cycles. As illustrated in Fig. 7(a), the real part of the permittivity (*ε*′ initially increases and then decreases as the number of cycles decreases. The average *ε*′ values followed the order S20P2 > S10P3 > S30P1 > S5P4. Notably, the S20P2 sample has the highest *ε*′ value, indicating superior electrical energy storage compared with the other samples. This result could be attributed to the more uniform distribution of the SiBCN phase in S20P2, which enhanced the polarization and improved the energy storage capacity [38,59]. In addition, as the frequency increases, *ε*′ for all samples tends to decrease, reflecting typical frequency dispersion behavior, as dipole polarization cannot keep pace with rapidly alternating electric fields [60]. As shown in Fig. 7(b), the imaginary part of the permittivity (*ε*″) indicates the capacity of the materials to dissipate electrical energy. The S20P2 sample has the highest *ε*″ value, suggesting that it possesses the strongest energy dissipation capability and, consequently, enhanced electromagnetic wave absorption capacity. This increase in *ε*″ likely results from the improved dielectric loss owing to the enhanced interfacial polarization and conduction loss mechanisms. The Raman results revealed an increased *I*D/*I*G ratio (1.42 for S20P2), indicating that more defects enhance both polarization and conductive losses by providing more charge accumulation sites and scattering centers [3]. These defects cause irregular conduction pathways, leading to energy conversion into heat. They also act as polarization centers, forming dipoles that rearrange under an external electric field, generating relaxation losses [8]. Additionally, the nonuniform electric field around defects, especially in multiphase materials, intensifies interface polarization, further increasing polarization losses [24,35]. In contrast, samples with fewer or excessive impregnation‒filtration cycles, such as S5P4 and S30P1, have lower *ε*″ values. This can be explained by the uneven distribution of SiBCN on the carbon fibers, which disrupts the conductive carbon fiber network, thereby reducing the overall interfacial area and lowering the dielectric losses. The dielectric loss tangent (tan*δ*e = * ε″/ε′) characterizes the dielectric loss capacity of the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites, as shown in Fig. 7(c) [36]. Among the four composites, S20P2 has the highest maximum and average tanδ*e values, indicating a superior EMW absorption capacity. Distinct peaks in the tan*δ*e curves for all samples are observed at approximately 3, 11, 15, and 17 GHz, suggesting that polarization relaxation phenomena are induced by the SiBCN phase. These results further support the conclusion that SiBCN enhances the ability of composites to dissipate EMW energy through polarization and conduction losses [47]. The polarization loss behavior of the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites during microwave energy absorption was further analyzed via Cole‒Cole semicircles. According to Debye theory, each semicircle corresponds to a distinct relaxation process, which can be expressed as Eq. (3) [24]:

Each semicircle in the Cole‒Cole plots corresponds to a distinct Debye relaxation process [27]. As shown in Fig. 7(d), the *ε*′–*ε′*′ curves for S5P4, S10P3, S20P2, and S30P1 over the 2–18 GHz range reveal multiple semicircles for all four composites. The Raman results indicate that the *I*D/*I*G ratio increases and then decreases across the samples (1.22 for S5P4, 1.28 for S10P3, 1.42 for S20P2, and 1.21 for S30P1). The increase in the *I*D/*I*G ratio reflects a higher defect density, which contributes to the multiple polarization relaxation processes observed in the Cole‒Cole plots. Additionally, the heterogeneous interfaces within these materials, such as Cf–HC, HC–air, HC–SiBCN, and SiBCN–air, also promote interfacial polarization [45]. In addition, the presence of numerous pores and the resulting multireflection effect increase the number of EMW transmission paths [34]. Consequently, the improved EMW absorption of the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites is due primarily to their unique microstructure and the synergistic interactions among the Cf, HC, and SiBCN phases, which together promote polarization relaxation, conductive loss, and multireflection.

The RL value is a key metric for determining the microwave absorption capacity of an absorber. Typically, absorbers with a minimum RL value of less than −10 dB are considered suitable for basic engineering applications [24]. As shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM, the untreated Cf preform achieves the RLmin of approximately −6.77 dB, whereas Cf/HC reaches approximately −10.33 dB, indicating limited EMW absorption capabilities. In sharp contrast, Figs. 8(a) and 8(b) demonstrate that all the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites efficiently attenuate the EMW energy, as their RLmin values are all less than −10 dB. Specifically, the S5P4 sample achieves the* RL*min of −22.36 dB at a thickness of 2.62 mm and a frequency of 8.62 GHz, with EAB of 5 GHz (12.98–17.98 GHz) at a thickness of 1.57 mm. Notably, the EAB of S20P2 fully encompasses the Ku-band and extends to half of the X-band, highlighting its superior EMW absorption properties. These results confirm that the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites provide robust EMW absorption with broad, effective absorption bandwidths. By selecting an appropriate precursor concentration and a number of impregnation‒filtration cycles, the SiBCN distribution becomes more uniform, leading to consistent and effective interfacial polarization. Additionally, optimizing the number of cycles creates multiple interfaces between Cf, HC, and SiBCN, which enhance interfacial polarization and dielectric loss. The graphitic carbon within the carbon fibers formed a network that improved the dielectric loss [24]. Among these samples, S20P2 demonstrates the best overall EMW absorption performance, achieving an ideal combination of interfacial polarization and dielectric loss.

From the above results, selecting an appropriate precursor concentration and number of impregnation‒filtration cycles is crucial for optimizing the EMW absorption performance of the composites. Previous studies have emphasized that minimizing surface reflection allows the EMW to penetrate deeper into the materials, thereby maximizing the energy dissipation within the absorber and reducing further reflection or transmission [25,35,45]. For optimal EMW absorption, it is essential that the impedance of the materials closely matches the characteristic impedance of free space. When these two impedances are equal, surface reflection is minimized, approaching near-zero reflection [8]. The impedance of an absorber, which is typically determined by its complex dielectric constant and permeability, can be evaluated via impedance matching. The average attenuation constants for S10P3 and S20P2 are clearly similar, and both are greater than those of S5P4 and S30P1. Because a larger attenuation constant indicates a greater ability to diminish the strength of electromagnetic waves as they propagate through the materials, these findings suggest that S10P3 and S20P2 provide more effective EMW attenuation.

A comparison of the EMW absorption performance of the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites developed in this study with those of other recently reported EMW absorbers is presented in Fig. 9(a) and detailed in Table S4 in the ESM [4,6,16,24,25,35,39,47,59−68]. However, the paraffin method, to some extent, disrupted the material’s structure, leading to unstable conductivity and reduced absorption performance [40,41]. The Cf/HC–SiBCN composites prepared in this work via a simple impregnation‒filtration method exhibited superior EMW absorption properties. Among them, the S20P2 composite exhibits the best performance, achieving RLmin of −58.13 dB and a broad effective absorption bandwidth of 7.38 GHz—outperforming many previously reported absorbers. As shown in Fig. 9(b), the carbon fibers in the composites form a continuous conductive network that dissipates EMW energy through Ohmic loss, converting it into resistive heat and increasing dielectric loss. Simultaneously, SiBCN introduces heterogeneous interfaces at phase boundaries (e.g., Cf/HC and HC/SiBCN), which promote multiple internal reflections of EMWs. These interfaces induce interfacial polarization that further enhances EMW attenuation through improved dielectric loss. Additionally, the finer pore structure of SiBCN enhances reflection losses. Optimizing the precursor concentration and number of impregnation‒filtration cycles improved the distribution of SiBCN, enhancing impedance matching with free space and reducing surface reflection. This results in more efficient EMW penetration and absorption. Consequently, the Cf/HC–SiBCN composites, exemplified by the S20P2 sample, represent highly promising candidates for advanced EMW absorption applications.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, low-density Cf/HC–SiBCN composites exhibiting multiple properties, including tunable EMW absorption, enhanced mechanical strength, good high-temperature stability, and low thermal conductivity, were successfully fabricated via a simple impregnation‒filtration process. By optimizing the precursor concentration and number of impregnation‒filtration cycles, a hierarchical Cf/HC/SiBCN structure was formed, ensuring uniform SiBCN coverage at the fiber overlap joints. This architecture, with a density of approximately 0.32 g·cm−3, endows the composite with notable mechanical strength, with the best performance reaching flexural strengths of 14.75±0.43 MPa in the *xy*-direction, 14.45±0.66 MPa in the *z*-direction, and compressive strength of 9.36±0.20 MPa in the *z*-direction, while retaining more than 64.78% of its mass above 1200 °C in air, indicating superior high-temperature stability. A low thermal conductivity, as low as 0.145 W·m−1·K−1, further provides effective thermal insulation ability. Notably, the composite prepared with the 20 wt% SiBCN precursor solution and two impregnation‒filtration cycles achieves RLmin of −58.13 dB and EAB of 7.38 GHz. This combination of robust mechanical strength, excellent thermal stability, low thermal conductivity, and tunable EMW absorption underscores the potential of Cf/HC–SiBCN composites as multifunctional materials, offering a unique balance of properties that meet diverse application needs.
