## 1 Introduction

With the rapid development of wireless communication and electronic devices, the escalating problems of electromagnetic (EM) interference and EM pollution have become severe because these unwanted EMs pose threats to the reliable operation of equipment, information security, and even human health [1−3]. High-performance EM-absorbing materials (MAMs) can dissipate incident EM energy by converting it into thermal or other energy forms, which can solve the aforementioned threats [4−8]. In general, ideal MAMs should possess a matched impedance and high attenuation coefficient, in which the former ensures that the EM wave can effectively penetrate into the interior of the material rather than be reflected at the surface, and that the latter can consume the EM energy by means of polarization loss and magnetic loss [9−12]. On the basis of the relationship between morphology design and absorption performance, various dielectric‒magnetic composites with special morphologies, such as core/yolk-shell structures [13,14], hollow microspheres [15,16], and hierarchical structures [17], have been designed and employed as efficient EM absorbents owing to the synergistic effects of EM and multi-interfacial polarization.

Among these composites, carbon nanofibers (CNFs) prepared by electrospinning and subsequent pyrolysis have emerged as promising candidates for realizing lightweight EM wave absorption because of their continuous long-range conductive networks, tunable dielectric properties, and design flexibility [18−22]. With the aim of regulating the impedance characteristics and introducing magnetic domains, the integration of pre-synthesized metal‒organic frameworks (MOFs) precursors into the electrospinning process has attracted attention for the fabrication of magnetic CNFs [23,24], and the commonly used MOF precursors include coordinated Co2+/Fe3+/Ni2+ species [25−28]. After the carbonization process, these Co2+/Fe3+/Ni2+ ions are reduced to magnetic particles that are completely wrapped by carbon layers and form abundant heterointerfaces, which are beneficial for the optimization of impedance matching and the enhancement of dipolar polarization, resulting in enhanced EM wave absorption [29−31]. However, the introduction of MOF precursors usually induces phase separation during spinning with the polyacrylonitrile (PAN) matrix, inhibiting the formation of heterointerfaces. In addition, these magnetic particles with large diameters are completely encapsulated in the framework of the carbon skeleton, so the surface of CNFs with high conductivity strongly reflects the incident EM waves, resulting in unmatched impedance and strong reflection [32]. Compared with the aforementioned electrospinning involving pre-synthesized MOF precursors, the strategy of post-coordination after the electrospinning process provides an integrated step for incorporating magnetic particles and avoiding the phase separation of dielectric‒magnetic heterointerfaces. Importantly, the growth of MOF precursors has been greatly restricted owing to the confined coordination effect, and these post-coordinated MOF precursors with small diameters anchored on the surface can be converted into small magnetic particles to balance the surface impedance and induce strong interfacial polarization [33]. For example, Xu *et al*. [34] successfully fabricated Ni‒Fe/CNF composites with optimized heteroatoms via hydrothermal epitaxial growth of hollow Ni‒Fe Prussian blue analogs on PAN fibers. The introduction of heteroatoms effectively modulates the electronic structure and interfacial polarization, and the synergistic effect of heterointerfaces and conductive networks improves the impedance matching; thus, a strong absorption of −56.7 dB and a broad absorption bandwidth of 6.7 GHz are achieved due to the enhanced dipole and defect-induced polarization. With the aim of understanding the effect of the structural evolution of MOFs on high-performance EM absorption, Cao and coworkers [35] proposed *ex situ* and *in situ* electrospinning strategies to synthesize Co-CNF composites. Owing to the small Co nanoparticles uniformly distributed on the carbon surface, the in-Co-CNF composites presented a lower reflection loss (*R*L) of −48.6 dB than the ex-Co-CNFs did, with an *R*L of −20.5 dB, due to the improved dipole/interfacial polarization and enhanced conduction loss.

Inspired by the aforementioned findings, herein, a confined-coordination growth strategy has been proposed to reduce the diameter of MOF precursors after electrospinning. After subsequent pyrolysis, these post-coordinated Co-MOF precursors are converted into small Co nanoparticles, which are anchored mainly on the surface of the CNFs, favoring the formation of dielectric‒magnetic heterointerfaces and triggering strong interfacial polarization. Moreover, the sublimation of Zn2+ species from MOF precursors inevitably produces structural defects to regulate the impedance characteristics and introduce defect polarization. As a result, the obtained CNFs with small Co nanoparticles and structural defects exhibit superior lightweight and highly efficient EM wave absorption: the minimum *R*L reaches −51.0 dB, and the effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) reaches as high as 7.33 GHz.

### 2.1 Synthesis of Co<sup>2+</sup>-PAN, Zn<sup>2+</sup>-PAN, and Co<sup>2+</sup>/Zn<sup>2+</sup>-PAN NFs

In a typical process, the synthesis of Co2+/Zn2+-PAN NFs is as follows. PAN (1.0 g) was added to 8 mL of N,N-Dimeythylformamide (DMF) at room temperature, and the mixture was stirred for at least 12 h until it became transparent and clear. After that, 1.5 g of C10H16CoO4 and 1.53 g of C10H14ZnO4 were dissolved in the three as-prepared solutions and stirred for 8 h. The well-mixed solution was loaded into a syringe with a 20-gauge needle. The modulation parameters were as follows: negative voltmeter, 2.59 kV; positive voltmeter, 7.24 kV; feeding rate, 0.06 mm/min; temperature, 25.4 °C; humidity, 81.2%; and distance from the spinneret to the receiver plate, 15 cm. When the spinning process was complete, the products were placed in an oven at 60 °C to dry. For comparison, Co2+-PAN and Zn2+-PAN NFs were prepared under the same conditions by using only 1.5 g of C10H16CoO4 and 1.53 g of C10H14ZnO4, respectively.

### 2.2 Synthesis of Co-MOFs@PAN, Zn-MOFs@PAN, and Co/Zn-MOFs@PAN NFs

Briefly, to promote the growth of MOF precursors, the obtained Co2+-PAN, Zn2+-PAN, and Co2+/Zn2+-PAN NFs were immersed in ethanol solution containing 2 g of 2-methylimidazole (2-MI) for 2 h, and the coordination between Co2+/Zn2+ ions and 2-MI led to the formation of smaller Co/Zn-MOF precursors than anchored on the surface of the PAN NFs.

### 2.3 Synthesis of Co@CNFs, porous CNFs (PCNFs), and Co@PCNFs

The obtained Co-MOFs@PAN, Zn-MOFs@PAN, and Co/Zn-MOFs@PAN NFs were pre-oxidized at 250 °C for 2 h to remove the organic solvents and then annealed at 800 °C for 2 h under an Ar atmosphere. The corresponding products were designated Co@CNFs, PCNFs and Co@PCNFs, respectively. The detailed synthetic process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

### 2.4 Characterizations

The morphologies and microstructures were characterized via a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; Quanta 600FEG, FEI, USA) and a field emission transmission electron microscope (FETEM; Tecnai F30G 2, FEI, USA). The crystal structure was examined via an X-ray powder diffractometer (XRD; D8-Advance, Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 0.15406 nm). Raman spectroscopy was performed with a Raman spectroscope (WITec Alpha300R, Germany). The surface chemical characteristics were investigated via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB 250, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The thermogravimetric (TG) curves were tested in the range of 30–800 °C under air conditions at a heating rate of 15 °C/min. The static magnetic properties were recorded by a cryogenic-CFMS-14T multifunctional physical property measurement system. The *R*L and radar cross section simulation results are presented in the supporting information. To determine the EM parameters of the samples, all samples were prepared by mixing 10 wt% composites with paraffin wax and pressing them into toroidal rings (inner and outer radius *φ*in = 3.0 mm, *φ*out = 7.0 mm).

## 3 Results and discussion

The morphologies of the Co-MOFs@PAN, Zn-MOFs@PAN, and Co/Zn-MOFs@PAN NFs were characterized via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images, as shown in Fig. 2. The surface of the PAN NFs clearly becomes rough after the post-coordination process, and these MOF precursors with small diameters are tightly anchored on their surface; the diameters are measured to be approximately 84, 153, and 118 nm, respectively. In addition, the elemental mapping images display the homogeneous distributions of C, N, O, Zn, and Co on the surface of the fibrous frameworks, demonstrating the structural uniformity of the MOF precursors. As shown in Figs. S1–S3 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), the XRD patterns of Co-MOFs@PAN, Zn-MOFs@PAN, and Co/Zn-MOFs@PAN NFs match well with the simulated patterns of ZIF-67 and ZIF-8, illustrating that these MOFs are ZIF-67 and ZIF-8 precursors. In Fig. S4 in the ESM, the TG curves indicate that these three NFs exhibit minimal mass loss (less than 5%) below 300 °C, which is attributed to the removal of adsorbed water. As the temperature increased, all the samples underwent significant thermal decomposition of the PAN NFs and the organic ligands in the MOF precursors. When carbonized at 800 °C, the Co-MOFs@PAN, Zn-MOFs@PAN, and Co/Zn-MOFs@PAN NFs were completely converted into Co@CNFs, PCNFs, and Co@PCNFs, respectively. Compared with the traditional electrospinning strategy, which includes pre-synthesized MOF precursors, these post-coordinated MOF precursors are tightly anchored on the surface, and their derivatives can decrease the surface conductivity and balance the impedance characteristics. Moreover, the diameter of the MOF precursors was restricted owing to the confined-coordination effect, resulting in strong interfacial polarization. The speculated mechanism of the confined-coordinated growth is illustrated in Fig. 2(d). After the electrospinning process, the Co2+/Zn2+ species are immobilized in the PAN matrix. Owing to the strong coordination ability between Co2+/Zn2+ species and 2-MI, these Co2+/Zn2+ species migrate outward to couple with 2-MI when Co-MOFs@PAN, Zn-MOFs@PAN, and Co/Zn-MOFs@PAN NFs are immersed in a solution of 2-MI. However, the growth of MOF precursors has been greatly restricted owing to the confined-coordination growth effect during the post-coordination process. Thus, the diameter of the MOF precursors was smaller than that in the coordination process in a free system.

After the pyrolysis process, the Co2+ species are reduced into Co nanoparticles, and the sublimation of Zn2+ species produces structural defects. The morphologies of the Co@CNFs, PCNFs, and Co@PCNFs are shown in Fig. 3. There are many Co nanoparticles anchored on the surface of the Co@CNFs (Fig. 3(a)) and many structural defects on the surface of the PCNFs (Fig. 3(b)). For the Co@PCNFs in Fig. 3(c), both Co nanoparticles and structural defects can be observed. The corresponding TEM images match well with the morphologies of the SEM images. The HRTEM image reveals that the lattice spacing of the Co nanoparticles is approximately 0.18 nm for Co@CNFs and Co@PCNFs, which corresponds to the (200) plane of metallic Co, confirming the successful reduction from Co2+ species to metallic Co nanoparticles. However, the PCNFs and Co@PCNFs possess highly porous frameworks owing to the sublimation of Zn2+ species during the pyrolysis process. Interestingly, some residual Zn2+ species are likely converted into single Zn atoms that are stabilized on the carbon skeleton [36], and this speculation is supported by the Zn elemental mapping images. Compared with the traditional electrospinning strategy, which includes pre-synthesized MOF precursors, in which MOF derivatives with large diameters are mainly distributed in the internal region of CNFs, the surface of CNFs with high conductivity strongly reflects the incident EM waves. In this post-coordinated strategy, the growth of MOF precursors was greatly restricted, and these small MOF precursors were anchored on the surface of the CNFs. The corresponding derivatives could balance the surface impedance and induce strong interfacial polarization, yielding superior EM wave absorption.

The crystal structures of the Co@CNF, PCNF, and Co@PCNF samples were characterized via the XRD patterns in Fig. 4(a). Clearly, there are three diffraction peaks at approximately 44.2°, 51.4°, and 76.2° for Co@CNFs and Co@PCNFs, which are attributed to the (111), (200), and (220) planes of Co (PDF#15-0806), respectively, suggesting the presence of Co nanoparticles. However, diffraction peaks related to Zn are not detected, demonstrating the presence of single Zn atoms [37]. Moreover, all the samples exhibit a broad diffraction peak at approximately 26°, corresponding to the (002) plane of the carbon skeleton. As presented in Fig. 4(b), all samples display two obvious characteristic peaks: the peak at 1350 cm−1 is related to structural disorder, such as defects and vacancies in carbon frameworks, and the peak at 1580 cm−1 arises from the in-plane vibration of sp2-hybridized carbon atoms. Thus, the intensity ratio of the D peak to the G peak (I*D/*I*G) is employed to evaluate the graphitization degree of the carbon component. For Co@CNFs and Co@PCNFs, reduced Co nanoparticles act as catalytic sites to produce graphitic carbon; thus, their *I*D/*I*G values are lower than those of PCNFs. In addition, the sublimation of Zn2+ species produces structural defects in Co@PCNFs; therefore, the corresponding *I*D/*I*G value is greater than that of Co@CNFs. Figure 4(c) shows the hysteresis loops of Co@CNFs and Co@PCNFs. Because of the small particles, the saturation magnetization values are only 17.1 emu/g for Co@CNFs and 5.2 emu/g for Co@PCNFs, and the decreased saturation magnetization of Co@PCNFs is ascribed to the incorporation of structural defects and nonmagnetic Zn atoms. The porous characteristics and specific surface areas of the Co@CNFs, PCNFs, and Co@PCNFs were analyzed via N2 adsorption‒desorption isotherms, as shown in Fig. 4(d). The typical type IV isotherm hysteresis indicates the presence of mesopores, and the sharp increasing trend at low pressure suggests the coexistence of micropores [38]. Both results match well with the pore size distribution in Fig. 4(e). Specifically, the specific surface area is 309.5 m2/g for Co@CNFs, 517.7 m2/g for PCNFs, and 616.7 m2/g for Co@PCNFs. For Co@PCNFs, the high specific surface area originating from structural defects could decrease the surface conductivity and regulate the band gap, and the presence of magnetic Co nanoparticles could introduce additional magnetic loss, resulting in matched impedance. XPS was conducted to investigate the elemental compositions of the Co@CNFs, PCNFs, and Co@PCNFs. As shown in Fig. 4(f), the survey spectrum confirms the presence of C, N, O, Co, and Zn. Figure S5 in the ESM clearly shows that the C 1s spectrum can be deconvoluted into C−C/C=C, C−N, C−O, and C=O bonds, respectively, demonstrating the presence of heteroatoms and oxygen-containing functional groups, which could act as dipole centers to respond to EM waves and induce dipolar polarization [39]. The high-resolution N 1s spectra can be fitted to pyridinic N, pyrrolic N, and graphitic N, as shown in Fig. 4(g) and Fig. S6 in the ESM. Graphic N is beneficial for increasing conductivity, whereas pyridinic and pyrrolic N promote relaxation polarization. In the Co 2p spectra (Fig. 4(h) and Fig. S7 in the ESM), the prominent peaks at 779.6 and 794.8 eV are assigned to the Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2 spin orbitals of metallic Co0, illustrating that Co2+ species have been completely reduced. With respect to the Zn 2p spectra (Fig. 4(i) and Fig. S8 in the ESM), the two peaks at 1021.9 and 1045 eV correspond to Zn 2p3/2 and Zn 2p1/2, respectively, and the binding energies are different from those of ZnO and metal Zn0, demonstrating the presence of single Zn atoms.

To illustrate the cooperative effects of small Co nanoparticles and structural defects on EM wave absorption, the maximum R*L and EAB of Co@CNFs, PCNFs, and Co@PCNFs were evaluated via a coaxial method with 10 wt% fillers, and the results are shown in Fig. 5. Because of the EM synergistic effects, the *R*L value of Co@CNFs is −39.8 dB at 2.5 mm, and its EAB reaches 7.12 GHz when the thickness is 2.0 mm. In contrast, the absence of magnetic Co nanoparticles leads to an inferior absorption intensity for the PCNFs, and the *R*L value of −15.4 dB and the EAB of 2.32 GHz are attributed mainly to dipolar polarization. Combining the cooperative effects of small Co nanoparticles and structural defects, it is anticipated that Co@PCNFs will exhibit remarkable EM wave absorption performance. At a thickness of 2.9 mm, the *R*L value reaches −51.0 dB, and the EAB achieves 7.33 GHz over the 2−18 GHz frequency range, covering the whole Ku band. This is primarily attributed to the volatilization of Zn species, which leave abundant micropores and mesopores that optimize the impedance matching. Additionally, uniformly dispersed small-sized Co nanoparticles within the carbon matrix exhibit a larger specific surface area and higher surface energy, thereby demonstrating greater magnetic anisotropy. Simultaneously, this uniform dispersion creates abundant “Co−C” heterointerfaces, which generate interfacial polarization. Radar cross-section (RCS) simulations were performed to evaluate the EM wave absorption performance over the angular range of −90° Figs. 5(j)−5(l), the simulation results reveal that the RCS reduction values of Co@CNFs, PCNFs, and Co@PCNFs are lower than those of the pure Al plates, and Co@PCNFs have the highest RCS reduction of 22.84 dB·m2 at 0°. These results suggest that these absorbent coatings, especially those of Co@PCNFs, could effectively reduce the radar scattering intensities of pure Al plates, highlighting the strong ability to reduce radar detectability.

To clarify the mechanism of EM wave absorption, the EM parameters of Co@CNFs, PCNFs, and Co@PCNFs were investigated, as shown in Fig. 6. Generally, the impedance characteristics and attenuation capability are two critical parameters that simultaneously determine the final absorption performance, and ideal MAMs should possess a matched impedance and high attenuation constant [40−43]. Figures 6(a)−6(c) show the impedance matching of Co@CNFs, PCNFs, and Co@PCNFs. For Co@PCNFs, these reduced small-sized Co nanoparticles encapsulated in the carbon matrix produce abundant heterointerfaces, and the generation of structural defects decreases the conductivity and regulates the impedance. In addition, these defects could act as active sites to respond to the incident EM wave and induce additional defect polarization [44,45]. Owing to the aforementioned merits, the satisfactory |*Z*in/*Z*0| value of approximately 1 for Co@PCNFs results in strong EM wave absorption capacity and a broad absorption bandwidth; thus, the strip area in the range of 0.8−1.2 for Co@PCNFs is larger than that for Co@CNFs and PCNFs. However, although the attenuation constant of Co@CNFs is greater than that of Co@PCNFs, as shown in Fig. S9 in the ESM, the inferior absorption performance further implies that the impedance characteristics and attenuation capability simultaneously determine the final performance [46−48]. Figures 6(d)−6(f) show the EM parameters of Co@CNFs, PCNFs, and Co@PCNFs, including the relative complex permittivity, relative complex permeability, and dielectric/magnetic tangent loss, respectively. Specifically, the *ε*′ values decrease with increasing frequency, which can be explained by the fact that the polarization vibration cannot keep up with the frequency and tends to lag, which is referred to as the frequency-dispersion phenomenon. However, the *ε*′′ values fluctuate from 2 to 18 GHz, suggesting the presence of multiple polarization resonances. Moreover, the higher *ε*′ and *ε*′′ values of the Co@CNFs are attributed to the perfect conductive networks without obvious defects. Because Co@CNFs exhibit the highest saturation magnetization, the *μ'* and *μ*″ values are greater than those of PCNFs and Co@PCNFs. As shown in Fig. S10 in the ESM, the *μ*″(*μ*)−2*f*−1 values of Co@CNFs, PCNFs, and Co@PCNFs fluctuate over the measured frequency range, implying that ferromagnetic resonance and eddy current loss contribute to magnetic loss at the same time [49]. Interestingly, the dielectric loss is greater than the magnetic loss, illustrating that dielectric loss is the dominant mechanism for EM wave attenuation. In this study, the growth of post-coordinated MOF precursors was greatly restricted owing to the confined-coordinated growth effect; thus, the diameter of the derivatives was smaller than that of the derivatives obtained from the coordination process in a free system. Compared with large Co particles, these small Co nanoparticles with high nano-curvatures can effectively induce a strong electric field on their external surface, which is beneficial for increasing dielectric loss (Fig. 6(g)) [50,51].

As illustrated in Fig. 7(a), the as-synthesized Co@PCNFs exhibit the best comprehensive EM wave absorption compared with that of Co@CNFs and PCNFs because of the cooperative effect of small Co nanoparticles and structural defects. For practical applications, the specific R*L (SRL), SRLlt = |*R*L|/(filler loading × thickness), and specific EAB (SEAB), SEABlt = EAB/(filler loading × thickness), were employed to evaluate the absorption performance, as shown in Fig. 7(b). The SRLlt and SEABlt values of Co@PCNFs are clearly greater than those of most reported absorbents obtained by electrospinning, including pre-synthesized MOF precursors, demonstrating that the confined-coordination growth strategy provides integrated insight into optimizing EM wave absorption performance [52−61]. The possible EM wave attenuation mechanism is as follows in Fig. 7(c). First, benefiting from the advantage of a high aspect ratio, CNFs with long-range conductive networks facilitate electron migration and hopping, leading to increased conduction loss [62]. Second, these small Co nanoparticles are completely wrapped by carbon layers, favoring the formation of abundant dielectric-magnetic heterointerfaces and triggering strong interfacial polarization [63,64]. Third, the sublimation of Zn2+ species produces vacancies or structural defects, effectively reducing the materials’ overall dielectric constant. This phenomenon promotes multiple reflections and scattering, thereby regulating impedance characteristics and enhancing defect polarization [65,66]. Moreover, these small Co nanoparticles not only cause magnetic loss to contribute to EM wave absorption but also induce a strong electric field to respond to the incident EM wave, resulting in increased absorption performance [67].

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, CNFs with small Co nanoparticles and structural defects were successfully fabricated via a confined-coordinated growth strategy. The results demonstrate that the post-coordination process can effectively reduce the particle size of MOF precursors and that these small Co particles can balance the surface impedance and promote interfacial electric field polarization. Moreover, the sublimation of Zn species introduces structural defects and triggers defect polarization. Owing to these cooperative merits, the prepared CNFs achieve a minimum *R*L of −51.0 dB and an effective absorption bandwidth of 7.33 GHz at a lower loading of 10 wt%, and they display greater SRL and specific effective absorption bandwidth than most absorbents prepared by the electrospinning strategy, including pre-synthesized MOF precursors. The CNFs developed in this work can be further applied in energy storage systems and sensing platforms in future research, leveraging their synergistic advantages of good conductivity and porous structure.
