## 1 Introduction

Energy and environmental issues necessitate the development of sustainable materials and technologies [1,2]. Various technologies have been presented [3,4], with fuel cell technology receiving significant attention for many years because of its high efficiency and low environmental impact for energy conversion [5]. Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) are preferred over other fuel cell types because of their solid-state structures and fuel selection flexibility [6]. However, the operating temperature for traditional SOFCs is typically high (above 800 °C), causing several issues. For example, if the working temperature is too high, the life of fuel cells is shortened. In addition, the high working temperature creates difficult working conditions for compatible materials. As a result, developing SOFCs that can operate at intermediate temperatures has become a top priority for SOFC development [6,7].

Different intermediate-temperature SOFCs have been proposed in recent decades [8], and proton-conducting SOFCs (H-SOFCs), which are SOFCs that use proton-conducting electrolytes, have emerged as a popular study area in the intermediate-temperature SOFC family [9]. Proton-conducting oxide electrolytes have lower activation energies than the most frequently utilized oxygen-ion-conducting oxide electrolytes, making them particularly suitable for low-temperature applications [10,11]. Furthermore, proton-conducting oxides exhibit favorable ionic conductivities at intermediate temperatures, allowing the cells’ ohmic resistance to be reduced, hence increasing fuel cell performance [12]. However, regular cathodes are inappropriate for H-SOFCs because their kinetics grow sluggishly as the working temperature decreases [13,14]. As a result, the development of new and high-performance cathode materials for H-SOFCs has been the community’s main priority [15,16].

Many efforts have been undertaken to create new cathode materials over the last few decades, some of which function exceptionally well [17,18]. Notably, many of these high-performance cathodes have cobalt as a major component [19]. Although using cobalt in the cathode helps increase oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) activity, cobalt-containing cathodes have several drawbacks, including high cobalt prices, cobalt evaporation, and significant thermal expansion, which must be addressed [20]. In comparison, ferrite cathode materials exhibit low overpotentials in H-SOFCs [21]. A recent study revealed that ferrite cathodes with correct tailoring can perform exceptionally well in H-SOFCs [22,23]. All of this evidence points to the high potential for the use of ferrite cathodes in H-SOFCs. SrFeO3 is a traditional cathode material for SOFCs, although it performs poorly in H-SOFCs. A recent work reported the use of the high-valence element Nb to modify SrFeO3, successfully improving the cathode performance of H-SOFCs [24], implying that the use of high-valence elements to modify SrFeO3 could be a practical approach to improve its performance.

Notably, the conventional strategy normally uses one dopant or two dopants to tailor the cathode materials for enhanced performance [25−27], which also applies to the modification of SrFeO3-based materials. However, each doping element has its own influence on the properties, and sometimes, the synergistic effect can be identified by using multiple dopants [28,29]. Thus, it would be interesting to explore the combination of a high-valence doping strategy with a multielement doping method to customize SrFeO3, to significantly improve the performance of SrFeO3-based cathodes for H-SOFCs. In this work, Nb, Ta, Mo, and W elements are utilized to tailor SrFeO3 to determine the effect of each element on cathode performance. The multielement doped oxide is then formed by combining equal molar ratios of Nb, Ta, Mo, and W. The structure, diffusion ability of charge carriers, and electrochemical performance of the cathode oxides with and without multielement doping are investigated and compared.

### 2.1 Materials preparation, characterization, and electrochemical catalytic activity

A solid-state process was used to make SrFe0.9X0.1O3 (X = Nb, Ta, Mo, and W) oxides. Metal carbonates or metal oxides were fully mixed via the ball milling process for 24 h with ethanol as the solvent. The resulting mixture was dried, and the powder was collected, followed by calcination at 1300 °C for 6 h to produce the target powders. The Nb, Ta, Mo, and W-doped SrFeO3 samples were designated SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW, respectively. To manufacture the multielement-doped oxide, equal molar ratios of Nb, Ta, Mo, and W were utilized, resulting in the oxide formula SrFe0.9Nb0.025Ta0.025Mo0.025W0.025O3, also known as multielement-doped SrFeO3 (ME-SFO). ME-SFO oxide was also produced via a solid-state reaction technique, with the precursor calcined at 1300 °C for 6 h to yield the target phase. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to determine the phase purity of the materials. The morphology and elemental distribution of the as-prepared material were investigated via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) combined with energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS). The electrical conductivity relaxation (ECR) method was used to characterize the proton and oxygen transport capacities of the materials [30]. Dense SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, SFW, and ME-SFO bars were prepared, and the direct-current (DC) four-probe approach was used to measure the conductivity of the oxides. The conductivity change can be measured by rapidly switching the atmosphere from air to moist air or changing the O2 partial pressure. The relaxation time can represent the rate of proton and oxygen diffusion. Symmetrical cell experiments were used to evaluate the electrochemical performance of the cathode catalysts. SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, SFW, and ME-SFO cathodes were tested, and they were painted on both sides of dense BaCe0.7Zr0.1Y0.2O3−*δ* (BCZY) proton-conducting electrolyte pellets, establishing the cell structure of the cathode catalyst/BCZY electrolyte/cathode catalyst. Before depositing the cathode on the electrolyte, the electrolyte surface was polished with sandpaper, which can increase the roughness of the electrolyte and thus increase the degree of contact between the cathode and the electrolyte. The as-prepared symmetrical cells were evaluated at various temperatures under wet air conditions via electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The frequency range for the EIS test was from 1 MHz to 0.1 Hz.

### 2.2 Fuel cell performance

To assess the performance of the cathodes for H-SOFCs, BCZY proton-conducting electrolyte-based H-SOFCs were constructed via a copressing and cosintering technique [31,32]. The BCZY powder was mixed with NiO to make the anode powder, and 20 wt% starch was added to the anode as the pore former, which was then pressed in a die under a pressure of approximately 30 MPa to form the anode substrate. The BCZY electrolyte powder was then placed on the anode substrate and pressed together with the anode under a pressure of approximately 50 MPa to form a green anode/electrolyte bilayer. The bilayer was sintered at 1350 °C for 6 h to densify the electrolyte, resulting in a half-cell. The cathode powder was mixed with terpinol and ethyl-cellulose at a weight ratio of 5 : 5 : 1, forming the cathode ink, in which ethyl-cellulose was used as the pore former. The cathode ink was then brush-printed on the electrolyte surface and co-fired at 900 °C to form complete cells. These fuel cells were tested under working conditions, using humidified H2 as fuel and static air as an oxidant. The electrochemical performance of the fuel cells was assessed via an electrochemical workstation. The resistances of the cells were determined via the EIS technique. The operational stability of fuel cells was tested by providing a constant current density and measuring the cell voltage over time. In addition, the current (*I*)–voltage (*V*) curve and EIS results of the fuel cell after the long-term test were determined and compared with those of the original state. The cycling stability of the fuel cells was characterized by cycling the cell at a temperature range from 600 to 700 °C under a constant applied voltage. The current density of the cell during the cycling process was recorded. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to observe the microstructure of the fuel cells after the tests.

### 3.1 Material structures

Figure 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of the synthesized SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, SFW, and ME-SFO. All the materials have pure phase structures with no trace of impurities. A relatively high calcination temperature of 1300 °C was used to prepare the powder, as the use of lower calcination temperatures cannot produce a pure phase powder. Figure S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) shows the XRD patterns of ME-SFO samples fired at different temperatures. Evident impurities can be observed when the calcination temperature of 1100 °C is used. When the calcination temperature reached 1200 °C, some impurity phases remained, suggesting that even a temperature of 1200 °C was not sufficient to make a pure-phase ME-SFO material. The pure-phase ME-SFO material can be obtained when the calcination temperature reaches 1300 °C. There are slight XRD peak shifts between pure-phase SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW, which are most likely caused by the differing ionic radii of Nb, Ta, Mo, and W. However, the peaks for ME-SFO clearly shift to lower angles, indicating that the lattice was expanded via the multielement doping strategy. The XRD results were refined to obtain detailed information about the crystal structure of the samples. The Rietveld refinement results in Figs. 1(b)–1(f) indicate that the materials are cubic structures in the space group *Pm** 3¯m*, with the lattice parameters (*a* = *b* = *c*) of 3.90, 3.90, 3.92, 3.92, and 3.96 Å for SFNb, SFTa, SEMo, SFW, and ME-SFO, respectively. The increased lattice parameter of ME-SFO cannot be explained just by the ionic radii of the dopants, as ME-SFO has a larger lattice than do SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW, showing that the multielement doping strategy influences the material’s lattice volume. It has been reported that multielement doping can lead to different electronic structures and lattice distortions than those of normal oxides [33], resulting in unique features [34]. Thus, the increased lattice parameter for ME-SFO is most likely related to multielement doping.

### 3.2 Oxygen vacancies

Because oxygen vacancies (VO) are known to be critical for the mobility of oxygen ions and cathode activity [35], the VO content in various oxides is estimated. Figures 3(a)–3(e) show the XPS O 1s curves for SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, SFW, and ME-SFO. The ratio of oxygen species associated with surface oxygen vacancies (O22− or O−) to lattice oxygen (Olat) has been reported to represent the surface VO content of an oxide [36,37]. The ratios are 0.66, 0.48, 0.96, 0.79, and 1.13, indicating that ME-SFO has the highest VO content, which potentially increases the cathode catalytic activity.

### 3.3 Diffusion abilities of charge carriers

The improved VO content may also increase the oxygen and proton diffusion abilities. The transportation of oxygen ions relies on oxygen vacancies, and an increased VO content could improve the migration ability of oxygen ions. In addition, the formation of oxygen vacancies is the prefactor for the formation of proton defects, which can be expressed with the following equation: * H2O+VO∗∗+OO×⇔2OH∗. Therefore, the increased VO content in ME-SFO can increase the number of proton defects in the oxide, thus potentially enhancing proton diffusion. To test this hypothesis, the ECR experiment was conducted to further examine oxygen and proton diffusion [38,39]. The conductivity of a material is regulated by its charge carrier, and changes in the oxygen partial pressure can affect its conductivity, whereas the relaxation time can reflect the oxygen diffusion and surface exchange capacities of a material [40,41]. Figure 4(a) shows the ECR curves of SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, SFW, and ME-SFO in an environment with abrupt changes in oxygen partial pressure from air (21% O2) to 50% O2. The relaxation time of ME-SFO is much shorter than that of SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW, indicating that the multielement doping method results in faster oxygen diffusion and surface exchange rates for SrFeO3-based oxides. The relaxation time followed the trend of SFTa > SFNb > SFW > SFMo > ME-SFO, whereas the VO content followed the trend of SFTa O content can promote the oxygen diffusion and surface exchange. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that VO has a significant influence on electrode ORR activity [42]. Figures 4(b) and 4(c) show the results of fitting the ECR curves to determine the oxygen diffusion coefficient (D*O) and the surface exchange coefficient (*k*O). Instead of compromising the *D*O and *k*O values of SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW, an obvious synergistic effect is observed, with the *D*O and *k*O values of ME-SFO being several times greater than those of the other oxides, implying that the multielement-doped oxide can improve the oxygen diffusion and surface exchange rates compared with the SrFeO3 oxide with only one dopant.

(d) ECR curves for SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, SFW, and ME-SFO tested in an atmosphere abruptly changed from dry to wet air at 600 °C. (e) *D*H and (f) *k*H for SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, SFW, and ME-SFO.

Similar results are observed when proton diffusion and surface exchange rates are measured. Figure 4(d) shows the ECR curves of SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, SFW, and ME-SFO during the transition from dry to wet air (3% H2O). Because of the possibility of proton defects forming as a result of the interaction between VO and H2O, the conductivity changes, and the relaxation time of ME-SFO is shorter than those of the other four materials, implying that the multielement doping design results in faster proton diffusion and surface exchange rates. More notably, unlike the minimal variation in ECR tests caused by changing the oxygen partial pressure, the change in water partial pressure in the atmosphere has a more profound effect on the relaxation time of the tested oxides. By shifting the atmosphere from dry to moist air, the relaxation period for SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW is shown to be a few thousand seconds or even tens of thousands of seconds. The long relaxation period indicates that proton transport in these oxides is weak. In contrast, ME-SFO tested under the same conditions had a relaxation time of approximately 500 s, which is substantially shorter than those of the other oxides, indicating that the proton diffusion rate of ME-SFO is much higher than those of SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW. The proton diffusion coefficient (*D*H) and surface change coefficient (*k*H) can be calculated by fitting the ECR curves, as shown in Figs. 4(e) and 4(f). ME-SFO had much higher *D*H and *k*H values (30.1×10−5 cm2∙s−1 and 17.5×10−4 cm∙s−1) than the other four oxides with a single dopant. The increase is significantly greater than that for *D*O and *k*O, implying that multielement doping may have a greater impact on proton diffusion than on oxygen transport. Notably, SFMo and SFW have very low *D*H and *k*H values, even compared with those of SFNb and SFTa.

### 3.4 Electrochemical catalytic activity of cathode oxides

Previous studies have shown that using a multielement doping design improves the production of *V*O and enhances charge carrier diffusion. The cathode performance of ME-SFO is projected to be superior to that of SFO materials doped with only one dopant, and the electrochemical performance of ME-SFO is investigated and compared with those of SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW. Symmetrical cells are made with a dense BCZY electrolyte sandwiched between the cathodes. Figure 5(a) shows typical EIS plots of symmetrical cells with SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, SFW, and ME-SFO evaluated at 700 °C. The arcs represent only the electrode contribution, as the capacitance of the arcs is on the order of 10−6–10−5 F∙cm−2, which is the typical response for the electrode and much larger than that for the electrolyte. The polarization resistance of ME-SFO is lower than those of the other four cathodes that use only one dopant, implying that the ME-SFO cathode reaction kinetics are faster than those of SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW. The distribution of relaxation time (DRT) given in Fig. 5(b) shows that the cathode reaction can be divided into three regions: high-frequency, middle-frequency, and low-frequency ranges. During DRT analysis of the EIS data, the impedance spectra, comprising the frequency, real (*Z*′), and imaginary (*Z*″) parts, were imported into Relax IS software. Gaussian basis functions were employed for discretization, with automatic inductance compensation applied during preprocessing. The regularization parameter *λ* was set to 1×10−4 for second-order Tikhonov regularization. Following validation of the fitting results, the final DRT spectrum was extracted for subsequent analysis. The medium-frequency resistance (*R*MF) and low-frequency resistance (*R*LF) are comparable for these five cathodes, with some variations. In contrast, resistances in the high-frequency range (*R*HF) vary significantly. The ME-SOF cathode has the lowest *R*HF, and the resistance increases from SFNb to SFTa, SFW, and finally SFMo. It has been reported that the *R*HF represents the charge transfer process, which includes the movement of protons and oxygen ions [43]. According to the findings of the preceding study, SFMo and SFW have higher oxygen diffusion rates than SFNb and SFTa do, although SFNb and SFTa have greater advantages in terms of proton diffusion. Compared with those of SFMo and SFW, the *R*HF values of SFNb and SFTa are lower, indicating that proton diffusion is more crucial in the cathode reactions of H-SOFCs. The ME-SFO cathode, with the highest *D*O and *D*H, enhances charge transfer and hence lowers the *R*HF. Figure 5(c) shows the area-specific resistance (ASR) values of SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, SFW, and ME-SFO tested from 500 to 700 °C, with ME-SFO having a lower ASR value than the other cathodes across the entire testing temperature range. Furthermore, the ME-SFO cathode has a much lower activation energy (*E*a) of 0.96 eV than the other cathodes. ME-SFO has a substantially lower *E*a, most likely due to its excellent proton diffusion ability [44]. Notably, the *E*a values of SFNb and SFTa are lower than those of SFMo and SFW, confirming that improved proton diffusion reduces the total *E*a of the cathode.

### 3.5 Performance of cathode catalysts in fuel cells

ME-SFO has a low ASR, indicating that it may perform well in H-SOFC applications. As a result, the performance of the ME-SFO cathodes under H-SOFC working conditions was further examined and compared with those of the SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW cathodes. Figure 6(a) depicts the performance of the fuel cell with the SFNb cathode. The peak power density (PPD) of the SFNb cell is 538, 866, and 1139 mW∙cm−2 at 600, 650, and 700 °C, respectively. This fuel cell output is comparable to the published report for H-SOFCs with an SFNb cathode [24]. The SFTa cell performs similarly to the SFNb cell, with PPD values of 451, 643, and 1034 mW∙cm−2 at 600, 650, and 700 °C, respectively, as shown in Fig. 6(b). In comparison, the SFMo and SFW cells have slightly lower performance. At 600, 650, and 700 °C, the PPDs of the SFMo cell are 339, 531, and 907 mW∙cm−2, whereas the PPDs of the SFW cell are 436, 605, and 970 mW∙cm−2, respectively, as shown in Figs. 6(c) and 6(d). Although these cathodes work decently for fuel cells, they are still inferior to some newly designed cathodes for H-SOFCs [45,46]. In comparison, the fuel cell with the ME-SFO cathode performs admirably, producing PPDs of 724, 1239, and 1748 mW∙cm−2 at 600, 650, and 700 °C, respectively, as shown in Fig. 6(e). The performance is much greater than that of cells using SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW cathodes, as shown in Fig. 6(f), indicating that the multielement doping method increases the cathode performance and thereby increases the fuel cell output. The cross-sectional SEM images of the cells show that all of the tested cells had comparable morphologies, implying that the microstructure issues affecting fuel cell performance are insignificant.

Figures 7(a)–7(e) show the EIS data for the SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, SFW, and ME-SFO cells. The ME-SFO polarization resistance (R*p) is 0.039 Ω∙cm2, which is lower than those of the SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW cells, which are 0.075, 0.093, 0.111, and 0.098 Ω∙cm2, respectively. Although *R*p includes contributions from both the anode and the cathode, all tested cells employ the same anode, implying that the difference in *R*p stems mainly from the different cathode materials. This finding is consistent with the symmetrical cell test, which shows that ME-SFO has the lowest ASR value. Figure 7(f) shows the ohmic resistance (*R*o), *R*p, and total resistance (*R*t) of the tested cells. The *R*o is similar for all the cells, whereas *R*p shows a significant difference, leading to a varied *R*t. The smallest *R*p for the ME-SFO cathode results in the smallest *R*t, thus increasing the fuel cell output compared with those of the other cathodes.

Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 8, the cell using the ME-SFO cathode outperforms not only the cells using SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW cathodes but also all previously reported H-SOFCs using SrFeO3-based cathodes [24,39,47−57]. Compared with previous SrFeO3-based cathodes, the ME-SFO cathode has a lower *R*p and produces more fuel cell output, increasing the performance of SrFeO3-based cathodes for H-SOFCs to a new level and demonstrating the benefit of using a multielement doping design to tune the SrFeO3 cathode.

Aside from outstanding fuel cell efficiency, the ME-SFO cathode exhibited good stability in fuel cell operation. Figure 9(a) depicts the long-term stability test of the fuel cell employing the ME-SFO cathode under the fuel cell operating conditions. After 150 h of operation with a constant current of 200 mA∙cm−2, the voltage of the fuel cell remains constant, indicating that the fuel cell with the ME-SFO cathode has outstanding operational stability. The microstructure of the ME-SFO cell after the long-term test is also shown in the figure, demonstrating a good connection between the cathode and the electrolyte. Retesting the *I*–*V* curve of the cell after long-term operation revealed no significant drop in the PPD, validating the operational stability of the fuel cells. The EIS plot of the cell after the test, which is also shown in Fig. 9(b), exhibits a similar *R*o and *R*p to those of the initial cell, indicating good cathode and interfacial stability. The good operational stability of the ME-SFO cathode may partially originate from its good chemical stability against CO2 and H2O, as demonstrated in Fig. S2 in the ESM. The ME-SFO powder was treated in a 20% CO2- or 30% H2O-containing atmosphere. XRD was then employed to examine the phase composition of the ME-SFO material before and after treatment. No extra peaks can be observed for the ME-SFO material after treatment with CO2 or H2O, suggesting good chemical stability of ME-SFO against CO2 and H2O.

### 3.6 Cycling stability of ME-SFO cathode in H-SOFCs

While the ME-SFO cathode demonstrates stability under steady-state fuel cell operation at fixed temperatures, its performance under realistic thermal cycling conditions—a critical parameter for practical fuel cell durability—requires evaluation. To assess cycling stability, a fuel cell employing the ME-SFO cathode was subjected to repeated thermal cycles between 600 and 700 °C under a constant voltage of 0.7 V, with heating and cooling rates of 3 °C∙min−1. As shown in Fig. 10(a), the current density significantly decreases with increasing number of cycles. Specifically, the current density at 700 °C decreased from an initial value of 2149 to 1464 mA∙cm−2 after 10 cycles, retaining only 68% of its initial value. This substantial degradation highlights a key drawback of the ME-SFO cathode, despite its high initial output. Posttest analysis of the cycled cell (Fig. 10(b)) reveals the root cause: A distinct gap formed at the cathode/electrolyte interface, indicating partial delamination of the ME-SFO layer. This loss of contact directly explains the performance degradation [58]. Delamination is strongly linked to the high thermal expansion coefficient (TEC) of ME-SFO. As illustrated in Fig. 10(c), while ME-SFO has a moderate TEC below 600 °C, its TEC increases sharply to 30.73×10−6 °C−1 above 600 °C. This value is significantly higher than that of the BCZY electrolyte (10.24×10−6 °C−1) [23]. The high TEC of ME-SFO is inherited from the intrinsic nature of the SrFeO3 material. We measured the TECs of SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW, and the results are shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW exhibit similar thermal expansion behavior to ME-SFO. The TEC value is relatively low in the low-temperature range, reaching 16.58×10−6, 16.91×10−6, 15.48×10−6, and 15.59×10−6 °C−1 for SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW, respectively. However, the TEC values dramatically increase to 30.62×10−6, 35.24×10−6, 31.59×10−6, and 31.27×10−6 °C−1 for SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW, respectively, in the high-temperature range. This result indicates that the multielement doping method does not significantly increase the TEC compared with those of the single-element doped samples. The resulting thermal mismatch generates stresses during repeated heating and cooling cycles, causing the cathode to peel away from the electrolyte. Consequently, mitigating the high thermal expansion of ME-SFO and its detrimental effects on cycling stability is essential for advancing its application in H-SOFCs.

### 3.7 Enhancing cycling stability with negative thermal expansion oxides

To address the detrimental thermal expansion of ME-SFO and enhance cycling stability, we incorporated NdMnO3 (NMO) [50], a negative thermal expansion (NTE) oxide, into the cathode. Unlike conventional materials that expand upon heating, NMO contracts volumetrically with increasing temperature, effectively counterbalancing the high thermal expansion of ME-SFO. Composite cathodes with varying ME-SFO : NMO weight ratios (9 : 1, 8 : 2, and 7 : 3) were evaluated in H-SOFCs. The ME-SFO (90%)+NMO (10%) cathode delivers PPDs of 855, 1374, and 1862 mW∙cm−2 at 600, 650, and 700 °C, respectively, as shown in Fig. 11(a), slightly exceeding the performance of the fuel cell when the single-phase ME-SFO cathode is used. The EIS plot shown in Fig. 11(b) reveals the reduced ohmic (*R*o = 0.108 Ω∙cm2) and polarization (*R*p = 0.027 Ω∙cm2) resistances versus those of the ME-SFO cell. Considering the relatively low catalytic activity of NMO, this improvement likely stems from enhanced cathode/electrolyte interfacial contact due to mitigated thermal mismatch.

*
Fig. 11Performance of fuel cells using (a) ME-SFO (90%) + NMO (10%), (c) ME-SFO (80%) + NMO (20%), and (e) ME-SFO (70%) + NMO (30%) cathodes and (b, d, f) corresponding EIS plots tested at 700 °C.

Increasing the NMO content to 20% (ME-SFO (80%) + NMO (20%)) yields comparable PPD values (877, 1390, and 1888 mW∙cm−2 at 600, 650, and 700 °C, respectively), as shown in Fig. 11(c), suggesting diminishing performance returns. Notably, while its R*o further decreases to 0.103 Ω∙cm2 (indicating superior interfacial contact), *R*p increases to 0.029 Ω∙cm2 (Fig. 11(d)). This trade-off implies that the greater proportion of less catalytically active NMO begins to offset interfacial gains. This trend intensifies with ME-SFO (70%) + NMO (30%), where PPD decreases significantly (671, 1211, and 1691 mW∙cm−2 at 600, 650, and 700 °C, respectively), and *R*p increases to 0.059 Ω∙cm2 at 700 °C, as shown in Figs. 11(e) and 11(f), confirming that excessive NMO degrades overall cathode activity. Chemical compatibility testing between ME-SFO and NMO confirmed that the performance differences did not originate from interfacial reactions. The composite ME-SFO + NMO powder was annealed at 900 °C (cathode fabrication temperature), and XRD analysis before and after treatment (Fig. S4 in the ESM) revealed no new phases. The absence of additional diffraction peaks indicates that no reaction occurs during cofiring, ruling out a significant reaction between these two phases.

Crucially, all the composites exhibited dramatically improved thermal cycling stability compared with that of pure ME-SFO (Fig. 12). The ME-SFO (90%) + NMO (10%) cell showed only 12.6% current density degradation after 10 cycles (2206 → 1928 mA∙cm−2 at 700 °C), which was a marked improvement over ME-SFO with a 32% loss. Postcycling SEM confirmed enhanced cathode–electrolyte adhesion. The optimal performance emerges with ME-SFO (80%) + NMO (20%): It maintains near-zero degradation while delivering high PPDs. SEM verified perfect interfacial integrity after cycling. Although ME-SFO (70%) + NMO (30%) also retains excellent interfacial stability, its lower power output makes ME-SFO (80%) + NMO (20%) the preferred composition, achieving the dual objectives of high performance and exceptional cycling durability. Additionally, the fuel cell with SFO (80%) + NMO (20%) also exhibited good operational stability, as indicated in Fig. S5 in the ESM. The voltage of the cell remained stable for more than 100 h, and no evident degradation was observed. This result also suggests good long-term stability for the ME-SFO (80%) + NMO (20%) cell, and the improved cycling stability does not impair the operational stability of fuel cells.

While the performance of the ME-SFO (80%) + NMO (20%) cell is encouraging and comparable to that of state-of-the-art H-SOFC cathodes, it remains inferior to that of several recently developed alternatives [18,59]. This indicates the potential for further optimization of this cathode system. Given the moderate catalytic activity of NMO, enhancing its activity through strategic doping could improve the overall cathode performance. Furthermore, optimizing the cathode/electrolyte interface to reduce total cell resistance represents another promising approach [60]. Such interface engineering would leverage the excellent cycling stability and operational robustness to maximize fuel cell performance.

## 4 Conclusions

The multielement-doped ME-SFO oxide has been successfully developed as a cathode catalyst for H-SOFCs. Compared with SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW oxides with only one dopant, the use of multielement doping allowed ME-SFO to demonstrate a noticeable synergistic effect, with catalytic activity of the ME-SFO outperforming those of SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, and SFW. Compared with SFNb, SFTa, SFMo, or SFW, ME-SFO significantly improved the proton and oxygen diffusion kinetics, dramatically altering the cathode catalytic activity. As a result, the ME-SFO cathode had the smallest ASR among all investigated cathodes, indicating its superior cathode catalytic activity. As a result, using the ME-SFO cathode catalyst in H-SOFCs resulted in higher fuel cell performance than previously reported for SrFeO3-based cathodes for H-SOFCs. While demonstrating excellent operational stability with high performance at constant temperatures, ME-SFO suffers from poor thermal cycling stability due to its high TEC, causing cathode–electrolyte interface degradation after the testing cycles. To address this problem, the NTE oxide NMO was employed to balance the high thermal expansion of ME-SFO. The incorporation of NMO in the cathode effectively improved the interfacial integrity and thus enhanced the cycling stability of fuel cells, with ME-SFO (80%) + NMO (20%) resulting in the best performance. The use of the ME-SFO (80%) + NMO (20%) cathode simultaneously increased the fuel cell output and allowed the cell to resist 10 cycles without observable degradation, resulting in the high catalytic activity of ME-SFO and overcoming its high thermal expansion problem, which made this material suitable as a cathode for H-SOFCs.
