## 1 Introduction

Significant improvements in the thrust-weight ratio of turbine engines have been achieved with increasing advances in aviation technology, which has led to increasing service temperatures (short-term service temperatures above 1500 °C), such as turbine inletsand tail nozzles, forcing the thermal structural component materials to have stronger temperature-bearing capacities [1−4]. However, the widely used nickel-based superalloys for turbine engine materials have a limited service temperature of only 1200 °C and do not exceed 1500 °C even when they are coated with thermal barrier coatings [5,6], which makes the development of advanced thermal protection systems (TPSs) for turbine engines with high thrust-weight ratios urgent.

Niobium alloys are promising substitutes for nickel-based superalloys for the hot end parts of aircraft engines, such as combustion chambers, turbines, and tail nozzles, because of their high melting point (~2468 °C), moderate density, and superior mechanical strength at high temperatures [7,8]. Nevertheless, the “pest” catastrophic oxidation of niobium alloys is easily produced when they are exposed to an oxygen-containing environment at high temperatures (above 600 °C) [9]. That is, the oxidation product layer (Nb2O5) peels off from the substrate due to the high growth stress, damaging the mechanical properties of the substrate and greatly restricting its further application [10]. In addition, turbine engines usually face serious hot corrosion (during the ignition stage, the temperature ranges from 900 to 1000 °C), except for high-temperature oxidation, which accelerates the failure of niobium alloys [11]. For example, tail nozzles are usually subjected to hot corrosion due to the deposition of fuel impurities (Fig. 1). For shipboard engines and gas turbines, corrosive salt mixtures (mainly Na, S, and Cl) obtained from humid air in the ocean and fuel impurities deposit on the surface of hot-end components, leading to acceleration of corrosion damage [12]. All of the above factors have pushed researchers to pursue an efficient method for preventing the oxidation and corrosion of niobium alloys at high temperatures, even at extremely high temperatures.

Currently, many efforts have been made to develop TPSs for turbine engines, and the use of a niobium alloy coated with a thermal protective coating is undoubtedly considered the most efficient method to reach long-term oxidation/corrosion resistance owing to its advantages of a short development period, low fabrication cost, and tailored properties [13−15]. In particular, depositing a silicide coating on niobium alloys has been recognized as the most suitable method because it can form a dense SiO2 layer at high temperatures to isolate the substrate and external oxygen/corrosive media [16−18]. However, the growth thermal stress continues to accumulate when the silicide coating is exposed to air at high temperatures. Thus, a single silicide coating has a strong tendency to crack vertically and even peel off due to its low fracture toughness, which greatly restricts the long-term service of niobium alloys [19,20]. In addition, the single silicide coating showed a limited protective ability when facing a more severe hot corrosion environment (Na2SO4 + NaCl) since it tended to form a porous/fragmented corroded layer and peeled off in subsequent corrosion/oxidation due to the catalytic effect of sulfur and chlorine [21]. Therefore, how to form a robust oxide/corroded layer with high chemical stability and high strain tolerance at elevated temperatures is the key scientific issue for the long-term service of silicide protective coatings.

Motivated by such considerations, depositing an extra protective layer to form silicide-based composite coatings is a potential solution to long-term service problems, and many works have focused on fabricating modified silicide-based coatings to improve oxidation/molten corrosion resistance [22−24]. For example, He et* *al*. [25] reported a novel Zr–Y-modified NbSi2 coating on niobium alloys by combining magnetron sputtering and halide-activated pack cementation (HAPC) methods, which resulted in better hot corrosion resistance than a single NbSi2 coating at 1200 °C. Zhu *et* *al*. [26] fabricated a MoSi2–ZrSi2–SiC composite coating on niobium alloys via the spark plasma sintering (SPS) method, which aimed to improve long-term service by reducing the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) mismatch. The results showed that the composite coating was crack-free without any defects and that the produced oxide scale possessed compact morphologies after oxidation at 1500 °C, exhibiting enhanced antioxidation properties. Despite remarkable progress in modified silicide-based coatings, several issues that urgently need to be addressed for industrial applications exist [27−29]: (1) The production of modified silicide-based coatings depends on a complex formation process and strict requirements for equipment; (2) volume changes in high-temperature products cannot be avoided, causing cracks/pores and shortening service life. To overcome these shortcomings, constructing a modified silicide-based coating with a preset porous outer layer is considered an efficient solution. For the expected design, a dense SiO2 glass film can be formed at the silicide/outer layer interface during the early high-temperature process, and a portion of the pores can also be filled. Moreover, the porous outer layer can not only transform into a skeleton structure with high chemical stability but also favor the release of the large thermal growth stress produced by volume expansion, endowing the composite coating with a robust oxide/corroded layer.

To achieve our above design to obtain a modified silicide-based coating on niobium alloys against oxidation/molten salt corrosion at high temperatures, in this work, a reliable and efficient method, named liquid plasma-assisted particle deposition and sintering (LPDS), was proposed. It was developed from conventional plasma electrolytic oxidation (PEO) and is characterized by high voltage and high temperature, accompanied by instant and dense plasma spark discharges at the coating/electrolyte interface. As a result, the added HfC nanoparticles in the electrolyte migrated and deposited on the silicide coating surface, forming a NbSi2/HfC–HfO2 composite coating. In this work, the formation mechanism of particle deposition and the sintering of the outer layer was revealed. Moreover, the hot corrosion behavior at 900 °C and high-temperature oxidation behavior at 1500 °C were investigated, and the possible thermal protective mechanisms were revealed, which provided a new idea for developing an advanced modified silicide-based coating on turbine engines.

### 2.1 Coating preparation

The fabrication of the modified silicide-based coating included two steps: (1) formation of the substrate silicide inner layer via the HAPC method and (2) deposition of an extra protective outer layer on the surface of the silicide coating via LPDS technology. In our work, a commercial Nb521 alloy with a chemical composition of Nb–5W–2Mo–1Zr (wt%) was selected as the substrate, and NbSi2-coated Nb521 was obtained by using buried infiltration powders of Si, NaF, and Al2O3. The modified NbSi2-based composite coating was then prepared via the LPDS technique with the addition of HfC nanoparticles into the electrolyte. The detailed experimental procedures of the NbSi2 inner layer and nanoparticle deposition layer were reported in our previous work [30].

### 2.2 Hot corrosion test

To evaluate the hot corrosion resistance of our prepared composite coating, a mixed salt (Na2SO4 + NaCl, with a mass percentage of 3:1) environment at 900 °C was used to simulate the service environment of the tail nozzle. Before the hot corrosion test, all the samples were cut to a size of 10 mm × 10 mm × 2 mm and coated with the composite coating. Then, the saturated salt solution was uniformly sprayed onto the coating surface, and the amount of salt was controlled at 4 mg·cm−2 after evaporation of the water. All the samples were subsequently placed in alumina crucibles and then subjected to high temperatures in an electric furnace (KSL-1200X-H, Kejin, China) at 900 °C for different durations. After the corrosion test, the hot corrosion resistance was obtained from the weight of the corrosion gain. To reduce experimental errors, all the test samples were boiled to remove residual salt before measurement. Moreover, the single NbSi2 coating was tested for comparison at the same time.

### 2.3 High-temperature oxidation test

The high-temperature oxidation resistance of the composite coating was evaluated via an isothermal oxidation test at 1500 °C. All the samples with a size of 10 mm × 10 mm × 2 mm were placed in an alumina crucible, placed in an open-ended oxidation furnace (KSL-1700X, Kejin, China) at a heating rate of 3 °C·min−1, and held for 100, 300, or 500 min, respectively. After oxidation, the samples were taken from the furnace directly and cooled in air, and the oxidation resistance was obtained from the mass gain and thickness of the oxide scale/residual NbSi2 layer.

### 2.4 Characterization

A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Helios Nanolab600i, FEI, USA) coupled with an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200x, FEI, USA) were used to analyze the microstructure evolution of the coatings before and after the high-temperature test, as well as the oxide scale/residual NbSi2 layer. An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Empyrean, PANalytical, The Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation was used to obtain the phase constituents of the coating surface before and after the high-temperature test.

### 3.1 Microstructure of NbSi2/HfC–HfO2 composite coating

To construct a robust modified NbSi2-based composite coating against hot corrosion and high-temperature oxidation, HfC nanoparticles were added to the electrolyte to form an HfC‒HfO2 deposition layer via high-frequency plasma discharge. On the one hand, HfC can act as an oxygen-consuming phase, reducing the inward oxygen flux during the early oxidation process [31]. On the other hand, the oxidation products (HfO2) possess a low oxygen permeation rate and provide the Hf-rich skeleton structure with a high melting point after high-temperature service, which is beneficial to the long-term service of the coating [32]. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the coating surface presents a typical porous structure similar to that of the conventional PEO coating. Notably, many island-shaped structures dominated the coating surface, and the XRD patterns (Fig. 2(c)) demonstrate that they were mainly composed of Nb2O5, SiO2, and deposited HfC, as well as the HfO2 phase produced by *in* *situ* oxidation of HfC. The weight ratio of the HfO2 phase:HfC phase was ~84:16, which was obtained by separating the diffraction peaks and calculating them via Jade 9 software. This special morphology is attributed to the strong plasma spark discharge at the NbSi2 surface/electrolyte interface, and the molten oxides are sprayed out from the discharge channel. In a substance, the expected design of the micro/nanopore structure possesses a large strain tolerance, which can withstand the larger volume change of the phase transition during the heating and cooling process. Figure 2(b) indicates that the composite coating includes a main protective NbSi2 inner layer of ~120 μm and an extra particle deposition outer layer of ~45 μm. The corresponding EDS map shows a uniform element distribution and an Hf-rich layer covering the NbSi2 surface, implying the successful preparation of the composite coating aligned with the expected coating structure. To further observe the fine microstructure of the deposited HfC–HfO2 layer, TEM experiments were conducted on the coating surface, as shown in Fig. 2(e). The mixed area, including the nanocrystalline region and amorphous region, is observed, and the amorphous phase is speculated to be amorphous SiO2. The marked areas of f and g in Fig. 2(e) are the HfO2 phase with the zone axis of [001] and the HfC phase with the zone axis of [010], respectively, which is confirmed by the corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns. These findings provide ample evidence that the HfC–HfO2 protective layer was successfully deposited on the NbSi2 surface, resulting in the modified silicide-based composite coating. In fact, the amorphous SiO2 phase within the deposition outer layer is derived from two main aspects: (1) the plasma spark discharge results in a stronger surface oxidation effect of NbSi2 ; (2) the electrolyte with the addition of sodium silicate provides a certain number of * SiO32− and transforms into SiO2 during the subsequent PEO process. Moreover, in* *situ* oxidation of the HfC particle surface is achieved due to the strong oxidation effect of the LPDS process (the transient temperature of the microzone reaches ~7000 K [33]), resulting in HfO2 surrounding the original HfC. During HfC–HfO2 deposition and sintering of the outer layer, the oxidation products of NbSi2 act as the skeleton of the deposition outer layer, simultaneously with HfC/HfO2 filling the skeleton structure and forming the sintering outer layer. At the same time, amorphous SiO2 plays an important adhesive role between different nanoparticles and provides good bond strength. The detailed formation mechanism of the composite coating is discussed in Section 3.2.

### 3.2 Formation mechanism of particle deposition and sintering layer

Given the locally unique environment of increasing electrolyte temperature and high-frequency plasma discharge field, LPDS is characterized by high voltage/temperature. As a result, a special phenomenon in which instant and dense plasma spark discharges are formed at the coating/electrolyte interface is observed, resulting in the directional migration of nanoparticles toward the anode interface, which is different from conventional PEO. Therefore, revealing the formation mechanism of the LPDS coating and the deposition/sintering process of added nanoparticles is necessary, as it can guide the design and fabrication of modified silicide-based coatings with tailored properties.

As shown in Fig. 3(a), the current density/applied voltage vs. time profiles indicate that the film formation of the LPDS process can be divided into three stages. These stages are as follows: (1) Stage Ⅰ of the current density continues to rise; (2) Stage Ⅱ of the current density abnormally decreases; and (3) Stage III of the current density slowly/abnormally increases. For Stage Ⅰ, a thin passivation film is formed on the surface of NbSi2 with increasing applied voltage (≤ 300 V). Then, the defects in the passivation film are preferentially broken down as the voltage continues to rise (> 300 V), and large amounts of weak sparks characterized as uniform and dense appear on the anode. As a result, the molten oxide of Si/Nb induced by discharge flows out in the discharge channel, thereby forming the Nb2O5–SiO2 layer at the NbSi2 layer/electrolyte interface. Moreover, many HfC nanoparticles in the electrolyte migrated toward the Nb2O5–SiO2 layer driven by a strong electric field, subsequently sintering and forming a ceramic coating. When the applied voltage reaches 600 V, the current density increases to the maximum value, and the increased plasma spark discharge provides more energy, promoting more HfC accumulation at the NbSi2/electrolyte interface. In Stage II, the resistance increases, and the current density abnormally decreases with increasing processing time due to the thickened ceramic coating. The nanoparticles near the anode participate in the repetitive cycle process of “melting‒condensation” and sintering with the sprayed molten oxide into the coating, despite the relatively slow growth rate of the coating (Fig. 3(b)). In Stage III, the current density begins to rise slowly as the processing time increases, which is ascribed to the strong and dense plasma discharge [34]. The increased plasma spark discharge rapidly promoted the deposition and sintering of large amounts of HfC particles to form a thick nanocomposite outer coating [35].

In addition, a special microarea is constructed by increasing the electrolyte temperature and high-frequency discharge field, inducing strong instantaneous discharge, and the microarea temperature can reach 7000 K [33]. As a result, a certain number of HfC nanoparticles near the discharge channel are oxidized and transformed into the HfO2 phase, resulting in the formation of HfC–HfO2 deposition layer. Therefore, the deposition and sintering of HfC nanoparticles during the LPDS process can be summarized as the repetitive film-forming process of “migration–deposition/melting–condensation”. Notably, the type of nanoparticle that migrates is an important factor that affects the formation of composite coatings. For the HfC nanoparticles in the electrolyte, the methods of migration toward the anode interface mainly include turbulent diffusion, driving by an electric field, and driving by a pressure difference, as shown in Fig. 3(c). Among them, driving by an electric field is considered the most important drive that promotes the deposition of HfC. Therefore, clarification of the migration driving force and the deposition mechanism of the added nanoparticles is an important basis for designing and preparing nanocomposite coatings with tailored properties.

### 3.3 Hot corrosion behavior of NbSi2/HfC–HfO2 composite coating at 900 °C

The hot corrosion behavior of the composite coating was evaluated at 900 °C for 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 150, and 200 h. Figure 4 shows the hot corrosion kinetics curves of the coatings with different systems, which followed a parabolic behavior. The hot corrosion behavior of the samples with different coatings can be reflected from the kinetic law of the corrosion mass with increasing time, as listed in Table 1, as well as the fitting results of the corrosion kinetics for the samples with different coatings. The single NbSi2-coated sample shows a corrosion gain of 20.54 mg·cm−2 after a corrosion time of 100 h, and the coating completely fails after 150 h, resulting in the catastrophic oxidation of the substrate niobium alloy. In contrast, our designed composite coating shows improved hot corrosion resistance, with a corrosion gain of only 13.94 mg·cm−2 even when the corrosion time is extended to 200 h, simultaneously resulting in an intact coating structure. As shown in Fig. 4(b), the single NbSi2 coating has a higher corrosion rate than the composite coating does, especially in the last 50 h, indicating that the single NbSi2 coating cannot effectively provide long-term protection to the substrate. For the composite coating, the corrosion rate constant is low and shows an accelerated corrosion rate after 100 h of corrosion, implying that the hot corrosion process is dominated by the oxidation reaction in the later stage due to the insufficient amount of molten salt on the coating surface. Evidently, the extra HfC–HfO2 deposition layer on the NbSi2 layer is essential for enhancing the anticorrosive corrosion properties.

After a corrosion time of 100 h, there was little change in the XRD pattern, implying that severe oxidation of the NbSi2 layer dominated this corrosion stage because the produced corrosive pores provided more diffusion channels for the corrosive medium and oxygen, coincident with the increased rate of mass gain. Moreover, the peak of HfSiO4 is detected on the coating surface as corrosion progresses, probably due to the suitable thermochemical environment provided by the corrosion/oxidation products.

Figure 6 shows the variation in the surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the composite coating corroded with mixed salts at 50, 100, and 200 h. According to the macroscopic morphologies after hot corrosion (Figs. 6(a1), 6(b1), and 6(c1)), all of the samples exhibit an intact coating structure without peeling and obvious cracks, even though the corrosion time extends to 200 h, indicating the excellent hot corrosion resistance of the coating. When the corrosion time is 50 h (Fig. 6(a2)), the coating surface is dominated by a sintered and agglomerated particle structure, which is mainly composed of a mixed phase of Na2Si2O5, NaNbO3, and Hf-rich oxides, as confirmed by the EDS results listed in Table 2. Moreover, the corroded layer has a certain number of pore structures that are different from the original morphology of the LPDS coating. The possible reason is that the volatile phases (CO2, SOx*, and Cl2) produced by the oxidation of HfC and the decomposition of molten salt escape from the coating hinder the formation of the SiO2 network. Moreover, the corresponding cross-sectional back scattered electron (BSE) image (Fig. 6(a3)) shows that the corroded layer (~47 μm) is only slightly thicker than the original deposition layer (~45 μm) after a corrosion time of 50 h, and the thickness of the NbSi2 layer decreases from ~120 to ~116 μm. This implies that the reactions of this stage are mainly the transformation from the deposition layer into a corroded layer and a slight oxidation of the NbSi2 layer. In fact, a corrosive medium barrier layer (SiO2 glass layer) can be formed due to the early oxidation of NbSi2, which can block the penetration of the molten salts due to the low solubility of SiO2 in the molten salt of Na2SO4. As the hot corrosion progressed to 100 h, the grown NaNbO3 dominated the surface of the corroded layer, and some penetrating corroded pores were observed (Fig. 6(b2)), which provided more transmission channels for oxygen and the corrosive medium. Therefore, the corroded layer clearly thickens to ~66 μm, and the thickness of the residual NbSi2 layer decreases to ~103 μm (Fig. 6(b3)), indicating accelerated corrosion/oxidation of the NbSi2 layer in the substrate. In addition, the BSE image shows a layered structure of the corroded layer, and the outer corroded layer with bright regions is speculated to be the Hf-rich skeleton on the basis of the EDS results, which indicate excellent chemical stability in a corrosive medium environment. When the corrosion time further increased to 200 h, the corroded layer surface showed a slight change in morphology except for an increase in pore diameter (Fig. 6(c2)). The possible reason is that the residual salts on the surface are insufficient, and the late-stage hot corrosion is dominated by the oxidation of the NbSi2 bottom layer. The enlarged pores act as channels for external oxygen molecules, leading to rapid thickening of the oxide scale, as shown in the BSE image in Fig. 6(c3). The corroded layer increases to ~78 μm, and the residual NbSi2 layer decreases to ~91 μm. In addition, the thickened corroded layer is caused mainly by the oxidation of NbSi2, which is free of the contribution of the Hf-rich corroded outer layer. In summary, the composite coating exhibited excellent hot corrosion resistance and still possessed an NbSi2 bottom layer of ~91 μm after 200 h of corrosion, whereas the single NbSi2 coating failed and resulted in the catastrophic oxidation of the niobium alloy. Our composite coating is expected to be applied to turbine hot-end components for long-term service.

As hot corrosion progresses, the grown Nb2O5 with a high oxygen diffusion coefficient [42] provides more channels for inward oxygen flux and reacts with a portion of molten salt permeation, forming NaNbO3. On the one hand, the NaNbO3 and some small pores produced due to the escape of the volatile phase (CO2, Cl2) provide more channels for the oxygen/corrosive medium, accelerating the failure of the coating. On the other hand, the oxidation of HfC is accelerated by the catalysis of Cl2 to form more HfO2 phases, which can consume more molten slats and lead to the formation of protective corrosion products via Eq. (10). Simultaneously, SiO2 reacts with HfO2 and Na2O, forming HfSiO4 and Na2SiO5. As a result, a protective corroded layer composed of Na2Si2O5 + SiO2 + HfO2 + HfSiO4 + NaHfSiO5 is formed on the NbSi2 substrate layer, which can effectively hinder the permeation of molten salts.

As the corrosion time further increases, a granular structure of the corroded layer surface (Fig. 6(c2)) is formed by sintering corrosion products, which are dominated by large amounts of the grown NaNbO3. Moreover, the size of the corroded pores increases significantly during the later hot corrosion stage because of the severe hot corrosion around the pores. Notably, an obvious double-layer structure of the corroded layer (Fig. 6(c3)) is produced after the later hot corrosion stage, whereas the thickness of the Hf-rich outer corroded layer slightly changes, implying that hot corrosion is dominated by the oxidation of the NbSi2 layer in this stage. In this stage, the substance of the hot corrosion is that the external oxygen penetrates into the produced corroded layer via the pores and NaNbO3/Nb2O5 with a high oxygen diffusion coefficient, leading to the contiguous oxidation of the NbSi2 bottom layer. Although later oxidation is inevitable, the NbSi2/HfC–HfO2 composite coating results in the lowest corrosion gain compared with that of the single NbSi2 coating, and the residual thickness of the NbSi2 layer is still ~91 μm. This implies that our composite coating still has good protective properties for the niobium alloy even after a hot corrosion time of 200 h. The superior hot corrosion resistance provided by the HfC–HfO2 deposition layer is mainly attributed to two aspects: (1) HfC/HfO2 acts as the consumption phase of the oxygen/molten salts, and a SiO2 film layer can form on the NbSi2 layer surface during early hot corrosion, which results in a strong protective system for the niobium alloys; (2) the Hf-rich corroded skeleton structure possesses high hot corrosion stability and provides a large strain tolerance for the corroded layer. The corroded layer exhibits an intact structure after 200 h of hot corrosion, greatly increasing the service life of the coatings.

### 3.5 High-temperature oxidation behavior of NbSi2/HfC–HfO2 composite coating at 1500 °C

For aviation turbine engines, the short-term service temperature of hot-end components even exceeds 1500 °C with increasing engine thrust-weight ratio. Therefore, more stringent high-temperature oxidation resistance performance at 1500 °C is essential for the thermal protection of niobium alloys in addition to their superior hot corrosion resistance.

Figure 11 shows the oxidation behavior analysis and microstructures of NbSi2/HfC–HfO2 coating after oxidation at 1500 °C in air. The single NbSi2 coating causes fatal destruction after exposure to air for 300 min at 1500 °C, whereas our designed and prepared composite coating maintains an intact coating structure free of peel, even as oxidation progresses for 500 min (Fig. 11(a)). Undoubtedly, a single NbSi2 coating has a limited protective effect on niobium alloys at a high temperature of 1500 °C due to its short effective service time, confirming that NbSi2/HfC–HfO2 composite coating is a potential thermal protective material for niobium alloys at 1500 °C. The XRD patterns of the composite coatings subjected to various oxidation times are shown in Fig. 9(b). The peak of HfC cannot be detected after the oxidation time of 100 min, and the peak of HfO2 is weak when the oxidation time is extended to 300 min. As expected, HfSiO4 exhibits the strongest peak intensity after the oxidation time of 300 min, implying that the reaction between HfO2 and SiO2 dominates the oxidation process later. After an oxidation time of 500 min, the oxide scale surface was mainly composed of HfSiO4, Nb2O5, and SiO2, which followed our previous research [43] on the high-temperature oxidation resistance evaluation of the NbSi2/HfC–HfO2 coating at 1200 °C. On the basis of the weak intensity change in the XRD pattern from the oxidation time of 300–500 min, few phase changes are obtained, implying a slow oxidation rate of the composite coating. In addition, the thickness of the residual silicide bottom layer is an essential indicator for evaluating the protective performance of modified silicide-based coatings at high temperatures, and the residual NbSi2 layer/oxide scale thicknesses of our composite coatings subjected to different oxidation times are shown in Fig. 11(c). When the oxidation procedure is finished (500 min), the oxide scale thickness reaches ~72 μm, and the NbSi2 layer thickness decreases from ~120 to 96 μm but still maintains ~80% of the original thickness. The slower thickening rate of the oxide scale during later oxidation further implies that an extra protective layer is formed on the oxide scale surface, thereby reducing the oxidation of the NbSi2 bottom layer.

The surface microstructural evolution of the composite coatings subjected to oxidation at 1500 °C for 100, 300, and 500 min is described in Figs. 11(d)–11(f), respectively. As shown in Fig. 11(d), a raised structure similar to that of the original LPDS coating is observed after 100 min of oxidation, which can be considered the mixed phase of HfO2, SiO2, Nb2O5, and HfSiO4 on the basis of the XRD pattern combined with the EDS scanning point results listed in Fig. 11(h). In addition, the slight surface morphological change implies that the oxygen flux can be reduced by the HfC‒HfO2 deposition layer, protecting the NbSi2 bottom layer from severe oxidation. After an oxidation time of 300 min, the porosity of the oxide scale surface significantly decreases, accompanied by the production of some granular structures. On the basis of the EDS results (Fig. 11(e)), these granular structures are speculated to be the mixed phase of HfO2, HfSiO4, and the grown Nb2O5/SiO2. As oxidation progresses to 500 min, a dense oxide scale surface free of pores and cracks is obtained (Fig. 11(f)). Moreover, the oxide scale surface is composed of some SiO2 flat structures with Hf-rich refractory phases, which is confirmed by the corresponding elemental analysis. These refractory phases play important roles in the construction of the oxide scale skeleton because of their high melting point (mainly HfSiO4) and chemical stability [32]. Moreover, the produced Nb2O5/SiO2 glass phase fills the gaps within the oxide scale skeleton, results in the formation of a dense oxygen barrier layer, and reduces the oxidation rate of the composite coating.

The cross-sectional BSE image of the composite coating shows that the composite coating has a three-layer structure, including an Nb5Si3 layer, a residual NbSi2 layer, and an outer layer of oxide scale, as shown in Fig. 11(g). The thickened Nb5Si3 layer is a result of the high-temperature diffusion of Si/Nb elements due to the differentiated diffusion coefficient, which is considered the stress relief layer used to alleviate the thermal stress at the substrate/NbSi2 interface [44]. To our delight, our composite coating shows a residual NbSi2 layer of ~96 μm, even though the oxide scale layer thickens to ~72 μm, indicating that it still possesses good protection to the niobium alloys after oxidation for 500 min. Moreover, the EDS mapping/scanning point results corresponding to Fig. 11(g) indicate that the produced oxide scale can be divided into a Nb2O5 + SiO2 inner layer and a Hf-rich outer layer with a dense structure free of pores and cracks. The above results provide ample evidence that our designed NbSi2/HfC–HfO2 coating shows superior antioxidation performance at 1500 °C, which is expected to be applied to the high-temperature service of large thrust-to-weight ratio turbine engines.

The construction of the HfC–HfO2 outer layer is the key factor for the superior oxidation resistance of the composite coating. The oxidation process of the composite coating at 1500 °C involves the formation of a dense oxide scale with a highly stable Hf-rich skeleton, which can be divided into three stages. In the ΔT1 stage, HfC can consume large amounts of oxygen and provide more HfO2 with the coating surface, thereby reducing the inward oxygen flux. Moreover, slight oxidation of the NbSi2 surface is achieved to form an Nb2O5 + SiO2 barrier layer, which can provide an extra protective effect on the niobium alloy. In the ΔT2 stage, accelerated oxidation of NbSi2 is achieved due to the depletion of HfC, resulting in large amounts of Nb2O5 + SiO2. This stage can be described as the process of self-healing pores since the glass phase is continuously provided by the oxidation products of NbSi2 to form a SiO2 glass network. Simultaneously, the HfSiO4 new phase forms rapidly in this stage, which provides the supporting skeleton of the produced oxide scale. As oxidation progresses (ΔT3 stage), HfO2 is insufficient because of continuous consumption, and the oxidation of the NbSi2 inner layer dominates the inward oxygen flux, promoting the formation of a dense SiO2 glass layer. After the oxidation time of 500 min, the oxide scale shows a dense structure without pores/cracks, significantly reducing the oxidation rate of the coating. Overall, our designed and prepared NbSi2/HfC–HfO2 composite coating shows the expected superior oxidation resistance at 1500 °C. The improvement in the antioxidation performance compared with that of the single NbSi2 coating is attributed mainly to the successful construction of the HfC–HfO2 deposition outer layer. On the one hand, HfC within the outer layer can consume oxygen to form the HfO2 phase, reducing the inward oxygen flux and preventing severe oxidation of the NbSi2 bottom layer during early high-temperature oxidation. On the other hand, the Hf-rich skeleton structure anchored to the SiO2 glass layer is formed as expected after oxidation at 1500 °C, which provides the high thermal stability and large strain tolerance of the dense oxide scale. As a result, the thermal protection system of niobium alloys is mutually constructed by the first HfC–HfO2 deposition outer layer and the second silicide layer of NbSi2, resulting in superior antioxidation performance.

### 3.7 Thermal stress release mechanisms for long-term service at high temperatures

Interlayer peeling of the oxide scale is the most important factor affecting the long-term service of the silicide coating. For the NbSi2 coating, Yoon and Kim [49] reported that the large growth stress developed from the volume expansion provided by the phase change is the main reason for failure, leading to accelerated oxidation and even peeling in a short time after high-temperature service. Therefore, clarification of the stress relaxation mechanisms for different protective coatings is undoubtedly beneficial for the design and preparation of advanced thermal protection coatings. In fact, the superior long-term thermal protective effect of the NbSi2/HfC–HfO2 composite coating can be attributed to two main aspects: (1) both the preset voids and the formed Hf–Si–O skeleton provide a high strain tolerance, significantly releasing the thermal growth stress at the oxide/coating interface; (2) the deposition layer of HfC–HfO2 shows slight volume expansion during the phase transition at high temperatures. For the single NbSi2 coating, relaxation of the growth stress can be achieved only by plastic deformation or cracking of the oxide scale, as shown in Fig. 15(a). ## 4 Conclusions

A HfC–HfO2-modified NbSi2 composite coating was prepared by HAPC combined with LPDS as a thermal protection coating on niobium alloys. The corresponding hot corrosion behavior at 900 °C and high-temperature oxidation behavior at 1500 °C were evaluated, and the following results were obtained.

1) HfC‒HfO2 modification is the result of a repetitive film-forming process of “migration‒deposition/melting‒condensation”, and driving by a high-frequency plasma discharge electric field is considered the most important drive that promotes the formation of the HfC‒HfO2 deposition layer.

2) Compared with the single NbSi2 coating, the NbSi2/HfC‒HfO2 composite coating significantly improved the hot corrosion resistance, with the lowest corrosion gain of 13.94 mg·cm−2 and an ~91 μm residual thickness of the NbSi2 layer after 200 h. HfC/HfO2, which acts as the consumption phase of oxygen/molten salts, produces a SiO2 film layer during early hot corrosion, and the Hf-rich corroded skeleton structure, which provides a large strain tolerance for the corroded layer, is responsible for the improvement.

3) NbSi2/HfC‒HfO2 composite coating shows excellent superhigh-temperature oxidation resistance of the ~72 μm residual NbSi2 layer at 1500 °C for 500 min. The improvement in antioxidation performance is attributed to the following: (i) HfC within the outer layer can consume oxygen to form the HfO2 phase, reducing the inward oxygen flux and preventing severe oxidation of the NbSi2 bottom layer during early high-temperature oxidation; (ii) the Hf-rich skeleton structure anchored to the SiO2 glass layer provides high thermal stability and high strain tolerance of the dense oxide scale.

4) Multiple stress release mechanisms of the NbSi2/HfC‒HfO2 composite coating at high temperatures are critical factors for long-term service: (i) The preset voids provide an extra growth environment at the oxide scale; (ii) the robust Hf–Si–O network structure with high thermal/chemical stability possesses high strain tolerance; and (iii) there is a small volume change within the HfC‒HfO2 layer deposited due to the low volume expansion rate of the possible reactions.

Our prepared HfC‒HfO2-modified NbSi2 composite coating in this work can potentially be applied in multiple service environments characterized by a wide temperature range. In future work, our experimental designs might consider oxidation tests at higher temperatures and hot corrosion tests in other molten salt environments, such as CMAS or CMAS + molten salt. Moreover, efforts to measure the residual stress value of the coating after exposure to different high-temperature service environments never stopped.
