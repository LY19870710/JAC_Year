## 1 Introduction

The optical thermometric technique based on the fluorescence intensity ratio (FIR) in phosphor materials has garnered significant attention because of its advantages of noncontact measurement, rapid response, and high spatial and temperature resolutions, which facilitate temperature detection in harsh environments or for fast-moving objects [1−5]. FIR technology employs two emission intensities as monitoring signals, which confers advantages such as high accuracy and sensitivity. An ideal FIR temperature sensing material should exhibit distinguishable peaks with elevated absolute and relative sensitivity (*S*a and *S*r) values [1−8]. Conventional studies [9,10] on thermometric materials have focused primarily on the thermally coupled level pairs (TCLs) of rare earth ions, such as the 4F7/2 and 4F3/2 levels of Nd3+. In these systems, it is challenging to simultaneously increase both *S*a and *S*r due to the opposing changes in population at the upper and lower levels with increasing temperature [6−10]. Recently, significant advancements have been achieved in the development of novel thermometric strategies, including the utilization of metal-to-metal intervalence charge transfer (IVCT) states [11−13].

Lanthanide ions (Pr3+ or Tb3+) and transition metal ions (M*n*+ = Ti4+, Nb5+, V5+, etc.) can establish the IVCT state, which interferes with the luminescence of lanthanide ions by providing a thermal-quenching pathway [14,15], thereby introducing a significant temperature dependence in the luminescence. Numerous Pr3+-doped oxides, including Ca2LaNbO6, Na2La2Ti3O10, Gd2ZnTiO6, La2Zr2O7, CaSc2O4, and Sr2GeO4, have been demonstrated to exhibit Pr3+–M*n*+ IVCT states that interfere with the luminescence of Pr3+ for optical thermometry [14−19]. Indeed, the application of Pr3+-doped oxides for optical temperature sensing has been explored by monitoring the FIR of thermally coupled 3P1–3H5 and 3P0–3H5 emissions [17−19]. However, owing to the relatively small energy separation between these two emissions, the sensitivity has yet to reach a satisfactory level.

Polarization-enhanced photoluminescence (PL) has been reported in Pr3+-doped Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3 [20], K0.5Na0.5NbO3 (KNN) [21], and Er3+- and Sm3+-doped (Ba,Ca)(Ti,Sn)O3 [22,23] lead-free ferroelectric ceramics, indicating that the PL performance is highly sensitive to variations in local crystal symmetry. However, these host ceramics typically remain in the ferroelectric phase within the tested temperature range, exhibiting relatively large domain sizes and demonstrating lower temperature sensitivity. Considering that polar nanoregions (PNRs) in relaxor ferroelectrics exhibit high sensitivity to temperature across an extensive range [24,25], the integration of PNRs with PL may facilitate optical thermometry with enhanced sensitivity and a broad operational temperature range. In this study, we present a conceptual design that integrates relaxor ferroelectricity and PL to achieve ultrahigh-sensitivity optical thermometry in KNN-based ceramics. CaTiO3 (CT) was incorporated into the KNN system to facilitate the formation of relaxor ferroelectric structures [26] and enhance PL [27]. We engineered Pr-doped ceramics to achieve optimal PL intensities across varying concentrations of CT and Pr. The temperature-dependent PL spectra provide a noncontact temperature detection method utilizing the FIR, demonstrating the potential application of these ceramics in optical temperature sensing.

### 2.1 Material preparation

(1−*x*)K0.5Na0.5NbO3–*x*CaTiO3 (KNN–*x*CT, *x* = 0.1, 0.15, 0.175, 0.2, 0.25, and 0.3), and KNN–CT:*y*Pr (*y* = 0.001, 0.005, 0.01, and 0.03) ceramics were synthesized via the traditional solid-state reaction method. High-purity K2CO3 (99.5%), Na2CO3 (99.8%), Nb2O5 (99.5%), CaCO3 (99%), TiO2 (99.8%), and Pr6O11 (99.9%) were used. The weighed raw materials were ball milled with ethanol for 12 h. The dried slurries were calcined at 850 °C for 4 h, remilled for 12 h, dried, pressed into disks under a pressure of 200 MPa, and sintered in the range of 1160–1220 °C for 2 h under muffled conditions. The heating rate was 10 °C/min, but the cooling rate was not controlled (namely, furnace cooling). The obtained ceramics were polished such that the surface was parallel and smooth, with a thickness of 0.15 mm, to measure the optical and electrical properties.

### 2.2 Characterizations of materials

The optical transmission spectrum from 200 to 1600 nm was measured via an ultraviolet‒visible‒near-infrared spectroscope (UV‒vis‒NIR; UV‒3600i Plus, Shimadzu, Japan). The phase structure was characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/Max-IIIC, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation. The microstructures of the original surfaces were observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-IT800, SHLs, Japan). The bulk densities of all the samples were measured via the Archimedes method. The samples were polished and coated with a silver electrode on both sides for subsequent dielectric characterization. The temperature dependences of the dielectric constant (* ϵr) and dielectric loss (tanδ*) were tested with an impedance analyzer (E4294A, Agilent, USA) at frequencies ranging from 1 to 100 kHz. The PL excitation (PLE) and emission spectra of the KNN–CT:*y*Pr ceramics were measured via a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Spectrofluorometer FS 5, Edinburgh Instruments, UK) equipped with a xenon lamp as the excitation source. For temperature-dependent PL characterization, ceramic samples were mounted on a sample rod of an Oxford OptistatDN-V cryostat.

### 3.1 Optical properties

Figure 1(a) shows the transparency of the KNN–*x*CT ceramics polished to a thickness of 0.15 mm. Within the measured wavelength range, all the samples exhibit broad transmission spectra starting at 400 nm. The ceramics with *x* = 0.15 have a high optical transmittance of 69.4% at 780 nm and 78% at 1336 nm. The transparency of these KNN–*x*CT ceramics investigated in this study is comparable to the best performance reported in the literature [28,29]. The phenomenon of light transmittance approaching zero at wavelengths shorter than 400 nm is attributed primarily to interband transitions.

Transparency is mainly defined by free electrons, the position of the Fermi energy, and the type of band structure in a solid. During the transmission of light through ceramics, band gap energy (*E*g) plays a crucial role. Generally, a solid can appear transparent as long as the energy of the photons traveling through it is not absorbed in the bandgap of the material. As indicated by the Tauc equation, the relationship between the direct transition *E*g and the absorption coefficient (* α) is shown in Eq. (1) [30]:

(1) (αhν)2=A(hν−Eg)

where h* is Planck’s constant (4.1357×10−15 eV), * ν is the photon frequency, and A* is a constant. The photon frequency (* ν) and α* can be calculated according to Eqs. (2) and (3):

(2) ν=cλ

(3) α=1tln(1T)

where *c* is the speed of light (3×108 m/s), *λ* is the wavelength of the incident light, *t* is the thickness of the sample, and *T* represents the transmittance. According to Eq. (1), *E*g can be obtained by extrapolating the linear portion of the * (αhν)2 vs.  hν curve to zero, as shown in Fig. 1(d). Figures 1(b) and 1(c) illustrate the evolution of the transmittance and band gap energy in KNN–x*CT. For *x* ≤ 0.2, the variation in transmittance is consistent with the change in bandgap. However, for *x* > 0.2, a distinct trend emerges, which may be attributed to the formation of a secondary phase, KTiNbO5. Notably, for ceramic materials, solely associating transmittance with the band gap is overly simplistic. The influence of the microstructure on the transmittance should be considered concurrently.

As presented in Fig. 2, Pr doping does not significantly influence the bandgap or transmittance of the ceramics. The highest transmittance is observed for the KNN–CT:*y*Pr compositions with *y* = 0.005, where the values for 0.175CT:0.005Pr and 0.3CT:0.005Pr are 79.11% and 76.39% at a wavelength of 1336 nm, respectively. In contrast, both 0.175CT:0.01Pr and 0.3CT:0.01Pr resulted in the lowest transmittance, which may be correlated with the existence of impurity diffraction observed in the XRD patterns (Fig. 3). The translucency of these ceramics indicates a high density and a uniform distribution of fine grains and pores, which will be further substantiated in the following section.

### 3.2 Phase structure and microstructure morphology

To achieve oxide ceramics with high optical transparency, a commonly employed strategy is to eliminate all potential light scattering sites, which necessitates the following conditions [26,29]: high density, absence of pores (or pore sizes smaller than the wavelength of light), elimination of secondary phases, small grain size (comparable to the wavelength of light), isotropic lattice structure, and a surface that is as smooth as possible. These factors are predominantly interrelated and exert mutual influence on one another. For example, a decrease in porosity frequently results in an increase in density.

Figure 3(a) presents the XRD patterns of the KNN–*x*CT ceramics. All diffraction peaks of pure KNN are consistent with the previously reported XRD patterns of orthorhombic KNN [28,29]. Upon the introduction of cubic CT (*x* = 0.1–0.3), the two peaks (002/200) at approximately 45° merged into a single sharp (200) peak, indicating a transformation of the crystal structure from orthorhombic to pseudocubic. The addition of even 10% CT markedly decreases the orthorhombic distortion characteristic of KNN. As mentioned above, ceramics with an isotropic lattice structure exhibit reduced light scattering [31], thereby significantly enhancing optical transparency. The solid solubility is within the investigated range of KNN–CT compositions, which aligns with the findings for KNN–SrTiO3 [32]. Figure 3(b) reveals that for *x* = 0.1 and 0.15, the diffraction peaks are asymmetric, indicating slight orthorhombic distortion or a mixture of phases. Notably, the (200) peak shifts to a higher angle with increasing CT content. In the KNN–CT system, the Ca2+ ion (0.99 Å) is smaller than both the K+ ion (1.33 Å) and the Na+ ion (1.02 Å), whereas the Ti4+ ion (0.605 Å) is also smaller than the Nb5+ ion (0.64 Å). Moreover, the potential formation of oxygen vacancies for local charge compensation also tends to cause lattice contraction. Therefore, after Ca–Ti is incorporated to replace K–Na–Nb, the unit cell is expected to contract, leading to a shift of the XRD peaks toward higher angles due to this contraction. As a result of Na2O evaporation, a minimal quantity of the secondary phase KTiNbO5 was observed when *x* ≥ 0.2 in KNN–CT. The doping of Pr does not modify the crystal structure of the ceramic. However, it effectively suppresses the formation of the secondary phase to a certain degree, as shown in Fig. 3(d). A trace amount of the KTiNbO5 secondary phase was identified in the KNN–CT:*y*Pr composition at *y* = 0.01, leading to a reduction in both the bandgap and transmittance (Fig. 2). Given that no secondary phase is observed at higher doping levels, it can be inferred that the impurity phase in KNN–CT:0.01Pr arises solely from the fabrication process.

The SEM results shown in Fig. 4(a) reveal that the pure KNN sample contained many cubic grains with a size of approximately 1.36 inhibited grain growth in the KNN–CT system. As illustrated in Fig. 4, all samples containing the CT additive comprised uniform submicron-sized cubic grains with a limited number of pores that were comparable in size to the grains. The average grain sizes of the KNN–*x*CT samples were approximately 0.2 and 0.15 µm for *x* = 0.1 and 0.3, respectively. The uniform submicron grain size facilitates a relative density of the ceramics exceeding 99% (Fig. 4(f)). A high density, cubic lattice structure, submicron grain size, and pore size are then feasible for achieving high optical transparency in the KNN–CT system. Notably, this high optical transparency is achieved solely through the incorporation of CT into KNN ceramics via a straightforward ceramic process. In addition, the incorporation of Pr resulted in a marginal reduction in the grain size (Figs. S1 and S2 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). Pr was also observed to be uniformly distributed throughout the ceramics (Fig. S3 in the ESM).

### 3.3 Electrical properties

Given that dielectric spectroscopy has proven to be an effective technique for investigating ferroelectric and relaxor systems [25,33], we conducted dielectric experiments on these KNN–*x*CT materials. Figure 5 shows the temperature dependence of * ϵr and tanδ* measured at selected frequencies for the KNN–*x*CT ceramics. Similar to previous reports for pure KNN [34], phase transitions are observed at 400 and 196 °C for pure KNN, which correspond to the cubic-to-tetragonal and tetragonal-to-orthorhombic transitions, respectively. A relaxorlike broad dispersive dielectric maximum can be observed in KNN–*x*CT with *x* = 0.1–0.2. Like other relaxor ferroelectrics, this maximum does not indicate a phase transition to a low-temperature ordered state; rather, it arises from the rapidly increasing polydispersity of the relaxation spectrum upon cooling, leading to a deviation of * ϵr at a specific temperature—dependent on the experimental frequency—from its static value [35].

Importantly, the polarization vs. electric (*P*–*E*) field measurements reveal the absence of a long-range ordered ferroelectric state in KNN–*x*CT with *x* = 0.1–0.2, as evidenced by the detection of a slim hysteresis loop, which is characteristic of relaxors [24,35], as illustrated in Fig. 6. Well-defined PNRs can be directly observed in the Pr-doped KNN–0.175CT by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis, as shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM. The relaxor behavior of KNN–CT ceramics can be attributed to two primary factors: First, the incorporation of nonferroelectric phase CT inhibits the formation of a long-range polar state within the KNN ferroelectric matrix; second, a size effect [36] related to the reduction of ceramic grains to submicron dimensions occurs (Fig. 4).

*
Fig. 6P‒E* loops of KNN‒*x*CT ceramics at 50 kV/cm.

Figure 5 clearly illustrates that as the content of CT increases, the temperature at which * ϵr attains its maximum value decreases. The dielectric maximum in relaxors is a direct consequence of the dynamics of PNRs [25,35]. At lower temperatures, these dynamics slow, leading to a decrease in  ϵr when the longest relaxation times exceed the experimental frequency window. This behavior indicates that the average relaxation time of a PNR is shorter in samples with higher CT contents. Consequently, it can be inferred that the average size of the PNRs in KNN–CT decreases with increasing CT concentration, likely due to the increased disorder imposed on the ferroelectric KNN matrix. The dielectric constant also exhibits a pronounced decrease with increasing CT content, which can still be attributed to the increased disorder. Importantly, a moderate level of Pr doping does not significantly influence the dielectric properties of the KNN–CT ceramics but rather results in only minor changes in the magnitude of the dielectric constant and the maximum dielectric temperature (Figs. S5 and S6 in the ESM).

In general, the exceptional physical properties of relaxor ferroelectrics are attributed to the presence of so-called PNRs. PNRs exhibit dynamic behavior between Burn’s temperature (T*B), below which PNRs are observed, and the freezing or transition temperature (*T*f), below which PNRs either evolve into a ferroelectric microdomain state or transition into a dipole‒glass state [24,37]. *T*B is typically significantly higher than the maximum point of the dielectric constant *T*m, whereas *T*f is generally much lower than *T*m. For the prototypical relaxor PbMg1/3Nb2/3O3, *T*B has been reported to be 620 K, approximately 350 K above *T*m, whereas *T*f is measured at 230 K [37]. On the basis of prior experience, although this is an indirect result, the *T*f of relaxor ferroelectrics can be determined by fitting the Vogel–Fulcher equation, whereas *T*B can be obtained by fitting the Curie–Weiss equation [25,37]. Figure S7 in the ESM illustrates the relevant processing steps, resulting in the determination of *T*B = 536 K and *T*f = 325 K for the KNN–0.175CT ceramics. In other words, PNRs can exist across a broad temperature range, and in addition to their significant influence on ferroelectric, piezoelectric, and dielectric properties, they may also serve as local structural control parameters for modulating luminescence and other related characteristics.

### 3.4 PL properties

The PLE and PL properties of Pr-doped 0.825KNN–0.175CT ceramics (hereafter referred to as 0.175CT) and 0.7KNN–0.3CT ceramics (hereafter referred to as 0.3CT) were systematically investigated. As illustrated in Fig. 7(a), the PLE spectrum of 0.175CT:0.001Pr ceramics was recorded at an emission wavelength of 610 nm, revealing two prominent excitation peaks centered at 332 and 450 nm. The 332 nm peak shows a broad spectrum, which could originate from the 4f → 5d transition of Pr3+, host absorption, and Pr3+–Nb5+/Ti4+ IVCT [13,17]. The peak at 450 nm could be attributed to the 3H4 → 3P2 transition of Pr3+. Additionally, the 0.3CT:0.001Pr sample exhibited analogous excitation spectra characterized by two prominent peaks (Fig. S7 in the ESM).

*

(d) Comparative analysis of PL emission intensity for 0.175CT:*y*Pr ceramics. em denotes the emission wavelength used for PLE spectra, while ex represents the excitation wavelength used for PL spectra.

The PL spectra of the 0.175CT:*y*Pr ceramics are presented in Figs. 7(b) and 7(c). Under excitation at 332 nm, the emission spectra exhibit two peaks at 561 and 610 nm, corresponding to 3P0 → 3H5 and 1D2 → 3H4, respectively. In contrast, under excitation at 450 nm, the emission spectra display five prominent peaks at 531, 551, 610, 618, and 654 nm, which correspond to 3P1 → 3H5, 3P0 → 3H5, 1D2 → 3H4, 3P0 → 3H6, and 3P0 → 3F2, respectively, as schematically illustrated in Fig. S9 in the ESM [21]. This phenomenon can be attributed to the severe cross-relaxation (CR) between the 4f-5d state and the 3H4 level, i.e., 4f-5d + 3H4 → 1D2 + 1D2 (Fig. S9 in the ESM). This results in the 1D2 → 3H4 peak at 610 nm being significantly stronger than the other peaks when excited at 332 nm. Furthermore, the emission intensity initially tends to increase but subsequently decreases, reaching its maximum at a doping concentration of 0.01. The increasing portion is attributed to the increasing number of luminescent centers, whereas the decreasing portion arises from the concentration-quenching effect. The 0.3CT:*y*Pr samples exhibit concentration quenching at lower Pr contents (Fig. S8 in the ESM), which can be attributed to the increased Ca content providing a greater number of red luminescent centers through the substitution of Pr.

The IVCT energy in Pr3+-doped KNN‒CT ceramics can be estimated via an empirical relationship expressed as Eq. (4) [38]:

(4) IVCT(Pr3+)=58,800−49,800χ(Mn+)d(Pr3+−Mn+)

where *χ*(M*n*+) (Nb5+ = 1.85, Ti4+ = 2.05) is the optical electronegativity and *d*(Pr3+–M*n*+) (*d*(Pr3+–Nb5+) = 3.05 Å, *d*(Pr3+–Ti4+) = 3.18 Å) is the shortest Pr3+–M*n*+ bond distance [39,40]. The energies of the IVCT bands for Pr-doped KNN and Pr-doped CT are calculated as 28,593 cm−1 (350 nm) and 26,696 cm−1 (375 nm). The variation in IVCT energy accounts for the observed shift behavior, wherein the broad excitation peak transitions from 332 nm (for 0.175CT:*y*Pr) to 360 nm (for 0.3CT:*y*Pr).

Temperature-dependent PL spectra of the 0.175CT:0.01Pr ceramics were recorded under excitation at 332 nm (Fig. 8(a)) and 450 nm (Fig. 8(b)). Over the temperature range of 300–500 K, the PL intensity of the 1D2 → 3H4 transition at 610 nm rapidly decreased with increasing temperature, whereas that of the 3P0 → 3H5 transition at 561 nm decreased only slightly and remained nearly constant. This temperature-dependent behavior markedly differs from that observed in other Pr3+-doped systems, such as La2Zr2O7 [17] and Ca2LaNbO6 [14].

*
Fig. 8Temperature-dependent emission spectra of 0.175CT:0.01Pr ceramics, recorded from 300 to 500 K under excitation at (a) 332 nm and (b) 450 nm, along with corresponding CIE coordinate diagrams of emitted colors.

To elucidate the temperature-dependent luminescent intensity of Pr-doped KNN–CT, the temperature-dependent population evolution of the excited states of interest is schematically illustrated via a configurational coordinate diagram, as shown in Fig. S8 in the ESM. The distinct decreasing trends observed in the 1D2 → 3H4 transition and the 3P0 → 3H5 transition can be attributed to the interference effect caused by IVCT states of Pr3+–Nb5+ and Pr3+–Ti4+. Upon pumping to Pr3+–Ti4+ IVCT, there was a population allocation between 3P0 and 1D2 states. With increasing temperature, the electrons populated in the 3P0 state relaxed to the low-lying 1D2 state via Pr3+–Ti4+ IVCT, which changed the relative population between the 3P0 and 1D2 states. On the other hand, the population distribution and the 3P0 → 3H5 and 1D2 → 3H4 transitions are also influenced by temperature-dependent electrical polarization [20]. Therefore, FIR between 3P0 → 3H5 and 1D2 → 3H4 transitions was strongly affected by temperature. Furthermore, the corresponding CIE chromaticity coordinates also vary with changes in temperature (Figs. 8(c) and 8(d)). Under excitation at 332 nm, the emission color shifts from red to white, with the coordinates transitioning from (0.5653, 0.3434) to (0.2718, 0.3641). In contrast, under excitation at 450 nm, the emission color shifts from light red to tangerine, with the coordinates changing from (0.5301, 0.4517) to (0.3877, 0.5761).

Figure 9(a) presents the so-called FIR of the 1D2 → 3H4 emission to the 3P0 → 3H5 emission by referring to the maximum emission intensity at 610 and 561 nm in the PL spectra. ## 4 Conclusions

In summary, the incorporation of CT results in KNN ceramics with submicrometer grain sizes, high densities, and pseudocubic phases. This modification yields a transmittance of 78% at 1336 nm and 69.4% at 780 nm, along with relaxor ferroelectric characteristics for the sample containing 0.175 CT. Furthermore, Pr3+ doping does not affect the microstructure or transmittance of KNN–CT transparent ceramics. However, it significantly enhances the PL intensity. By utilizing the temperature-dependent PL and FIR models of the 3P0 → 3H5 and 1D2 → 3H4 transitions, a noncontact optical temperature detection technique can be realized. Under excitation at 332 nm, the FIR increases by as much as 75-fold when the temperature increases from room temperature to 500 K, resulting in exceptional absolute sensitivity (*S*a = 0.139 K−1) and relative sensitivity (*S*r = 2.69 %/K), indicating significant potential for application in optical temperature sensors. This exceptional FIR performance is believed to be closely linked to the temperature-sensitive polar nanoregions in KNN–CT. Our results demonstrate that the integration of specialized polar nanostructures with PL may enhance optical thermometry by providing improved sensitivity and a broad operational temperature range.
