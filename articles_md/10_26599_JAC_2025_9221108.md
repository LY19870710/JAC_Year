## 1 Introduction

Reversible solid oxide cells (R-SOCs) are highly efficient devices capable of interconverting chemical energy and electrical energy [1,2]. Nevertheless, the commercialization of conventional R-SOCs with oxygen ion-conductive electrolytes is hindered by the durability and cost arising from the high operating temperature [3,4]. Reversible protonic ceramic electrochemical cells (R-PCECs) have the potential to operate at 400–600 °C because of the excellent conductivity and small activation energy (*E*a of 0.44–0.6 eV) of proton-conducting electrolytes [5]. Reduced operating temperatures can increase the system’s economy and longevity by enabling the use of less costly components and enhancing material stability [6]. However, the insufficient activity and durability of the oxygen electrode remain challenges for the evolution of R-PCECs [7]. First, the oxygen electrode should exhibit substantial catalytic performance for both the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER) within a low temperature range [8]. Second, the oxygen electrode must be sufficiently tolerant to steam because water is generated in or fed to the oxygen electrode when the R-PCEC is operated in the fuel cell (FC) or electrolysis cell (EC) mode [3,9,10]. Third, the oxygen electrode is always under polarization when the cell is working, which may lead to element segregation [11].

Although commercial La0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3−*δ* (LSCF) has been utilized as the oxygen electrode of R-PCECs, it has sluggish ORR and OER kinetics and insufficient stability [12,13]. Double-perovskite oxides, such as PrBaCo2O5+*δ* and PrBa0.5Sr0.5Co1.5Fe0.5O5+*δ* (PBSCF), have demonstrated high catalytic activity for R-PCECs [14,15] but still suffer from stability issues due to A-site cation segregation [16]. Other materials, such as BaCo0.4Fe0.4Nb0.1Sc0.1O3−*δ* [17], Fe0.6Mn0.6Co0.6Ni0.6Cr0.6O4 [18], and PrSrCo1.8Nb0.2O6−*δ* [19], have been developed. Nevertheless, most research has focused on increasing the catalytic activity, paying little attention to the durability of oxygen electrodes under high-humidity conditions [20,21]. Compared with the oxygen ion-conducting electrolyte-based R-SOCs operated under a typical steam concentration of 50 vol% or above, most of the R-PCECs operated under a relatively low steam concentration (~3 vol%) [22,23]. In practical applications, a high steam concentration can benefit steam utilization and the efficiency of R-PCEC for water electrolysis [24]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for a comprehensive investigation of the stability of oxygen electrode materials under high steam concentrations, including the factors affecting their performance and the underlying degradation mechanisms.

Among the oxygen electrode materials, perovskite-structured BaFe0.4Co0.4Zr0.1Y0.1O3−*δ* (BFCZY) exhibits proton‒electron‒oxygen ion triple conduction and high catalytic activity for the ORR and OER at low temperatures [25,26]. However, it suffers from stability issues in humid air. According to Lee *et al*. [27], the polarization resistance (*R*p) of the BFCZY oxygen electrode significantly increased from 0.818 to 1.893 Ω·cm2 after testing at 550 °C with 3 vol% H2O for 100 h. The rapid degradation (0.551 Ω·cm2/h, 550 °C, 50 vol% H2O) of the BFCZY oxygen electrode was also similarly noted by Marcos [28]. Qi *et al*. [29] reported the formation of BaCO3 impurities in BFCZY after calcination in humidified air. Nevertheless, systematic studies on the degradation mechanisms of BFCZY under cell operating conditions are currently lacking. It has been reported that A-site doping of K, Na, and Li in BFCZY can facilitate proton uptake and conduction pathways, resulting in high electrocatalytic activity and durability for both ORR and OER [30]. Additionally, the partial doping of Ni and Mg in the B-site of BFCZY can dramatically enhance the catalytic activity and stability in humidified air [31−33]. In addition, heterointerface engineering shows great potential for enhancing the electrocatalytic performance and durability of materials for R-SOCs [34,35]. For example, the LSCF oxygen electrode modified with Pr1−*x*Ba*x*CoO3−*δ*–BaCoO3−*δ* heterointerfaces exhibited high activity resulting from improved kinetics for surface exchange of oxygen, surface diffusion of protons, and O2 and H2O decomposition processes [36]. In addition, by constructing a La0.6Sr0.4Co*x*Fe*y*O3−*δ*–PrCoO3–Co3O4–PrO2 polyphase heterostructure, the cell showed 2.5 times greater electrochemical activity and excellent durability [37]. Gu *et al*. [38] also discovered that the heterostructure between BaTb0.8Co0.2O3 and BaCoO3 (BCO) can enhance the electrochemical performance by significantly accelerating the charge transfer kinetics.

In this work, the degradation mechanism of the BFCZY oxygen electrode at 550–650 °C under 0–50 vol% steam was comprehensively studied. A straightforward solution infiltration technique was used to create BCO–BFCZY heterointerfaces in the oxygen electrode. The BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode exhibited significantly enhanced activity and durability for the ORR and OER, resulting in the high performance and durability of the R-PCECs. Furthermore, the ORR/OER kinetics and durability of the BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode were examined through density functional theory (DFT) research.

### 2.1 Preparation of materials and catalyst infiltration

BFCZY and BCO oxides were prepared via a chemical sol–gel approach as described in our previous work [39]. An ammonia aqueous mixture was then added to adjust the pH to 7. The solution became a gel when the water evaporated, and the primary precursor was obtained by heating the gel at 300 °C for 10 h. Finally, the precursor underwent calcination at 1000 °C for 5 h to produce BFCZY powder. After dry pressing into 20 mm diameter pellets, BFCZY was sintered at 1250 °C for 5 h.

The precursor solution of 0.1 mol/L BCO catalyst for infiltration was fabricated via the same method as that reported in Ref. [40]. The BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode was prepared by infiltrating 2/3/4 µL of BCO solution into the BFCZY electrode and firing at 850 °C for 2 h.

### 2.2 Cell fabrication

Symmetrical cells: Commercial BaCe0.7Zr0.1Y0.1Yb0.1O3−*δ* (BCZYYb7111) electrolyte powder (Ruier Powder Materials Co., Ltd., China) mixed with 1% NiO (sintering aid) was dry-pressed into pellets (13.0 mm) and then sintered at 1450 °C for 5 h. BFCZY was mixed with ethylcellulose-terpineol (5 wt%) at a mass ratio of 1 : 1 to prepare an oxygen electrode slurry. The BCZYYb7111 electrolyte pellet was subsequently coated with an electrode slurry via screen printing on each side, with an effective area of 0.283 cm2. The symmetrical cells were subsequently sintered at 950 °C in a furnace for 3 h.

Three-electrode cells: First, 1 g of commercial BCZYYb7111 powder mixed with a 1% NiO sintering aid was dry-pressed to create electrolyte pellets (20.0 mm), which were then sintered at 1450 °C for 5 h. The BFCZY oxygen electrode was screen-printed in the same way as symmetrical cells. Ag, as a reference electrode, was applied to the side of the BCZYYb7111 electrolyte with a brush. To ensure that the impedance of the electrodes on both sides can be well distinguished, the active electrode and the electrolyte edges are separated by 5 mm [41,42].

Single cells: The preparation of hydrogen electrode-supported half-cells was performed as described in Ref. [24]. The BFCZY oxygen electrode (0.196 cm2 by screen printing) in a single cell was calcined at 950 °C for 3 h in an atmosphere of air.

### 2.3 Material characterizations and electrochemical measurements

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation was used to analyze the XRD patterns of the BFCZY and BCO samples. The morphology of the BFCZY and BCO–BFCZY pellets and cells was observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; MAIA3 LMH, Tescan, the Czech Republic). The microstructures of the BFCZY and BCO–BFCZY samples were investigated via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnao G2 F20, FEI, USA). A Raman spectroscope (SENTERRA, Bruker, Germany) was used to analyze the phase structure of the treated BFCZY samples.

The electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) of the symmetrical cells were evaluated at 550–650 °C in air with 0–50 vol% H2O (50 sccm). The concentration of steam was regulated through a temperature-controlled water bubbler. The EIS data of the symmetrical cells were measured through an electrochemical workstation (SP-300, BioLogic, France) at frequencies ranging from 100 mHz to 5 MHz with an amplitude of 10 mV.

The durability of the three-electrode cell was evaluated at 600 °C in 30% H2O–air (50 sccm). The polarization current density was 500 mA/cm2. The BFCZY electrode working in anodic polarization was characterized by an EC electrode, and the other electrode working in cathodic polarization was characterized by an FC electrode. Upon stopping the polarization current for 20 min, an EIS test of the three-electrode cell was conducted within the frequency range of 50 mHz to 5 MHz with an amplitude of 10 mV.

For single-cell measurements, the hydrogen electrode was fed dry H2 (20 sccm), and the oxygen electrode was exposed to dry air (40 sccm) in FC mode. In EC mode, the oxygen electrode was fed 30% H2O–air (40 sccm). The density‒voltage‒power density (*I*‒*V*‒*P*) curve and EIS (0.1 Hz to 5 MHz, 10 mV) at 550–700 °C of the cells were tested via an electrochemical workstation.

### 2.4 Computational methods

DFT calculations were carried out via the first-principles simulation package atomic-orbital-based *ab initio* computation at USTc (ABACUS 3.6.3) [43,44]. Optimized norm-converting Vanderbilt (ONCV) pseudopotentials and hierarchically optimized standard atomic orbitals of SG15-V1.0 pseudopotentials were used. A cutoff energy of 100 Ry and a threshold of 1×10−7 Ry were applied to the wave functions. In all the models, the maximum force convergence on atoms was set to 0.04 eV/Å. The 2 × 2 × 2 supercell structures of BFCZY and BaCoO3 were used. Other details can be found in our previous work [39]. For the surface structures of BFCZY and BaCoO3−BFCZY, the lower 5 atomic layers are still constrained. The DFT-D3 method was employed to incorporate van der Waals correction, and the finite difference method was used to obtain the vibrational modes of the absorption molecules. Equation (1) calculates the formation energy of an oxygen vacancy (* Ev):

(1) Ev=Edefect_refect+0.5×EO2−Ebulk

where  Edefect_refect and  Ebulk represent the total energies of the optimized structures with or without an oxygen vacancy, respectively, and  EO2 is the energy of O2.

The hydration energy (∆E*hydr) was computed via Eq. (2):

(2) ΔEhydr=E2OHO⋅−Edefect+EH2O(g)

where * E2OHO⋅ corresponds to the electronic energy of the material structure incorporating two protons, and  EH2O(g) corresponds to the energy of a H2O molecule [45].

The changes in Gibbs free energy that take place when moving from the beginning to the end of the reaction state were evaluated as Eq. (3):

(3) ΔG=ΔE+ΔZPE−TΔS

where ∆E* represents the electronic energy change derived from ground-state DFT calculations, ∆ZPE denotes the zero-point energy change of the adsorbates, *T* is the temperature, and ∆*S* corresponds to the entropy change. For H2O contamination, the reaction equation for BaCoO3 is shown in Eq. (4):

(4) 2BaCoO3+2H2O→2Ba(OH)2+2CoO+O2

The equation used in calculating the reaction Gibbs free energy is shown in Eq. (5):

(5) ΔG=ΔE0K+ΔZPE+ΔHT,298.15K−T×ΔST,0K

where  ΔG is the reaction Gibbs free energy,  ΔE0K is the electronic energy at 0 K, ∆ZPE is the zero-point enthalpy,  ΔHT,298.15K is the temperature-dependent enthalpy at 298.15 K and  ΔST,0K is the entropy at 0 K.

### 3.1 Degradation mechanism of BFCZY oxygen electrode

To gain deeper insights into the degradation mechanism of BFCZY in humid air, both powder and bulk samples were prepared and characterized under different conditions. Figures 1(a) and 1(b) show surface SEM images of the dense BFCZY pellet before and after holding at 550 °C in 30 vol% H2O–air for 100 h, respectively. The surface of the untreated BFCZY pellet was almost clean, whereas the heat-treated pellet had small particles on the grains. The results from energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) show that the exsolved particles are Ba-enriched phases (Fig. 1(c)). A similar phenomenon has been confirmed in previous reports that these nanoparticles may be formed by the chemical reaction of Ba with H2O and CO2 in air [46,47].

Figure 1(d) shows the XRD patterns of the BFCZY powder kept in air at 550–650 °C with different steam concentrations. After treatment in humidified air, a second phase with characteristic peaks at approximately 24° is observed, which can be attributed to orthorhombic BaCO3 (Pmcn* space group). The intensity of the BaCO3 characteristic peaks increases with decreasing temperature and increasing steam concentration, indicating that BFCZY is less stable under low-temperature and high-humidity conditions. In contrast, no BaCO3 (PDF#71-2394) was observed after the samples were kept at 650 °C in dry air for 100 h, which is consistent with previous studies [29,48]. The XRD refinement results of BFCZY before (Fig. 1(e)) and after (Fig. 1(f)) the treatment indicate that the amount of BaCO3 in BFCZY was approximately 2.82% after treatment for 100 h at 550 °C in 30 vol% H2O–air. The high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) image of the treated BFCZY shows lattice fringe spacings of 0.37 and 0.29 nm, as demonstrated in Fig. 1(g), which are consistent with the (111) facets of BaCO3 and (110) facets of BFCZY, respectively [49].

The BaCO3 second phase of BFCZY after treatment was also confirmed by the Raman spectra, as shown in Fig. 1(h). H2O may aggravate the separation of Ba in BFCZY to form Ba(OH)2 on the surface, which in turn interacts with CO2 to form BaCO3 [50,51]. The stability of BFCZY against H2O at different temperatures may be related to the thermodynamic stability of BaCO3 [52]. Figure 1(i) shows the O 1s XPS spectra of BFCZY before and after treatment. The spectra were resolved into four subpeaks, including lattice oxygen (O2−), highly oxidative oxygen (O22−/O−), adsorbed oxygen (OH−/O2), and adsorbed H2O [53,54]. As shown in Fig. 1(i), the content of O2− in BFCZY increases from 8.7% to 17.6%, whereas O22−/O− decreases from 16.7% to 15.3% after steam treatment. As reported, the amount of oxidative oxygen in the BFCZY oxygen electrode material is related to the concentration of oxygen vacancies, which serve as routes for ionic transport and active sites for electrochemical reactions [55,56]. The Co 2p and Fe 2p XPS data of BFCZY before and after treatment were also fitted, as shown in Fig. S4(a) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The Co 2p and Fe 2p XPS data both revealed a decrease in the content of high-valent elements. This reduces the electron transport path [57] and the oxygen vacancy concentration [58]. These results cause a decline in the performance of the BFCZY oxygen electrode.

In addition, the oxygen desorption behavior of the BFCZY powder before and after storage at 600 °C in 30% H2O–air was investigated via oxygen temperature programmed desorption (O2-TPD). As presented in Fig. S1 in the ESM, the strength of both the α and β peaks decreases after treatment, indicating the decreased oxygen adsorption capacity of BFCZY [59,60]. The reduced oxygen vacancy concentration and decreased oxygen adsorption capability of BFCZY may lead to decreased catalytic activity for the ORR and OER [17].

The stability of the BFCZY oxygen electrode was further investigated by fabricating and measuring BCZYYb7111 electrolyte-based symmetrical cells. As demonstrated in Fig. 2(a), the degradation rates of *R*p of the BFCZY oxygen electrode are 0.011, 0.049, and 0.108 Ω·cm2/100 h when exposed to dry air, 3% H2O–air, and 30% H2O–air at 650 °C, respectively. The degradation rate clearly increases with increasing steam concentration, which may be attributed to the promoted Ba segregation in the highly humid atmosphere, as discussed above [61]. Furthermore, the BFCZY oxygen electrode has a higher degradation rate at lower temperatures. As observed in Fig. 2(b), under 30 vol% H2O–air, the degradation rates of the BFCZY electrode are 2.589, 0.384, and 0.108 Ω·cm2/100 h at 550, 600, and 650 °C, respectively. The EIS evolution of the BFCZY oxygen electrode during the durability test in 30 vol% H2O–air at 550, 600, and 650 °C is shown in Figs. 2(c) and 2(d) and Fig. S2(a) in the ESM, respectively. The corresponding distributions of relaxation time (DRT) analysis of the EIS data are also presented in Figs. 2(e) and 2(f) and Fig. S2(b) in the ESM. The EIS plots can be separated into three distinct peaks: a high-frequency peak (HF; > 1000 Hz) related to charge transfer, an intermediate-frequency peak (IF; 1–1000 Hz) associated with oxygen surface exchange, and a low-frequency peak (LF; 62,63]. The resistances in the LF and IF dominate the overall *R*p of the BFCZY electrode and increase with increasing test duration. The typical EIS curves of the BFCZY symmetrical cell treated with 3 vol% H2O–air under different oxygen partial pressures (*P*O2) are depicted in Fig. S3(a) in the ESM. The resistance in the IF of the BFCZY electrode increases with decreasing *P*O2 and shows a reaction order of 0.231 (~1/4) (Fig. S3(b) in the ESM), suggesting that the IF resistance might correspond to the oxygen surface exchange process. Thus, the degradation of BFCZY in humid air may be due to deteriorated surface-related reaction activity.

As presented in Figs. 2(g) and 2(h), no obvious morphological changes were detected in the morphology of the BFCZY oxygen electrode cross-sections before and after 100 h in dry air at 650 °C. In contrast, when the steam concentration is increased to 30 vol%, the BFCZY electrode shows particle coarsening and a decrease in porosity after the durability test (Fig. 2(i)). The electrode coarsening and densification issues become even more severe at 550 °C (Fig. 2(j)). The increased particle size and decreased porosity decrease the number of triple-phase boundary active sites for the ORR and OER, which leads to deterioration of the BFCZY electrode [64].

To further understand the degradation behavior of the BFCZY electrode under cell operating conditions, a three-electrode method (Fig. 2(k)) was employed. The stability test was conducted at 600 °C in 30 vol% H2O–air with a polarization current of 500 mA/cm2 (~1.0 V). The decay rate (*R*p) of the BFCZY electrode under cathodic polarization (0.798 Ω·cm2/100 h) is greater than that under anodic polarization (0.419 Ω·cm2/100 h), as revealed in Fig. 2(l). In other words, the degradation of the BFCZY oxygen electrode in the FC mode is more severe than that in the EC mode, which is consistent with previous studies [65,66]. The ohmic resistance (*R*o) of the BFCZY electrode remains almost constant under anodic polarization but gradually increases under cathodic polarization (Fig. 2(m)).

Interestingly, after the stability test, many white impurities are observed on the BFCZY electrode under cathodic polarization (FC mode), especially at the interface between the electrode and the electrolyte, whereas the electrode under anodic polarization (EC mode) shows no obvious change (Figs. 2(n) and 2(o)). The generated white impurity was verified to be BaCO3 via XRD characterization (Fig. 2(p)). In the FC mode, steam is generated at the BFCZY–BCZYYb7111 interfaces or triple phase boundaries. The generated steam may promote the segregation of Ba in the BCZYYb7111 electrolyte and the BFCZY electrode. The segregated Ba further reacts with H2O and CO2 in air to generate BaCO3, leading to a decrease in the performance of the BFCZY electrode. We believe that this interesting finding may help to explain why the degradation rate in the FC mode is much higher than that in the EC mode for R-PCECs.

### 3.2 Enhanced activity and durability of the BFCZY oxygen electrode

To increase the activity and durability of the BFCZY oxygen electrode, BCO-BFCZY heterointerfaces were created via a solution infiltration process. The XRD patterns in Fig. 3(a) show the distinctive peaks of BCO (PDF 74-0902) and BFCZY for the BCO infiltrated with BFCZY. The diffraction peaks of impregnated BFCZY are offset, and the interlayer spacing is slightly smaller, indicating that strain is generated within the lattice [67]. Figure 3(b) shows the TEM image of the BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode. The composition of the BCO nanoparticles was confirmed by HR-TEM and EDX, as shown in Fig. 3(c) and Fig. S5 in the ESM. As depicted in Fig. 3(d). HR-TEM revealed diffraction spacings of 0.29 and 0.35 nm associated with orthorhombic BCO(101) and BFCZY(110) perovskite, respectively, indicating that these two phases are well connected to each other with a clear heterointerface [68]. Figure S6 in the ESM and Fig. 3(e) show a picture of the BCO–BFCZY/BCZYYb7111 cross-section and a high-resolution image of the BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode, respectively. The oxygen electrode bonded well with the BCZYYb7111 electrolyte, and the thickness was approximately 13 μm. BCO nanoparticles can be observed on the porous BFCZY oxygen electrode (~7.5 wt%, 3 μL loading). The O 1s, Co 2p, and Fe 2p XPS spectra of BCO–BFCZY were fitted, as shown in Fig. S7 in the ESM. Compared with those in BFCZY, the amounts of oxygen in the high oxidation state and of Fe and Co in the low oxidation state increase, which suggests that more oxygen vacancies are formed at the surface. After being exposed to 30 vol% H2O–air for 100 h at 550 °C, the phase structure of BCO–BFCZY shows no change, demonstrating its high phase stability (Fig. 3(f)). A BCO coating with excellent stability in humid air may be of vital importance in suppressing the formation of BaCO3 in BFCZY [69]. To further confirm the stability of the BCO-coated BFCZY, a dense BFCZY pellet with a BCO coating was prepared and treated in 30 vol% H2O–air at 550 °C for 100 h. No BaCO3 was detected by EDX characterization, as indicated in Fig. S8 in the ESM.

The rate-determining steps can be inferred from the n* values obtained from the *R*p variation with * PO2 [71]. As shown in Fig. 4(e), the resistance in the IF range (R*IF) of the BCO–BFCZY electrode increases with decreasing * PO2, exhibiting a reaction order of 0.202 (~1/4). This suggests that R*IF may be associated with the oxygen surface exchange elementary reaction. Figure 4(f) presents the stability of *R*p for symmetrical cells with bare BFCZY and BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrodes with different loadings at 600 °C. The 3 μL BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode exhibited superior stability. The decline rate of *R*p decreases from 0.384 Ω·cm2/100 h (BFCZY) to 0.026 Ω·cm2/100 h for the 3 μL BCO–BFCZY when measured in 30 vol% H2O–air at 600 °C. In addition, even under a high steam concentration (50 vol%), the BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode demonstrates exceptional stability, with a low decay rate of 0.054 Ω·cm2/100 h. Furthermore, the stability of the BCO–BFCZY electrode outperforms that of recently reported oxygen electrodes, as summarized in Table S1 in the ESM. Although desirable improvements have been achieved, further optimization is needed for future commercial applications. Figure S9 in the ESM shows that the *R*o of the BCO–BFCZY electrodes remain consistent, indicating a robust electrode‒electrolyte interface. The evolution of the EIS of the 3 μL BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode during the durability test is presented in Fig. 4(g). No significant variation is observed, which matches the results derived from the DRT analysis (Fig. 4(h)). Research indicates that surface catalyst coatings can effectively enhance the stability of oxygen electrodes, which reduces the driving force for Ba segregation to suppress the oxygen vacancy concentration at the electrode/catalyst heterointerface [72,73]. Figure 4(i) shows the morphology of the 3 μL BCO–BFCZY electrode after the stability test, which is identical to that of the as-prepared electrode (Fig. 3(e)), confirming its high stability.

### 3.3 Understanding enhanced electrochemical activity and stability by heterointerface engineering

To better understand the increased electrocatalytic activity and durability of the BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode, DFT calculations were carried out. Figure 5(a) shows a schematic diagram of the ORR and OER reactions in the heterostructured BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode. The supercell structures of Ba8Co3Fe3Zr1Y1O28 and BaCoO3 were constructed. The calculated minimum energy structures of BFCZY, BFCZY–CoFe–OH, BFCZY–CoY–OH, BaCoO3, and BaCoO3–OH are shown in Fig. S10 in the ESM. Figure 5(b) shows the formation energy of *E*v and hydration energy of BaCoO3 and BFCZY at different O sites (BFCZY–CoY and BFCZY–FeCo). For BFCZY, the smallest *E*v is 1.100 eV (BFCZY–FeCo), which is higher than the 0.799 eV of BaCoO3 [74]. The lower *E*v enhances the catalytic activity of the BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode [45,75]. The hydration energy of BaCoO3 (0.406 eV) is much lower than that of BFCZY–FeCo (1.049 eV) but higher than the hydration energy of BFCZY-CoY (−1.5217 eV). Figure 5(c) shows the projected densities of states (PDOS) of BaCoO3 and BFCZY after hydration. The O-p band center of BaCoO3 (2.24 eV) is slightly lower than that of BFCZY (2.45 eV), which is closer to the Fermi level, indicating that BaCoO3 has enhanced ORR and OER activities after hydration. The surface structures of BFCZY and BaCoO3–BFCZY are shown in Figs. S11(a) and S11(b) in the ESM. The O-p band center of the BaCoO3–BFCZY surface (2.90 eV) is slightly lower than that of BFCZY (2.97 eV), which is closer to the Fermi level, as shown in Fig. S11(c) in the ESM. The adsorption energy of O2 can be obtained in Fig. S11(d) in the ESM as −2.12 and −4.00 eV for BFCZY and BaCoO3–BFCZY, respectively. Additionally, the Gibbs free energy of the reaction of H2O with BaCoO3 is shown in Fig. 5(d). The information regarding the Gibbs free energy change (* ΔG), entropy change ( ΔS), and specific heat capacity at constant volume (C*v) for BaCoO3, Ba(OH)2, H2O, and CoO oxides, as well as the Gibbs free energy change of H2 and O2 depending on temperature, is provided in Fig. S12 in the ESM. This result indicates the improved stability of BaCoO3 with increasing temperature in a humid atmosphere. These DFT results confirm the enhanced ORR and OER activity and favorable stability of the heterostructured BCO–BFCZY electrode under high steam concentration conditions, which is consistent with the experimental results.

### 3.4 Performance of R-PCECs with the BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode

R-PCECs containing the BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode loaded with 3 μL BCO demonstrated enhanced electrochemical performance and durability in both FC and EC modes. As demonstrated in Fig. 6(a), the peak power densities (PPDs) of the cell with the BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode at 550, 600, 650, and 700 °C are 0.44, 0.62, 0.82, and 1.04 W/cm2, respectively. Figure 6(b) shows the typical EIS plots of the PCCs measured at temperatures ranging from 700 to 550 °C under open-circuit voltage (OCV) *R*o values of 0.13, 0.16, 0.17, and 0.19 Ω·cm2, and *R*p of 0.06, 0.13, 0.28, and 0.59 Ω·cm2 are obtained at 700, 650, 600, and 550 °C, respectively. This implies that *R*o at temperatures exceeding 600 °C and *R*p at temperatures below 600 °C are the primary factors limiting cell performance. Figure 6(c) shows the current‒voltage (*I*‒*V*) plots of the cell tested in EC mode at 550–700 °C when the hydrogen electrode was exposed to hydrogen and the oxygen electrode was fed 3 vol% H2O–air. The current densities at 1.3 V are 0.65, 0.98, 1.53, and 2.41 A/cm2 at 550, 600, 650, and 700 °C, respectively. Varying the H2O concentration from 3 to 50 vol% improved the performance of the electrolysis cell, as shown in Fig. 6(d). Figures 6(e) and 6(f) show the contrast of PPDs and electrolysis current densities (at 1.3 V) of the single cell with those of the bare BFCZY and BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrodes at 600 °C. The R-PCECs with the BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode show much better durability in the EC mode at 600 °C in 30% H2O–air (Fig. 6(g)). In addition, when the cell alternately operates between the EC (1.3 V) and FC modes (0.75 V) under 30 vol% H2O–air at 650 °C, stable performance (Fig. 6(h)) is demonstrated. An SEM image of the cell cross-section after durability was measured in EC mode is shown in Fig. 6(i), which consists of a BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode, a BCZYYb7111 electrolyte (~10 μm), and a Ni-BCZYYb7111 hydrogen electrode. The bonding between the BFCZY oxygen electrode and the BCZYYb7111 electrolyte is well maintained, and there is no noticeable coarsening in the BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode after the durability measurement, confirming its high stability.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, the degradation mechanism of the BFCZY oxygen electrode under humid air has been systematically studied. The degradation can be ascribed to the formation of BaCO3 due to the water-promoted segregation of elemental Ba, which could affect the oxygen surface exchange of BFCZY. The degradation is more severe at lower temperatures, at higher steam concentrations, and in fuel cell mode. Both the activity and stability of the BFCZY oxygen electrode can be remarkably improved through the construction of BCO–BFCZY heterointerfaces via BCO coating. Moreover, DFT calculations confirmed the high ORR and OER activities and the high stability of the BCO–BFCZY electrode. Furthermore, the R-PCECs with the BCO–BFCZY oxygen electrode exhibited enhanced performance and stability in both the FC mode and the EC mode. This study established a foundation for research on the stability of oxygen electrodes and proposed a promising technology to improve the electrochemical performance and durability of oxygen electrodes for R-PCECs.
