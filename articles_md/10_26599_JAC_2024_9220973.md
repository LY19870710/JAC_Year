## 1 Introduction

Thermal insulation performance and life span are two pivotal indexes of various protective coatings, including thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) [1−3], abradable seal coatings (ASCs) [4−6], and environmental barrier coatings (EBCs) [7−10], and so on [11−16]. Thermal insulation is determined by thermal conductivity, i.e., the lower the thermal conductivity, the higher the thermal insulation [1,17−19]. The life span of coatings is affected by hardness, fracture toughness, thermal expansion coefficient (TEC), high-temperature stability, etc. [1,2,8,20−22]. High hardness and fracture toughness can help coatings resist impacts from outside and inside high-speed particles, which enable them to maintain structural integrity [1,23,24]. Matching TECs between a bond coat (BC) (12×10−6–16×10−6 K−1 at 1200 °C) and ceramic coatings (5×10−6–11×10−6 K−1 at 1200 °C) can reduce the thermal stress at the interface [24,25]. Excellent stability means that coatings do not experience phase transitions at high temperatures [24,26−31]. Low thermal conductivity, high toughness and TECs, and excellent phase stability are necessary for protective coatings.

Traditional TBC yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) ceramics are widely used because of their high fracture toughness, hardness, and TECs [2,24,26]. However, YSZ has relatively high thermal conductivity [17,30,31] and insufficient phase stability [24], which limits its further application. Ferroelastic rare-earth tantalates (RETaO4) [1,2,23,32] and niobates (RENbO4) [1,2] have been studied as new protective coating materials because of their high working temperatures and relatively low thermal conductivity. Compared with other oxides [33−43], RETaO4 and RENbO4 exhibit unique ferroelastic toughening mechanisms, and their working temperatures can be higher than tetragonal–monoclinic (T–M) transition temperatures because it is a second-order process [1]. Compared with RETaO4, RENbO4 has the advantage of low price, which reduces the cost, meaning that RENbO4 has great potential in civil areas. Many researchers have systematically studied RENbO4, finding that RENbO4 has high fracture toughness, excellent oxygen barrier properties, and TECs compatible with substrates [44−46]. However, single-phase RENbO4 ceramics display relatively low hardness (3.3–6.7 GPa) and Young’s modulus (108.1–121.3 GPa), with significantly increased thermal conductivity at high temperatures (≥ 700 °C). To address these shortcomings, we designed and synthesized composite materials to synergistically optimize their mechanical and thermal properties according to previous studies. For example, Limarga *et al*. [48] have realized low thermal conductivity in ZrO2-based composites, which is much lower than that of YSZ. Wang *et al*. [49] introduced YSZ ceramics into tantalates to improve the fracture toughness and reduce thermal conductivity. Both compositions and structures play essential roles in the regulation of properties, and it is possible to concurrently boost the thermal and mechanical properties [47−53].

For composite materials, it is essential to fully consider the chemical compatibility and phase stability of the two constituent phases at high temperatures, which is critical for their long-term use. Typically, selecting two-phase materials composed of the same elements can effectively prevent elemental diffusion reactions and ensure good chemical compatibility. Therefore, RE3NbO7, which has a high hardness (7.8–12.3 GPa) and Young’s modulus (126.3–221.4 GPa), is the preferred second-phase material for RENbO4 [43,45,50]. Previous studies have found that RE3NbO7 exists in two crystal structures, namely, fluorite and weberite. The fluorite structure provides thermal radiation shielding at high temperatures to reduce the thermal conductivity of the composites. Among fluorite RE3NbO7 materials, Tm3NbO7 is favored because of the heavy atomic mass (168.9 g·mol−3) and large atomic radius (109 pm) of the Tm element. Heavy atoms within the material can act as strong phonon scattering centers, thereby reducing the thermal conductivity, and a short atomic radius is beneficial for the anharmonic vibrations of the lattice, which can enhance the TECs [49,50]. These principles are also applicable to TmNbO4. Therefore, RE3NbO7 and RENbO4 exhibit complementary mechanical and thermal properties, and Tm3NbO7 can be introduced into TmNbO4 to form composites that overcome their individual limitations. Additionally, internal thermal stresses within the composite should be considered due to differences in the TECs and modulus between the two ceramic phases. The thermal stress (* σ) is estimated by Eq. (1) [52]:

(1) σ=E1−ν⋅T⋅ΔTEC

where  ν, T*, and *E* are the Poisson’s ratio, the temperature, and the Young’s modulus of the composites, respectively, and ΔTEC is the difference in TECs between the two ceramics in the composites. Thermal stress is affected mainly by ΔTEC. Tm3NbO7 (TEC = 11.1×10−6 K−1 at 1500 °C) is used to increase the properties of TmNbO4 (TEC = 10.4×10−6 K−1 at 1500 °C) because they exhibit similar TECs, which can reduce the inner thermal stress.

Herein, we propose a strategy to synergistically optimize the thermal and mechanical properties of TmNbO4 by introducing Tm3NbO7 as a second phase. We successfully designed and fabricated TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites with varying compositions via spark plasma sintering (SPS). The high-temperature stability, microstructures, and thermal and mechanical properties of TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites were characterized, and we focused on the evolution of hardness, toughness, modulus, thermal conductivity, and TECs in the TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites.

### 2.1 Synthesis of materials

Single-phase TmNbO4 and Tm3NbO7 ceramics were synthesized via a solid-state reaction method, and Tm2O3 (average particle size ≤ 20 µm, Ding Sheng Era Technology, China) and Nb2O5 (average particle size ≤ 20 µm, Ding Sheng Era Technology, China) powders with a purity higher than 99.99% were used in this work. The detailed process was reported in our previous paper [54]. The studied TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites were mixed by ball-milling within C2H5OH in a nylon jar, and the content of TmNbO4 was reduced from 80 to 20 wt%, as shown in Table 1. Abbreviations (Abbr.) of TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites were determined on the basis of the content of TmNbO4, as listed in Table 1. Dense TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites were synthesized via SPS (SPS-632lx, Fuji, Japan), and the heating rate, final sintering temperature, holding time, and pressure were 100 °C·min−1, 1500 °C, 10 min, and 100 MPa, respectively. The prepared ceramic bulk had a diameter of 20 mm and a thickness of 2 mm. Before other measurements, the sintered bulks were held at 1000 °C for 3 h to remove the graphite on their surface because the graphite papers were used to cover TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 powders during SPS.

### 2.2 Structural characterizations

Crystals were determined by an X-ray diffraction device (XRD; MiniFlex 600, Rigaku, Japan) with a detection range of 2*θ* ranging from 25° to 65°, and the scanning speed was 5 (°)·min−1. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-7800F, JEOL, Japan) and an energy-dispersive spectroscope (EDS; 6751A-6UUS-SN, Thermo Scientific, USA) were used to observe the grain sizes, pores, and elemental distributions, and we utilized image analysis software (Image-Pro Plus) to statistically determine the equivalent grain diameter on the surfaces of each sample. Before SEM and EDS measurements, each sample was polished with diamond sandpaper and then hot corroded at 1300 °C for 5 h until grain boundaries formed.

### 2.3 Measurements of thermal properties

TECs of TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 and 7YSZ at 200–1400 °C were measured within a thermal expansion device (DIL 402, NETZSCH, Germany) at a heating rate of 5 °C·min−1. The thermal diffusivity (*λ*) at 25–1000 °C was measured via laser reflection technology (LFA 457, NETZSCH, Germany) at a heating rate of 10 °C·min−1, and Ar gas was used for protection. The post-test data were fitted using a radiation and impulse correction model. To obtain the thermal conductivity of dense bulk samples, the density was measured using the Archimedes drainage method. The thermal conductivity (*k*) was calculated based on thermal diffusivity, heat capacity (*c**p*), density (*ρ*), and porosity (*ϕ*) (Eq. (2)):

(2) k=λ⋅ρ⋅cp1−4ϕ/3

where the heat capacity (*c**p*) was obtained from the Neumann‒Kopp law. The heat capacity results obtained using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC; NETZSCH 204F1, Germany) are shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). This work discussed the thermal conductivity of dense ceramics. To further analyze the effects of thermal radiation on the thermal conductivity, the transmittance of each sample was measured via a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR; Nicolet iS 10, Thermo Scientific, USA), and the measurement wavelengths ranged from 2.5 to 25.0 μm.

### 2.4 Measurements of mechanical properties

Two different methods, namely, nanoindentation (iNano instrument, iMicro, USA) and ultrasonic reflection methods (UMS-100, TECLAB, France), were used to measure the Young’s modulus and hardness. During nanoindentation, the distributions of the Young’s modulus (*E*nano) and hardness (*H*nano) in an area of 300 µm × 300 µm was detected by an *E*–*H* mapping (three-dimensional (3D)) model, and the load was 100 mN. Furthermore, the advanced dynamic *E*–*H* model was used to construct load‒depth curves, which were used to estimate the wear resistance. During ultrasonic reflection measurement, the Young’s modulus (*E*) was determined by the density and transverse (*V*t) and longitudinal (*V*l) acoustic velocities (Eq. (3)):

(3) E=ρVt2(3Vl2−4Vt2)Vl2−Vt2

Furthermore, the average acoustic velocity (*V*a) was used to present the phonon speed (Eq. (4)):

(4) Va=[13(1Vl3+1Vt3)]−13

The Vickers hardness (*H*V) and fracture toughness (*K*IC) were estimated within an automatic indenter (HMV-G-FA, Shimadzu, Japan) based on indentation load (*F* = 4.9 N), the half-length of the indent diagonal (*d*), and the crack length (*c*) [55,56] (Eqs. (5) and (6)):

(5) HV=0.464Fd2

(6) KIC=0.018EHV⋅Fc1.5

### 2.5 Measurements of high-temperature stability

TmNbO4 and Tm3NbO7 might react with each other at high temperatures, which would destroy the structural integrity and properties of TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites, and their high-temperature stability was measured by holding at 1500 °C for 10 h. After heat treatment, the crystals and microstructures of TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites were further characterized via XRD and SEM. Furthermore, the chemical compatibility between Al2O3 and TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites was estimated by holding at 1500 °C for 10 h. Al2O3 is a thermal growth oxide (TGO) in TBCs and ASCs because its BC is oxidized to form Al2O3 after high-temperature service [57]. The excellent chemical compatibility between Al2O3 and TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites could prolong their life span. The 70 wt% TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites were mixed with 30 wt% Al2O3 powder, and then the mixtures were pressed into discs with a diameter of 15 mm and a thickness of 1 mm. The discs were finally held at 1500 °C for 10 h, and XRD was used to detect reactions between Al2O3 and TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites.

### 3.1 Crystals and microstructures

Figures 1(a) and 1(b) show that the XRD patterns of TmNbO4 and Tm3NbO7 are corresponding to those of PDF#23-1424 and PDF#24-1128, respectively, indicating that TmNbO4 is crystallized into a monoclinic (M) phase and Tm3NbO7 is crystallized into a cubic (C) fluorite phase. The space groups (SGs) of TmNbO4 and Tm3NbO7 are *I*2 and *Fm** 3¯m*, respectively. Figure 1(b) shows that the XRD intensity of Tm3NbO7(111) peak increases with an increase in its content, which conforms to our design. Figures 1(c) and 1(d) show crystals of M-TmNbO4 and C-Tm3NbO7, respectively. During cooling, TmNbO4 undergoes a ferroelastic tetragonal‒monoclinic (T–M) transition, and the Tm and Nb atoms exhibit different arrangements, as shown in Fig. 1(c). In C-Tm3NbO7, the arrangements of Tm, Nb, and O atoms are disordered. In detail, Nb and Tm have 1/4 and 3/4 possibilities to occupy the same cationic sites, respectively, while O atoms and vacancies have 7/8 and 1/8 possibilities to occupy the same ionic sites, respectively, as shown in Fig. 1(d).

Some papers have documented features of M-RENbO4 and C-RE3NbO7 ceramics based on differences in crystals [1,32,59,60]. The second-order ferroelastic T–M transition results in M-RENbO4 ceramics having high working temperatures, and ferroelastic domains increase their fracture toughness [23,44,45]. The disordered arrangements of RE, Nb, and O atoms lead to C-RE3NbO7 ceramics exhibiting low and glass-like thermal conductivity and high TECs, and their high stiffness originates from strong Nb–O and RE–O bonding strengths [58,61,62]. Therefore, the aforementioned characteristics are intrinsic properties of M-TmNbO4 and C-Tm3NbO7 ceramics, and the synthesized composites maintain their structures after SPS.

Figure 2(a) shows the typical grains of each sample, and obvious grain refinements are induced during SPS. A percolation effect between the two phases of the material occurs, which results in grain growth being mutually inhibited by the two-phase grains. The grains of TmNbO4 and Tm3NbO7 can be further distinguished by the back-scattered electron (BSE) model, and the dark grains are TmNbO4. The combinations of TmNbO4 and Tm3NbO7 grains are excellent because no obvious pores or cracks are found among the grains. Figure 2(b) shows the EDS mapping of 40 wt%, and the content of Nb in the TmNbO4 grains is greater than that in the Tm3NbO7 grains.

### 3.2 Young’s modulus and fracture toughness

The Young’s modulus is affected by the strain, stress, and content of each phase in the composites. The Voigt model can be used to estimate the Young’s modulus of composites when each component is subjected to the same strain [62]. The Reuss model can be used to estimate the Young’s modulus of composites when each component is subjected to the same stress [62]. The aforementioned two models represent the upper and lower limits of the modulus, and they are calculated by Eqs. (7) and (8), respectively [52,62]:

(7) EVoigt=fVEC+(1−fV)EM

(8) 1EReuss=fVEC+1−fVEM

where *f*V and *E*C represent the volume fraction and Young’s modulus of C-Tm3NbO7, respectively, and *E*M represents the Young’s modulus of M-TmNbO4. Figure 3(a) shows that the experimental Young’s modulus (Exp. *E*) of most samples is within the ranges of *E*Voigt and *E*Reuss, except for the sample with 20 wt% TmNbO4, which may be caused by experimental errors. The XRD and SEM results show that the combination of TmNbO4 and Tm3NbO7 grains is excellent, which enhance modulus. The rule of mixture (ROM) is used to estimate the evolution of hardness, which is similar to the meaning expressed by Eq. (7) [52]. Figure 3(b) shows ROM. The *H*V values are similar to the experimental values (Exp. *H*V). The grain refinements contribute to the increased hardness, while the presence of residual stresses, phase distribution heterogeneity, and interface effects within the material are not accounted for in the ROM predictions, which lower hardness. Therefore, Exp. *H*V is slightly lower than ROM *H*V. In addition, the hardness of the composites increases with the increasing content of Tm3NbO7, and the variation trend aligns with our expectations. Accordingly, the hardness and modulus of TmNbO4 are successfully enhanced by compositing Tm3NbO7.

Figure 3(c) shows comparisons of hardness and fracture toughness among the synthesized composites, La3NbO7 [61], La2Zr2O7 [20], 7YSZ [63], and others [1,2]. Most oxides exhibit fracture toughness values of less than 3.0 MPa·m1/2, and 7YSZ is widely applied as TBC owing to its high toughness (3.5 MPa·m1/2). The fracture toughness of ferroelastic YTaO4 increases to 3.0±0.4 MPa·m1/2 because of the effects of HfO2, which possesses a unique ferroelastic toughening mechanism [64]. Table 2 and Fig. 3(c) show that the sample with 80 wt% TmNbO4 exhibits a fracture toughness as high as 3.3±0.4 MPa·m1/2, which is better than that of most oxides and comparable to that of 7YSZ [20,61,63−65]. Several factors lead to high fracture toughness of the synthesized composites, including ferroelastic toughening, enhancements of grain boundaries, and grain refinements.

Previous articles have shown that ferroelastic domains can absorb fracture energy by switching domains and that crack bridging further consumes fracture energy to increase the fracture toughness of RENbO4 [45]. Figure 4(a) shows that abundant of intergranular cracks are found in TmNbO4, which is the dominant crack propagation mode. Furthermore, lots of intergranular cracks exist around the indentation, which reduces the stress concentration around the indentation. It is believed that combinations among the TmNbO4 grains are relatively weak, and the massive intergranular cracks indicate that the crack propagation resistance at the grain boundaries is lower than that within the grains. Each RENbO4 grain exhibits differential ferroelastic domain orientations, which means that different arrangements of atoms weaken combinations among grains. Additionally, crack bridging and bifurcation are also found in TmNbO4, and they can consume fracture energy and reduce the stress concentration around crack tips to increase toughness. Composites with 80 wt% TmNbO4 possess much shorter crack lengths than those of TmNbO4, which are consistent with the high fracture toughness. The number of intergranular cracks is much lower than that of TmNbO4, as shown in Fig. 4(b), indicating that combinations among grains are enhanced by the introduction of the second-phase Tm3NbO7. Both intergranular and transgranular cracks dominate the propagation modes. Furthermore, obvious microcracks are found around indentations, which can release stress and increase fracture toughness, in addition to cracks bridging and bifurcating. In the composites with 20 wt% TmNbO4, transgranular cracks are the dominant propagation mode, as shown in Fig. 4(c), which is similar to the situation of single-phase RE3NbO7 [61]. The effects of crack bifurcation and bridging are relatively weak based on their number, and cracks tend to be bridged when they are close to grains of TmNbO4. TmNbO4 grains are softer than Tm3NbO7 grains in terms of modulus and hardness, causing cracks to bridge around the TmNbO4 grains.

### 3.4 TECs and thermal stress

Figure 7 shows that TECs of the synthesized composites increase with increasing temperature, and Tm3NbO7 has higher TECs than TmNbO4, which leads to Tm3NbO7/TmNbO4 composites displaying higher TECs than TmNbO4 [27,64]. The TECs of the synthesized composites increase continuously with increasing temperature and are better than those of 7YSZ. Above 1200 °C, TECs of 7YSZ increase abruptly due to a phase transition, which introduces undesirable thermal stress and failure of the coatings. Thus, 7YSZ has working temperatures less than 1200 °C. The T–M transition temperatures of RENbO4 are less than 1000 °C, and TECs of samples with TmNbO4 are continuous with increasing temperature because the T–M transition is a second-order process, and the incidental changes in unit cell volume and TECs can be omitted. Therefore, the synthesized composites exhibit working temperatures as high as 1500 °C.

where f*v is the volume fraction of C-Tm3NbO7 in the composite ceramic, * TECsC and  TECsM represent the TECs of C-Tm3NbO7 and M-TmNbO4 ceramics, respectively. Therefore, it is effective to predict TECs of TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites based on ROM.

The differences in TECs (ΔTEC) between TmNbO4 and Tm3NbO7 ceramics lead to inner thermal stress, as indicated by Eq. (1), which is also affected by temperature. Considering that TBCs, EBCs, and ASCs are applied at temperatures higher than 1000 °C, the inner thermal stress at 1000–1500 °C is estimated and shown in Fig. 7(c). The inner thermal stress (0.021–0.289 GPa) first increases with increasing temperature, and it reaches the maximum at 1400 °C due to the maximum ΔTECs between TmNbO4 and Tm3NbO7. With further increases in temperature, the inner thermal stress is slightly reduced at 1500 °C. The inner thermal stress among the TmNbO4 and Tm3NbO7 grains is much lower than that between the BC and ceramic coatings of TBCs, EBCs, and ASCs because ΔTECs between BC and ceramic coatings are much greater.

### 3.5 Thermal conductivity

Figure 8 shows that the thermal conductivity of TmNbO4 (2.40–3.82 W·m−1·K−1) first decreases with increasing temperature from 25 to 700 °C and then increases abruptly from 700 to 900 °C, which is caused by thermal radiation. Tm3NbO7 exhibits glass-like thermal conductivity (0.92–1.21 W·m−1·K−1 at 25–1000 °C), which increases slightly with increasing temperature. The glass-like thermal conductivity is caused by the disordered crystals and obvious differences in bonding strength between the Tm–O and Nb–O bonds [60]. The thermal conductivity of the composites containing Tm3NbO7 decreases linearly with increasing temperatures, and no increments of thermal conductivity are found at the current measurement temperatures. The thermal radiation of TmNbO4 is believed to be effectively inhibited by compositing Tm3NbO7, and the lowest thermal conductivity is realized in composites containing 20 wt% TmNbO4 (1.20 W·m−1·K−1 at 1000 °C). Compared with those of 8YSZ and TmNbO4, the reductions in thermal conductivity are as high as 37.7% and 58.9%, respectively, and the TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites exhibit lower thermal conductivity than (Yb2Zr2O7)0.2(Sm2Sn2O7)0.8 composites [75], and perovskite-type ceramics [76], 8YSZ [77], RETa3O9 [54], and RE3NbO7 [61] high-entropy ceramics. Obviously, TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites can provide excellent thermal insulation performance.

### 3.6 High-temperature stability

Figure 11(a) shows the XRD patterns of Al2O3 and TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites after holding at 1500 °C for 10 h, and obvious XRD peaks of Tm3Al5O12 are found in each sample. Clearly, Al2O3 powder reacts with TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composite, and Tm element plays a key role in this process. The content of Tm3Al5O12 increases with increasing Tm3NbO7 content, which indicates that Tm3NbO7 and Al2O3 react with each other, and a similar situation has been reported in HECs of RE3NbO7 fluorite [89]. Additionally, the intensity of the TmNbO4 XRD peak in the composite with 20 wt% TmNbO4 is stronger than that of the Tm3NbO7 peak, indicating that most of the Tm3NbO7 has reacted with Al2O3 to form Tm3Al5O12. RETaO4 ceramics exhibit excellent Al2O3 chemical compatibility, and no obvious reactions are found between RETaO4 and Al2O3 after high-temperature heat treatment [90]. TmNbO4 displays characteristics similar to those of RETaO4. Figure 11(a) shows that the intensity of the Tm3NbO7 XRD peaks decreases with increasing Tm3NbO7 content, indicating that the reactions between Tm3NbO7 and Al2O3 become more pronounced with increasing Tm3NbO7 content. Therefore, the Al2O3 chemical compatibility of TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites decreases with increasing Tm3NbO7 content, and a relatively low content of Tm3NbO7 is beneficial for their high-temperature application.

*
Fig. 11XRD patterns of synthesized ceramics after heat treatment at 1500 °C for 10 h. (a) Al2O3 chemical compatibility; (b) high-temperature stability.

Figure 11(b) shows the XRD patterns of TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites after being held at 1500 °C for 10 h, and the XRD peaks of Tm2O3 and Nb2O5 are found in each sample. The intensities of the XRD peaks belonging to Tm2O3 and Nb2O5 decrease with decreasing TmNbO4 content, while the XRD peaks of Tm3NbO7 are obvious. Accordingly, Tm3NbO7 exhibits better high-temperature stability than TmNbO4, and TmNbO4 is decomposed into Tm2O3 and Nb2O5 after heat treatment at 1500 °C for 10 h in the present study. Macauley et al.* [49,91] studied the phase stability and microstructures of ZrO2–YO1.5–TaO2.5 and reported that fluorite Y3TaO7 was stable at 25–1500 °C, whereas YTaO4 may decompose into YTa3O9 and YTa17O19 after holding at 1500 °C for 100 h. Additionally, YTaO4 has a melting point of approximately 2100 °C, while Y3TaO7 has a melting point of approximately 2250 °C [91]; a high melting point is beneficial for the high-temperature stability. Furthermore, RENbO4 undergoes a ferroelastic T‒M phase transition at 400–1000 °C [69,92], which may also affect the phase stability of TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites. Therefore, RE3NbO7 ceramics exhibit better high-temperature stability than RENbO4 ceramics. Figure 12 shows the microstructures of TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites after holding at 1500 °C for 10 h, and the grain sizes are bigger than those shown in Fig. 2, which is caused by the decomposition of TmNbO4. The grain growth during the holding process can affect the performance of composites. For samples with 60 and 40 wt% TmNbO4, the percolation effect and mutual inhibition of grain growth are related to the uniform distribution of two-phase grains [93]. A more balanced phase content results in a more uniform grain distribution. During prolonged holding, the grain growth of the two phases inhibits each other and prevents further growth. Consequently, the grain size of the sample with 20 wt% TmNbO4 is significantly larger than those of the samples with 40 and 60 wt% TmNbO4. Therefore, the composites exhibit optimal high-temperature stability when the TmNbO4 content is 60 wt%.

## 4 Conclusions

Fluorite Tm3NbO7 oxides are successfully introduced into ferroelastic TmNbO4 to form TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites, and the fracture toughness and thermal insulation performance are synergistically boosted in this work. Composites with 60 wt% TmNbO4 exhibit the best comprehensive properties, including high toughness (3.0±0.1 MPa·m1/2) and TECs (11.1×10−6 K−1 at 1500 °C), low thermal conductivity (1.68 W·m−1·K−1 at 1000 °C), and excellent high-temperature stability. This work focuses on the toughening mechanisms and evolutions of thermal conductivity and TECs, and we uncover their dominators, which shine lights for regulating properties of various materials. The introduction of Tm3NbO7 enhances the bonding strength among grains in TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites; consequently, more energy is needed during crack propagation. Furthermore, the simultaneous effects of microcracks, crack bridging and bifurcation reduce the stress concentration around crack tips, and they are indispensable for high toughness. TECs of composites are effectively estimated by ROM due to the excellent combinations among grains, and their similar TECs result in negligible inner thermal stress, which is favorable for their applications. Minimum interfacial thermal resistance exists in the designed composites, and fluorite Tm3NbO7 with glass-like thermal conductivity can effectively inhibit high-temperature thermal radiation. Hence, the low-temperature thermal conductivity is close to that predicted by the model with minimum interfacial thermal resistance, and the TmNbO4/Tm3NbO7 composites display excellent thermal insulation performance at high temperatures.
