## 1 Introduction

There is a growing demand for better-performing thermal protection materials (TPMs) that operate under relatively high temperatures, extreme heat fluxes, and severe oxidation to offer effective thermal protection for next-generation rockets and hypersonic vehicles [1]. Owing to their superior melting points, ultrahigh-temperature ceramics (UHTCs) [2], including carbides, diborides, and nitrides of group IV–V elements, are considered desirable candidates for aerospace TPMs. UHTC systems such as ZrC, HfC, ZrB2–SiC, and ZrC–SiC have been intensively investigated over a prolonged period of time [3]. However, extensive research has suggested that ablation resistance remains the predominant factor limiting their application in future TPMs [4,5]. For example, when exposed to extreme ablative environments, the loose and porous structure of Zr(Hf)O2 is almost nonprotective for the matrix, whereas the significant evaporation of oxides of Si and B results in rapid loss of materials [6].

This motivated research on novel UHTC systems, including high-entropy ceramics (HECs) [7−11], medium-entropy ceramics (MECs) [12−14], and other single-phase multicomponent ceramics with nonequimolar compositions [6,15−17]. These innovative materials have garnered significant research interest because of their improved thermal stability, higher hardness, lower thermal conductivity, and enhanced intrinsic oxidation resistance [7,18−22]. Furthermore, recent studies have begun to explore multiphase multicomponent ceramics, given that multiphase design has been demonstrated to achieve enhanced comprehensive performance of HECs, MECs, etc. [23−28]. These new paradigms are expected to meet the stringent performance requirements of next-generation TPMs.

Ablation and oxidation resistance remain the primary focuses in the research of UHTCs. However, most current work has focused mainly on the mechanical or thermophysical properties of multiphase multicomponent ceramics, and their oxidation behavior is still largely unexplored [23,25−27]. According to research on single-phase multicomponent UHTCs, their enhanced oxidation resistance can be partially attributed to improved thermodynamic stability. For example, Hf0.25Ta0.25Zr0.25Nb0.25C exhibited a higher oxidation onset temperature (1058 K) than the corresponding monocarbides did [18]. More importantly, the formation of a multi oxide scale, which effectively retarded oxygen diffusion and blocked heat flow, played a key role in enhancing the ablation and oxidation resistance of multicomponent UHTCs, such as Hf0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2C and Hf0.5Zr0.3Ti0.1Ta0.1C [29−31]. It is believed that preferential oxidation behavior governs the formation of multiple oxide scales [32,33].

However, owing to the extended composition range and more complex phase assemblage, the established oxidation mechanisms of UHTCs may not fully account for the oxidation behavior of multiphase multicomponent ceramics. Specifically, it is commonly assumed that during high-temperature oxidation, the constituent phases in multiphase ceramics do not react with each other because of their likely equilibrium state [34,35]. Nevertheless, given the preferential oxidation of thermodynamically favored elements in multicomponent ceramics [32,33], once oxidation is initiated, the primary equilibrium state of multiphase multicomponent ceramics is destabilized by composition evolution caused by preferentially oxidized element consumption. This allows the reaction between the constituent multicomponent phases during oxidation. Additionally, considering the metastable states reported in some dual-phase high-entropy ceramics [36,37], these reactions are more likely to occur. However, these potential reactions, together with their impact on ablation performance, remain unclear. Although a previous work reported an improvement in the ablation performance of dual-phase multicomponent ceramics (i.e., Hf0.61Zr0.26Ti0.13C–(Hf0.30Zr0.32Ti0.38)5Si3) [38], the degradation of oxidation resistance was observed in dual-phase multicomponent diborides [37]. Furthermore, the relationship between this process and the preferential oxidation behavior of multiphase multicomponent ceramics has rarely been studied. These unresolved issues are becoming bottlenecks in the future development of multiphase multicomponent UHTCs.

In this study, a multiphase multicomponent carbide, Hf–Zr–Ti–Ta–C, consisting of Hf-rich carbide, Ta-rich carbide, and Ti-rich carbide, was fabricated and ablated at approximately 2718 K. This Hf–Zr–Ti–Ta–C system was selected on the basis of the results of our previous work [31]; it exhibited relatively superior ablation resistance due to the self-healing ability and low volatility of its oxide layer. On the basis of the experimental investigation in conjunction with thermodynamic analysis, a solid-state diffusion process between constituent multicomponent phases during ablation was revealed. Furthermore, the coupling between this solid-state diffusion process and the preferential oxidation of thermodynamically favored elements was also thoroughly analyzed. In particular, the synergistic mechanism of solid-state diffusion and preferential oxidation in multiphase carbides was revealed for the first time. More importantly, their synergistic role in the ablation performance was also elucidated. More broadly, the findings of this work offer a preliminary basis for the composition design of future multiphase multicomponent UHTCs.

### 2.1 Fabrication of the multiphase multicomponent carbide

The multiphase multicomponent carbide Hf0.46Zr0.39Ti0.08Ta0.07C–Ta0.79Ti0.12Hf0.06Zr0.03C–Ti0.55Ta0.28Hf0.08Zr0.09C was prepared on a carbon/carbon (C/C) composite through reactive melt infiltration (RMI). First, a mixed metal powder comprising 50 at% Hf–30 at% Zr–10 at% Ti–10 at% Ta was ball milled in anhydrous ethanol at 50 r·min−1 for 24 h, followed by drying at 40 °C for 48 h. In this step, ZrO2 grinding balls of various sizes (*ϕ*5, 15, 20, and30 mm) were selected for the ball-milling process. The raw materials included Hf, Zr, and Ti powder (*d* ≈ 40 μm, 99.2% purity; Jinzhou Haixin Metal Materials Co., Ltd., China) and Ta powder (*d* ≈ 5 μm, 99.9% purity; Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China). The C/C composites possess the structure of repeatedly overlapping layers of 0° nonwoven carbon fiber cloth, a chopped fiber web, and 90° nonwoven fiber cloth with needle-punching. The carbon fiber used in the C/C preform is T700. During RMI, the C/C composite (size: *ϕ*30 mm × 10 mm, density: 1.00 g·cm−3, weight: 7 g, modal pore diameter: 41.2 μm) was embedded into 45 g of Hf–Zr–Ti–Ta mixed powder within a graphite crucible (46.5 mm in diameter and a height of 50 mm). Heat treatment was performed via an induction furnace (VIF-10, Nanjing Boyuntong Instrument, China) evacuated to a vacuum (vacuum degree: 1.0×10−3 Pa) with a molecular pump. The temperature was initially increased to 2400 K at a heating rate of 100 K·min−1, where it was maintained for 20 min. Finally, the C/C composite was densified to a density of 7.09 g·cm−3 by the RMI process. Moreover, its porosity decreased from 45.13% to 1.78%.

### 2.2 Ablation test

The ablation test was performed with a plasma torch device. The flow rates of H2 and Ar2 were maintained at 2000 and 180 L·h−1, respectively. The working current and voltage were set as 699 A and 64 V, respectively. The test specimen, with dimensions of *ϕ*30 mm × 10 mm, was mounted in a water-cooled copper holder positioned 55 mm from the plasma gun nozzle (diameter: 5.5 mm). The temperature of the central ablation region sample was measured as ca. 2718±14 K via an optical pyrometer (HR602-B, Chi Rui Measurement Control, China) with an accuracy of ±0.5%. The model was calibrated by the manufacturer, and the calibration method can be found in JJF 1187-2008. The heat flux of the plasma torch was measured to be 6.48±0.13 MW·m−2 by a heat flux sensor with an accuracy of ±2% (GD-B3-12 M, Beijing East Summit Tech, China). The model has been calibrated by the manufacturer. The calibration method can be found in GB/T 10294. The test duration was 120 s. ### 2.3 Characterization

The morphology was examined by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; MIRA4 LMH, TESCAN, The Czech Republic) in conjunction with an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS; Ultim-Max-50, Oxford Instruments, UK). The element content and distribution were observed via an electron probe microanalysis system (EPMA; Jxa8230, JEOL, Japan). The phase structure was characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, UK) operating at a scanning rate of 0.5 (°)·min−1 within the range of 5°–80°. To analyze the phase content of the as-formed multiphase multicomponent carbide coating, Rietveld refinement of the XRD patterns was carried out via TOPAS v4.2 software. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images and high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images were obtained with a (scanning) transmission electron microscope (Talos F200x, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an EDS system. The TEM samples were prepared via a focused ion beam (FIB; Helios Nanolab 600i, FEI, USA). The thermodynamic data and diagrams were calculated via the phase and equilibrium modules of FactSage 8.2, respectively.

### 3.1 Microstructure and constituents of multiphase multicomponent carbides

Figures 1(a) and 1(b) show the SEM analysis of the polished cross-section of the as-fabricated composite, and a relatively dense multiphase structure of the carbide coating with a thickness of 300–400 μm can be observed. The EDS mapping results (Fig. 1(c)) revealed that the matrix phase, which appeared gray, was Hf-rich, whereas some Ta-rich carbide phases, which appeared white, were present in the coating as isolated phases. In addition, minor elongated Ta-rich carbides of micrometer or submicron size are uniformly dispersed among the matrix phase (yellow box in Fig. 1(b)). In addition, some dark gray Ti-rich carbide phases that agglomerate around the Ta-rich carbides (Figs. 1(b) and 1(c)) were also observed. The compositions of the Hf-rich, Ta-rich, and Ti-rich multicomponent carbides were determined to be Hf0.46Zr0.39Ti0.08Ta0.07C, Ta0.79Ti0.12Hf0.06Zr0.03C, and Ti0.55Ta0.28Hf0.08Zr0.09C, respectively, through EDS point scanning analysis (Fig. 1(d) and Table 1). Notably, according to the theoretical equilibrium compositions (Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)), this three-phase system is in a metastable state, which may be attributed to the short holding time during RMI and the subsequent rapid cooling rate. To further characterize the structure of the multiphase multicomponent carbide coating, XRD was carried out on the sample. As shown in Fig. 1(e), the peaks of HfC (JCPDS 39-1491), TaC (JCPDS 74-1223), and Ti8C5 (JCPDS 65-3842) were detected in the XRD patterns, which were in accordance with the EDS results, and the volume ratios of these phases were calculated to be 71.08, 18.76, and 10.16 vol% (Fig. S1 in the ESM), respectively. Moreover, the slight shifts in the peak positions of these phases suggested that they were solid solution phases. Notably, the inner part of the as-fabricated composite was composed of a C/C matrix and single-phase Hf-rich carbide. The composition of this Hf-rich carbide can be determined as (Hf0.47Zr0.36Ti0.08Ta0.09)C via EDS analysis.

The multiphase multicomponent carbide is formed in two stages. First, once the pyrolytic carbon contacts the molten Hf‒Zr‒Ti‒Ta alloy, a continuous thin carbide layer forms, separating the remaining molten alloy from the pyrolytic carbon. According to the Ellingham diagram [39], the reaction between Hf and carbon is thermodynamically more favorable, whereas Zr and Ti are in the next place, followed by Ta. Thus, the as-formed carbide layer in the first stage is Hf enriched. In this context, the carbon atoms need to pass through this carbide layer via vacancy diffusion to react with the metal melt. The diffusion of C promoted the nucleation of multicomponent carbides. However, during nucleus growth, differences in diffusion rates among transition metal elements result in composition segregation, thus promoting the formation of multiphase multicomponent carbides [40]. Additionally, the diffusion coefficients of Hf in carbides are suggested to be higher than those of Ta [41]. Therefore, the development of Hf-rich carbide grains has become more favorable both kinetically and thermodynamically. However, during the relatively short period of heat preservation, the remaining Ta formed a Ta-rich carbide phase, which was embedded in Hf-rich carbide. In summary, multiphase multicomponent ceramics were formed through the combination of kinetics and thermodynamics factors.

### 3.2 Ablation performance of the multiphase multicomponent carbide

The ablation test was performed by a plasma torch device, and during the test, the surface temperature of the multiphase multicomponent carbide reached approximately 2718 K. For comparison, the Ta-containing single-phase multicomponent carbide (Hf0.50Zr0.30Ti0.10Ta0.10C) obtained in previous work [31] was also ablated under identical test parameters, and it achieved a similar surface temperature (approximately 2753 K). Notably, compared with single-phase multicomponent carbides (high-entropy and nonequimolar systems) and conventional binary carbides (ZrC, HfC–ZrC), multiphase multicomponent carbides significantly improved the ablation resistance. Both the negative values of LAR and MAR obviously distinguish it from other UHTCs. The significantly lower LAR and MAR suggest minor mass consumption and better dimensional stability of the multiphase multicomponent carbide. In summary, multiphase multicomponent carbides demonstrated enhanced ablation resistance.

#### 3.3.1 Surface oxide layer

To elucidate the potential reactions between the constituent multicomponent phases and the mechanisms responsible for the enhanced ablation performance, phase evolution was thoroughly investigated. As demonstrated in Figs. 3(b) and 3(d)–3(f), a relatively smooth and intact surface oxide layer can be observed in the macroscopic and microscopy images of the ablated samples. The set of XRD patterns indexed to HfO2 (JCPDS 43-1017) strongly suggests that the surface oxide layer consisted of Hf-rich oxides (Fig. 3(c)), despite the small peaks indicating the existence of tiny amounts of Hf-Ta-rich oxides (Hf6Ta2O17, JCPDS 44-0998). Additionally, it can be inferred from the holes on the ablated surface (Fig. 3(d)) that the volatilization of low melting point oxides, such as Ta- and Ti-rich oxides, escaped from the oxide layer, leaving evacuation channels. Moreover, the ablation fringe (Fig. 3(e)) implies that some as-formed melt was blown away by the high-velocity gas flow.

To gain deeper insights into the overall oxidation behavior, cross-sectional observations were performed via SEM and EPMA. Unlike the multiphase matrix, the surface oxide layer exhibited a relatively homogeneous morphology (Fig. 4(a)), indicating that it might be composed of single oxide products. This agrees reasonably well with the above XRD analysis, which suggests that monoclinic Hf-rich oxides comprise the surface oxide layers. However, the phase assemblage of the subsurface oxide layer seems to be more complex, and secondary oxide phases (i.e., light gray phases) still remain among the dominant oxide phases (Figs. 4(a) and 4(b)). Electron probe X-ray micro-analysis (EMPA) mapping provided a clearer perspective of the phase evolution among the ablated samples and revealed that these secondary oxide phases were Ta-rich phases. Notably, the Ti-rich phase disappeared among this subsurface oxide layer. However, both Ta-rich and Ti-rich phases were retained among the underlying oxidation-affected matrix, and their structure and composition seem to have been significantly altered. Moreover, some Hf-rich regions (also O-rich) appeared around the oxidation-affected Ta-rich and Ti-rich phases (Fig. 4(b), indicated by the white arrow in the Hf map); given the fine dispersion of the primary Ta-rich and Ti-rich phases, these Hf-rich regions connected and developed into a network. In addition, on the basis of the EPMA analysis, the phase evolution among the ablated samples can be briefly summarized as follows (Fig. 4(c)): Hf-rich, Ta-rich, and Ti-rich phases were retained in the underlying oxidation-affected matrix. Within the subsurface oxide layer, the Ti-rich phase disappeared, leaving only Hf-rich and Ta-rich phases. Furthermore, only the Hf-rich phase was observed for the surface oxide layer.

#### 3.3.2 Oxidation-affected matrix

As depicted in the magnified view of the triple-junction region (Fig. 5(a)), all of the carbide phases in the matrix were significantly altered. Overall, mismatches with the oxidation thermodynamics of multicomponent carbides were discovered in the triple-junction region among oxidation-affected matrix. As indicated in Refs. [32,33], the oxide formed on the Ta‒Ti‒Hf‒Zr‒C system should be Hf rich because of the preferential oxidation of Hf. While the oxides in the three-phase region are Zr-rich or Ti-rich, this suggests that some underlying reactions of multiphase multicomponent carbides remain unknown. However, the Hf-rich carbide matrix away from the triple-junction region presented a different composition evolution, and its Hf-rich oxides appeared to agree with the preferential oxidation of Hf. This further indicates that the reactions between multicomponent ceramic phases are responsible for the unique compositional evolution. To be more specific, given the intrinsic metastable state of the three-phase multicomponent carbide, the re-equilibration of the constituent carbide phases is likely to be initiated by the high temperature during ablation, resulting in the solid-state diffusion of metal elements between carbide phases. In this context, preferential oxidation and solid-state diffusion occur synchronously in this three-phase multicomponent carbide.

#### 3.3.3 Subsurface oxide layer

As mentioned earlier, the Ti-rich phase disappeared from the subsurface oxide layer. To investigate the compositional and structural evolution of this layer, a TEM sample was lifted from the region across the retained Ta-rich phase and Hf-rich phase (Figs. 10(a) and 10(b)). As shown in the HAADF-STEM image and EDS elemental map, the Ta-rich carbide was completely oxidized, while some Ta‒Ti-rich oxides formed among it (Figs. 10(d) and 10(e)). Indexing the corresponding SAEDs (Figs. 11(a) and 11(b)) revealed that they were orthorhombic oxides and tetragonal oxides, respectively. Matching was obtained with JCPDS 97-010-8463 (Ta2O5) and JCPDS 04-002-5363 (Ti0.5Ta0.5O2). It can be inferred from the phase diagram of the TiO2‒Ta2O5 system that the Ta‒Ti-rich oxides are likely formed by precipitation during cooling [44]. Moreover, EDS elemental mappings of the area around the oxidized Ta-rich carbide revealed the existence of surrounding Hf-rich oxides with bright contrast (Fig. 10(c) and 10(d)). The high Hf content and relatively uniform morphology clearly distinguished these samples from the other parts of the TEM sample (Figs. 10(d) and 10(f)). The corresponding SAEDs can be indexed to monoclinic HfO2 (JCPDS 43-1017, Fig. 11(c)). This evident enrichment in Hf might be caused by preferential oxidation behavior [38].

#### 3.4.1 Thermodynamic analysis

To investigate the underlying ablation mechanisms, equilibrium calculations of the multiphase multicomponent carbide system were carried out. As shown in Table 2, the three-phase carbides were in a metastable state at 2718 K because their final equilibrium phases were Ta0.43Ti0.28Zr0.19Hf0.10C0.98, HfC, and minor carbon. Therefore, if the sample is heated during ablation, the re-equilibration of the metastable three-phase system (i.e., the solid-state diffusion of metal elements) will occur and lead to compositional evolution until the equilibrium composition is reached [45,46]. An observed compositional gradient (Fig. S2(c) in the ESM) and the as-formed likely interlayer (Figs. S2(a) and S2(b) in the ESM) substantiated the existence of this solid-state diffusion, and recent reports also validated a similar solid-state diffusion process [24]. The equilibrium calculations with different oxygen inputs (Table 3) confirmed the preferential oxidation of the Hf component in the carbides. More importantly, thermodynamic calculations indicate the synchronous occurrence of mutual solid-state diffusion between the multicomponent carbide phases and preferential oxidation. During this reaction process, the solid-state diffusion of metal elements between the primary phases led to the formation of a new Ta-rich carbide (e.g., Ta0.46Ti0.30Zr0.20Hf0.02C0.84 in Table 3). This analysis revealed that, theoretically, the solid-state diffusion of metal elements between the constituent carbide phases might occur in multiphase multicomponent carbide systems during high-temperature oxidation. The preferential oxidation occurred synchronously with this solid-state diffusion.

#### 3.4.2 Solid-state diffusion among the oxidation-affected matrix and its coupling with preferential oxidation behavior

Since the solid-state diffusion among the oxidatively affected matrix was driven by the re-equilibration of the metastable three-phase system, it can be inferred from the equilibrium composition (Tables 2 and 3) that the Zr in Hf-rich carbides diffuse into Ta-rich and Ti-rich carbides (Fig. 13(b)), making the Hf-rich carbides closer to their equilibrium compositions (HfCs). For Ta-rich and Ti-rich carbides, to achieve an equilibrium composition, the mutual diffusion of Ta–Ti between them can be expected (Fig. 13(b)). Notably, the above analysis primarily considers the major elements in each multicomponent carbide phase. For example, the contents of Ta and Ti in Hf-rich carbides are relatively low compared with those of the major elements, resulting in a much smaller diffusion amount. To facilitate overall analysis, they were therefore omitted.

Returning to the experimental observations in the oxidation-affected matrix, the oxidation products were Zr-rich and Ti-rich oxides (see Section 3.3.2). This confirmed that the thermodynamically driven solid-state diffusion of Zr and Ti between the constituent carbides was preferable. In fact, this can be explained from a kinetic perspective: The relative diffusion rates of metal elements in transitional metal carbides are Ti > Zr > Ta. Ti is likely to be the fastest diffusing species among the component elements because it has the highest atomic mobility at high temperatures and the smallest lattice parameter [41]. However, Ta is considered to be the slowest inter-diffusing species, possibly due to the lower vacancy formation energy of Ta, and its carbide tends to act as the host materials during the solid-state diffusion process, and Ti is incorporated into the Ta-rich carbide “host” first [41]. Therefore, the Ta-rich carbide would be more significantly influenced by Ti diffusion (blue arrow in Fig. 13(b)), and Zr diffusion seemed to have a lesser effect. However, the Ti-rich carbide was more profoundly affected by Zr diffusion than by the slowest Ta diffusion (yellow arrow in Fig. 13(b)). As shown in Fig. 6(d), the experimental observation that the formation of a Ti-rich phase in the primary Ta-rich carbide phase agreed reasonably well with this analysis. Apparently, the accompanying oxygen invasion caused these Ti-rich phases to eventually transform into Ti-rich oxides. Likewise, the Zr enrichment in oxides of the Ti-rich carbide phase might also be formed by a similar process. Overall, the solid-state diffusion process significantly altered the composition of the residual matrix. Due to solid-state diffusion and the accompanying oxidation, the diffusing elements (i.e., Ti or Zr) consequently formed oxides (Fig. 13(b)). The significant depletion of Ti and Zr resulted in the enrichment of Ta in the residual carbide phases (Fig. 13(e)).

Additionally, the formation of Zr-rich and Ti-rich oxides rather than the theoretically predicted Hf-rich oxide. This suggests that the thermodynamically driven solid-state diffusion of Zr and Ti between the constituent carbides prevailed over the preferential oxidation of Hf in the triple-junction region. Nevertheless, the preferential oxidation of Hf remained the dominant reaction in the single-phase region. More importantly, it can be inferred from the Hf-consuming tendency (Fig. 13(e)) in the triple-junction region that the preferential oxidation of Hf and the solid-state diffusion process between the constituent carbide phases were not isolated. Instead, they are strongly coupled. According to previous work [38], preferential oxidation significantly depleted Hf in the oxidation-affected zone, which included the triple-junction region (Figs. 13(b) and 13(c)).

#### 3.4.3 Preferential oxidation behavior of the subsurface layer

As indicated earlier, a less pronounced tendency for Ti enrichment was observed among the subsurface oxide layers, indicating a lower degree of Ti diffusion caused by the solid-state diffusion process. More importantly, the formation of predicted Hf-rich oxides in this triple-junction region instead of Zr-rich oxides strongly suggested that preferential oxidation became the dominant reaction regime within this subsurface oxide layer. This may be caused by the higher local pO2 and can also be confirmed by thermodynamic calculations (Table 3): The dominant reaction was a solid-state reaction with a minor oxygen input (0.5 mol O2) since the majority of the product was a Ta-rich carbide formed through solid-state diffusion. As the O2 input increased, the proportion of Hf-rich oxide in the products significantly increased, indicating that preferential oxidation gradually became dominant. Interestingly, the unique micron-sized oxides formed in the triple-junction region were found to be more Hf-rich than those in the single-phase region (Fig. 10(f)). Given the coupling between the solid-state diffusion process and preferential oxidation behavior, as Zr and Ti were consumed by the solid-state diffusion process to form the corresponding oxides (Fig. 10(e)), this would subsequently facilitate the formation of highly Hf-rich oxides (Figs. 13(b) and 13(c)). Additionally, considering that the Hf component is the most preferentially oxidized species, the preferential oxidation behavior would also promote further enrichment of Hf in oxides [38]. The above inferences could be substantiated by the remarkable depletion of Hf among the oxides of the secondary phase (Fig. 10(e)). Furthermore, owing to the finely dispersed secondary phase (Fig. 1(b)), these Hf-rich oxides around the triple-junction regions consequently evolved into a micron-sized network within the oxide layer (Fig. 13(c)). This *in situ*-formed network and nanosized oxide skeletons together constitute a dual-scale structured subsurface oxide layer.

In addition, the disappearance of the Ti-rich phase in the oxide layer could be ascribed to the solid solution between the oxidation products, given that TiO₂ can dissolve in HfO₂ at high temperatures (> 2273 K) [47] and that Ti is considered the fastest diffusing species in transitional metal oxides [48,49]. This also provided a rational explanation for the successive disappearance of Ta-rich particles among the surface oxide layer, given that a solid solution of oxides can be facilitated by accelerated atomic mobility at elevated temperatures in the surface oxide layer. In addition, due to the relatively high evaporation rates of Ta- and Ti-rich oxides, volatilization loss might act as a secondary reason for their successive disappearance.

### 3.5 Mechanisms responsible for enhancing ablation performance

First, the thermodynamically driven solid-state diffusion process between constituent carbide phases affects the ablation performance. As indicated earlier, the compositions of the three-phase carbides beneath the oxide layer were significantly altered due to the solid-state diffusion process (Figs. 13(a) and 13(b)). Specifically, in the triple-junction region, the Ta content in the residual second phase increased substantially (Fig. 13(e)). Remarkably, the thermodynamic predominance diagram revealed that the stable temperature ranges for Ta-carbide were significantly broader than those for all the other carbides (Fig. 13(f)). This means that the composition evolution enhanced the thermodynamic stability of the residual multiphase multicomponent carbide. As a result, their stability and oxidation resistance can be sustained at a relatively high oxygen partial pressure. This could retard the oxidation of the materials during ablation. Notably, the thermodynamic stability advantage of Ta-rich carbides during oxidation or ablation has been proven by several works [18,50]. Moreover, it is believed that Ta-rich oxides can inhibit the phase transition of oxides, thereby improving the stability of oxide scales [50]. Moreover, a quantitative evaluation of the volatility loss of Ta-, Ti-, and Nb-related oxides indicated that the volatility loss of Ta-related oxides is approximately ten times lower than that of Ti and Nb [31]. Therefore, a better ablation resistance of a moderate Ta-enriched carbide is expected. However, owing to the relatively low melting point, excessive oxidation products of Ta might deteriorate the ablation performance at relatively high temperatures (2773–3273 K) [31].

Moreover, owing to preferential oxidation and its coupling with the solid-state diffusion process, a dual-scale structured oxide layer developed (Figs. 13(a) and 13(c)). This unique structure is expected to enhance the mechanical properties of the oxide layer. During ablation, mechanical erosion caused by gas dynamic shear stress generally leads to cracking, abscission and even failure of the oxide layer [51]. The *in situ*-formed micron-sized oxide network embedded in the nanosized oxides effectively deflected the crack growth induced by mechanical erosion (Fig. 13(d)). Moreover, the oxide network also bridged the crack faces and hindered the rapid propagation of cracks (Fig. 13(d)), thus avoiding spalling of the protective oxide layer to some extent. This implies that the structural evolution of the oxide layer could enhance its ability to scour hot high-speed flow during ablation.

In summary, the solid-state diffusion process between the underlying constituent carbide phases improved the thermodynamic stability of the materials. Moreover, the preferential oxidation behavior led to the formation of a more durable and adherent protective oxide layer. The synergistic effect of these favorable factors comprehensively enhanced the ablation performance. More broadly, some preliminary remarks can be made on the design of multiphase multicomponent UHTCs. First, it is essential to determine the equilibrium state of the system and predict the possible solid-state diffusions. Second, identifying the sequence of preferential oxidation of elements is critical for recognizing the depleting elements during ablation. On the basis of these analyses, the compositional evolution of the system during ablation could be preliminarily predicted, thus providing an effective reference for composition optimization.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, a multiphase multicomponent carbide, Hf–Zr–Ti–Ta–C, consisting of Hf-rich carbide, Ta-rich carbide, and Ti-rich carbide, was fabricated. Through studying the ablation behavior, a thermodynamic-driven solid-state diffusion process between the constituent multicomponent phases during ablation was revealed. The solid-state diffusion of metal ions (mainly Zr and Ti) occurred among the oxidation-affected three-phase regions beneath the oxide layer, leading to the formation of Zr- and Ti-rich oxides. As a result, the Ta content in the residual carbide phase increased substantially. Importantly, this composition evolution enabled the thermodynamic stability of the residual carbide phases to be sustained at a relatively high oxygen partial pressure during ablation. Moreover, the coupling between preferential oxidation behavior and the solid-state diffusion process facilitated the *in situ* formation of a micron-sized Hf-rich oxide network in the oxide layer. This structure effectively enhanced the mechanical properties of the oxide layer through mechanisms such as crack deflection and crack bridging. Therefore, the oxide layer offered better protection against the scouring of hot high-speed flow. In summary, the synergistic effect of the solid-state diffusion process and preferential oxidation behavior optimized the ablation performance of the multiphase multicomponent carbide under extreme conditions.

In a broader context, this study suggests that broader structural and compositional tunability of multiphase multicomponent ceramics could provide additional possibilities for more desirable synergistic effects. Thus, multiphase multicomponent UHTCs hold great potential for achieving superior ablation resistance and are worthy of further exploration.
