## 1 Introduction

With the rapid advancements of the Internet of Things (IoT), sensing technology is increasingly becoming the core of future intelligent systems, with important applications in industry, health monitoring, transportation, and other fields [1,2]. With the development of modern agriculture toward industrialization, informatization, and intelligence, agricultural environment monitoring and assessment have become prominent issues. Ammonia is a colorless, water-soluble, and poisonous gas with a pungent odor [3,4]. Nearly 90% of global ammonia emissions originate from organic nitrogen in amino fertilizers and animal manure in agriculture [5]. NH3 irritates the eyes of poultry, affects growth and metabolic functions, reduces the body’s antioxidant capacity, causes liver damage, and in severe cases, leads to death [6−8]. According to the environmental parameters and environmental management indicators of intensive pig farms in China, the NH3 concentration in pig houses should not exceed 33 ppm. In addition, ammonia is a noxious pollutant gas in the atmosphere, which is a considerable threat to the healthy development of both humans and the natural environment [9]. Therefore, it is essential to establish an agricultural environmental monitoring system by combining gas sensors, microcontrollers, the IoT, and other technologies to implement effective monitoring of agricultural NH3 concentrations and achieve advanced regulation.

The accuracy, stability, and usable life of the monitoring system are significantly affected by gas sensor technology, which is essential for the realization of online gas monitoring. Traditional gas monitoring techniques, such as spectroscopy, chromatography, and detector tubes, suffer from high costs, complexity, and long delays [10]. Since resistive gas sensors are easy to miniaturize and integrate, with simple process structures and strong stability, they are used in many gas detection applications. Among them, gas-sensitive sensing layer materials, as the core of resistive gas sensors, also face difficulties such as poor anti-interference capabilities and low sensitivity at room temperature [11,12]. Currently, there has been a shift toward new varieties of room-temperature, extremely sensitive micro/nano gas sensors [13].

The MXene series, created in 2011 by selectively etching MAX-phase materials, has garnered significant interest because of its unique properties [14,15]. Numerous theoretical simulation calculations and experimental studies have shown that MXene family members have high metal conductivity, abundant reactive sites, and unique microstructures, which may be employed as potential gas-sensitive sensor materials [16−19]. Ti3C2T*X* is the most stable of the many MXene materials, with a mature preparation scheme and the cheapest price, so it is more widely used. Although MXenes are also sensitive to ammonia at room temperature, they suffer from a narrow intrinsic MXene band gap and low chemical stability, which leads to poor gas-sensitive properties and selectivity of the sensor material [20,21]. Currently, many scholars have formed heterostructures by compositing MXenes with other materials, which are used to construct new gas-sensitive sensors with enhanced gas sensitivity [22−24]. Zhang *et al*. [25] synthesized a Co3O4/MXene composite sensor via a simple hydrothermal method, which achieves excellent responsiveness to low concentrations of formaldehyde at room temperature and a recovery time of only 5 s for 10 ppm. In 2020, Li *et al*. [26] created an NH3 gas sensor using a PANI/Ti3C2T*X* hybrid-sensitive film. In 2021, Wang *et al*. [27] developed a self-supplied poly(vinyl alcohol)/MXene sensor with the ability to be sensitive to H2O molecules, which can be applied for humidity monitoring, and this composite sensor has significantly greater sensitivity than intrinsic MXene. In 2022, Zhang *et al*. [28] developed a high-performance TiO2/Ti3C2T*X*-based gas sensor for UV-assisted excitation for trace ammonia detection. However, it is also necessary to use an external light source drive to improve sensor sensitivity with high power consumption. Although there are many reports on Ti3C2T*X* and complex ammonia sensors, their characteristics, such as responsiveness, response/recovery rates, and baseline drift, have yet to be improved.

Further studies on the modulation of the micromorphology of MXene sensor materials with surface modifications could pave the way for practical sensing applications. Molybdenum oxide (MoO3), an n-type semiconductor material with a unique layered structure, has gained notable attention in gas sensors because of its tunable bandgap structure (2.8–3.6 eV) and high electron mobility [29,30]. In particular, gas sensors prepared with thermodynamically stable α-MoO3 phase have high response values to reducing gases, but issues such as excessive working temperatures and inadequate long-term stability still require immediate attention [31]. In summary, the construction of a metal oxide MoO3 and Ti3C2T*X* heterojunction and the synergistic effect between the components are expected to yield greenhouse ammonia-sensing materials with enhanced gas-sensitive properties.

Hence, this study reports the utilization of an extremely sensitive MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* gas sensor for ammonia monitoring in a smart agricultural environment at room temperature. The ultrasonically compounded MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* gas-sensitive material was coated onto a fork-finger electrode substrate using the droplet method to construct a planar-type gas sensor. Compared with the intrinsic MoO3 gas sensor, the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* gas sensor has better responsiveness and detection rates, and has a certain sensitizing effect on NH3. Notably, the sensor has good selectivity and repeatability. By means of density functional theory (DFT), the effects of various adsorption surfaces of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* on the gas-sensitive characteristics of NH3 were revealed from several perspectives, including adsorption energy, charge transfer, differential charge density, and density of states (DOS). Finally, to verify the feasibility of the application of the sensor, circuit modules of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensor, WiFi, alarm module, and temperature and humidity were designed, and an agricultural ammonia intelligent monitoring system based on MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* gas-sensitive sensors was developed, which can capture data from the cloud and visualize the data via online monitoring (Fig. 1).

### 2.1 Materials

Titanium aluminum carbide powder (Ti3AlC2) was obtained from Neoene Technology Inc., China. Hydrofluoric acid (HF, 40%) was acquired from Chongqing Chuandong Chemical Co., Ltd., China. Sodium molybdate (Na2MoO4·2H2O, 99%) was acquired from Chongqing Yuexiang Chemical Co., Ltd., China. L-cysteine (C3H7NO2S, 99%) was provided by Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China. Citric acid (C6H8O7·H2O, 99.5%) and anhydrous ethanol (C2H6O, 99.5%) were purchased from Chengdu Colon Chemical Co., Ltd., China. There was no need for additional purification because all the chemical reagents were analytically pure.

### 2.2 Synthesis of Ti3C2T<i>x</i>, MoO3, and MoO3@Ti3C2T<i>x</i>

Ti3C2T*X* is primarily prepared by etching Ti3AlC2 with HF. First, 2 g of black Ti3AlC2 powder was weighed and gradually added in small amounts to a 100 mL polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) beaker containing 20 mL of 40% HF and 15 mL of deionized water (DI). The temperature was set to 40 °C, and the magnetic stirring reaction was conducted for 12 h. After the reaction, centrifugal washing was performed until the pH was greater than 6. Finally, the Ti3C2T*X* powders were obtained by vacuum drying at 50 °C for 12 h. The MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* composite material is shown in Fig. 2(a). In 40 mL of deionized water, 300 mg of sodium molybdate and 500 mg of L-cysteine were added. The mixture was subsequently stirred at room temperature for 1 h, after which 10 mg of citric acid was added. After stirring, the mixture underwent a hydrothermal reaction at 170 °C for 24 h. Then 300 mg of sodium molybdate and 500 mg of L-cysteine were added to 40 mL of DI water. After stirring for 1 h, citric acid (10 mg) was added. Following agitation, the mixture underwent a hydrothermal reaction at 170 °C for 24 h. The resulting black MoS2 was centrifugally washed and dried at 60 °C for 12 h. After drying, black MoS2 was obtained. In a tube furnace (OTF-1200X, Hefei Kejing Technology Co., Ltd, China), MoS2 was annealed at a heating rate of 2 °C/min and held at 400 °C for 1 h to complete the annealing process, resulting in gray-blue MoO3 powder. MoO3 and Ti3C2T*X* were added to deionized water in a 2 : 1 mass ratio. After 15 min of sonication, the mixture was stirred for 1 h. Finally, the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* composite material was obtained after centrifugation and drying.

### 2.3 Characterization and measurements

The sample’s crystal structure was examined via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; XRD-6100, Shimadzu, Japan) under Cu-Kα radiation. The scanning speed (2*θ*) was set to 2 (°)/min, and the scanning range was from 5° to 90°. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Apreo 2S+, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was utilized to explore the microsurface morphology and size of the materials. The chemical composition and binding energy distribution of the sample surface were investigated via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Escalab 250xi, Thermo Fisher, USA).

### 2.4 Fabrication and measurement of gas sensors

The overall structure of the sensor developed in this paper includes a planar sensor substrate, sensor material, sensor base, and sensor cap. Au/Pt interdigital electrodes (3 mm × 3 mm) with a 0.25 mm thickness make up the planar sensor substrate. First, the interdigital electrodes were repeatedly ultrasonically cleaned with ethanol and deionized water before being dried. Next, a small amount of gas-sensitive material and deionized water were placed in a mortar and ground into a slurry, which was then dripped onto the digital electrode. Finally, the test pins of the sensor base were connected to two Pt wires. After the welding package was completed, the tube was capped. The pins at the other end of the sensor base were used to test its gas sensitivity and to connect the gas sensor signal processing module designed later.

As shown in Fig. 2(b), the NH3 performance test was performed at room temperature (25 °C, 60% RH). The gases used in this work, including air, NH3, H2S, CH4, C2H4, NO2, and CO, were purchased from Chongqing Jiaqing Gas Co., Ltd., China. NH3, H2S, CH4, C2H4, NO2, and CO were used as target gases, and air was used as the background gas. The DGD-III dynamic gas distribution system (Elite Tech, China) was used to control target gas and background gas intake to configure various concentrations of target gas, and the configured gas was injected into the gas sensitivity test platform system (CGS-8, Elite Tech, China) to analyze the gas sensitivity performance of the sensor. Then, it was connected to the upper computer by the RS232 bus to record the resistance output signal. An important factor in assessing the effectiveness of gas sensors is the response value. The rate of resistance change, which is defined below, serves as a representation of the sensor’s response value (*S*) (Eq. (1)) [32]:

(1) S=|Ra−Rg|Ra×100%

where *R*a represents the stable resistance value of the sensor in air. When the gas-sensitive material reacts with the measured gas, it reaches a stable value, represented by *R*g.

### 2.5 DFT calculation method

In this work, DFT calculations were performed via the Castep package. For structural optimization and performance calculations, the following optimization parameters were applied: the exchange‒correlation function employed the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) function of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) [33,34]. Grimme’s DFT-D2 method was employed to correctly describe the van der Waals interactions, which was based on the addition of the conventional Kohn–Sham DFT energy semiempirical dispersion. A 400 eV truncation energy was used for wavefunction expansion, and the self-consistent accuracy energy was set to 1.0×10−5 eV/atom. The maximum force was set to 0.03 eV/Å, and the maximum stress was set to 0.05 GPa to ensure that a stable and optimized model was obtained. The MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* Brillouin zone integrals were obtained via a 1 × 1 × 1 *K*-point grid for geometry optimization. A 20 Å vacuum layer was added to the material models along the *Z*-axis direction to account for the periodic boundary effect [35].

To estimate the stability of the interface of the heterostructure model, the binding energy (*E*b) is explained as Eq. (2) [36]:

(2) Eb=EMoO3@Ti3C2TX−ETi3C2TX−EMoO3

where * EMoO3@Ti3C2TX denotes the energy of the composite model,  EMoO3 denotes the energy of the metal oxide model, and  ETi3C2TX denotes the energy of Ti3C2TX*. When * Eb is negative, the composite behavior can be spontaneous, and the smaller the value is, the more stable the composite system is. The smallest distance that exists between the atoms of the two composites is known as the binding distance (D*b).

The adsorption energy is used to describe the strength of the interaction force between MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* and NH3. Adsorption energy (*E*ad) is described in Eq. (3) [37]:

(3) Ead=Esub+gas−Esub−Egas

where * Esub+gas is defined to evaluate the energy of the adsorption system,  Esub denotes the total energy of the gas-sensitive material, and  Egas denotes the energy of the gas molecules. The adsorption distance (D*ad) denotes the minimum distance from the atom of the gas molecule to the atom of the sensor material after the end of the adsorption of the gas. In addition, the Hirshfeld charge distribution is used to calculate the charge transfer (*Q*T) to analyze the interactions in the entire system. Differential charge density is employed to investigate charge transfer and interactions [38].

### 3.1 Characterization and electronic structure analysis

The XRD patterns of pure Ti3AlC2 and etched Ti3C2T*X* are shown in Fig. S1(a) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). After the selective etching of Ti3AlC2 with the HF solution, the characteristic Al(104) peak of Ti3AlC2 is weakened to a large extent, and the (004), (107), and (109) crystal diffraction peaks become wider and lose their intensities compared with their previous positions. The characteristic typical strong peak (002) is shifted to a lower diffraction angle and becomes wider, which is revealed at 8.46°. This is almost in agreement with previous reports confirming the successful exfoliation of Ti3AlC2 and the preparation of organ-shaped Ti3C2T*X* [39]. Figure 3(a) displays the XRD patterns of pure MoO3, Ti3C2T*X*, and MoO3@Ti3C2T*X*, in which the diffraction peaks of MoO3 are almost the same as the diffraction peaks of MoO3 in the standard PDF card (PDF#76-1003). The XRD patterns of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* and MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* have similar characteristic peaks to those of MoO3, and the peaks corresponding to the MoO3 (020), (110), (040), (021), (111), (060), and (002) crystal planes and two distinct characteristic peaks corresponding to the Ti3C2T*X* (020) and (110) crystal planes at 8.46° and 60.4° prove the successful composite of Ti3C2T*X* and MoO3.

The morphologies and microstructures of the samples were investigated via SEM. SEM images of compact Ti3AlC2 and organ-like Ti3C2T*X* are displayed in Figs. S1(b) and S1(c) in the ESM, respectively. After etching, the layer spacing of Ti3C2T*X* can reach 379.6 nm. The EDS elemental mapping images of Ti3C2T*X* (Fig. S1(d) in the ESM) confirm the existence of Ti, C, F, and O. The SEM images of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* (Figs. 3(c) and 3(d)) clearly show that the lamellar MoO3 is uniformly adhered to the lamellar structure of Ti3C2T*X*, and the spacing of the lamellae is increased to 710.9 nm. The insertion of MoO3 into the organ-shaped Ti3C2T*X* layer increases the layer spacing of the Ti3C2T*X* gas-sensitive material, which expands its surface-specific area and provides additional active sites for gas adsorption.

XPS is used to analyze the elemental composition and atomic valence of a sample [40]. The survey spectrum of Ti3C2T*X* containing F, O, Ti, and C is displayed in Fig. S1(e) in the ESM. The six peaks fitted to the Ti 2p map can be attributed to the characteristic bonds of Ti(II), Ti(III), and Ti–C in Fig. S1(f) in the ESM [41]. The PS spectra of the C 1s are divided into five peaks, which are classified as C–C (284.800 eV), C–O (286.400 eV), O=C–O (288.860 eV), C–Ti–O (282.900 eV), and C–Ti (281.560 eV) (Fig. S1(g) in the ESM), which suggests that Ti3C2T possesses typical carbon bonding. Three peaks, representing Ti–O, Ti–OH, and Ti–H2O, could be convoluted from the O 1s spectra (Fig. S1(h) in the ESM) [42], suggesting the existence of –OH groups on the surface of Ti3C2T*X*. The survey spectrum of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* (Fig. 3(f)) reveals the coexistence of Mo, O, Ti, and C, which is in good agreement with the EDS mapping results (Fig. 3(e)). The Ti 2p spectrum can be classified into Ti–C, Ti(II), Ti(III), and Ti–O bonds (Fig. 3(g)) [43]. In addition, the C 1s spectrum is divided into four components located at approximately 282.090, 284.770, 286.610, and 288.910 eV, corresponding to the Ti–C, C–C, C–O, and C=O bonds, respectively (Fig. 3(j)) [44]. The Mo 3d spectrum has peaks at 232.907 and 236.062 eV that are assigned to Mo6+, and the two peaks located at 231.672 and 234.542 eV belong to Mo5+ (Fig. 3(h)) [45,46]. This indicates that some of the Mo6+ ions are reduced by Ti ions and that oxygen vacancies have been successfully formed on MoO3@Ti3C2T*X*. In Fig. 3(i), the O 1s spectrum is divided into three peaks at 530.600, 531.200, and 532.700 eV, corresponding to lattice oxygen (OL), defective oxygen (OV), and adsorbed oxygen (OC), respectively [47]. In particular, oxygen vacancies and adsorbed oxygen can generate additional active sites for redox reactions and enhance the gas-sensing effect. Figure 3(k) shows that the high-resolution XPS spectrum of F 1s can be divided into two peaks at 687.8 and 685.213 eV, corresponding to the C–F and Ti–F bond, respectively, confirming the presence of –F functional groups on the surface. In summary, the XPS results demonstrate the successful composite of Ti3C2T*X* and MoO3.

To further demonstrate the formation of heterojunctions, the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* heterojunction model was simulated, and its electronic structure information was revealed. The experimentally prepared Ti3C2T*X* is chemically very active with various functional groups (–O, –F, and –OH). Therefore, this paper mainly establishes initial cell models of Ti3C2O2, Ti3C2F2, and Ti3C2(OH)2, as shown in Fig. 4(a). The lattice parameters of Ti3C2T*X* are *a* = *b* ≠ *c*, *α* = *β* = 90°, and *γ* = 120°, and the space group is *p*63/*mmc*. The cellular model of MoO3 belongs to the orthorhombic structure with a space group of *Pbnm*(62) in Fig. 4(c). Considering the stability of the low-index surface, the MoO3 (010) surface is selected for the subsequent composite model establishment, as depicted in Fig. 4(d) for the MoO3(010) model after sectioning. The lattice parameters of Ti3C2T*X* and MoO3 have different angles, so the initial protocells are cut along the (001) direction, and the redefined Ti3C2T*X* (001) lattice is obtained and plotted in Fig. 4(b) for the three Ti3C2T*X* models, where the lattice parameters are shown in Table 1. The lattice mismatch rate of the established heterojunction model is considered to be less than 5%, which can reduce the structural defects at the interface and strengthen the electrical properties of the composite model. Therefore, a heterogeneous structure of 5 × 3 × 1 Ti3C2T*X* models and a 4 × 4 × 1 MoO3 model is established (Fig. 4(e)), which is consistent with existing studies that aimed to establish heterostructures [46]. The binding energies of the three composite heterostructure models are shown in Fig. 4(g). According to the differential charge density plots in Fig. 4(f), Ti3C2T*X* primarily transfers charge to the MoO3 surface by acting as an electron donor, resulting in the formation of a blue electron cloud around the O atoms on the MoO3 surface, which coincides with the results of the transfer charge calculations, that is, the MoO3 surface receives an electrically negative charge (Fig. 4(h)). The charge redistribution between the composite interfaces reflects the electron transfer process, further demonstrating the heterostructure formation of MoO3 and Ti3C2T*X*. The electronic DOS and the partial wave density of states (PDOS) of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* heterojunction model are used to investigate the atomic orbital overlap phenomenon and interfacial interactions at the interface of MoO3 and Ti3C2T*X* and to analyze the reasons for the formation of its stable structure. The DOS plot of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* (Fig. 4(i)) spans the Fermi energy level, indicating that the material has high conductivity, which is beneficial for sensing at room temperature. The 2p orbitals of the O atoms of MoO3 undergo strong orbital overlap states with the surface functional group atoms F, O, and H of Ti3C2T*X*, suggesting that strong interfacial coupling occurs in the MoO3@Ti3C2O2, MoO3@Ti3C2F2, and MoO3@Ti3C2(OH)2 systems in the PDOS of Fig. 4(j). This strong coupling phenomenon implies the formation of covalent bonds, which can be used as good electron transport channels to promote gas adsorption.

### 3.2 NH3 sensing performance

With the goal of lowering the power consumption of sensors in practical applications, this paper focuses on exploring the sensor’s gas-sensitive performance at room temperature. All gas-sensing measurements were made at 60% relative humidity and 25 °C at room temperature, with the exception of the humidity effect test on the sensor. NH3 in the range of 0–200 ppm was examined to evaluate the concentration-response dynamic characteristics of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensor. When the initial resistance of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensor was stable, NH3 was injected. When the sensor adsorbed NH3 to saturation, NH3 was released. Following this procedure, as exhibited in Fig. 5(a), the resistance of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensor increases from 237 to 499 MΩ in the concentration range of 0–200 ppm NH3. In Fig. S2 in the ESM, the response values *S* of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensor at 5, 20, 40, 80, 100, and 200 ppm NH3 were 14.78%, 24.37%, 47.83%, 79.67%, 87.29%, and 110.55%, respectively. The gas-sensitive response of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensors increased with increasing NH3 concentration, and the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensor resistor resistance values recovered to the initial values. The responses of the MoO3 and MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensors to 5 ppm NH3 were 3.87% and 14.7%, respectively, as shown in Figs. 5(b) and 5(e). Therefore, the responsiveness of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensor to 5 ppm NH3 was approximately 3.8 times greater than that of the intrinsic MoO3 sensor, indicating that the introduction of MoO3 into the Ti3C2T*X* material composite resulted in a certain degree of sensitization to NH3. To reflect the response of the Ti3C2T*X*-based sensor to the target gas more intuitively, a functional relationship was fitted to the gas-sensitive response values. MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* fitting function for different concentrations of NH3 is * y=−0.003x2+1.121x+6.95. The fitted R*2 was 0.996, indicating that there is a high degree of fit between 0–200 ppm NH3 and the response of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensor. In contrast, as demonstrated in Fig. 5(e), the fitted *R*2 of the response of the MoO3 sensor to NH3 is 0.994, which is not as good as the fitted curve of the response of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensor to NH3.

In Fig. 5(c), the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensor has a response time of approximately 14 s and a recovery time of approximately 24 s for 20 ppm NH3 gas, and the tests with different gas concentrations demonstrate almost the same response–recovery time, which suggests that the sensor has high sensitivity and fast detection capability. Compared with Fig. 5(f), the response–recovery time of the MoO3 sensor for NH3 gas is approximately 56 s/28 s, which is greater. Figure 5(d) shows that the response–recovery time increases as the gas concentration increases. Joint analysis with SEM images shows that the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* material has a greater specific surface area than does MoO3, which provides more adsorption–desorption channels for gas adsorption. Moreover, the high electrical conductivity of Ti3C2T*X* can accelerate electron transfer, which effectively accelerates the adsorption–desorption rate of gas molecules and shortens the response–recovery time.

A high-performance gas sensor should have good stability and repeatability [48]. As depicted in Fig. 6(a), the response of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* to NH3 was tested for four consecutive cycles at 30 and 50 ppm. Under the same concentration conditions, the response value of NH3 had a small range of variation, indicating that the sensor also had excellent repeatability. ### 3.3 Gas sensing mechanism

In this work, the adsorption mechanism of NH3 is explored from the perspective of gas adsorption characteristics based on the established stable MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* model. The computationally optimized two-class model for NH3 adsorption by MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* and the model for NH3 adsorption by MoO3 are shown in Fig. 8(a), where MoO3@Ti3C2T*X*–NH3-I indicates that MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* adsorbs NH3 on the surface of Ti3C2T*X*, and MoO3@Ti3C2T*X*–NH3-II shows that MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* adsorbs NH3 on the surface of MoO3. Compared with the adsorption energy of intrinsic MoO3 for NH3 adsorption (−0.026 eV), the adsorption energy of NH3 adsorption by MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* is somewhat increased. In addition, the adsorption energies of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X*–NH3-I are −0.512, −0.126, and −0.541 eV, respectively. The adsorption energies of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X*–NH3-II are −0.107, −0.093, and −0.09 eV, respectively. This finding shows that the composite structure plays a certain role in sensitization. Moreover, this adsorption process is a low-energy associative adsorption, which does not trigger chemisorption phenomena, leading to desorption difficulties, which is consistent with the fast response and desorption conclusions obtained from the testing of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensor for NH3 at room temperature.

*
Fig. 8(a) Adsorption models and adsorption energy of NH3 on MoO3 and MoO3@Ti3C2TX* surfaces. (b) DOS of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X*–NH3-I system. (c) PDOS of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X*–NH3-I system (embedded differential charge density of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X*–NH3-I). (d) DOS of MoO3–NH3 system and (e) PDOS of MoO3-NH3 system.

The DOS plots of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* before and after the adsorption of NH3 are similar to those in Fig. 8(b), indicating that electron transfer occurs within the system. This electron transfer can change the electronic state density distribution of the adsorbent, thus affecting its gas-sensitive performance. The differential charge density plots of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X*–NH3-I system are embedded in Fig. 8(c), and MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* is surrounded by predominantly charge aggregates, reflecting the electropositive nature of NH3, in accordance with the Hirshfeld charge analysis method. As shown in Fig. 8(d), in the DOS plots of intrinsic MoO3 before and after the adsorption of NH3, the overall curve change is not obvious, and the main change is the shift of the MoO3−NH3 curve to the left. To analyze the phenomenon of orbital overlap and interaction between the sensing material interface and the adsorbed gas atoms, PDOS studies were carried out. Figure 8(e) shows the PDOS plots of intrinsic MoO3 before and after the adsorption of NH3, and the N 2p and H 1s peaks of the NH3 molecules and the O 2p peak of the gas-sensitive material almost did not form a resonance hybridization peak. In addition, in the MoO3@Ti3C2O2-NH3-I system PDOS shown in Fig. 8(c), the N 2p of the gas molecule NH3 and the O 2p orbitals of the Ti3C2O2 substrate material at −7.37 and −1.97 eV have similar overlapping peaks, and at −6.13 to 1.63 eV, the N 2p of NH3 and the O 2p and Ti 3d orbitals have similar overlapping peaks, indicating the occurrence of hybridization. Moreover, orbital peak hybridization occurred in the Ti 3d and H 1s spectra of the MoO3@Ti3C2F2-NH3-I system at 3.79 eV. The PDOS of MoO3@Ti3C2(OH)2 adsorption of NH3 is shown in Fig. 8(c). The N 2p orbital of the NH3 molecule at −9.16 eV forms a resonance hybridization peak with the O 2p and H 1s orbitals in MoO3@Ti3C2(OH)2. A resonant hybridization peak is formed at −5.09 eV between the O-2p orbitals in MoO3@Ti3C2(OH)2 and the p orbitals of the N 2p atoms in the NH3 molecule. In summary, the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* composite interface can enhance the hybridization between the atomic orbitals and NH3. This hybridization can cause charge transfer or electron rearrangement after the adsorption of NH3 on MoO3@Ti3C2T*X*, which enhances the adsorption capacity of the sensing material for NH3, and thus promotes its gas-sensitive performance.

Studies have shown that the gas-sensing performance depends on the gas adsorption capacity of the sensing material and the amount of chemisorbed oxygen [54]. Researchers have conducted an in-depth analysis of the sensing response of Ti3C2T*X* to ammonia [55,56]. In this study, when exposed to NH3, the resistance of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* composite sensor increases, indicating that the electrical performance and resistance of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* primarily depend on Ti3C2T*X*, exhibiting behavior similar to that of p-type semiconductors [56]. This suggests that Ti3C2T*X* plays a major role in charge carrier conduction. When MoO3 is in contact with Ti3C2T*X*, due to the higher work function of MoO3, a Schottky junction is formed at the interface of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X*. Here, a possible mechanism is proposed to explain the enhanced NH3-sensing response of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* gas sensors, as shown in Reactions (4)–(6) [57,58]:

(4) O2(gas)→O2(ads)

(5) O2(ads)+e−→O2−(ads)

(6) 4NH3+3O2−→2N2+6H2O+3e−

On the one hand, the lower the adsorption energy is, the easier the adsorption process is. From DFT calculations, the synergistic effect of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* model significantly reduces the adsorption energy of NH3, enhancing the ability of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* to capture and adsorb NH3, as well as facilitating charge transfer, thereby improving its gas-sensing performance. Chemisorbed oxygen ions O2− have been reported to have a significant effect on gas detection performance [54]. At room temperature, the adsorbed oxygen molecules on MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* accept electrons from the surface, forming oxygen ions (O2−) shown in Fig. 9(a) [8,59]. It has been reported that a redox chemical reaction occurs between adsorbed oxygen ions (O2−) and ammonia molecules, leading to charge transfer [4,9,58]. In Fig. 9(b), the NH3 molecule interacts with O2− to produce many electrons. This electron transfer to Ti3C2T*X* leads to hole-electron complexation, which results in a decrease in the majority carrier hole concentration in the Ti3C2T*X* material. Moreover, the heterojunction barrier height increases, which narrows the conductive pathway of Ti3C2T*X*, thereby increasing the resistance of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* gas sensor. Consequently, the formation of interfacial Schottky junctions and charge regulation significantly enhances the gas sensitivity of MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* toward NH3.

### 3.4 Design and measurement of MoO3@Ti3C2TX-based monitoring system

To achieve the portable detection of ammonia gas in agriculture, an MoO3@Ti3C2T*X*-based online monitoring system was designed. As illustrated in Fig. 10(a), the whole system hardware consists of a microcontroller, NH3 sensor, signal conversion module, ADC module, temperature and humidity sensor, organic light-emitting diode (OLED) display, buzzer alarm module, and power supply. The collected data are uploaded to the OneNET IoT platform via WiFi, and at the same time, they are downloaded to the visual monitoring interface of the supercomputer, which can be viewed at any time. In Fig. 10(b), the circuit diagram of the designed gas sensor can achieve the conversion of the resistance signal of the resistive sensor into a voltage signal and filtering. For gas monitoring system devices, aesthetics, moisture, drop, and corrosion resistance need to be considered, so an online monitoring device with an encapsulated enclosure was designed (Fig. S3 in the ESM). Figure 10(c) shows a flowchart of the software design of the gas monitoring system, which mainly uploads the sensor data to the cloud server through the MQTT protocol. First, initialize the system, enter the correct WiFi network SSID and password to successfully connect to WiFi, and then configure the server information to connect to the cloud server. In an infinite while loop, the values of the temperature and humidity sensors and the NH3 concentration are read. Moreover, the OLED displays show the collected temperature, humidity, and gas concentration data in real time. If the collected gas concentration is greater than the set threshold, a buzzer alarms. Finally, the collected gas sensor concentration, temperature, and humidity sensor values are packaged into a message in JSON format, and the publish() function of the MQTT client is called to publish the message to the cloud server. Historical data can be viewed in the cloud, and the historical datasets can be exported. To achieve the monitoring of data visualization, the upper visualization interface was created through the data visualization view function of the OneNET IoT development platform, which can be used on PCs or mobile phone clients and can display the collected temperature, humidity, and NH3 concentration curves in real time. The environmental quality standards for livestock farms strictly stipulate the minimum concentration limit of NH3 gas in livestock farms, and the concentration of NH3 gas in livestock farms should be less than 33 ppm. Therefore, this paper creates the trigger NH3_alarm based on the cloud platform, which exceeds the threshold value of 33, and sends an email to the user.

In addition, as shown in Fig. 10(e), the sensing system designed in this paper has great potential not only in the field of agriculture but also in the fields of ammonia emission in the built environment, food spoilage, and industry.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, a room-temperature MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* ammonia sensor with high sensitivity and selectivity was developed via a composite modification method. Owing to the unique microscopic morphology and abundant active sites of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensing layer, it has excellent responsivity and detection rate for NH3, and the response value for 5 ppm NH3 reaches 14.78%, which is 3.8 times greater than that of the intrinsic MoO3 sensor, with a response–recovery time of 14 s/24 s. This layer has excellent cycling stability and anti-interference capability. DFT calculations also verified that the hybridization phenomenon between the atomic orbitals of the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensing material and the NH3 molecule promoted electronic rearrangement and increased the adsorption capacity of NH3, which in turn promoted its gas-sensitive performance. Finally, the MoO3@Ti3C2T*X* sensor was integrated with an online NH3 monitoring system, including WiFi and a microcontroller, and a performance application test and analysis were carried out, which proved that the system could achieve accurate monitoring of the NH3 concentration and real-time feedback alarm of the trigger NH3_alarm. Overall, it can promote the convergence and development of the sensing and monitoring field under the IoT scenario.
