## 1 Introduction

Aluminum oxide (Al2O3) ceramics have been extensively applied in many fields involving electronics, aviation, and chemical engineering, owing to their good overall physical and mechanical properties, such as excellent electrical insulative, high hardness, and good high temperature and corrosion resistance [1,2]. The reactive air brazing (RAB) technique of ceramics was developed at the beginning of the 21st century [3,4], which can be mainly attributed to the simplicity and convenience of the process and the good service performance of joints at higher temperatures in air. RAB of Al2O3 ceramics is an effective method to obtain complicated or large-sized Al2O3 ceramic composite components, which can be applied in the electronic substrate and fuel cell [4,5]. Up to now, brazing fillers for RAB of Al2O3 ceramics are mainly involved in Ag–CuO (or Ag–Cu) [4−8], Ag–Al [9], Ag–CuO–Pt [10], Ag–CuO–TiH2 [11], and Ag–SiO2 [12]. However, the Ag–CuO-based fillers cannot endure reducing atmosphere at higher temperatures for long time since CuO can be reduced into the elemental Cu, resulting in the generation of pores at the interface and a sharp decrease in joint strength [13−15]. Indeed, the joint strength of Al2O3/Al2O3 joints was relatively low while employing Ag–Al and Ag–SiO2 fillers [9,12]. Actually, employing the lower melting point of metal oxides (such as PbO, Bi2O3, MoO3, V2O5, and B2O3) will inevitably degrade the high-temperature resistance of the joints, while adopting metal oxides with over-high melting points could bring about poor wettability on the Al2O3 ceramic substrate. Therefore, either poor service performance in reducing atmosphere or relatively low joint strength can be produced in the present RAB joints of Al2O3 ceramics to metal or itself, so it is quite necessary and urgent to seek suitable RAB fillers for Al2O3 ceramics.

As we know, AgNbO3 is perovskite-type composite oxides with good thermal and chemical stability. AgNbO3 was usually prepared by the reaction between Ag, AgO or Ag2O, and Nb2O5 in air [16,17], and mainly used for photocatalysis and energy storage [18−20]. Actually, AgNbO3 has certain metallicity, which is expected that good interfacial bonding can be produced between AgNbO3 and Ag. In particular, the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of AgNbO3 is comparable with that of Al2O3 compared with other employed oxides for RAB of ceramics, such as SiO2 [21,22], Al2TiO5 [23], and CuAlO2 [24], which can alleviate the thermal mismatch between Al2O3 ceramics and Ag to a certain extent.

In this work, we firstly performed the reactive air wetting and brazing of Al2O3 ceramics using Ag–Nb2O5 fillers, and investigated wettability, joint mechanical properties, and microstructural evolution. Moreover, first-principles calculations on the work of adhesion (*W*a) and charge density difference (CDD) on the related interfaces (Ag/Al2O3, Ag/AgNbO3, and AgNbO3/Al2O3 interfaces) were carried out based on the density functional theory (DFT) to illuminate experimental results. Indeed, *W*a is defined as the work required to divide the interface into two free surfaces per unit area [25], which can be used to theoretically characterize the interfacial bonding strength, and the CDD analysis contributes to visually understanding interatomic interactions at the interfaces.

### 2.1 Experimental methods

The Al2O3 ceramic columns (3.70 g/cm3, 99%) were prepared by hot-pressed sintering, and then cut into the circular plates of *ϕ*20 mm × 3‒5 mm for wetting and brazing experiments. After cleaning with mild alkaline solution, the ceramic plates were smoothed by a polishing machine using 180‒1600 mesh diamond grinding disks, and the brazing surfaces were polished with a diamond polishing agent. Finally, the ceramic plates were ultrasonically cleaned for 10 min in acetone. Six brazing fillers (Ag–(0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12)Nb2O5, in mol%) were prepared by mixing high-purity Ag (~5 μm, 99.99%) and Nb2O5 powders (~10 μm, 99.5%) at a speed of 60 r/min for 48 h in absolute ethanol. After drying and grinding, the six mixed powders were compacted and then crushed into pieces of ~0.2 g for wetting experiments, and compacted into thin sheets of ~*ϕ*20 mm × 100 μm for brazing experiments. The similar preparation process was described in our previous work [26].

The reactive air wetting and brazing experiments were performed in a  muffle furnace (LHT 08/18, Nabertherm, Germany). The temperature was increased to 1150 ℃ at a rate of 10 ℃/min and kept for 30 min, and then decreased to 500 ℃ at a rate of 5 ℃/min, followed by furnace cooling. The six apparent contact angles of each solidified Ag–Nb2O5/Al2O3 couple were measured from three directions using the high-temperature contact angle measuring instrument (OCA15LHT-SV, Dataphysics, Germany), and the arithmetical average value was taken as the mean one. As shown in Fig. 1(a), the Ag–Nb2O5 thin sheet was sandwiched between two Al2O3 ceramic plates, then placed together in the muffle furnace under a loading of ~50 kPa for RAB, and finally the as-formed joints were cut into cuboid blocks (10 mm × 5 mm × 5 mm). The shear strength of the Al2O3/Al2O3 joints was measured using an electronic universal material testing machine (DDL100, DOLI, Germany) on a shear clamp (Fig. 1(b)), and the arithmetical average value of four joints with the same Nb2O5 content was taken as the mean one. The surface and cross-sectional microstructures of wetting couples,  the microstructures of joint cross sections, and the typical shear fracture surface were characterized and analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The phase compositions of the top surface of a wetting couple and the joint fracture surface were identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD).

### 2.2 Computational methods

The Vienna *ab-initio* simulation package code was employed for DFT calculations [27]. The exchange-correlation potential was processed by a generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) scheme [28]. During the calculations, a convergence criterion of 10−4 eV and a plane wave kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV were adopted. The * Γ-centered k*-point grid with a *k*-spacing of approximately 0.3 Å−1 was used for structural relaxation, energy calculations, and electronic property assessment of bulk, surface, and interface models. In terms of relaxation, both the lattice frequency and atomic position were subjected to full relaxation for bulk models, while only atomic position was required for complete relaxation for surface and interface models. A vacuum layer of 15 Å along the *c*-axis was introduced to mitigate long-range interactions during the calculation of boundary conditions.

The stable structure models of Ag (*Fm** 3¯m*), Al2O3 (*R** 3¯C*), and AgNbO3 *(Pbcm*) were selected for the first-principles calculations. In accordance with Bramfitt’s theory of lattice mismatch [29], the three types of interface models of (4×3 5) Ag(111)/(1×2) Al2O3(110), (2×11) Ag(111)/(1× 34) AgNbO3(001), and (1×2) AgNbO3(001)/(1×1) Al2O3(100) have the ideal matching degree with an error of 3(001)/Al2O3(110) model at a low lattice mismatch is too large to be calculated, the Al2O3(100) plane with slightly higher surface energy is used instead of the Al2O3(110) plane to obtain an effective model with lower atom number and low lattice mismatch. Al2O3(100) has only one terminated surface, while both Al2O3(110) and AgNbO3(001) have two terminated surfaces, namely Al-terminated and O-terminated Al2O3(110) (Al–Al2O3, O–Al2O3) surfaces and Ag–O-terminated and Nb–O-terminated AgNbO3(001) (Ag–O–AgNbO3, Nb–O–AgNbO3) surfaces (Fig. S1 in the ESM). By converging the calculations for the interlayer spacing variation of each model with full relaxation of atomic positions (Table S1 in the ESM), the minimum numbers of atomic layers for the six surface models were determined: Ag–O–AgNbO3(001) and Nb–O–AgNbO3(001) models with 5 atomic layers as well as Al–Al2O3(110), O–Al2O3(110), Al2O3(100), and Ag(111) models with 7 atomic layers. Figure 2 and Table S2 in the ESM show the seven interface models constructed by stacking the optimal surface model and the corresponding lattice parameters, respectively. It can be observed that two distinct stacking positions of the O-terminated Ag(111)/Al2O3(110) interface model are formed at the top and center. Consequently, three stacking modes appear in the Ag(111)/Al2O3(110) interface model (Figs. 2(a)‒2(c)).

As the Nb2O5 content is 0.5% or 1%, the orthorhombic AgNbO3 grains are discretely distributed near the triple line (Figs. 4(a) and 4(b)), and the AgNbO3 grains aggregate together when the Nb2O5 content is increased to 2% (Fig. 4(c)). With the further increase of the Nb2O5 content, orthorhombic AgNbO3 grains are invisible, indicating that the chemical reaction becomes more and more intense. In particular, AgNbO3 grows upward along the surface of metal drop when the Nb2O5 content is 8% or 12%. Therefore, as-formed AgNbO3 gradually evolves from discrete to continuous distribution with the Nb2O5 content increasing from 0.5% to 12%, accompanied with the increase of a spreading area.

Figure 6 shows SEM images and 3D models of cross sections of Ag–Nb2O5/Al2O3 couples. As shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(e), when the Nb2O5 content is less than 1%, a small amount of AgNbO3 phases is distributed at the interface. As the Nb2O5 content is increased to 2%, a ridge is observed around the solidified droplet (Fig. 6(b)), and meanwhile a discontinuous reaction layer is formed at the interface (Fig. 6(f)). The ridge at the triple line is mainly derived from the precipitation and diffusion of the reaction product (AgNbO3) and Nb2O5. With the Nb2O5 content further increasing from 2% to 4%, the ridge becomes elongated, and the reaction layer turns into a continuous layer and connects with the ridge (Figs. 6(c) and 6(g)). However, this ridge becomes massive with the Nb2O5 content reaching 12%, and the reaction layer becomes discontinuous again, which can be mainly related to the formation of a huge ridge. In this case, much more Nb2O5 remains in the ridge.

Based on the above discussion, the positive effect of the Nb2O5 addition on the wettability of Ag on the Al2O3 substrate is mainly due to the formation of AgNbO3. When the Nb2O5 content exceeds 2%, the intense chemical reaction leads to the formation and bulging of the ridge. Due to the pinning effect of the ridge to liquid drop, the decrease in a contact angle is mainly derived from the balance between the driving force of liquid Ag and the shape of the ridge, rather than the further spreading of liquid drop [32,33].

### 3.2 Brazing

Figure 7 shows the SEM images of the Al2O3/Al2O3 joint cross sections, indicating that the brazing seam width ranges from ~40 to 60 μm. Figures 8(a)‒8(e) show a typical cross-sectional SEM image of the Al2O3/Ag–4Nb2O5/Al2O3 joint and the corresponding EDS elemental mappings, respectively, indicating that a small amount of Ag and O co-exists in the reaction layer, and most Nb element is distributed in the reaction layer. Table 1 presents elemental compositions of typical microstructures and possible phases in Fig. 7. By combining the EDS elemental mapping and the above-mentioned XRD analyses, the four phases are determined as Ag, AgNbO3, Nb2O5, and Al2O3. As shown in Figs. 7(a)‒7(d), the AgNbO3 layer gradually thickens and becomes almost continuous at the interface as the Nb2O5 content increases from 0.5% to 4%. It is noteworthy that a small amount of Nb2O5 can be observed within the AgNbO3 layer (Figs. 7(c) and 7(d)), which can hardly determine mechanical properties of the joint. As the Nb2O5 content increases to 8% or more, the remaining Nb2O5 increases substantially, and a large amount of AgNbO3 is formed in the interlayer, which can lower the joint strength due to the disappearance of the continuous Ag layer (Figs. 7(e) and 7(f)). In particular, the partial bonding between two bulk Al2O3 ceramics is only via the brittle AgNbO3 phase, inducing the generation of microcracks (Fig. 7(f)).

Figure 9(a) shows the variation of average shear strength of Al2O3/Ag–Nb2O5/Al2O3 joints with the Nb2O5 content. The average shear strength firstly climbs and then decreases with the Nb2O5 content varying from 0.5% to 12%, and reaches the maximum of ~65.1 MPa while employing Ag–4Nb2O5. As we know, the variation of the joint shear strength is closely related to the interfacial behavior and interlayer microstructure. During the brazing process, solid Nb2O5 aggregates at the interface and reacts with liquid Ag and O2 from air to form AgNbO3 on the ceramic substrate, and then liquid Ag nucleates and solidifies on the surface of the AgNbO3 layer during the cooling process (Fig. 8(f)). Actually, AgNbO3 accumulates on the ceramic surface and gradually becomes a continuous and thin layer, which can contribute to the strengthening of interfacial bonding. Moreover, the continuous thin AgNbO3 layer between Ag and Al2O3 ceramic substrate contributes to a decrease in the gradient of CTE, thereby reducing the residual thermal stress [34,35]. However, the overproduction of brittle Nb2O5 and AgNbO3 phases in the interlayer can induce the formation of microcracks and bring about the discontinuous Ag layer, producing weak bonding.

Similarly, the fracture behavior of Al2O3/Ag–Nb2O5/Al2O3 joints is closely related to the interfacial and interlayer microstructures. From fracture morphologies and phase compositions (Figs. 5(b) and 9(b)), the Al2O3/Ag–4Nb2O5/Al2O3 joint fracture mainly occurs at the interlayer/substrate interface, with the crack passing across the two interfacial layers and Ag layer. Actually, the fracture of the Al2O3/Ag–(0.5‒2)Nb2O5/Al2O3 joints mainly occurs at the interface as a result of the poor interfacial bonding of Ag and Al2O3 (Fig. 9(d)), and most of the Al2O3/Ag–(8, 12)Nb2O5/Al2O3 joints break off in the interlayer due to the disappearance of the continuous Ag layer and the formation of the brittle AgNbO3 phase (Fig. 9(f)). However, the final fracture of the Al2O3/Ag–4Nb2O5/Al2O3 joints appears at the interface or in the ceramic substrate (Fig. 9(e)), showing relatively good interfacial bonding.

### 3.3 Interfacial bonding and electronic properties

*W*a of Ag/Al2O3, Ag/AgNbO3, and Al2O3/AgNbO3 interfaces in the Ag–Nb2O5/Al2O3 system were assessed to investigate theoretically the effect of the Nb2O5 addition on interfacial bonding. *W*a of the interface model is obtained by calculating the energy difference between two relaxed slab models and their relaxed stacked model, which is expressed by Eq. (2):

(2) Wa=(Eαslab+Eβslab−Eα/βslab)/A

where * Eαslab and  Eβslab are the two energies of the relaxed  α (Ag(111) or AgNbO3(001)) and  β (Al2O3(110), AgNbO3(001), and Al2O3(100)) slabs, respectively;  Eα/βslab is the energy of the relaxed Ag(111)/Al2O3(110), Ag(111)/AgNbO3(001), or AgNbO3(001)/Al2O3(100) interfaces; A* is the contact area of the * α and  β slabs. The method to obtain the preliminary spacing distances for stack models is described in the ESM. As shown in Table 2, the Ag–O-termination for the Ag(111)/AgNbO3(001) interface has the highest W*a, indicating excellent interfacial bonding, which could explain that the joint facture mainly occurs at the AgNbO3/Al2O3 interface rather than the Ag/AgNbO3 interface in the shear test (Figs. 9(b)‒9(d)). *W*a of the Ag–O–AgNbO3(001)/Al2O3(100) model is higher than those of three Ag(111)/Al2O3(110) models, indicating good reliability of the Ag/AgNbO3/Al2O3 structure.

The charge transfer at the Ag/Al2O3, Ag/AgNbO3, and Al2O3/AgNbO3 interfaces was investigated by CDD, which can directly reflect the bonding state at the interface and the gains and losses of electrons [36]. Figure 10 shows CDDs for the Ag(111)/O–Al2O3(110)-center, Ag(111)/Ag–O–AgNbO3(001), and Ag–O–AgNbO3(001)/Al2O3(100) interfaces with the corresponding plane-average charge density. Therefore, the formation of the Ag/AgNbO3/Al2O3 structure is beneficial to improve the joint strength due to the good interfacial bonding and the gradient change of CTE.

## 4 Conclusions

With the Nb2O5 content increasing from 0.5% to 12%, the contact angle decreases monotonously from ~71.6° to 32.5°. The average joint shear strength increases firstly and then drops, reaching the maximum strength of ~65.1 MPa while employing Ag–4Nb2O5. The discrete, discontinuous, and almost continuous AgNbO3, bulk Nb2O5 and AgNbO3 (no continuous Ag interlayer), and bridged AgNbO3 interlayer with microcracks are formed in the interlayer in turn with the increase of the Nb2O5 content. The formation of an almost continuous thin AgNbO3 layer at the interface contributes to the improvement of wettability and joint strength. *W*a at the Ag–O-termination for the Ag(111)/AgNbO3(001) interface is much higher than those of Ag(111)/Al2O3(110) and AgNbO3(001)/Al2O3(100) interfaces, and meanwhile *W*a of the Ag–O–AgNbO3(001)/Al2O3(100) interface is higher than those of three Ag(111)/Al2O3(110) interfaces, indicating good reliability of the Ag/AgNbO3/Al2O3 structure. In addition to the Ag–O ionic bond, the respective formation of Ag–Ag metallic bond and Al–O ionic bond at the Ag/AgNbO3 and AgNbO3/Al2O3 interfaces contributes to the interfacial bonding.
