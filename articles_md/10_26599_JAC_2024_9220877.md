## 1 Introduction

SiC-based ceramic matrix composites (SiC-CMCs) are promising candidates for replacing high-temperature alloys and Ni-based superalloys in the hot end components of engine combustion chambers due to their light weight, high specific strength, and resistance to high-temperature oxidation and creep at elevated temperatures [1−3]. However, the harsh service environment dominated by water vapor can cause corrosion of SiC-CMCs, leading to rapid mass loss and subsequent performance degradation [4,5]. Therefore, advanced thermal/environmental barrier coatings (T/EBC) are urgently needed to improve the service life of SiC-CMCs under harsh operating conditions. Additionally, with increasing service temperatures, corrosion may occur due to calcium–magnesium–aluminosilicate (CMAS; CaO–MgO–AlO1.5–SiO2) molten salt, which can take the form of volcanic ash or airborne sand [6]. Thus, ideal T/EBC materials should not only have low thermal properties but also effectively mitigate CMAS corrosion of the SiC-CMCs matrix at high temperatures.

Although traditional T/EBC materials for CMCs, such as mullite (3Al2O3·2SiO2), mullite/yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ), and commercialized mullite/Ba1−*x*Sr*x*Al2Si2O8 (BSAS), have been extensively investigated due to their good thermal and chemical compatibility with the matrix [7,8], their high-temperature resistance and resistance to CMAS corrosion are weak, and they cannot effectively protect the base materials above 1300 ℃ [9]. Therefore, new T/EBC materials that are resistant to molten CMAS corrosion are urgently needed. Rare-earth (RE) disilicates are considered promising materials for T/EBC applications due to their low thermal conductivity, matched coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) to SiC-CMCs, and excellent high-temperature stability [10−12]. For example, β-RE2Si2O7 (RE = Sc, Lu, and Yb) demonstrate similar CTE to SiC and no polymorphs at high temperatures, both of which are key requirements for T/EBC materials [13]. While the interaction behavior of a single RE2Si2O7 with molten CMAS has been studied, weak corrosion resistance to high-temperature CMAS was found, resulting in a corrosion layer of 400–700 μm after 48 h of corrosion at 1300 ℃, which is still unacceptable for T/EBC materials of only 200 μm [14,15].

Previous studies have reported that Multivariate RE-disilicate solid solutions exhibit improved corrosion resistance, as well as the physical properties required for T/EBC applications. Lv *et al*. [16] reported on binary RE-disilicate (Yb1–*x*Ho*x*)2Si2O7 solid solutions, which showed better thermo-physical properties compared to the individual RE2Si2O7. Furthermore, Maier *et al*. [17] prepared ternary RE-disilicate (Y,Yb,Lu)2Si2O7 solid solutions, which demonstrated excellent resistance to water vapor corrosion compared to the individual RE2Si2O7. When the number of RE elements exceeds four, a multicomponent RE2Si2O7 is formed, which is also known as high-entropy disilicate ceramics (HE RE2Si2O7) [17]. High-entropy materials, referring to multi-principal solid solutions consisting of four or more metal structural elements, have garnered widespread attention due to their outstanding performance [18−20]. For example, Sun *et al*. [21] synthesized multicomponent (Er0.25Tm0.25Yb0.25Lu0.25)2Si2O7 composites by the hot press sintering method and revealed that this high entropy disilicate composites had good CMAS properties at 1500 ℃. Wang *et al*. [22] prepared high-entropy ceramics (Y1/6Yb1/6Er1/6Sc1/6Gd1/6Eu1/6)2Si2O7, which showed lower thermal conductivity compared to single-component rare-earth disilicates. Therefore, all the progress demonstrated an appealing perspective on the design of novel T/EBC with multiple RE species in a single phase pure solid solution [23,24]. Meanwhile, Zhang *et al*. [25] reported on new high-entropy ceramics (Dy1/7Ho1/7Er1/7Tm1/7Yb1/7Lu1/7Y1/7)PO4 with lower thermal conductivity than their single-component counterpart, but the thermal expansion coefficient of HE (7RE1/7)PO4 (5.96×10−6 ℃−1 from room temperature (RT) to 900 ℃) did not match that of SiC-CMCs ((4.5–5.5)×10−6 ℃−1), which would cause thermal stress and coating failure. At present, most of the T/EBC mainstream materials on SiCf/SiC matrix materials are still high entropy rare earth silicates and corrosion resistance is one of the most important properties for T/EBC materials. Even though high-entropy rare earth disilicate has excellent CMAS corrosion resistance properties compared to their single RE counterparts, but still the recession layer depth is about 100–300 μm, which is not considered a really good candidate against CMAS resistance for the T/EBC coating which has a thickness of only a few tens to hundreds.

Thus, in this work, a multicomponent RE-disilicate T/EBC (Lu1/7Yb1/7Sc1/7Er1/7Y1/7Ho1/7Dy1/7)2Si2O7 ((7RE1/7)2Si2O7) was prepared by solid reaction process. The phase composition, micro-morphology structure, and high-temperature phase stability of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). To value its feasibility as T/EBC materials, the thermophysical property and the CMAS corrosion resistance of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 were further evaluated. The phases and morphologies of specimens were characterized following their exposure to molten CMAS for various durations. Some factors that influenced the high-temperature molten CMAS corrosion resistances were figured out and discussed. The results will be helpful to provide a significant RE doping strategy in the design of high-entropy RE disilicates, and lay a theoretical foundation for the development of a new generation of EBC materials.

### 2.1 Preparation of bulk (Lu1/7Yb1/7Sc1/7Er1/7Y1/7Ho1/7Dy1/7)2Si2O7

Commercially available powders of Lu2O3, Yb2O3, Sc2O3, Er2O3, Y2O3, Ho2O3, Dy2O3, and SiO2 (99.9% purity, HWRK Chem Co., Ltd., China) were used as raw materials to synthesize single-phase (Lu1/7Yb1/7Sc1/7Er1/7Y1/7Ho1/7Dy1/7)2Si2O7 followed a stoichiometric ratio. The mixed powders were ball-milled in a planetary ball mill for 12 h at the speed of 300 r/min, with agate balls and ethanol as media. The resulting slurry was dried at 70 ℃ for 24 h to obtain final powders. Phase-pure (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 powders were prepared at 1500 ℃ for 2 h by pressure-less synthesis method. For the fabrication of bulk materials, the prepared (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 powders were ball milled for 24 h, dried in the oven, and cold-pressed under a pressure of 10 MPa for 2 min to get green bodies. Then, the green bodies were sintered in air at 1600 ℃ for 10 h to prepare (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics.

### 2.2 Phase composition and microstructure of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics

The microstructure analysis and elemental mapping of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 powders were performed using a scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM; Tecnai G2 F20, FEI, USA) with attached high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) probe and energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS).

The phase compositions of samples and corrosion products were determined by XRD (D8 Advanced, Germany) using Cu Kα radiation with a step size of 0.02° at a scanning rate of 2 (°)/min. The microstructures and element distributions of samples and corrosion products were analyzed by a scanning electron microscope (SU8010N, Hitachi, Japan) with an attached energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS; TECNAI G220, FEI, USA) system. The lattice constant analysis was obtained by XRD Rietveld refinements using a general structural analysis system (GSAS) program. An X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; Nexsa, Thermo Fisher, USA) was applied to analyze the chemical states of the elements of the specimen.

### 2.3 Thermal and mechanical properties of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics

The thermal diffusion coefficient (*D*th) of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics was measured using the laser flash analysis (LFA457, NETZSCH, Germany) in an argon atmosphere at room temperature to 1200 ℃. The HE (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 bulks with the size of *ϕ*12.7 mm × 2 mm were used and coated with platinum and graphite layers to prevent heat radiation. The isobaric heat capacity (*c**p*) was calculated through the Neumann–Kopp rule in terms of constituent oxides (RE2O3 and SiO2) according to Refs. [10−14]. Also, *ρ* of the (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 bulk was measured by Archimedes drainage method. Thus, the thermal conductivity (*κ*) of the HE (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 bulk was obtained according to Eq. (1):

(1) κ=Dth⋅cp⋅ρ

where *c**p* and *ρ* are the heat capacity and the density of the samples, respectively. CTE of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics from room temperature to 1400 ℃ with a heating rate of 5 ℃∙min−1 in nitrogen atmosphere were investigated by a thermal expansion instrument (DIL 402C, NETZSCH, Germany). The testing bulks were rectangular bars with dimensions of 3 mm × 4 mm × 20 mm.

The reduced modulus and hardness characterization were performed by a nanoindenter (Agilent G200, KLA-TENCOR, USA).

### 2.4 CMAS corrosion tests of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics

Considering the actual application environment, the ability of as-prepared (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics to resist CMAS corrosion was tested in this work, and a model CMAS composition of 33CaO–9MgO–13AlO1.5–45SiO2 was used. CaO, MgO, Al2O3, and SiO2 powders were mixed according to the above stoichiometric ratio, and then annealed at 1550 ℃ for 4 h and quenched in water. By repeating the above steps once, the CMAS glass for this study was obtained. Then, the CMAS glass powders were mixed with ethanol and then uniformly coated on the surface of bulk samples with the amount of 30 mg∙cm−2. Then the samples were heat-treated at 1300 ℃ for 2 and 48 h in a furnace. To investigate the corrosion mechanism, the CMAS powers were mixed and added into (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 powders to press into disks with a diameter of 12 mm, and then annealed at 1300 ℃ for 2 h. The mass ratios of CMAS/(7RE1/7)2Si2O7 were 1/20.

### 3.1 Composition and microstructure of high-entropy (7RE1/7)2Si2O7

Figure 1 displays the XRD patterns of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 powders and standard XRD peaks of seven individual RE2Si2O7 components (RE = Lu, Yb, Er, Sc, Y, Ho, and Dy) obtained from the standard powder diffraction file (PDF) database. It can be observed that the diffraction peaks of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 powders are located in between the seven single rare earth disilicates and are similar to the peaks of β-Yb2Si2O7. This indicates that the (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 powders synthesized have a β-monoclinic structure. Furthermore, among the seven individual RE-disilicates considered, Yb2Si2O7, Lu2Si2O7, and Sc2Si2O7 retained a β polymorph until their melting point, whereas Y2Si2O7, Ho2Si2O7, Er2Si2O7, and Dy2Si2O7 underwent a polymorphic phase transformation upon heating [9,11]. It is suggested that the high entropy effect led to the formation of a single phase in the multicomponent (7RE1/7)2Si2O7, despite the different crystal structures of the individual RE-disilicates. The existence of a single β-monoclinic (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 suggests that the multicomponent disilicate has excellent structural stability.

To further clarify the crystal structures and elemental homogeneity of the HE (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 powders on the nanoscale, the TEM analysis was performed, as shown in Fig. 3. Figure 3 presents the TEM and EDS images of the (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 powders. As shown in the TEM bright-field image, the micro-morphology of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 powders is irregular and lamellar. ### 3.2 Thermal properties of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 bulk

The thermal properties, including the thermal conductivity and CTE, are important parameters to determine the validity of the T/EBC. Figure 6 shows the thermal properties of bulk (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 from room temperature to 1500 K. Figure 6(a) presents the *c**p* curve of bulk (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 according to the Neumann–Kopp rule. *c**p* (0.53−0.65 J·g−1·K−1) of the (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics increases as the temperature rises from 273 to 1500 K. This increase in the specific heat capacity is attributed to the enhanced phonon scattering caused by the large volume expansion with increasing the temperature [26].

It can be observed that the thermal conductivity decreases from 3.04 to 1.81 W·m−1·K−1 as the temperature rises from 273 to 1500 K. Subsequently, it gradually increases to 1.89 W·m−1·K−1 at 1273 K, which may be attributed to thermal radiation at high temperatures [22]. Evidently, the thermal conductivity of the as-synthesized (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics is significantly lower than that of the single-component rare earth silicate. Furthermore, the thermal diffusivity (α*) and thermal conductivity (*κ*) of the (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics decrease with increasing the temperature, indicating a dominant contribution from phonon harmonic scattering.

In this work, the outstanding thermal performance of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics can be explained by Debye’s phonon theory, where the thermal conductivity can be reduced by phonon scattering for insulating materials. Here, the thermal conductivity is expressed by Eq. (2) [27]:

(2) α=13νmΛ

where *v*m represents the average sound velocity; *Λ* denotes the mean free path of phonon. Meanwhile, *Λ* mainly consists of four parts (Eq. (3)) [28]:

(3) 1Λ=1Λphonon+1Λdefect+1Λboundary+∑i1Λi

where *Λ*phonon is the phonon mean free path that is determined by phonon–phonon scattering; *Λ*defect and *Λ*boundary correspond to the phonon-defect and the phonon-boundary scattering, respectively; *Λ**i* is caused by other scattering factors [29]. Among the four scattering mechanisms, the phonon–phonon scattering is mainly determined by the temperature, which means that *Λ*phonon varies with the temperature [30]. That is, *Λ*phonon denotes the intrinsic thermal conduction of the materials, and external factors such as defects affect the other three scattering mechanisms.

The mean free path of phonons in crystalline materials can be calculated by Eq. (4) [27]:

(4) Λ=Λ0[exp(θDbTn13)−1]

where *Λ*0 is the pre-exponential factor; *b* denotes a constant approximately equal to 2; *θ*D represents the Debye temperature; *T* is the temperature; *n* corresponds to the number of atoms in the unit cell. At high temperatures, *Λ*−1 can be approximated as Eq. (5) [13]:

(5) 1Λ≈CΛ0[exp(θDbTn13)−1]+D=(bTn13θD)T+(D−12C)

Equation (5) shows that there is a linear relationship between *Λ*−1 and the temperature (*T*). In Eq. (2), the average sound velocity (*v*m) has little to do with the temperature. Therefore, the inverse thermal diffusivity (*α*−1) is also approximately linearly related to the temperature (*T*) (Eq. (6)) [31]:

(6) 1Λ≈1α≈(bTn13θD)T+(D−12C)

Obviously, the reciprocal thermal diffusivity (*α*−1) is proportional to the temperature (*T*). According to the discussions above, the calculated *α*−1 is plotted with brown triangles in Fig. 6(d). It can be seen that *α*−1 increases linearly with the increase of temperatures, and the fitting relationship is shown in Eq. (7) in a dotted blue line:

(7) α−1=0.00121T+0.53177

Notably, *α*−1 deviates from the linear line beyond 1073 K and decreases gradually, which suggests a prominent thermal radiation resistance of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics. The intrinsic thermal diffusivity recalculated by substituting fitted *α*−1 into Eq. (7) is illustrated with the green curve in Fig. 6(d). It decreases smoothly from 1.13 to 0.42 mm2·s−1 in the range of room temperature to 1473 K. Based on these results, the minimum intrinsic thermal conductivity is calculated to be 1.39 W·m−1·K−1 at 1473 K after removing the thermal radiation effect, which is displayed with the brown line in Fig. 6(d). In conclusion, the low conductivity of the as-prepared materials can be attributed to the following three reasons:

First, for crystalline materials with defects, there is a negative correlation between thermal conductivity and phonon scattering coefficient. The phonon scattering coefficient is dominated by defects (Eq. (8)) [32]:

(8) Γi=fi{(ΔMiM)2+2[6.43γ1+ν1−ν(Δδiδ)]2}

where the subscript *i* symbolizes a kind of lattice defect, and *f**i* represents the concentration of the defect. *M* and *δ* represent the average atomic mass and the ionic radius of the lattice site, respectively. Δ*M**i* *= M − M**i*, Δ*δ**i* *= δ − δ**i*, where *M**i* and *δ**i* denote the atomic mass and the ionic radius of a specific defect, respectively. *γ* and *ν* are the Grüneisen parameter and the Poisson’s ratio, respectively. In (Lu1/7Yb1/7Sc1/7Er1/7Y1/7Ho1/7Dy1/7)2Si2O7, Lu, Yb, Er, Sc, Y, Ho, and Dy atoms are randomly distributed at the RE lattice sites. The mismatch of atomic mass and ionic radius at these sites gives rise to a lot of defects, which enhance phonon scattering and result in reduced thermal conductivity. Secondly, the low conductivity of the as-prepared materials has to do with the strengthened phonon scattering from the cocktail effect of HECs. The disordered elemental distribution contributes to the enhanced synergistic effect of multicomponent elements and the increased number of scattering centers. Finally, as recognized by Clarke’s principle for the selection of TBCs, materials with expected low thermal conductivity commonly exhibit four principal characteristics, namely high atomic mass, complex structure, a collection of diverse atoms per molecule, and non-directional bonding [33,34]. The fabricated (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics in this work just satisfy well with the descriptions above with multicomponent elements randomly distributed in unit cells.

The cyclosilicate is also a multicomponent Ca–RE–Si product, which is derived from the structure of pseudo-wollastonite; α-CaSiO3 and crystallizes in the space group C*2*/c*, with *Z* = 4 [37,38]. The larger the radius of RE3+, the closer to the radius of Ca2+, and the more stable structure of Ca3RE2(Si3O9)2 [39]. For high-entropy ceramics, the CMAS corrosion resistance depends on the average radius of RE3+. The average RE3+ radius of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics is 0.860 Å, which is close to the Ca2+ radius (1.0 Å). Meanwhile, the reason for the excellent corrosion resistance of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics can be further explained by the cooperative increase of elements. (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 incorporates Lu, Yb, Er, Sc, Y, Ho, and Dy elements. The formation enthalpies of Ca3RE2(Si3O9)2 (RE = Lu, Yb, Er, Sc, Y, Ho, and Dy) are different. Hence, Lu, Yb, Er, Sc, Y, Ho, and Dy ions present a gradient velocity in the precipitation of Ca3RE2(Si3O9)2 [42,43]. Yb, Lu, Sc, and Y with small ionic radii will slow down the formation of reaction product Ca3RE2(Si3O9)2 which is comprised of Lu, Yb, Er, Sc, Y, Ho, and Dy elements (Table 4). Studies have shown that the smaller the radius of RE3+, the weaker the affinity with Ca2+ and the lower the reactivity, so the corrosion resistance is stronger [10−15]. Consequently, Yb, Lu, Sc, and Y in (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 effectively mitigate CMAS corrosion.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, the multicomponent β-(7RE1/7)2Si2O7 disilicate, dense and phase-pure environmental barrier coating materials, were successfully prepared by a solid reaction process. The thermal properties and corrosion resistance of the (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics were systematically studied. The (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 ceramics exhibit excellent thermal properties, and their thermal conductivity is lower than that of one-component silicate materials, only 1.81 W·m−1·K−1 at 1000 ℃. Meanwhile, the interaction of (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 bulk samples with molten CMAS at 1300 ℃ for 2 and 48 h was studied in detail. (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 disilicate shows significantly enhanced CMAS resistance at 1300 ℃ compared with those single RE-containing disilicates. The present results indicate that (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 solid solution possesses good capability when it interacts with molten CMAS at high temperatures, and its reaction layer is only 22 μm after the corrosion of 48 h at 1300 ℃. Hence, the current results show the improved (7RE1/7)2Si2O7 corrosion resistance achieved by the synergistic effect of multiple-component rare-earth disilicate and validate the corrosion resistance optimization through high entropy engineering of rare earth species as advanced T/EBC coating materials.
