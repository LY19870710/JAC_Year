## 1 Introduction

To further improve the operating temperature and thermal efficiency of turbine engines, thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) are extensively employed on hot sections of engines to protect them from high temperature gas [1−4]. In recent years, several oxides with low thermal conductivity and better thermophysical performance have been developed as promising candidates for TBC materials [5−7]. However, existing TBC materials, including yttria stabilized zirconia (YSZ) [8−11], LaZr2O7 [12,13], LaMgAl11O19 [14,15], and BaZrO3 [16], display partial transparency to infrared thermal radiation within turbine chambers (typically 1–5 μm) [17], allowing a significant portion of thermal radiation to penetrate the coating and be absorbed by the underlaying substrate. This leads to increased substrate temperature and reduced thermal protection. According to Mie scattering theory, nanoparticles in nanostructured YSZ (nYSZ) TBCs are expected to enhance the thermal radiation scattering, thereby improving the thermal radiation blocking and thermal insulation of the coating [18]. However, the low nanoparticle retention during the plasma spraying process significantly limits their effectiveness. To address these challenges, this study introduces an innovative method for coating nanoparticles with a carbon film to suppress the melting and merging of nanoparticles, thereby improving the nanoparticle retention rate and thermal radiation blocking of TBCs.

The thermal radiation blocking performance of TBCs is predominantly governed by their optical properties, particularly their absorption and scattering coefficients. When the absorption coefficient exceeds 10,000 m−1, TBCs become opaque, decreasing the substrate temperature and approaching the opaque coating limit [19]. To increase the absorption coefficient, secondary phases such as GdMnO3 [20], Yb2Zr2O7 [21], and doped rare earth zirconate [22,23] have been incorporated into semitransparent TBCs. These additions enhance the near-infrared thermal radiation absorption, achieving absorption coefficients in the range of a few thousand m−1. More recently, composite materials with microscale Pt nanoparticles dispersed in a GdZr2O7 ceramic matrix have been developed. The composite exhibited an exceptionally high absorption coefficient of approximately 3×104 m−1, which was attributed to the resonance frequencies of red-shift plasmon and the multipole resonances of Pt nanoparticles [24]. However, despite these advances, the limited absorption band restricts their effectiveness in improving the thermal insulation performance of TBCs.

Alternatively, increasing the scattering coefficient has been proven to be a more effective strategy for improving the thermal radiation blocking ability of TBCs. Higher scattering coefficients lead to increased thermal radiation reflectance at the exterior surface of the coating, which directly reduce the radiative heat transfer and substrate temperature [19]. The scattering behavior is affected primarily by the coating microstructure, notably the pores and microcracks. Previous studies have shown that plasma-sprayed (PS) TBCs possess increased porosity, resulting in increased scattering coefficients when larger feedstock powder sizes or extended spraying distances are used. This leads to a decrease in the substrate temperature by as much as 40 °C due to the enhanced surface reflectance of thermal radiation [25,26]. However, excessive porosity, particularly from globular and inter splat pores, weakens the splat‒splat connection, leading to a decrease in the mechanical properties of the TBCs, such as the Young’s modulus (*E*) and fracture toughness [27−29]. Therefore, it remains a critical challenge to improve the scattering coefficients while maintaining their mechanical characteristics.

The nYSZ TBCs have garnered significant attention because of their remarkable mechanical performance in high-temperature applications [30−32]. These coatings typically exhibit a bimodal microstructure, consisting of dense regions formed from fully molten YSZ particles and porous nYSZ regions resulting from un-melted or partially molten particles [33−36]. Considering that nanoparticles substantially increase the YSZ/air interface, Mie scattering theory predicts that they effectively scatter thermal radiation [18], thereby improving the thermal radiation blocking ability. However, as far as we are aware, there is a significant deficiency in the existing research regarding the thermal radiation properties of nYSZ TBCs. A major challenge associated with nYSZ TBCs is the poor retention of nanoparticles during the plasma spraying process, as they are highly prone to melting when heated by a plasma flame. Currently, to preserve the nanoparticles, they are encapsulated in semi-melted feeding powders, leading to a low preparation efficiency. Furthermore, the presence of semi-melted particles weakens the interlayer bonding, thereby compromising the mechanical properties [27]. Our previous research has shown that coating ceramic particles with a carbon film can effectively suppress particle melting and prevent the merging of molten particles during the plasma spraying process. Similarly, this approach can be utilized to preserve nanoparticles without generating semi-melted particles, which not only enhances the thermal radiation blocking but also improves production efficiency while maintaining the mechanical properties of the coatings.

In the present study, PS TBCs incorporating varying fractions of nYSZ particles were prepared using different nanoparticle mixing techniques. The thermal radiation properties were characterized, and the thermal insulation performance under service conditions was modeled. To further explore the scattering mechanism of nanoparticles, simulations of the electric field and far-field behavior were conducted using COMSOL Multiphysics software. Additionally, the high-temperature stability of the thermal radiation properties was evaluated.

#### 2.1.1 Fabrication of nYSZ@carbon composite powder

The fabrication of nYSZ@carbon film composite powder comprised two main stages, specifically, spray drying and vacuum pyrolysis. Initially, commercial nYSZ particles were dispersed in a water-soluble starch solution and stirred for 20 min to form a homogeneous slurry. The mass ratio of nYSZ, starch, and deionized water was 1 : 2 : 5. The slurry was then spray-dried using a pneumatic spray dryer, with the parameters outlined in Table 1. The resulting nYSZ@starch powder underwent stabilization in air at a consistent temperature of 210 °C for 24 h, followed by carbonization at 900 °C for 200 min under vacuum at a rate of temperature change of 2 °C/min to yield the nYSZ@carbon composite powder.

#### 2.1.3 nYSZ coating deposition process

Coatings featuring 8YSZ and various proportions of nYSZ and nYSZ@carbon were deposited using an A-2000 plasma spraying system from Sulzer Metco AG, USA, which is equipped with a SG-100 plasma gun from Thermach LLC, USA. To obtain free-standing coatings for thermal radiation property measurements, the coatings were deposited on sacrificial carbon sheets (40 mm × 40 mm × 5 mm). Argon was selected as the primary gas, while a mixture of 70% argon and 30% hydrogen served as the secondary gas. Since the plasma spraying parameters directly affect the plasma flame temperature and the molten state of the feedstock powder, which subsequently affect the microstructure and properties of the coating, all the samples were processed under identical spraying conditions, as detailed in Table 3. Coatings with various nYSZ mass fractions, namely, 0% (YSZ), 15% (nYSZ-15), and 30% (nYSZ-30), and different nYSZ@carbon mass fractions, namely, 15% (nYSZ@C-15) and 30% (nYSZ@C-30), were prepared. To calculate the absorption and scattering coefficients, coatings with different thicknesses (210±20 and 270±20 μm) were prepared for each composition.

Following the plasma spraying process, heat treatment at 800 °C in an air environment was performed to remove the carbon substrate and strip away the carbon film on the nYSZ surface, referred to as heat treatment I. To investigate the high-temperature stability of the coatings, all the samples were subjected to heat treatment at 1300 °C in an electron furnace for 100 h, referred to as heat treatment II.

#### 2.2.1 Structural characterization

The coatings’ microstructure was analyzed with a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; CLARA, Tescan, Czech Republic) that features a backscattered electron (BSE) imaging detector. The morphology of nYSZ@carbon was analyzed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM; X-MaxN 80T IE250, Oxford, UK), which operated at 200 kV. An energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDX; Xplore30, Oxford, UK) was utilized to conduct the elemental analysis. Mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) was employed to assess the distribution of open pores.

#### 2.2.2 Thermal property evaluation

The spectra for infrared reflectance and transmittance of the free-standing coatings were obtained using a Perkin Elmer Lambda 1050+ spectrometer (USA), covering a wavelength range of 0.25–2.5 μm, along with a Thermo Nicolet iS50 Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (USA), which operates in the 2.5–14 μm wavelength range. Both instruments were fitted with an integration sphere accessory (M29056B, PIKE, USA). To assess the thermal diffusivity (*α*), free-standing coatings measuring *ϕ*12.7 mm in diameter were examined using a Netzsch LFA 427 laser flash analyzer, USA, in a helium environment over a temperature interval ranging from 200 to 1300 °C. Both surfaces of the coatings were covered with graphite for the absorption of flashing energy. The thermal conductivity (*K*) was assessed using Eq. (1):

(1) K=ρ⋅cp⋅α

where *ρ*, *α*, and *c**p* denote the measured density, thermal diffusivity, and specific heat capacity, respectively. The coating density was assessed by the Archimedean drainage method, while *c**p* was estimated in accordance with the Neumann–Kopp law based on earlier studies [37−39].

#### 2.3.1 Simulation of thermal radiation scattering

The infrared thermal radiation scattering behaviors, including the electric field and far field pattern, were simulated through the wave optics module of COMSOL Multiphysics software. The splat and pore structure were simplified as a ceramic block (5 μm × 5 μm × 3 μm) with an ellipsoidal pore (2 μm × 1.8 μm × 1.2 μm). The simulated incident thermal radiation wavelength was set as 1.5 μm, corresponding to a wavelength with higher energy density within the turbine chamber.

### 3.1 Influence of nYSZ on thermal radiation and thermal insulation properties

The microstructures of the nYSZ@carbon particles and the as-prepared feedstock powder are presented in Fig. 2. The nYSZ particles, produced via the pneumatic spray drying process, agglomerated into spherical structures with a diameter of approximately 3.0 μm, as shown in Fig. 2(a). The nYSZ particles were uniformly coated with water-soluble starch. In contrast to the hollow spherical structure formed by conventional micro-sized YSZ particles observed in our previous work [42], nYSZ particles formed solid spheres during the spray drying process. This difference can be attributed to several factors. First, the smaller droplet size generated during pneumatic spray drying facilitates more efficient water evaporation, leading to lower internal pressure. Second, the higher diffusivity and fluidity of the nYSZ particles enable rapid movement and rearrangement during water evaporation. Moreover, owing to their greater specific surface area and surface energy, nYSZ particles are more prone to self-assembly. After vacuum pyrolysis, the nYSZ powders retained their spherical morphology, but the surface became rougher, and no coating was detected by SEM, as shown in Fig. 2(b). However, the EDS mapping results in Fig. 2(c) confirmed the presence of carbon on the nYSZ surface. This can be attributed to the relatively lower carbon film thickness and the low mass of carbon atoms, which results in weak scattering under electron beams and a reduced generation of secondary electrons. To further investigate the microstructure and composition of nYSZ particles, TEM analysis was conducted, as shown in Fig. 2(d). The high-resolution TEM image of the nYSZ@carbon particles reveals two distinct regions: an ordered interior YSZ domain and a highly disordered surface domain. The TEM image of pure nYSZ particles in Fig. 2(e) clearly shows an ordered structure. Distinct lattice stripes are observed on the surface of the YSZ region in the nYSZ@carbon particles, with an interplanar spacing of 0.19 nm. The EDS mapping results in Fig. 2(f) indicate that the disordered surface region, with a thickness of approximately 15 nm, is primarily composed of carbon. This disordered structure arises because the vacuum pyrolysis temperature (900 °C) is insufficient to convert carbon into crystalline graphite. The choice of a lower pyrolysis temperature serves two purposes: (1) to prevent the sintering of nanoparticles at high temperatures and (2) to ensure that the amorphous carbon film, characterized by weaker carbon‒carbon bonds, is more easily oxidized and removed during the heat treatment process. The SEM morphology of the feedstock powder prepared by the centrifugal spray drying process is shown in Fig. 2(g), and all the powders exhibit a typical spherical morphology with a diameter range of 40±10 μm. Figures 2(h1), 2(h2), and 2(h3) reveal the magnified images of YSZ, nYSZ and nYSZ@carbon feedstock powders, which reveal that the YSZ feedstock powder consists of micro-sized YSZ particles with dimensions of approximately 1.0 μm, while the nYSZ and nYSZ@carbon feedstock powders consist of micro-sized YSZ particles and nYSZ particles, with the nYSZ particles showing excellent dispersibility.

The images and microstructure morphology of the prepared coatings are presented in Fig. 3. Figure 3(a) displays the optical image of the sprayed coating. Due to the abundant grain boundaries and pores within the coatings, the as-sprayed YSZ and nYSZ coatings exhibit high visible light reflectance and appear white in color. In contrast, the nYSZ@C coating appears dark gray, which is attributed to the strong absorption of visible light by the carbon film within the coating. Under the oxygen-free environment of a plasma flame, the carbon film on the nYSZ particle surface remains solid during the plasma spraying process and is retained within the coating after impact with the substrate. Figure 3(b) shows an optical image of nYSZ@C coating before and after heat treatment at 800 °C. After heat treatment, the carbon film is entirely removed, and the coating transitions from dark gray to white. This suggests that the carbon film is readily eliminated under the service conditions of TBCs. More importantly, a significant number of nanopores are generated in situ* within the coating upon removal of the carbon film. To further investigate the changes in the carbon film and YSZ particles during heat treatment, the coatings were ground into microparticles for TEM analysis. Figures 3(c) and 3(d) show the TEM images and corresponding electron diffraction patterns of nYSZ@C-30 before and after heat treatment, while Figs. 3(e) and 3(f) present the corresponding high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images of the selected local regions. Prior to heat treatment, a diffuse pattern between the diffraction rings of YSZ was observed due to the wide scattering of electron beam by the amorphous carbon film, which is further observed in the HRTEM image in Fig. 3(e). However, after heat treatment, the diffuse diffraction pattern disappears, and only sharp diffraction fringes corresponding to YSZ are observed. Figure 3(f) indicates that no carbon film on the YSZ surface was observed after heat treatment, leaving behind a highly ordered atomic structure on the YSZ particle surface.

Figures 3(h1)–3(h5) present fracture SEM images of the as-sprayed coatings, revealing splat–splat interactions and columnar grain structures in all the samples. Three typical pore morphologies [43], originating from the splat-based layered architecture, are observed: pores at inter-splat boundaries form during rapid solidification; intra-splat cracks arise from thermal stress and the relaxation of tensile quenching stress; additionally, globular pores occur due to incomplete bonding between splats or the presence of semi-melted particles. Notably, almost no nYSZ particles are observed in the nYSZ-15 coating, as shown in Fig. 3(g2). This is due to the higher surface energy of the nYSZ particles, which make them more prone to melting during plasma spraying, causing them to merge with the melted micro-YSZ particles from the solid splats. Figure 3(h) reveals that as the nYSZ fraction in the feedstock powder increases, some nYSZ particles become visible at the inter-splat positions in the nYSZ-30 coating. In contrast, nYSZ@C-15, with a relatively lower nanoparticle fraction in the feedstock powder, still contains a considerable number of nYSZ particles in the coating. As the nYSZ@carbon fraction in the feedstock powder increases, the number of nYSZ particles in the nYSZ@C-30 coating increases significantly. Importantly, these nanoparticles are found not only at the inter-splat positions but also within the well-melted splats. This is because the high thermal conductivity and infrared emissivity of the carbon film inhibit the melting of the nYSZ particles, which encapsulate them within the molten droplets. Upon collision with the substrate and subsequent solidification of the droplets, the nanoparticles are preserved within a well-melted splat, as illustrated in Fig. 3(i). Importantly, during heat treatment I, nanopores are formed in situ* on the surface of nYSZ particles as the carbon film is removed from the coating, leading to an increase in the porosity of the coating. With increasing nYSZ particles, the porosity of the coating increases, as illustrated in Fig. 3(j). Therefore, the carbon film on the surface of the nYSZ particles can prevent the nanoparticles from melting and merging during the plasma spraying process, thereby preserving the nanoparticles within the coating and enabling the *in situ* formation of nanopores during heat treatment, as displayed in the schematic illustration of Fig. 3(k). These nanopores effectively enhance the contact thermal resistance between particles. Additionally, owing to the refractive index differences between YSZ and air, the scattering effect of nanoparticles on infrared thermal radiation is expected to be significantly enhanced.

Figures 4(a) and 4(b) illustrate the hemispherical reflectance and transmittance, respectively, of samples with different thicknesses. Notably, at wavelengths of 3 and 4.25 μm, both the reflectance and transmittance exhibit distinct valleys, corresponding to absorption peaks associated with –OH and CO2, respectively. For coatings with a specific composition, the reflectance increases as the thickness increases within the 0.6–7.0 μm wavelength range. This behavior can be explained by the weak absorption of the coating, and the increase in coating thickness extends the transmission path of infrared thermal radiation and increases the frequency of scattering events. This phenomenon is known as the through-thickness volume scattering effect. In contrast, the magnified image in Fig. 4(a) reveals that, within the 0.25–0.5 μm range, the reflectance remains nearly unchanged with increasing coating thickness. This can be explained by the restricted propagation of thermal radiation due to strong absorption, as evidenced by the near-zero transmittance observed in the corresponding region of Fig. 4(b). For nYSZ-15, which contains minimal nanoparticles, the reflectance and transmittance closely resemble those of traditional YSZ coatings at similar thicknesses. As the nanoparticle content increases, the reflectivity of the coating within the 1–5 μm range substantially increases, accompanied by a corresponding decrease in transmittance. Notably, the reflectance of the nYSZ@C-15 coating is significantly greater than that of nYSZ@30 at similar thicknesses, which is partly due to the higher nanoparticle content in nYSZ@C-15 and the *in*-*situ* formation of nanopores around these nanoparticles, which further increase the reflectance.

Figures 4(c) and 4(d) display the scattering and absorption coefficients obtained from the reflectance and transmittance data using the four-flux model. Within the wavelength interval of 0.6–7 μm, all the coatings exhibit a reduction in the scattering coefficient with increasing wavelength. This trend can be attributed to the presence of numerous microcracks and pores in the coatings, which act as primary scattering centers for thermal radiation, with sizes typically smaller than 1 μm. Mie scattering theory indicates that the scattering cross-section (C*s,*λ*) for a particle with diameter *d* can be formulated as a power series (Eq. (2)):

(2) Cs,λ=83πd24ξ4|n2−1n2+2(1+35n2−2n2+2ξ2+…)|2

where *n* denotes the complex relative refractive index and *ξ* = π*d*/*λ* denotes the parameter related to size. As the wavelength of incident thermal radiation increases, both *ξ* and *C*s,*λ* decrease, leading to a gradual reduction in the scattering coefficient. In the 0.25–0.5 μm range, when the scattering particles are larger than the wavelength of the incident light, both geometric scattering and Mie scattering contribute. When the wavelength is much smaller than the size of the scattering particles, geometric scattering dominates, resulting in a very small effective scattering cross-section. As the wavelength increases, Mie scattering becomes predominant, leading to an increase in the scattering cross-section and, consequently, the scattering coefficient. The average scattering coefficients of nYSZ@C-15 and nYSZ@C-30 increase significantly, from 32,082 to 43,949 and 81,324 m−1, respectively, compared with 33,310 and 39,309 m−1 for nYSZ-15 and nYSZ-30, respectively. Thus, the introduction of nanoparticles significantly enhances the scattering and reflectance of YSZ TBCs to thermal radiation within the turbine chamber. When the wavelength exceeds 6 μm, the absorption increases due to multiple phonon absorptions. Additionally, the absorption coefficients increase with increasing scattering coefficient due to the increased thermal radiation transfer path.

To gain more insight into the influence of nYSZ particles on the transition of infrared thermal radiation, the distribution of the electric field and the far-field scattering pattern were modeled using the wave optics module in COMSOL Multiphysics software, as illustrated in Fig. 5. Owing to the significant size disparity between the pores and the coating, only a portion of splat structure within the coating was selected for simulation. The conventional YSZ coating was modeled as a ceramic block (5 μm × 5 μm × 3 μm) with an ellipsoidal pore (2 μm × 1.8 μm × 1.2 μm), while the nYSZ@C coating was simplified as a YSZ structure filled with particles with a diameter of 400 nm, as displayed in Fig. 5(a). The incident wavelength was set as 1.5 μm, corresponding to a wavelength with higher energy density within the turbine chamber. The incident light was directed along the *z*-axis, with the electric field oscillating along the *x*-axis. Perfectly matched layers (PMLs) were applied around the simulation region to prevent back-reflection from external boundaries, as illustrated in Fig. 5(d).

*
Fig. 5COMSOL Multiphysics simulation results of infrared thermal radiation scattering: (a) simulation model, (b) electric field, and (c) far-field pattern of YSZ; schematic of (d) incident thermal radiation, (e) electric field, and (f) far-field pattern of nYSZ@C.

The scattered electric field of YSZ in Fig. 5(b) exhibits a relatively simple distribution. The wave fronts are scattered by the ellipsoidal pores due to the refractive index contrast. Owing to the large pore size in the direction perpendicular to the thermal radiation transfer, the scattering of incident radiation is predominantly concentrated in the 180° direction (opposite to the incident direction), as displayed in Fig. 5(c), a characteristic that is confirmed by the far-field pattern. This behavior is indicative of geometric scattering and suggests that the pores interact with the incident infrared radiation, leading to a diffraction pattern typical of simpler scattering systems. In contrast, nYSZ@C, which contains ceramic nanoparticles within the pores, exhibits a more complex scattering pattern. The presence of nanoparticles introduces additional scattering centers, resulting in local field enhancements, as shown in the enlarged image in Fig. 5(e). These modifications indicate that the nanoparticles serve as additional scattering sources, thus influencing the overall scattering characteristics. The nanoparticles likely induce resonant scattering effects, enhancing or suppressing scattering in specific directions. The far-field scattering pattern reveals that the nYSZ@C coating not only demonstrates strong scattering in the 180° direction but also shows substantial scattering throughout the entire backward region (90°–270°), as shown in Fig. 5(f), which is consistent with Mie scattering characteristics. Therefore, the simulation results clearly demonstrate that nanoparticles within the coating play a pivotal role in modulating the scattering process by creating additional resonant scattering effects, thereby altering both the local electric field and far-field scattering behavior.

To quantify the impact of nYSZ particles and their scattering effects on the thermal insulation performance of TBCs, a two-flux model was employed to simulate the heat flow and temperature variation throughout the coating under operational conditions, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Figure 6(a) shows the thermal diffusivity of the coating measured by a laser flash analyzer, while Fig. 6(b) illustrates the corresponding thermal conductivity calculated by Eq. (1). All samples exhibit the greatest thermal diffusivity and thermal conductivity at RT, with both gradually decreasing as the temperature increases. This behavior stems from anharmonic phonon scattering, suggesting that the primary mechanism for heat transfer is phonon conduction. Upon reaching a certain temperature threshold, however, the thermal conductivity begins to rise. This is because, at higher temperatures, the radiative heat flux increases sharply (related to the fourth power of the absolute temperature) and contributes significantly to the total heat transfer, resulting in an increase in the thermal conductivity. As the concentration of nanoparticles in the coating increases, the thermal conductivity decreases significantly. This can be explained from three perspectives: (1) The increase in nYSZ particles enhances phonon scattering at the grain boundaries, reducing the mean-free path of phonons and thereby lowering the thermal conductivity; (2) the porosity in the coating causes local variations in the heat flux, hindering heat transfer [27]; (3) the nanopores formed on the surface of the nYSZ particles, with sizes less than the mean-free path of air, are nearly non-conductive due to the Knudsen effect, effectively reducing the thermal conductivity of the coating [44]. The reduction in thermal conductivity results in an increased contribution of radiative heat flux in high-temperature environments. Therefore, as the nanoparticle content increases, the critical temperature at which the thermal conductivity undergoes a transition decreases.

To further evaluate the thermal insulation performance of the nYSZ@C coating, a thermal insulation testing system of TBC was established, as shown in Fig. 7(a). The system consists of a muffle furnace, a temperature controller, and a temperature monitor. A K-type thermocouple was directly welded to the surface of the substrate and positioned at the center to ensure measurement accuracy. The top surface of the TBCs was heated using a muffle furnace, while the substrate backside was exposed to air and dissipated heat through natural convection. The muffle furnace was ramped from RT to 1100 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min and subsequently held at the target temperature. ### 3.2 Thermal radiation stability of nYSZ coatings at high temperature

Figure 8 presents the SEM image of the fractured coatings following heat treatment at 1300 °C for 100 h. Figure 8(a) displays the SEM image of the conventional fractured YSZ coating, which is characterized by prominently coarsened columnar grains. The SEM image of fractured nYSZ-30 is presented in Fig. 8(b). Due to the relatively low content of nYSZ particles, the coating is not significantly different from the conventional YSZ coatings in terms of coarsened grains and markedly reduced porosity. The SEM image of fractured nYSZ@C-30 in Fig. 8(c) shows relatively high porosity caused by coarsened nYSZ particles. Compared with the sprayed coatings, all the heat-treated coatings exhibit a significant reduction in porosity. During thermal processing, intra-splat cracks were repaired, and some intersplat pores evolved into convex, bridge-like structures as a result of reduced surface energy, as displayed in Fig. 8(d). As the bridge connections increased, discrete chain-like pores formed within the coating, which was particularly noticeable in the conventional YSZ and nYSZ coatings, as illustrated in Fig. 8(e). Simultaneously, the columnar grains within the layered structure gradually coarsen, leading to a significant reduction in the number of grain boundaries. This reduction in the number of grain boundaries and microcracks decreases the scattering sources and, consequently, the scattering coefficients of the coating. The higher surface energy and lower activation energy of nYSZ particles result in a stronger driving force for sintering [35]. As a result, the nanoparticles gradually coarsen and merge during heat treatment, forming larger particles with the average particle size increasing from approximately 65 to 700 nm, as displayed in Fig. 8(f). The densification and coarsening of nanoparticles reduce the number of nanopores within the coating, thereby weakening its scattering effect on thermal radiation. However, previous studies [47] have suggested that when the size of coarsened nanoparticles approaches the wavelength of infrared thermal radiation, particularly in the 1–2 μm range, the scattering coefficient is expected to increase with increasing particle size.

To analyze the phase transitions of the coatings during heat treatment, XRD tests were performed on both the YSZ and nYSZ@C-30 coatings. The as-sprayed coatings are all in the tetragonal phase, exhibiting characteristic tetragonal phase diffraction peaks. The enlarged XRD patterns at 2θ* values of approximately 35°, 59.5°, and 74° reveal that the diffraction peaks after heat treatment become broader or exhibit shoulder features. The distinct tetragonal peaks before heat treatment overlap and become asymmetric after heat treatment, which is consistent with the diffusion-driven partitioning of Y2O3 at high temperatures. This partitioning results in Y-rich cubic zones and Y-depleted tetragonal zones [48]. The emergence of the cubic phase increases the crystal symmetry, reducing peak splitting and causing some diffraction lines to merge.

Specifically, the average reflectance decreases from 78.12% and 79.62% to 73.43% and 76.77%, respectively, compared with the pre-heat treatment values. In contrast, the reflectances of nYSZ@C-15 and nYSZ@C-30 remain nearly unchanged as the particle size after heat treatment approaches the wavelength of thermal radiation, enhancing the scattering effect. The reflectance changes only slightly, from 81.32% and 87.88% to 80.18% and 87.43%, respectively. Additionally, the average reflectance in the 4–6 µm range increases for all the coatings, which can be attributed in part to the development of distinct chain-like pores during thermal processing.

## 4 Conclusions

This study addresses the challenges associated with the semitransparency to thermal radiation, low nanoparticle retention, and formation of semi-melted powder during the plasma spraying process of nYSZ TBCs. A novel approach in which nYSZ particles are coated with a carbon film to prevent them from melting and merging during the plasma spraying process is presented. This method significantly improves nanoparticle retention and enhances production efficiency. Furthermore, nanopores formed *in situ* on the nanoparticle surface with the removal of the carbon film at 800 °C. These nanopores, in combination with nanoparticles, significantly enhance the thermal radiation-scattered electric field and far-field pattern. As a result, the nYSZ@C coating demonstrates considerably greater thermal radiation reflectance and scattering coefficients than conventional YSZ coatings. Simulations using the two-flux model reveal that, under operational conditions, the substrate temperature of the nYSZ@C coating can be reduced by up to 26.26 K due to decreased radiative heat flux and further by up to 111.2 K when the reduced thermal conductivity is considered. Thermal insulation tests indicate that, when heated at 1100 °C at the TBC surface, the temperature of the substrate coated with nYSZ@C-30 is 30 °C lower than that of the substrate coated with YSZ. This further visually demonstrates the superior thermal insulation properties of the nYSZ@C coating.

Notably, the nYSZ@C coating maintains high reflectance and scattering coefficients even after heat treatment at 1300 °C for100 h. This remarkable durability is attributed to the coarsening of the particle size, which becomes comparable to the wavelength of thermal radiation, thereby further enhancing the scattering effects. These findings highlight the significant potential of nYSZ-based TBCs in improving the thermal radiation blocking and thermal insulation performance of TBCs. The coating method of carbon film proposed in this study not only improves nanoparticle retention in nTBCs but also enhances their overall thermal insulation capabilities, offering a promising avenue for the development of advanced thermal protection systems.
