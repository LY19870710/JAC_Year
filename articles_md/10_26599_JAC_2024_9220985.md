## 1 Introduction

With the rapid development of microelectronics, piezoelectric films are gaining increasing attention as key functional components in high-performance micro-electro–mechanical systems (MEMSs), such as sensors, actuators, and energy harvesters [1−3]. Owing to their superior piezoelectric coefficients, lead-based piezoelectric films, including Pb(Zr,Ti)O3 (PZT) [4−6] and Pb(Mg,Nb)O3–PbTiO3 (PMN–PT) [7,8], have been extensively studied. However, these materials usually display a low power efficiency and figure of merit (FOM) [9−11] because of their large dielectric constants coupled with piezoelectric coefficients. Furthermore, driven by the increased awareness of environmental protection, there is a strong push to develop lead-free piezoelectric films [12−16] to replace toxic lead-based ones.

Multiferroic BiFeO3 (BFO) is a very attractive yet less studied lead-free piezoelectric material. As the first discovered room-temperature single-phase multiferroic, BiFeO3 exhibits a strong coupling between electric, magnetic, and elastic order parameters; hence, BiFeO3 has become the centerpiece of research for a variety of functional devices. Since the report of a large remnant polarization (*P*r, 50–60 μC/cm2) of thin-film BFO in 2003 [17], the studies of BFO films have increased. However, the miniaturization and integration of piezoelectric BiFeO3 films into complementary metal oxide semiconductor (CMOS)-Si technology has become a perennial challenge, mostly due to the conflict between a continuously shrinking thermal budget in semiconductor processing and the necessity of a high substrate temperature for crystallizing and orienting perovskite-type ferroelectrics. Compared with PZT films, of particular interest of BFO thin films are a larger intrinsic polarization (*P*r ≈ 100 μC/cm2, which is the largest among all reported ferroelectrics) [18], a higher Curie temperature (*T*C ≈ 830 °C) [19], and a much smaller dielectric constant (*ε*r ≈ 200) [20]. These characteristics endow BFO with great potential to compete with PZT films in piezoelectric micro-electro–mechanical system (piezo-MEMS) applications, especially those requiring high stability (higher *T*C) and a better energy conversion efficiency (smaller *ε*r).

For the piezoelectric properties of BFO films, which are mostly strain-engineered epitaxial films approximately a few hundred nanometers thick or thinner [21−23], investigations have been centered on the out-of-plane piezoelectric response characterized by the longitudinal *d*33 piezoelectric coefficient (“*d*33 mode”). With respect to the performance of piezoelectric films in the majority of piezo-MEMS devices, the transverse piezoelectric mode characterized by the transverse piezoelectric coefficient *e*31,f (or *d*31,f) is a more popular choice than the *d*33 mode [24,25]. Furthermore, compared with the measurement of a longitudinal displacement of a few nanometers for the *d*33 coefficient of a micron- or submicron-thick piezoelectric film, the transverse *e*31,f measurement records a remarkable field-induced deformation of a clamped film cantilever with a similar film thickness. Such deformation is on the order of hundreds of nanometers or even several microns along the vertical direction of the free-end tip of the cantilever. Therefore, *e*31,f measurement has been deemed not only as state-of-the-art test for piezo-MEMS applications but also a more accurate, reliable characterization method for piezoelectric films.

Currently, however, there are only a few scattered reports on the *e*31,f-based electromechanical properties of BFO films, many of which were prepared via the industrially scalable magnetron sputtering method [26−28]. In our previous report [29], by introducing a LaNiO3 buffer layer and controlling the post-deposition processing atmosphere (an oxygen-rich *in-situ* soaking and cooling atmosphere) of the film, we successfully sputter-deposited high-quality BFO films with enhanced transverse piezoelectric properties on LaNiO3-buffered Pt/Ti/SiO2/(100)Si (*e*31,f ≈ −2.8 C/m2) substrates. However, a systematic investigation on the relationships between processing temperature (one of the key parameters that must be considered for the growth of a crystalline film and its integration compatibility), structure (e.g., phase structure, crystalline orientation, and chemical structure including defect chemistry, etc.), and the transverse piezoelectric response of sputtered BFO films is lacking, which prevents a comprehensive understanding of this lead-free piezoelectric film prepared via sputter deposition. In this work, we report high-quality piezoelectric BFO films deposited on LaNiO3-buffered Pt/Ti/SiO2/(100)Si substrates via a radio frequency (RF) magnetron sputtering process and reveal the impact of substrate temperature (ranging from 300 to 650 °C) on the crystalline quality and orientation, defect chemistry, and associated electrical properties of the films, especially the transverse piezoelectric properties. XRD analyses reveal that these BFO films can be well crystallized at a substrate temperature as low as 350 °C. Moreover, compared with those films deposited at higher substrate temperatures of 500 and 650 °C, not only a more desirable crystallographic orientation of (100) but also a stoichiometric chemical composition has been achieved in the BFO film deposited at 350 °C, leading to enhanced electrical properties. These properties include a large remnant polarization (~60 μC/cm2), a much reduced dielectric constant (220–240) in comparison with that of PZT (~1000) [5,6], and a low leakage current (−5 A/cm2@200 kV/cm) and loss (≤ 0.086, 100 Hz–1 MHz). Together, these characteristics endow the BFO film with excellent transverse piezoelectric performance, featuring a large *e*31,f coefficient (−2.8 C/cm2), a large figure-of-merit (FOM, 4.0 GPa), a high power efficiency (2.9), and a large signal-to-noise ratio (1.5 C/cm2). A comparison with the the representative results reported confirms the intrinsic nature of this large *e*31,f coefficient and its associated piezoelectric performance. This enhanced electromechanical performance, in combination with a CMOS-compatible processing temperature of 350 °C (no post-growth annealing is required), has laid a solid foundation for the integration of BFO films into Si-based piezo-MEMS devices.

## 2 Experimental

*Preparation of BFO films*: In this work, BFO films were deposited on SiO2/(100)Si substrates by using the RF magnetron sputtering technique in a multi-target vacuum chamber with a base pressure of 2.0×10−4 Pa. The Pt bottom electrode with a Ti adhesion layer was first deposited on the substrates (10 mm × 20 mm × 0.5 mm) in a pure argon atmosphere with a sputtering pressure of 0.3 Pa, and the substrate temperature was maintained at 300 °C during the sputtering process. The substrate temperature was then set at 300, 350, 500, and 650 °C, at which the LaNiO3 (LNO) buffer layer was deposited using a fixed Ar : O2 flow ratio of 4 : 1 and a sputtering pressure of 0.3 Pa. Under each substrate temperature, a BFO ferroelectric film was subsequently deposited on the LNO layer with a sputtering pressure of 1.2 Pa. Finally, the as-grown BFO thin film heterostructures were cooled to room temperature in pure oxygen at a pressure of 7.5 Pa before they were removed from the vacuum chamber. A detailed summary of the processing parameters is given in Table 1. Note that commercially available sputtering targets with nominal compositions of Ti, Pt, LaNiO3, and Bi1.05FeO3 were used to deposit different layers in the thin-film heterostructures. Excess bismuth oxide (5% molar ratio) was added to compensate for the loss of bismuth during the sputtering process. The BFO and LNO ceramic targets have purity of 99.9% (Hefei Anjing Crystal Materials Co., Ltd, China), while the Pt and Ti metallic targets have 99.99% purity (ZhongNuo Advanced Material (Beijing) Technology Co., Ltd, China). All the sputtering targets in this study have a cylindrical shape (50 mm in diameter and 5 mm in thickness). The BFO and LNO ceramic targets have purity of 99.9%, while the Pt and Ti metallic targets have 99.99% purity.

*Crystalline structure and elemental chemical* characterization: Crystallographic information, including phase structure, crystalline quality, and orientations of BFO films, was obtained via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/max-2500PC, Rigaku, Japan). The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of BFO films were characterized using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Hitachi SU-70, Hitachi, Japan). The chemical composition and elemental valence states of Bi, Fe, and O in the films were analyzed via an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS; AZtec X-Max, Oxford, UK). The nanoscale microstructures of the films were analyzed via transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-2010, JEOL, Japan).

*Electrical measurements*: Prior to electrical measurements, the BFO films were sputter-coated with circular Au top electrodes (200 μm in diameter) by DC sputtering using a shadow mask. The frequency-dependent dielectric properties were examined using a high-precision LCR digital bridge (QuadTech 7600plus, IET Labs, USA). The leakage current densities, polarization–electric field (*P*–*E*) hysteresis loops, and corresponding polarization switching currents of BFO films were measured using a precision LC ferroelectric tester (Radiant Technology, USA).

*Characterizations of transverse piezoelectric responses:* The transverse piezoelectric coefficients (*e*31,f) of BFO films were derived by measuring the tip displacements of BFO/LNO/Pt/Ti/SiO2/Si cantilever beams, which were diced from the BFO thin film heterostructures with dimensions of 20 mm (length) × 3 mm (width) × 0.5 mm (thickness). A thin Pt layer of ~10 nm thickness was coated on the surface of as-grown BFO films via magnetron sputtering, and the clamping edge of the soon-to-be-diced cantilever beams was left blank. The clamping edge was used not only to clamp the cantilever onto a fixture but also to make electric contact with the Pt bottom electrode. The top Pt layer was connected to a signal generator through a very fine gold wire to avoid disturbing the piezoelectric actuation of the cantilever beam. The piezoelectric vibration of the cantilever was generated by applying a sine wave electric voltage between the top and bottom Pt electrodes, and the resulting cantilever tip displacement *δ* was recorded using a laser Doppler vibrometer with an OFV-5000 controller and an OFV-505 sensor head (Polytec Inc. USA). The transverse piezoelectric coefficients (*e*31,f) of the BFO films were derived from *δ* based on Eq. (3). A schematic illustration of a cantilever beam and the piezoelectric measurement setup is shown in Fig. 7(b). Details about preparation of the cantilever beams and testing procedures can be found in our previous reports [29−31].

## 3 Results and discussion

The crystallization characteristics of the BFO films subjected to a series of substrate temperatures during their deposition, i.e., 300, 350, 500, and 600 °C (hereafter labeled as BFO@300, BFO@350, BFO@500, and BFO@650, respectively) were derived from the XRD 2*θ*-scan patterns shown in Fig. 1. Clearly, the BFO@300 film exhibits a poor crystallinity with only one extremely weak rhombohedral (200) peak, which can be attributed to the low adatom mobility at this low substrate temperature. Moreover, the BFO films deposited at elevated temperatures of 350, 500, and 650 °C are well crystallized into the bulk rhombohedral phase with strong diffraction peaks. Furthermore, the crystallographic orientation of the BFO films significantly improved with decreasing substrate temperature. First, a (111)-textured orientation is observed in the BFO film prepared at 650 °C. Second, the BFO film deposited at a moderate substrate temperature of 500 °C shows a randomly oriented polycrystalline microstructure, consisting of (100) (strongest), (110), (210), and (320) oriented grains of the rhombohedral phase. Finally, the BFO film deposited at a low substrate temperature of 350 °C shows excellent (100)-oriented grain growth, with only {*l*00} peaks observed in its XRD pattern. Notably, the diffraction peaks of the thin buffer layer of LNO overlap with those of BFO films of the same orientation because of close lattice matching.

This growth temperature-modulated crystallographic orientation of the BFO film can be attributed to the formation and evolution of residual film stress during its sputtering process at different substrate temperatures. A high substrate temperature (e.g., > 600 °C) facilitates the generation of misfit dislocations to relax the lattice misfit between the BFO/LNO bilayer film and the Pt/Ti/SiO2/(100) substrate [32,33]. Therefore, the BFO film is apt to grow on the (111) crystalline plane, which matches well with that of the underlying Pt layer, i.e., the Pt(111) plane, forming a (111)-preferred orientation [34]. Moreover, the BFO film prepared at a medium substrate temperature of 500 °C exhibits a microstructure consisting of randomly oriented polycrystallites, which jointly compensate for the lattice mismatch with the underlying Pt since the misfit relaxation via the formation of dislocations is greatly suppressed. Nevertheless, the interface buffering effect from the LNO layer, which tends to grow along its (001) plane on Pt [35], has made the {l*00} grains favorable among all the grain orientations. Finally, when the substrate temperature decreases to 350 °C, the residual film stress is compressive [36,37], which is dictated by the initial lattice misfit between the BFO film and platinized Si. This is because the formation of dislocations and the magnitude of tensile thermal stress in the BFO film originated during the cooling process after deposition have both become negligible at such a low growth temperature [38]. This compressive residual stress, together with the interface buffering effect, have promoted the formation of a (100)-texture in the BFO film. The shift of (200) peak towards a lower 2*θ* angle in the XRD pattern of BFO@350 film, in comparison with that of the BFO@500 film, reveals the existence of such stress.

The surface and cross-sectional SEM images of the BFO films are shown in Fig. 2. First, as clearly shown in Fig. 2(a), the BFO@300 film exhibits an extremely smooth surface and a dense, featureless cross-section, which agrees well with its amorphous structure from the XRD analysis. BFO films deposited at higher substrate temperatures, as shown in Figs. 2(b)–2(d), present a flat and crack-free surface with densely packed grains. The average grain size increases with increasing substrate temperature, as revealed by the statistical analyses of the bottom-left insets of Figs. 2(b)–2(d). The estimated average grain sizes are ~80, ~210, and ~480 nm for BFO@350, BFO@500, and BFO@650 films, respectively. Additionally, the shape of the grains evolves from columnar (BFO@350) to equiaxial (BFO@650), as observed from the cross-sectional SEM images in the upper-right insets in Figs. 2(b)–2(d). This morphological transition with respect to the substrate temperature fits the “structure zone model” well for sputtered films [39]. At a low substrate temperature, 350 °C, in this case, limited adatom mobility/diffusion leads to the formation of columnar grains with small in-plane/cross-sectional sizes [40]. Moreover, owing to much improved bulk diffusion, grains in the BFO@650 film can grow much larger and along both the out-of-plane and in-plane directions, resulting in a large and equiaxial grain morphology. This high substrate temperature has also led to strain relaxation and possible re-crystallization of the grains, resulting in {111}-textured growth matching that of the underlying Pt layer. Moreover, compared with the BFO@350 film, a roughened surface morphology was observed in the BFO@650 film. This phenomenon can be partially attributed to the formation of nanopores due to accelerated volatilization of Bi in the film at a high substrate temperature, in addition to a more significant film surface fluctuation caused by the coarse grains growing at a high temperature mentioned above. Finally, the BFO film deposited at a moderate substrate temperature of 500 °C exhibits a transitioning grain morphology between the BFO@350 and BFO@650 films. These SEM results agree fairly well with those from the XRD analyses above. Notably, the cross-sectional images in the insets of Figs. 2(a)–2(d) have clearly identified a BFO/LNO/Pt multilayer structure grown on Si, allowing for the determination of the thickness of each layer. The thickness of the LNO layer is estimated to be 110, 120, 150, and 180 nm for BFO@300, BFO@350, BFO@500, and BFO@650 films, respectively, and the thicknesses of their BFO layers are measured to be approximately 900 nm.

To verify that the BFO thick films are indeed in the bulk rhombohedral phase, we performed a detailed TEM analysis. Figure 3(a) shows a cross-sectional TEM image of the BFO@350 film (with a [010] zone axis), re-confirming a dense columnar nanograin structure with an average in-plane grain size of ~60 nm. Furthermore, the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns taken in Fig. 3(a) at different film thickness regions are displayed in Figs. 3(b)–3(d), which include two neighboring (001) grains rotating in-plane by 45° (marked by the red and yellow circles in Fig. 3(b)). However, there is a monoclinic distortion, exemplified as two sets of {101} diffraction spots with non-equal plane spacing (illustrated by the red and yellow circles in Figs. 3(b) and 3(c)), and such a distortion diminishes with increasing thickness (Fig. 3(d)). Furthermore, there are  12(111) superlattice diffraction spots in all three SEAD patterns marked by the light blue arrow in Figs. 3(b)–3(d), which can be attributed to the tilted Fe–O oxygen octahedra and indicate rhombohedral R*3*c* symmetry [41]. These TEM features are consistent with previous reports on BFO bulk ceramics or thick films [29,30,36].

EDS measurements were carried out to evaluate the elemental compositions of the films. As clearly shown in Fig. 4, except the adventitious carbon from the environment, only Bi, Fe, and O elements were detected in these films. The corresponding Bi : Fe atomic ratios of BFO@300, BFO@350, BFO@500, and BFO@650 films were estimated to be 1.15 : 1, 1.07 : 1, 1.05 : 1, and 0.82 : 1, respectively. The ratio is semi-quantitative but the trend is correct. With increasing substrate temperature, the excessive Bi element evaporates and eventually becomes deficient. The BFO films deposited at a low substrate temperature of 300 °C are expected to contain a small amount of excessive Bi, which is consistent with the use of a Bi-excessive sputtering target in the present case. The BFO@350 and BFO@500 films, deposited at intermediate substrate temperatures, have elemental compositions close to the chemical stoichiometry. The BFO film deposited at a high substrate temperature, i.e., the BFO@650 film, is severely Bi deficient. Moreover, the elemental EDS mappings of these four BFO films are shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), which confirm the homogeneous distributions of Bi, Fe, and O in these films with no visible elemental segregation. Notably, by optimizing other processing parameters during sputter deposition, it is possible to obtain close-to-stoichiometric BFO films at high substrate temperatures [42]. In the present study, to investigate the effects of substrate temperature on the crystallographic orientation and electrical properties of BiFeO3 thick films sputtered on LaNiO3/Pt/Ti/SiO2/Si, we intentionally kept all other processing parameters the same.

The crystalline quality, crystallographic orientation, nanograin morphology, and chemical composition of these BFO films significantly affect their electrical properties, which are graphically presented in Fig. 5 and summarized in Table 2. The polarization–electric field (P*–*E*) hysteresis loops of the BFO films are displayed in Figs. 5(a)–5(d). The elliptic-like *P*–*E* loop of the BFO@300 film is shown in Fig. 5(a), which agrees well with its amorphous structure and excessive amount of Bi [43]. On the other hand, BFO@350, BFO@500, and BFO@650 films exhibit distinctive ferroelectric *P‒E* loops. As displayed in Figs. 5(b) and 5(c), square-shaped *P‒E* loops with saturated squareness (*P*r/*P*max) values of ~0.8 and 0.9 are observed for BFO@350 and BFO@500 films, respectively. These well-behaved *P‒E* loops can be attributed to the good crystalline quality and a chemical composition close to stoichiometry. The main differences in their ferroelectric properties are the magnitude of the remnant polarization *P*r and built-in field Δ*E*. BFO@350 film show *P*r of ~60 μC/cm2 and large Δ*E* of ~179 kV/cm, while BFO@500 film displays *P*r of ~85 μC/cm2 and small ΔE of ~21 kV/cm. The larger *P*r of BFO@500 film can be attributed to its multiple rhombohedral grain orientations with a large polarization component [36], and its larger and equiaxial grains [37,40]. Moreover, the suppressed built-in field in the BFO@500 film may be attributed to its mixed crystalline orientations and boosted electrical resistivity of the LNO layer [35]. Notably, the built-in field in the BFO@350 film induces self-polarization, which is desirable for achieving stable and poling-free piezoelectric performance [29]. BFO@650 film exhibits a rounded *P–E* loop with inflated polarization values, which is due to a large conduction current contribution originated from the high-temperature deposition process (as further discussed below). Additionally, the corresponding polarization switching current loops (marked by dashed lines) of the three crystalline BFO films are plotted as insets in Figs. 5(b)–5(d). BFO@350 and BFO@500 films exhibit a pair of sharp current peaks located around their positive and negative coercive fields (±*E*c), confirming their good ferroelectric characteristics with a complete polarization reversal. Proportional to the conductivity of the BFO films, the background signal of the switching current is negligible in the BFO@350 and BFO@500 films. Moreover, incomplete polarization switching riding on a high background current is observed in BFO@650 film, implying an increased leakage current, which deteriorates its ferroelectric properties.

Compared with that of the BFO@650 film, a drastic reduction in the leakage current density of nearly 3 orders of magnitude is observed in the BFO@350 film (Table 2). This reduction in leakage current is accompanied by an increase in the dielectric strength. Moreover, the following chemical reactions are used to further understand the leakage current behavior in BFO films [44]. As shown in Reactions. (1) and (2), the volatilization of bismuth and the reduction of Fe3+ (Fe3+ → Fe2+) accelerate with increasing substrate temperature and in a reducing atmosphere, respectively. However, since an oxygen-rich atmosphere is used for sputtering and cooling of all the BFO films, the reduction chemical reaction in Reaction (2) has been suppressed. Therefore, the main point defects in our BFO films are bismuth (* V{Bi}⋅⋅) and oxygen ( VO⋅⋅) vacancses from the chemical reaction in Reaction (1):

(1) 2Bi3++3O2−⇄HightempLowtemp2VBi‴+3VO⋅⋅+Bi2O3(g)

(2) 2Fe3++O⇄Oxygen-deficientOxygen-rich2Fe2++VO⋅⋅+12O2(g)

Based on Reaction (1), the higher the substrate temperature, the greater the saturation pressure of volatile Bi2O3 and hence the generation of more bismuth and oxygen vacancies. These charged point defects lead to an increased leakage current and a greater dielectric loss of the film [44]. In the present study, owing to the suppression of the formation of bismuth and oxygen vacancies, a low defect concentration at a reduced substrate temperature (≤ 500 °C), as supported by the semi-quantitative EDS results (Fig. 4), is largely responsible for their reduced leakage current, especially for that of the BFO@350 film.

Consistent with the above discussion, the BFO@350 and BFO@500 films exhibit lower dielectric losses (tanδ*) than the BFO@650 film, as illustrated in Fig. 5(f) and its inset. An especially noticeable reduction in tan*δ* is observed in the BFO@350 film, which has a small value (≤ 0.086) throughout the frequency range of 100 Hz–1 MHz. Moreover, the dielectric constant (*ε*r) of the BFO@350 film is low and remains almost constant (243–217) over the measuring frequency range. Notably, such a reduced dielectric constant and loss tangent, together with frequency-independent stability, can help boost the piezoelectric energy transmission performance of the film (shown in Fig. 7), such as the figure of merit (FOM), signal-to-noise ratio, etc. On the other hand, both the BFO@500 and BFO@650 films display strong frequency dependency in their dielectric properties [34] throughout the full frequency range, which include the following features:

(1) Large and quickly decreasing *ε*r and tan*δ* in the low-frequency range of 100 Hz–10 kHz. This is due to low-frequency interfacial relaxation processes such as rearrangement of interfacial polarization charges, as well as the effect of electrical conduction, which inflates *ε*r and tan*δ* and diminishes with increasing measuring frequency.

(2) Close (BFO@500) or slightly lower (BFO@650) *ε*r, in comparison with that of the BFO@350 film, showing little changes in the medium frequency range of 10 kHz–100 kHz. This is because the bulk polarization relaxations, including those from ions, electrons and ferroelectric polarization, emerge as a competing loss mechanism with increasing frequency in this range. Since these frequencies are still not high enough, the polarization due to the above-mentioned bulk charge carriers can follow the change in the electric field, so both *ε*r and tan*δ* vary little with the frequency change in this range.

(3) Close (BFO@500) or slightly lower (BFO@650) *ε*r than that of the BFO@350 film in the high-frequency range of 100 kHz–1 MHz, decreases again with increasing frequency. This is characteristic of bulk polarization relaxations, which is dominant in this frequency range.

(4) The dielectric loss tan*δ* of the BFO@500 film decreases with increasing frequency and becomes saturated, while the tan*δ* of the BFO@650 film decreases and then increases with frequency from the middle to high frequency range of 10 kHz–1 MHz. These distinctive features can be attributed to the different chemical compositions and grain morphologies of the films. The BFO@650 film has severe Bi deficiency and coarser grains, which not only boosts its dielectric loss due to polarization relaxations in this frequency range but also leads to an enhanced frequency dependence [34].

Notably, at the high-frequency end, the BFO@500 film shows a close *ε*r to that of the BFO@350 film, which can be attributed to a dominant contribution from the (100)-oriented grains to the dielectric constants of both films. The BFO@650 film displays a lower *ε*r than the BFO@350 film when measured at a high frequency. This can be due to the orientation dependence of dielectric constant (i.e., *ε*r(111) is usually less than *ε*r(100) in the ferroelectrics with a rhombohedral phase).

To further understand the above discussion on electrical properties of the BFO films prepared at 350, 500, and 650 °C, XPS spectra providing insight into the defect chemistry were collected and are displayed in Fig. 6. The survey spectra in Fig. 6(a) reveal the XPS core-level peaks of Bi 4f, Bi 4d, Fe 2p, and O 1s, along with the C 1s peak (@284.4 eV) from adventitious carbon. No signals of other elements are detected in the survey spectra. The narrow spectra of Bi 4f, Fe 2p, and O 1s core levels of the BFO@350 film are displayed in Fig. 6(b). The observed doublets of the Bi 4f peak are the Bi 4f5/2 peak at 164.0 eV and Bi 4f7/2 peak at 158.7 eV caused by spin–orbit splitting. The binding energy difference of the doublets (5.3 eV) matches the theoretical value [45,46], which confirms the native trivalent state of Bi in the film. The spectrum of Fe 2p reveals the doublets of Fe3+ consisting of Fe 2p3/2 at 710.0 eV and Fe 2p1/2 at 723.6 eV with a splitting energy of 13.6 eV, which agrees with that of trivalent iron oxide [45]. The lack of a detectable signal of Fe2+ indicates a proper trivalent state of Fe element. Notably, the valence state of Fe3+ is further supported by its characteristic satellite peaks, with a binding energy 8.3 eV higher than those of Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 principal peaks [29,47]. Therefore, it can be inferred from Eq. (2) above that the oxygen vacancies caused by the valence fluctuations of Fe in the BFO films can be ignored. Moreover, the fitted narrow spectrum of O 1s core level in Fig. 6(b) consists of two peaks: The one with a binding energy of 529.1 eV can be assigned to O2− ions located at the lattice sites (OL) of the film, while the other one with a binding energy at 531 eV is related to surface-absorbed oxygen (OA) [48]. The XPS spectrum of the BFO@500 film is similar to that of the BFO@350 film, as shown in Fig. 6(c), which is consistent with their similar EDS results. Unlike those of the BFO@350 and BFO@500 films, the fitted narrow spectrum of O 1s core level of the BFO@650 film contains not only lattice oxygen OL (528.8 eV) and adsorbed oxygen OA (530.6 eV) peaks but also a small peak at 531.7 eV related to the oxygen vacancies (Ov), as shown in Fig. 6(d). Combining the above XPS and EDS results, it can be concluded that both BFO@350 and BFO@500 films exhibit stable valence states of the constituent elements with a composition close to the chemical stoichiometry. Their low leakage currents and dielectric losses can be attributed to these results, which come from the suppression of oxygen vacancies at a low substrate temperature.

Finally, the transverse piezoelectric responses of these BFO films are measured, and the e*31,f piezoelectric coefficients are calculated by recording the tip displacement of the cantilever beams diced from the BFO/LNO/Pt/Ti/SiO2/Si samples (Fig. 7). The details on fabrication of a piezoelectric cantilever beam are provided in the experimental section, and the *e*31,f test setup is displayed in Fig. 7(b). In this test, a unipolar sinusoidal alternating current (AC) voltage of 500 Hz (much lower than the resonant frequency of the cantilever beam [30]) is applied across the cantilever to excite a piezoelectric vibration, and a laser Doppler vibrometer is used to record the vertical displacement of the cantilever tip. The corresponding *e*31,f coefficient is then derived from the tip displacement by using Eq. (3):

(3) e31,f=d31s11,fE+s12,fE≅−hs2Ys3(1−νs)L2δV

where *h*s, *Y*s, and *ν*s are the thickness, the Young’s modulus, and the Poisson’s ratio of the Si substrate (*Y*s = 130 GPa and *ν*s = 0.278), respectively; *L*, *V*, and *δ* are the length of the piezoactive part of the cantilever, the applied voltage, and the cantilever tip displacement, respectively; * S11,fE and  S12,fE are the in-plane elastic compliances of the piezoelectric film under a constant applied electric field (E*).

The *e*31,f measurements for all crystalline BFO films were performed. Figure 7(a) shows the tip displacements and corresponding *e*31,f coefficients of the BFO/LNO/Pt/Ti/SiO2/Si cantilevers under a sweeping unipolar sinusoidal AC voltage from 1 to 7 V (peak to peak). The tip displacements of all the BFO film cantilevers increase in an approximately linear fashion with increasing applied voltage. This corresponds to a suppressed extrinsic contribution from domain wall movement [49,50], leading to a nearly constant *e*31,f for each of the BFO film cantilevers. Specifically, the cantilever beam of BFO@350 film shows a clearly larger tip displacement than those of the BFO@500 °C and BFO@650 °C films, resulting in a much higher *e*31,f coefficient. The computed average *e*31,f values in the applied voltage range are −2.8, −1.2, and −0.3 C/m2 for BFO@350, BFO@500, and BFO@650 films, respectively. These results verify the increase in the piezoelectric performance of BFO films with a preferred (100)-orientation [30,38]. Moreover, such an enhanced *e*31,f performance is also inevitably related to improved defect chemistry in the BFO@350 film [26], similar to the other electrical properties (Fig. 5 and Table 2). Notably, *e*31,f of the BFO@350 film is superior or comparable to those of (100)-textured and (100)- or (111)-epitaxial BFO films (from −0.7 to −4.1 C/m2) prepared at a much higher temperature [26−28,51,52]. Finally, in comparison with the working voltages of several volts for piezoelectric cantilevers, the large self-bias observed in the BFO@350 film (Δ*V* ≈ 16 V) ensures a stable, intrinsic piezoelectric response regardless of the polarity of the applied voltage [29], hence reducing the difficulty of prepoling treatment.

Based on Eq. (3), *e*31,f is a function of bulk piezoelectric coefficient (*d*31) and in-plane elastic compliances (* S11,fE and  S12,fE) of piezoelectric films. From the origin of piezoelectricity in ferroelectrics (“linearized electrostriction”), the d*31 coefficient of an epitaxial (100)/(001)-oriented film can be expressed as a function of electrostrictive coefficient (*Q*12) and its longitudinal electrical properties (relative dielectric constant (*ε*r) and remnant polarization (*P*r), Eq. (4)):

(4) d31=2Q12ε0εrPr

where *ε*0 is the vacuum permittivity.

Combining Eqs. (3) and (4), a comparison of the intrinsic *e*31,f coefficients between ferroelectric films of the same material and the same (100) (for trhombohedral phase) or (001) (for tetragonal phase) orientation can be made (Eq. (5)):

(5) (e31,f)1(e31,f)2≈(εrPr)1(εrPr)2

Equation (5) allows one to comprehend *e*31,f values of (100)/(001)-oriented films from the same material but obtained by different research groups or using different deposition methods, given that the longitudinal electrical properties *ε*r and *P*r of films have also been reported. This equation successfully circumvents the difficulties in obtaining reliable material data (electrostrictive coefficient and elastic compliances) for BFO films and helps to rationalize the measured *e*31,f values. In Fig. 8, we directly compare our measured *e*31,f and *ε*r*P*r values (as the common denominators in Eq. (5)) with those (as the numerators in Eq. (5)) in Refs. [26,27,30,51,52]. The resulting data points of the two ratios can be fairly fitted to a straight line (the orange dashed line in Fig. 8), regardless of the differences in deposition method, film thickness, processing temperature, and substrate. Notably, the four data points from sputtered BFO films have an *e*31,f ratio/(*ε*r*P*r) ratio closer to 1, suggesting that their piezoelectric performance is close to intrinsic. This is possibly due to a similar microstructure and better matched material constants than those in our sputtered BFO films (electrostrictive coefficient, *Q*12 and in-plane elastic compliances, * S11,fE and  S12,fE). In the top left region of Fig. 8, the higher e*31,f ratios in the pulsed laser deposition (pLD)-deposited (epitaxial) and sol-gel-deposited BFO films than their (*ε*r*P*r) ratios may be attributed to extrinsic piezoelectric responses from the field-induced movement of the domain walls and/or nanoscale phase boundaries. There are also data points reporting under-performed piezoelectric responses. For example, Aramaki *et al*. [26] reported a thickness-dependent piezoelectric property in BFO films, which they attributed to the existence of a high defect density bottom layer. In the thinnest BFO film (~450 nm) investigated, the *e*31,f value decreased to −0.9 C/m2. These data points are in the bottom right region of Fig. 8, indicating a defect-induced under-performance in the transverse piezoelectric response.

There are several commonly used *e*31,f-based composite metrics for the evaluation of piezo-MEMS devices, especially for piezoelectric sensors and energy harvesters [53]. These metrics include figure of merit (* FOM=e31,f2/(ε0ε33,f)) for evaluating the power generation of a piezoelectric film, power efficiency  2e31,f2/[(ε0ε33,ftan⁡δ)⋅((1−ν)/Y)Si] for characterizing output availability of the generated electric power, and signal-to-noise ratio, which describes the sensitivity of a piezoelectric device ( |e31,f|/ε33,ftan⁡δ). Based on the measured electrical parameters in Fig. 5(f) ( ε33,f=εr measured at 500 Hz in the present case) and Fig. 7(a), FOM, power efficiency and signal‒to‒noise ratio of the BFO@350 film are calculated to be ~4.0 GPa, ~2.9 C/m2, and ~1.5 C/m2, respectively. These metrics are very competitive compared with those of PZT films [54]. Notably, in the computation of these metrics, we used the measured converse e*31,f coefficient, which is comparable to the direct *e*31,f coefficient when there is a negligible contribution from the extrinsic piezoelectric response [50,55]. Since the converse *e*31,f coefficient of our BFO film is constant against the applied voltage, an intrinsic contribution is dominant; hence, such an estimation of direct *e*31,f can be made. However, an experimental measurement of the direct piezoelectric coefficient is necessary in the near future to obtain numbers corresponding to practical application scenarios, i.e., piezoelectric sensors and energy harvesters. Therefore, the numbers of piezoelectric metrics shown above are semi-quantitative, and their orders of magnitude rather than exact numbers can be used as references. The key points for our BFO@350 films as alternatives to PZT films in piezo-MEMS technology include a one-step process, a simple and lead-free composition, a much smaller dielectric constant with a low loss, and a reduced substrate/processing temperature. These points have shown great promise for the adoption of BFO films in integrated piezo-MEMS devices.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, a series of high-quality piezoelectric BFO thick films were prepared on LNO-buffered Pt/Ti/SiO2/Si(100) substrates via a RF magnetron sputtering process. The remarkable impacts of substrate temperature control (from 300 to 650 °C) on the crystalline quality and orientation, defect chemistry, and electrical properties of sputtered BFO thick films, especially the transverse piezoelectric properties, were successfully revealed. Our results showed that the sputtered BFO films were well crystallized into a bulk rhombohedral phase at a low substrate temperature of 350 °C. Compared with those sputtered at higher temperatures of 500 or 650 °C, the BFO film sputtered at 350 °C presented a desirable crystallographic orientation of (100) and a stoichiometric chemical composition, thereby leading to enhanced electrical properties. These properties include a large remnant polarization (~60 μC/cm2), a much reduced dielectric constant (243–217) and loss (≤ 0.086) in 100 Hz–1 MHz, and a low leakage current (2.4×10−5 A/cm2@200 kV/cm). Consequently, the BFO film exhibits an excellent transverse piezoelectric response, featuring a large *e*31,f ≈ −2.8 C/cm2, a large figure-of-merit of ~4.0 GPa, a high power efficiency (~2.9), and a large signal-to-noise ratio (~1.5 C/cm2). These enhanced electromechanical performances of the BFO thick film, dominated by a stable intrinsic piezoelectric response and achieved at a low processing temperature of 350 °C, have endowed this environmentally friendly material with great potential in Si-based piezo-MEMS applications.
