## 1 Introduction

The ever-growing traffic explosion in mobile communications has recently drawn increasing interest in the design of new microwave dielectric components for high data rates. A certain frequency range near the bottom of the millimeter-wave spectrum (30–80 GHz) is being used in the 5th generation cellular networks because of the enhanced spectrum bandwidth, which enables high-speed signal transmission [1−3]. To meet the needs of millimeter-wave applications, a low permittivity (*ε*r ≤ 15) for fast signal propagation speed, a high quality factor (*Q×f*) for better signal attenuation, and a near-zero temperature coefficient of the resonant frequency (*τ*f) for good operating environment stability of devices are the three most critical aspects [4,5].

In recent years, numerous gallates (Table 1) were explored with properties well-suited for millimeter-wave applications because of their low-*ε*r and high *Q×f*, such as gallium oxide (Ga2O3) [6], spinel AGa2O4 (A = Zn, Mg, Sr, and Ca) [7−9], perovskite (LaGaO3) [10], wurtzite-derived structure gallate (LiGaO2) [11], monoclinic AGa4O7 (A = Ca, Sr) [12] and garnet RE3Ga5O12 (RE = Nd, Sm, Eu, Dy, Yb, Y) [13], etc. For the mentioned Ga-based ceramics, the performance characteristic is that *ε*r values in 5.0–13.0, *Q×f* values between 15,000 and 298,000 GHz. However, their relatively large negative *τ*f values between −12.0 and −87.0 ppm/°C limit their practical applications.

To date, few gallates with positive or near-zero *τ*f values have been reported. With the synergy of “phase transition” and “rattling” effect, CaLaGaO4 [14] with a tetragonal K2NiF4-related structure, consisting of 1 : 1 stacking of the perovskite layer of (Ca/La)GaO3 and the rock-salt layer of Ca/LaO, presented an anomalous positive *τ*f of +2.3 ppm/°C, an *ε*r of 19.5, and a *Q×f* of 32,500 GHz, in sharp contrast to those (*ε*r ≈ 12.3, *Q×f* ≈ 63,900 GHz, *τ*f ≈ −56.3 ppm/°C) of orthorhombic olivine-structured CaLaGaO4. In addition, some tetragonal K2NiF4-type ALnAlO4 (A = Ca, Sr; Ln = La, Nd, Sm, Eu, Y) [15−22] ceramics with a *ε*r value of approximately 20.0 also demonstrated tunable *τ*f values ranging from negative (~ −55.0 ppm/°C) to positive (~ +5.0 ppm/°C). Hexagonal magnetoplumbite AB12O19 (A = Pb, Ba, Sr, Ca; B = Fe, Ga, Al) [23−29] possesses a 1 : 1 ordering distribution of the rock-salt block and spinel block stacking along the crystallographic *c*-axis, and the polyhedrons in the crystal structure present several connection modes. Rock-salt structured ceramics are an important type of microwave dielectric ceramics because of their low relative permittivity, low dielectric loss and adjustable *τ*f, such as Li2MgTiO4 (*ε*r = 15.1, *Q×f* = 97,629 GHz (at 8.2 GHz), and *τ*f = −3.8 ppm/°C) and Li3Mg2NbO6 (*ε*r value of 14.9, *Q×f* value of 100,965 GHz and *τ*f value of −22.0 ppm/°C) [30]. Spinel ceramics with low *ε*r values are representative with high *Q×f* values and large negative *τ*f values, such as ZnAl2O4 (*ε*r = 8.5, *Q×f* = 56,319 GHz, and *τ*f = −79.0 ppm/°C) [31] and LiGa5O8 (*ε*r = 10.5, *Q×f* = 124,040 GHz, and *τ*f = −60.2 ppm/°C) [32]. Therefore, it is reasonable to predict that Ga-based magnetoplumbite ceramics, such as K2NiF4-type compounds, might possess favorable dielectric properties.

To date, Ga-based magnetoplumbite AGa12O19 (A = Sr, Ba) has been intensively investigated and applied in permanent ferrites, microwave absorbers, thin films, and optical data storage [33−35]. In this work, magnetoplumbite-type structured SrGa12O19 ceramics were synthesized through the conventional solid-state reaction method, and unique large positive *τ*f values (≈ +55.7 ppm/°C) along with high *Q×f* values (≈ 64,705 GHz) were characterized. This unusually large positive *τ*f prompted us to study the influence mechanism of internal polarization, *Q×f*, and *τ*f. Therefore, the bond length and bond characteristics of SrGa12O19 ceramics were calculated via X-ray diffraction patterns using the Rietveld refinement method. The rattling or compression effects on the crystal structure, as well as structural stability, global instability index (GII), and bond strain index (BSI), were evaluated by the bond valence (BV) theory. The inherent permittivity and *Q×f* values of SrGa12O19 ceramics were analyzed via far-infrared (IR) reflectance spectra. The origin of the large positive deviation in *ε*r (Δ*ε*r ≈ +45.31%) and the relationship between the anomalous *ε*r and *τ*f of SrGa12O19 ceramics were explored comprehensively in this study.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

The SrGa12O19 ceramics were prepared via the solid-state reaction method, which uses high-purity SrCO3 (> 99.99%, Aladdin) and Ga2O3 (> 99.99%, Aladdin) powders as raw materials. Initially, the raw materials were calcined at 900 °C for 6 h to eliminate the effect of moisture on weight. Then, these starting materials were then accurately weighed according to the stoichiometric ratios and wet-milled for 4 h with anhydrous ethanol (> 99.7%, XiLong Scientific). These slurries were dried at 120 °C and calcined at 1100 °C for 6 h in a muffle furnace. After being re-milled and dried, the powders were ground with 5 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and uniaxially pressed into cylinders (10 mm × 5 mm). Finally, these pellets were sintered at 1410–1460 °C to obtain dense ceramics.

### 2.2 Characterizations

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Empyrean, PANalytical) was used to verify the phase purity and crystal structure of the as-prepared SrGa12O19 samples. The micromorphology of the thermally etched samples (1380–1430 °C for 30 min) was characterized by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; S4800, Hitachi). Raman spectra were collected with a Raman spectrometer (DXR, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a spectral line of 532 nm and a range of 200–800 cm−1. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images were measured by a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-2100, JEOL) at a working voltage of 200 kV. Fast Fourier transform (FFT) map, inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT) map, and lattice spacing were obtained using the Digital Micrograph software (Gatan Inc.). The Archimedes method was used to measure the bulk density of ceramics. The infrared reflectivity spectra were collected with a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (IFS 66v, Bruker) from 50 to 1000 cm−1. The microwave dielectric properties of sintered SrGa12O19 samples were determined via a network analyzer (N5230A, Agilent).

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1 shows the XRD patterns of the SrGa12O19 ceramics sintered at the optimal temperature of 1430 °C. The patterns can be indexed to the hexagonal structure according to the profile of the SrGa12O19 (PDF#01-082-0712) phase with *P*63*/mmc* space group. To further elucidate the crystal structure of SrGa12O19, Rietveld analysis was performed on the above XRD data using the hexagonal SrGa12O19 structural model, which converged to weighted profile factor (*R*wp) = 7.26%, profile factor (*R*p) = 5.63%, and goodness of fit indicator (*χ*²) = 3.27 (Fig. 2(b)). The lattice constants are calculated as *a* = *b* = 5.794(0) Å, and *c* = 22.822(5) Å. The refinement confirms that SrGa12O19 adopts a 1 : 1 ordering distribution (Fig. 2(a)), in which the spinel structure alternates with conduction blocks of the hexagonal close-packed rock-salt structure along the crystallographic *c*-axis. A molecular unit of SrGa12O19 (SrO·6Ga2O3) ceramics consists of a spinel block “*S*2+” with a formula ([Ga3+6O8]2+) and a rock-salt block “*R*2−” with a formula ([Sr2+Ga3+6O11]2−). The large cations Sr2+ are located at every fifth layer on the mirror planes perpendicular to the stacking direction. The trigonal bipyramid [Ga2–O5] is elongated and shares equatorial edges with the twinned [Sr–O12] and cornered with two face-sharing octahedra [Ga4–O6]. As shown at the bottom of Fig. 2(a), an “*S**2+” block and an “*R**2−” block coconstitute the unit cell of SrGa12O19 with an “*S*2+” block and an “*R*2−” block, where * denotes blocks rotated 180° around the hexagonal *c*-axis according to the {63} symmetry. Thus, SrGa12O19 can be described in the sequence of *RSR***S** [35]. The refined parameters, Wyckoff sites, and occupation of atoms are listed in Table 2. The 5-fold coordinated Ga23+ with tight dependence on the sintering temperature was randomly displaced in degrees from the center of its trigonal bipyramidal coordination polyhedron along the positive and negative directions of the *c*-axis with increasing temperature, resulting in structural deformation of the polyhedrons twinned [43].

Compared with ions with ideal bond valences, ions with weaker bonds are more easily polarized, so rattled cations with smaller bond valances contribute more to ionic polarization and *ε*r [44]. In addition, the coordination environment often affects the polarizability of rare earth ions, resulting in significant errors in polarizability [45]. Therefore, it is necessary to combine the bond strain index (BSI), bond valence theory, and Clausius‒Mossotti equation to study the contribution of ion polarizability to *ε*r [46]. In general, BSI is used to characterize the lattice-induced strain (Eq. (7)) [41]:

(7) BSI=∑in(di)2N

where *N* is the coordination number. Based on the calculation of BSI (Sr2+ ≈ 0.098, Ga13+ ≈ 0.092, Ga23+ ≈ 0.110, Ga33+ ≈ 0.168, Ga43+ ≈ 0.022, and Ga53+ ≈ 0.037 v.u.), most BSI values were greater than 0.05 v.u. [47], indicating that the overall rattling effect of SrGa12O19 is dominant, leading to relatively large positive deviations in Δ*ε*r/*ε*r and Δ*α*/*α*.

The *τ*f values of SrGa12O19 were between +55.7 and +66.2 ppm/°C (Fig. 5(c)), indicating a slight effect of sintering temperature on *τ*f. According to Ref. [48], *τ*f is heavily influenced by the coefficient of thermal expansion (*α*L), temperature coefficient of permittivity (*τ**ε*), and temperature coefficient of ionic polarizability (*τ**α*) (Eqs. (8) and (9)):

(8) τf=−(τε2+αL)

(9) τε=1εr(∂εr∂T)=(εr−1)(εr+2)3εr(ταm−3αL)

The measured *α*L value was 11.617 (Fig. 6); therefore, the calculated *τ**ε* value was −134.634 ppm/°C, and the calculated *τ*αm value was 8.489 ppm/°C for SrGa12O19 between 25 and 85 °C. Apparently, *τ**ε* was much more negative than*α*L; therefore, *τ**ε* played a predominant role in *τ*f. Since *ε*r and *α*L of the SrGa12O19 ceramics were positive and deterministic, the signs and magnitudes of *τ**ε* and *τ*f were affected mainly by the value of *τ*αm−3*α*L. The dominant rattling effect caused by the rock-salt block in SrGa12O19 increased the ionic polarizability (Δ*α*/*α* ≈ 23.56%) and *ε*r (Δ*ε*r/*ε*r ≈ 45.31%), causing the decrease of *τ*αm (8.489 ppm/°C) and a large negative value of *τ**ε* (−134.634 ppm/°C), eventually resulting in an anomalous positive value of *τ*f (+55.7 ppm/°C).

(11) fi=(Cμ)2(Egμ)2=(Cμ)2(Ehμ)2+(Cμ)2

where *C**μ*, * Ehμ, and  Egμ refer to the heteropolar, the homopolar, and the average energy gaps, respectively.

(12) χ=∑μFμ×χμ=∑μNbμ×χbμ

(13) χμ=(hΩpμ)24π(Egμ)2

(14) εμ=1+4πχμ

where h* is Planck’s constant; * Ωpμ is the plasma frequency;  Egμ is the average energy gap. As shown in Table 5, the sequence of average ionicity (Af*i) is *Af*i(Sr–O) > *Af*i(Ga5O) > *Af*i(Ga1O) > *Af*i(Ga4O) > *Af*i(Ga3O) > *Af*i(Ga2O), whereas the sequence of average permittivity (*Aε*) is *Aε*(Ga2O) > *Aε*(Ga3O) > *Aε*(Ga4O) > *Aε*(Ga5O) > *Aε*(Ga1O) > *Aε*(SrO). Combined with the data in Fig. 7(a), although the Sr–O and Ga2–O in the “*R*2−” block were the largest in *Af*i and *Aε*, respectively, the Ga5–O bonds in the “*S*2+” block made the greatest contribution to *f*i (31.09%) and *ε* (27.93%) in the whole SrGa12O19 structure, indicating the dominant role of Ga5–O bonds in the dielectric polarization of SrGa12O19.

The binding ability between cations and anions is evaluated by the bond energy (*E*), which can be calculated via Eq. (15) [53]:

(15) E=∑μ(tcEcμ+tiEiμ)

where * Ecμ,  Eiμ, t*c, and *t*i express nonpolar covalent energy, the ionic energy, the nonpolar covalent mixing coefficient, and the ionic mixing coefficient, respectively. The effect of structural stability on dielectric loss was evaluated by the lattice energy (*U*) of each chemical bond (Eq. (15)) [53]:

(16) U=∑μ(Ubcμ+Ubiμ)

where * Ubiμ and  Ubcμ represent the ionic and covalent contributions of the lattice energy in Am*B*n*-type chemical bonds, respectively. From Table 5, the sequence of average lattice energy (AE) can be sorted as AE(Ga3–O) > AE(Ga1–O) > AE(Ga2–O) > AE(Ga5–O) > AE(Ga4–O) > AE(Sr–O), and AE of Ga3–O bonds of 367.45 (kJ/mol) encloses the most extensive bond energy. The contribution of the “*S*2+” block (56.87%) to the *E* value is slightly higher than that of the “*R*2−” block (43.13%), indicating a higher binding ability between the cations and anions in the “*S*2+” block. In addition, the sorted sequence of the average lattice energy (AU), AU(Ga5–O) > AU(Ga3–O) > AU(Ga4–O) > AU(Ga1–O) > AU(Ga2–O) > AU(Sr–O), was consistent with the contribution of each kind of polyhedron to structural stability. The prominent contribution of the “*S*2+” block to *U* (74.88%) laterally confirmed the greater instability and overall dominant rattling effect of the “*R*2–” block.

However, only six of 42 modes of Raman activity were observed because of the influence of the cross-coverage of peaks or the low resolution of the apparatus. According to previous reports, the bands at 455–517 cm−1 were due to the A1g mode of compressed octahedra [Ga1O6] and [Ga5O6], while the bands at 548 and 743 cm−1 originated from the bending E1g modes of the rattling [Ga3O4] tetrahedra [55]. These modes were derived from the spinel block, and only the peak at 689 cm−1 originated from the rock-salt block, which was responsible for stretching [Ga2O5] bipyramidal polyhedra along the *c*-axis [56]. In general, with a decrease in the full width at half maximum (FWHM), the space of lattice vibration and nonharmonic vibration decrease, thereby resulting in a decrease in inherent dielectric loss [57]. Therefore, SrGa12O19 ceramics had the narrowest FWHM of 10.23 at 503 cm−1 and the lowest dielectric loss (largest *Q*×*f*) at the optimal sintering temperature of 1430 °C.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, dense SrGa12O19 microwave dielectric ceramics were synthesized via a conventional solid-state method at the sintering range of 1410–1460 °C. The crystal structure of SrGa12O19 was proven to be hexagonal magnetoplumbite with a P63*/mmc* space group by XRD, Rietveld refinement, and TEM. Notably, the optimal microwave dielectric properties of SrGa12O19 ceramics sintered at 1430 °C were obtained with *ε*r = 14.46, *Q*×*f* = 64,705 GHz, and an anomalous positive *τ*f = +55.7 ppm/°C. Co-analyzed with the calculation of bond valences and BSI, the rattling effect of the atoms in rock-salt block dominated the crystal structure and dielectric properties of SrGa12O19, resulting in positive deviation values of Δ*ε*r/*ε*r and Δ*α*/*α* of 45.31% and 23.56%, respectively and furthermore the anomalously large positive *τ*f of +55.7 ppm/°C. According to the P–V–L chemical bond theory, the “*S*2+” block made greater contributions to the *f*i (52.95%), *ε* (55.15%), *E* (56.87%), and *U* (74.88%) than the “*R*2−” block of SrGa12O19. Raman spectroscopy confirmed that the SrGa12O19 ceramic possessed the narrowest FWHM of 10.23 cm−1 at 503 cm−1, corresponding to the largest *Q*×*f* of 64,705 GHz. The *ε*0 value fitted by the infrared reflectivity spectra (11.33) was lower than the measured *ε*r value (14.46), and the fitted *Q*×*f* value (131,591 GHz) was twice the measured *Q*×*f* value (64,705 GHz) of the SrGa12O19 ceramics, indicating that the microwave dielectric property can be improved by further optimizing the synthesis process. Therefore, SrGa12O19 is a novel example of a *τ*f compensator in low-*ε*r ceramics, broadening the exploration and discovery of new materials in microwave dielectric ceramics.
