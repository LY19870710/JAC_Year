## 1 Introduction

Thermal radiation management is a critical challenge in many strategic areas. For example, in aerospace engineering, extreme thermal environments—such as spacecraft re-entry, propulsion systems, and extraterrestrial surface operations—demand advanced solutions for regulating radiative heat transfer [1,2]. According to the Stefan–Boltzmann law, the thermal emission intensity is directly proportional to the fourth power of the absolute temperature, making radiation control vital at high temperatures [3]. For systems operating at 1000–1600 °C, more than 80% of thermal radiation resides within the 1–6 μm infrared band (Fig. 1) [4]. Materials that strongly reflect this infrared band can serve as effective high-temperature shields since reflection reduces thermal radiation absorption. These materials are critical for thermal protection systems (TPSs) [5,6], rocket engine nozzles [7], and habitat insulation [8].

Porous ceramics have become the main candidate materials for high-temperature radiative shielding because of their adjustable microstructures and exceptional thermal stability. Recent advancements include refractory oxide composites [9], aerogel materials [10,11], mullite-based coatings [12,13], and composite coatings [14,15], which exhibit promising radiative reflectance above 1000 °C. Pores, as critical structural features of ceramics, directly influence radiative scattering and infrared reflectance. The interaction mechanisms involve both single-pore scattering (governed by Mie theory for pores comparable to the wavelength) and collective effects from pore networks, and the interference between scattered waves can increase back-scattering. However, the interaction between pore structure (size, density, morphology, etc.) and radiative performance remains poorly understood, even with prior studies yielding conflicting conclusions. Blanchard et al.* [16] found that yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) coatings with a median pore size of 0.2 μm had significantly higher reflectances than those of 1 μm, which was attributed mainly to the higher pore density, leading to more scattering events. Conversely, Akamine* et al.* [17] identified pore size optimization rather than density as the critical parameter in magnesium aluminate spinel systems, with 1–5 μm pores maximizing the reflectance at specific wavelengths (1.6 and 2.9 μm). This discrepancy may arise from differences in material refractive indices; for example, spinel (*n* ≈ 1.7) has a lower optical contrast with air (*n* = 1) than YSZ (*n* ≈ 2.1), reducing the dominance of density-driven scattering [15]. This discrepancy is further complicated by temperature-dependent studies: Steinborn *et al.* [18] reported that TiO2–Y2O3 ceramics containing 2–6 μm pores achieved peak reflectances across the 1–5 μm spectral range at 800 °C. Recent work by Jeon *et al.* [19] revealed that rare-earth doping in YSZ altered its thermal expansion coefficient, which in turn affected pore stability and scattering efficiency at elevated temperatures, suggesting that material composition and thermal history must be integrated into pore design. In addition to size parameters, morphology studies revealed that circular pores enhanced reflectance compared with elongated trench-like morphologies, while layered pore structures outperformed columnar structures through optimized photon path modulation [20−24]. For anisotropic pores, Chen *et al.* [25] demonstrated that elongated pores with high aspect ratios can extend the effective photon path length compared with that of spherical pores, reducing forward scattering in the 1–6 μm range. These discrepancies underscore the need for systematic analysis of pore‒radiation interactions to guide material design.

Here, we present a design-led strategy for engineering YSZ ceramics with tailored pores to enhance radiative reflection. As a benchmark thermal barrier coating material [19,26−28], YSZ provides an ideal platform for investigating microstructure‒property relationships. Combining Mie-scattering theory with the finite element method (FEM) and two-dimensional (2D) finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations, we systematically analyze how pore characteristics govern infrared scattering and reflectance. Guided by computational insights, we fabricate YSZ ceramics via sacrificial template and rolling-extrusion methods to achieve targeted pore structures. The optimized materials demonstrate exceptional broadband reflectance (1–6 μm) and thermal stability up to 1600 °C, advancing the development of high-performance radiative shields for aerospace applications. This work establishes a paradigm for microstructure-driven design of porous ceramics, bridging critical gaps between theoretical predictions, computational modeling, and scalable manufacturing.

### 2.1 Computation of scattering coefficients

The scattering properties of pores can be characterized by scattering coefficients, which represent the ratio of scattered wave intensity to incident wave intensity. For a single pore in ceramics, this value can be calculated by Mie-scattering theory and Maxwell’s equations. In this work, we use the FEM and COMSOL Multiphysics for calculations.

The electric field (*E*) in the frequency domain for a homogeneous and linear medium in which a radiation wave propagates is given as Eq. (1) [29]:

(1) ∇×(∇×E)−k02ϵrE=0

where * ∇ is the vector differential operator, and used to represent the spatial rate of change of the electric field;  k0 is the wavenumber in free space;  ϵr is the relative permittivity; the radiation wave is mathematically approximated by a plane wave with the boundary condition (Eq. (2)):

(2) n×[∇×(E+Eb)]−jkn×(E×n)=0

where  k is the wavenumber;  n is the unit normal vector;  j is the indicate phase;  Eb is the scattered field, defined as Eq. (3):

(3) Eb=E0exp(−2πjzλ)x^

where  E0 is the electric field magnitude;  λ is the wavelength;  exp(−(2πjz)/λ) represents the change in the electric field with the propagation distance ( z);  x^ indicates that the electric field propagates along the x*-axis.

The cross-sectional scattering ( σsca) is defined as Eq. (4):

(4) σsca=∬Sn⋅PdSSin

where  P is the power flow;  Sin=E02/(2Z0) where  Z0 is the impedance of free space. The scattering coefficient ( Qsca) is obtained by dividing the scattering cross section by the cross-sectional area ( A) of the scatterer (Eq. (4)):

(5) Qsca=σscaA

Based on these equations, the scattering coefficient can be calculated [30]. In addition, the back-scattering coefficient, which describes the intensity of the incident thermal radiation scattering in the opposite direction, is related to the reflectance. It can be calculated by changing the integration direction of the electromagnetic field. The model was simplified to 2D because of the computational cost of three-dimensional (3D) structure simulation. The 2D simulation is expected to capture the key features of 3D scattering, as single 2D and 3D pores show similar scattering coefficients and back-scattering coefficients (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)).

### 2.2 Computation of reflectance

The reflectance simulation of ceramics with different pore sizes and morphologies was performed in a 40 μm × 200 μm matrix using the FDTD method. As shown in Fig. S2(a) in the ESM, the pores were randomly distributed in the rectangular space of the YSZ matrix. Optical properties were calculated by simulating the propagation of vertically incident waves, and the wavelength range was 1–6 μm. Periodic boundary conditions were set at the side of the simulation area, and perfectly matched layer boundary conditions were set at the top and bottom. The reflectance monitor provides reflectance information. The refractive index is needed in the model calculation; the real part of the refractive index of the YSZ ceramics is from Wood *et al.* [31], while the extinction coefficient was set to zero since the YSZ ceramics do not have obvious absorption in the wavelength range of 1–6 μm and the absorption does not depend on the pore structure [25]. The reflectance of the fabricated ceramics was simulated based on their real microstructural morphologies. These morphologies were extracted through adaptive threshold segmentation of scanning electron microscope (SEM) images, preserving actual pore geometries with sub-0.05 μm feature resolution, as shown in Fig. S2(b) in the ESM. The width of the extracted image was 40 μm, and the depths were 30, 90, 180, and 300 μm to study the influence of the depth on the reflectance. All the simulations used a uniform mesh with a size of 0.05 μm, which is sufficient for spherical pores with a diameter of 0.5 μm. Wider matrix width and finer mesh size have little influence on the simulation results (Fig. S3 in the ESM).

### 2.3 Fabrication of directional pore structure

A schematic process diagram is shown in Fig. 2(a). 8 wt% YSZ (median diameter: 50 nm, Hwrk Chemical (Beijing) Co., Ltd.) and natural graphite flakes (median diameter: 3.288 μm, Fig. 2(b), Forsman Scientific (Beijing) Co., Ltd.) were homogenously mixed in deionized water. The particle size of graphite was measured by a laser particle size analyzer (Bettersize BT-9300H(T)) using anhydrous ethanol as the dispersion medium with 10 min ultrasonication. To optimize the slurry rheology, 2-propenoic acid and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (Bei Jing TongGuang Fine Chemicals Co.) were added as the dispersant and binder respectively. The slurry was subjected to controlled ball milling using zirconia grinding media (2 mm) at a 3 : 1 ball-to-powder mass ratio. Wet milling was conducted at 300 r/min for 24 h, achieving both effective particle dispersion and a moldable paste consistency. The processed slurry was transferred to a watch glass and subjected to thermal drying in an atmospheric oven at 80 °C. During the drying process, intermittent kneading was performed until the paste retained ~5% water content.

*
Fig. 2Fabrication process of directional pore ceramics. (a) Schematic diagram of directional pores made by rolling-extrusion method. (b) SEM images of graphite flake. The inset shows particle size distribution. SEM image and photographs of (c) pressed, (d) pre-calcinated, and (e) sintered samples.

Subsequently, the dried paste underwent iterative rolling to align graphite flakes within the YSZ matrix. Each rolling step induced a 60% thickness reduction, followed by folding and re-rolling. After 20 cycles, the paste was cut into cylindrical blocks with a diameter of 50 mm. These blocks were then pressed under approximately 20 MPa for 10 min to laminate and form green compacts (Fig. 2(c)). Following drying, the samples were pre-calcinated at 700 °C for 2 h at a heating rate of 2 °C/min in air to remove the graphite flakes and stabilize the YSZ matrix (Fig. 2(d)). Subsequently, the samples were sintered at 1600 °C for 2 h at a heating rate of 5 °C/min to form directional pore ceramics (Fig. 2(e)). After cooling to 90 °C, the samples were taken out and immediately subjected to reflectance measurements. The final products were labeled Gf-X* vol%, where *X* denotes the graphite volume percentage in the initial mixture.

### 2.4 Structural characterization and porosity measurement

The pore size distribution and porosity were measured using mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) on a Micromeritics-AutoPore V 9620 porosimeter. The measurement commenced at a pressure of 0.01 MPa and increased to 227.5 MPa (33,000 psia), covering a pore size range from 5 to 120 μm. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted using a Bruker D8 advanced X-ray diffractometer with a Cu-Kα source. SEM images were taken with an FEI Nova NanoSEM (5 kV), and the images were analyzed with the software Avizo2019.1.

### 2.5 Reflectance measurement

The hemispherical reflectance and transmittance in the spectral range from 1 to 2.5 μm were measured using a LAMBDA 1050 ultraviolet/visible/near infrared (UV/Vis/NIR) spectrophotometer. For the infrared region spanning 2.5−6 μm, a Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Vertex 80v, Bruker) equipped with a gold-coated integrating sphere was utilized to measure the hemispherical reflectance and transmittance, with each measurement being repeated three times to ensure accuracy.

### 3.1 Influence of pore size on reflection behavior

First, we studied the effects of pore size on optical properties. The scattering coefficient of pores as a function of wavelength was calculated by using COMSOL. The scattering coefficient, a critical physical quantity, quantifies the ability of pores to scatter thermal radiation; a higher scattering coefficient indicates a stronger scattering ability [32]. Figure 3(a) shows the scattering coefficients of pores with diameters of 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 μm. For a pore with a diameter of 0.5 μm, the scattering coefficient increased rapidly for wavelengths less than 1 μm, peaked, and then declined sharply for longer wavelengths. This suggests that small pores have high efficiency in scattering short waves and low efficiency in scattering long waves. When the wavelength exceeds 2 μm, the pore with a diameter of 0.5 μm almost lose their scattering ability. For a pore with a diameter of 1 μm, the scattering coefficient fluctuates within the 0.5–3.5 μm wavelength range, peaking at 1.8 μm and dropping significantly beyond 3.5 μm, indicating a weak scattering ability for wavelengths exceeding this threshold. In contrast, for pores with diameters of 2 and 4 μm, the scattering coefficient remains relatively high across the entire 1–6 μm wavelength range, without a pronounced downward trend, showing strong broadband scattering capabilities. Consequently, the smaller the pore size is, the earlier the scattering coefficient decreases with increasing wavelength.

The above phenomenon can be explained by the electric field distribution shown in Fig. 3(b), where the color represents the normalized electric field intensity. Taking a 2 μm wavelength of thermal radiation as an example (which corresponds to the radiation peak at 1400 °C), it is noted that subwavelength pores (d* Fig. 3(b-i)). As the pore size approaches the diffraction limit (*d* ≈ *λ*/2), constructive interference amplifies scattering (Fig. 3(b-ii)). Beyond the critical size threshold (*d* > *λ*), conditions become favorable for activating higher-order resonance modes, which significantly amplify the scattering capacity of the pores (Figs. 3(b-iii) and 3(b-iv)). This enhanced scattering is not limited to a single wavelength; it can extend across a broader spectrum because higher-order resonances can induce scattering at multiple wavelengths [33−36]. Therefore, to achieve optimal scattering within the 1–6 μm wavelength range, pore sizes should exceed 2 μm to trigger significant resonance.

In bulk ceramic systems, increasing the pore size under fixed porosity inherently reduces the pore density, creating a competing relationship that governs the optical performance. To investigate this relationship, we developed computational models with randomized pore distributions (Fig. 3(c) and Fig. S2(a) in the ESM) to simulate the reflectance behavior across varying pore sizes. Reflectance measurements spanning 20%–60% porosity (Fig. 3(d) and Fig. S4 in the ESM) demonstrate a consistent inverse correlation between pore size and reflectance at equivalent porosity levels. This phenomenon arises because enlarging pores within a constrained simulation area diminishes the number of light-scattering interfaces, thereby reducing the collective scattering efficiency and overall reflectance. Notably, the 0.5 μm pore ceramics exhibit wavelength-selective behavior: it sustains high reflectance within the 1–2.5 μm spectral range but undergoes a sharp decline at longer wavelengths. This decline occurs because pores smaller than the incident radiative wavelength exhibit diminished scattering efficiency.

Therefore, to achieve high reflectance across the 1–6 μm wavelength range, it is essential to employ a strategy that combines small and large pores. Specifically, incorporating a certain number of small pores (0.5–2 μm) enhances the reflectance for shorter wavelengths, while a sufficient number of large pores (greater than 2 μm) ensures the effective scattering of longer wavelengths. By striking this balance between pore sizes, we can optimize the reflectance throughout the entire spectrum.

### 3.2 Influence of pore shape and orientation on reflection behavior

In addition to the pore size, we further studied the influence of pore shape and orientation on the optical properties. Elliptical shapes were used to describe pores with anisotropic shapes (Fig. 4(a)). By defining the aspect ratio (*a*/*b*) of the major axis (*a*) to the minor axis (*b*), the anisotropy degree of the pore shape was quantified, and the orientation angle (*θ*) was introduced to describe the included angle between the major axis and the horizontal plane.

*

(d) Electric field distribution around pores; the color represents the normalized electric field intensity. (e) Matrix structures used in simulations. (f) Reflectance simulation results.

As shown in Fig. 4(b), the pore aspect ratios were systematically varied (a*/*b* = 1, 2, 4, and 8) while maintaining a constant cross-sectional area for all the anisotropic pores. The scattering coefficients exhibit a positive correlation with increasing pore aspect ratio. This suggests that pore shape influences the interaction between thermal radiation and materials. For the reflectance of a material, the more relevant parameter is the back-scattering coefficient, which was calculated by integrating the scattering intensity of the pore on the hemisphere with a backward solid angle (Fig. S5 in the ESM gives the setting in COMSOL). Therefore, we also calculate the back-scattering coefficient, as shown in Fig. 4(c). The results show that pores with higher aspect ratios have superior back-scattering, especially in the long-wavelength region (*λ* > 1.5 µm), which is crucial for improving the reflectance.

The increase in the scattering and back-scattering coefficients with increasing aspect ratio can be attributed primarily to the expansion of the scattering cross-section [23,25,37,38]. For small circular pores, as discussed above, their small scattering cross-section limits their scattering ability at long wavelengths (Fig. 4(d-i)). However, when the aspect ratio exceeds 1, as shown in Fig. 4(d-ii)–4(d-iv), the horizontal extension of the pores increases their projection area in the direction of incident radiation. This enlargement of the scattering cross-section allows for more effective scattering. Beyond the enlargement of the scattering cross-section, scattered waves within anisotropic pores may experience complex interference effects such as forward scattering interference, coherent back-scattering, and Anderson localization [39−41]. These interference effects play a crucial role in enhancing the multiple scattering of diffuse thermal radiation, thereby boosting the overall scattering capability. Owing to the expanded scattering cross-section and intricate interference effects, for pores with equivalent diameters of 1, 2, and 4 µm, increasing the aspect ratio can also improve their scattering ability across the 1–6 µm wavelength range (Figs. S6–S8 in the ESM). We also simulated the spectral reflectance of materials with different pore aspect ratios and a fixed porosity of 20% (Figs. 4(e) and 4(f) and Fig. S9 in the ESM). The reflectance improves with increasing aspect ratio. This is caused by the expanded scattering cross section and intricate interference effects.

Expanding on our analysis of pore shape, we investigated how the pore orientation affects their optical properties. We focused on pores with an equivalent diameter of 1 µm and aspect ratios of 2, 4, and 8, while varying the orientation angle from 0° to 90°. The simulation results, as presented in Fig. 5 and Figs. S10–S12 in the ESM, reveal that the scattering coefficient, back-scattering coefficient, and reflectance obviously decrease with increasing orientation. This decrease is due to the reduction in the scattering cross-section perpendicular to the thermal radiation, which diminishes the interaction between the pores and radiation, leading to a weakening of the resonance. These simulation results indicate the significant role of pore structure in controlling optical properties. By optimizing the scattering cross section within the 0.5–4 μm range, high aspect ratios, and low orientations, we can significantly enhance their ability to reflect thermal radiation.

### 3.3 Fabrication of target pore ceramics and microstructural analysis

To obtain the target pore structure as described above, the fabrication process of a sacrificial template combined with the rolling-extrusion method was adopted (described in Section 2.3). Natural graphite flakes were used as the sacrificial template material. The flaky morphology of these materials makes them ideal for creating high aspect ratio pores in ceramics [42]. The graphite flakes, with a median diameter of 3.288 μm and a size distribution ranging from 0.5 to 20 μm, are slightly larger than those of the diameter of 0.5–4 μm we targeted. This size selection accounts for the expected pore shrinkage after sintering and the balance between large and small pore sizes. Figures 6(a) and 6(b) show the cross-sectional microstructures of the sintered ceramics with varying graphite flake percentages from 0 to 80 vol%. Sub-micron- to micro-sized nearly elliptical pores are uniformly distributed throughout the ceramics, preferentially aligning with the rolling direction. This orientation is caused by the shear stress of graphite flakes during the rolling step. The subsequent pressing and sintering steps result in anisotropic, directionally arranged pore structures produced by the oxidation of graphite flakes.

Figure 6(c) shows the systematic evolution of pore size distributions through MIP. As the graphite content increases from 20 to 80 vol%, the dominant pore size range expands from 0.16–0.35 to 0.25–0.9 μm, reflecting both pore enlargement and widening of the pore size distribution. This morphological transformation correlates with the quantified porosity increase from 9% to 35%, as shown in Fig. 6(d). Notably, the measured porosity remains significantly lower than the initial graphite content because of material shrinkage during sintering. The maximum achievable porosity appears capped at 35%, as the addition of more than 80 vol% graphite compromises the structural integrity through discontinuous matrix formation, ultimately leading to complete structural collapse. To resolve MIP’s inherent limitations in quantifying pore morphology anisotropy, we performed SEM-based geometric analysis to measure major/minor axes, aspect ratios, and orientation angles.

The quantitative results in Fig. 6(e) reveal that increasing the graphite content increases the median major axis ( a¯) from 2.18 to 3.73 μm and the median minor axis from 0.56 to 1.15 μm, accompanied by a reduction in the median aspect ratio ( a¯/b¯) from 3.9 to 3.2. The increase in pore size with increasing graphite flakes is attributed to the reduced distance between pores, which promotes the merging of pores into larger pores, which is the consequence of the sintering process involving more graphite flake templates.

Orientation analysis (Fig. 6(f)) confirms the strong directional alignment of pores: over 80% of the pores exhibit orientation angles below 30°, with the angular distribution becoming more concentrated as the graphite content increases. Considering that the pore scattering cross section is associated primarily with the major axis, Fig. 6(g) further delineates the major axis size distribution. The size ranges from 0.2 to 6 μm, with the size distribution widening gradually as the graphite content rises. Notably, the pore sizes (0.2–6 μm) in the sintered samples are slightly smaller than the original particle sizes of graphite flakes (0.5–20 μm), a discrepancy attributed to the shrinkage during sintering. In the preceding analysis, the optimized pore parameters were established: major axis sizes ranging from 0.2 to 6 μm (target 0.5–4 μm), high aspect ratio aspect ratios (3.2–3.9), and good orientation (orientation angle less than 30°). These structural characteristics are anticipated to significantly enhance the thermal radiation scattering, thereby improving the overall reflectance performance.

### 3.4 Infrared reflectance performance of the directional pore ceramics

Figures 7(a) and 7(b) show the spectral reflectances of fabricated ceramics. The addition of graphite flakes significantly improves the broadband reflectance performance across the 1–6 μm spectral region, exceeding 0.94 in the 1–2.5 μm band and maintaining 0.85 at 5 μm. This exceptional reflectance arises from the enhanced scattering effect driven by a well-designed pore structure: optimized pore size distribution, high aspect ratios, and good orientation angles. The experimental results also demonstrate that increased porosity from the addition of more graphite flakes enhances the sample reflectance. This improvement occurs for two reasons. First, an elevated pore density increases the number of scattering events. Second, pore coalescence generates enlarged cavities with expanded Mie scattering cross-sections, which shifts the peak of high infrared reflectance to longer wavelengths, thereby maintaining high reflectance in a wider spectral range.

As shown in Fig. 7(c), the integrated reflectance exceeds 0.9 when exposed to thermal radiation between 1000 and 1600 °C, peaking at 0.945 for the Gf-80 vol% sample at 1600 °C—a 13.5% increase over that of the graphite-free samples (0.812). The resulting high infrared reflectance at elevated temperatures confirms that directional pore YSZ ceramics are exceptional thermal radiation-blocking materials.

To clarify the scattering-dominated optical response, FDTD simulations were performed using reconstructed 2D microstructures extracted through adaptive threshold segmentation of SEM images (Fig. 8(a) and Fig. S2(b) in the ESM). The electromagnetic field distribution across the cross-section (Fig. 8(b)) demonstrates that high-porosity samples exhibit more and wider scattering cross-sections, thus enhancing their scattering ability. The simulated reflectance spectra (Fig. 8(c)) align with the experimental trends, confirming that increased porosity enhances broadband reflectance. Notably, the scattering cross-sectional areas expand with incident depth until reaching a critical threshold at 180 μm (Fig. 8(c) and Fig. S13 in the ESM), beyond which the reflectance gains diminish. This defines the optical penetration depth limit. Since the sample thickness (1 mm) far exceeds this penetration depth (180 μm), near-zero transmission is observed (Fig. S14 in the ESM). Consequently, the directional pore ceramic effectively reflects more than 90% of the incident thermal radiation.

### 3.5 High-temperature stability

To evaluate the high-temperature stability, the ceramic samples were subjected to 1600 °C for 24 h. After cooling to room temperature, the crystal structure, pore distribution, and optical properties were comprehensively characterized. XRD analysis (Fig. 9(a)) confirms the retention of the tetragonal YSZ phase (PDF 82#1245), indicating that no significant phase transformation occurred during thermal treatment. Additionally, a rightward shift in the YSZ diffraction peak (Fig. 9(b)) primarily indicates lattice contraction, potentially originating from residual stress relaxation, phase evolution, grain growth, or densification processes. These changes reduce the porosity from 9%–35% (pre-treatment) to 5%–22% (Figs. 10(a)–10(d)), accompanied by a decrease in the pore size.

## 4 Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the infrared reflectance of YSZ ceramics can be significantly improved by combining simulation-guided pore design with controlled fabrication processes. Theoretical simulations identified two critical pore design principles: (1) optimized pore size distribution—smaller pores (0.5–2 μm) for efficient short-wavelength scattering and larger pores (> 2 μm) for extended spectral coverage—to maximize scattering across the 1–6 μm range; (2) high-aspect-ratio pores with low orientation angles to amplify back-scattering through expanded cross-sections. Guided by these findings, directional pore structures were fabricated via a rolling extrusion method using graphite flake templates. Experimental characterization confirmed the successful creation of pores with tailored parameters: major axis sizes of 0.2–6 μm, aspect ratios of 3.2–3.9, and orientation angles below 30°. The ceramics achieved exceptional broadband reflectance (> 0.9 across 1–6 μm) at temperatures between 1000 and 1600 °C, with the optimal sample (80 vol% graphite flakes) reaching a peak reflectance of 0.945—a 13.5% improvement over that of its non-porous counterparts. In addition, the materials retained structural integrity and reflectance stability even after prolonged exposure to 1600 °C, validating their suitability for extreme thermal environments. This work integrates theoretical insights with scalable fabrication, providing a universal framework for designing high-performance thermal shielding materials for aerospace, military equipment, and energy systems.
