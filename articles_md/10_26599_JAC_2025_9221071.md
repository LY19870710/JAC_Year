## 1 Introduction

Temperature is a crucial physical parameter that defines the state and properties of material and plays an essential role in both production and daily life [1−3]. Industries such as aerospace [4−7], steel metallurgy [8−11], and energy [12−15] have a high demand for high-temperature measurements. In particular, aviation engines are critical to modern industry, and their reliable operation is essential for both economic stability and national defense. The new generation of engine combustion chambers operates at temperatures up to 1800 °C, presenting challenges that traditional high-temperature sensors are unable to meet [16−20]. In recent years, researchers have successfully developed thin-film thermocouples (TFTCs) that can meet aeroengine temperature measurement needs [21−26]. Compared with traditional high-temperature sensors, TFTCs offer advantages such as compact size, rapid response, and high measurement accuracy. Moreover, their integration directly onto engine surfaces minimizes interference, enhancing the safety and efficiency of engine operation. The sensitive layer material of TFTCs includes mainly metal and semiconductor ceramic material. The probe-type TFTC prepared using WRe26–indium oxide (In2O3) has a thermoelectric potential of 93.7 mV and a Seebeck coefficient of 165.5 μV/°C at 700 °C [27]. A tungsten rhenium alloy is used to deposit Pt/PtRh13% TFTCs on aircraft gas turbine engine blades [28], which can be used to measure turbine blades at 980 °C. However, the temperature upper limit of the abovementioned TFTCs has not exceeded 1000 °C. With increasing research, new material and processes have been applied to the development of high-temperature TFTCs. Researchers have prepared In2O3/indium tin oxide (ITO) TFTCs through polymer-derived ceramic technology [25], with a temperature upper limit of 1200 °C and an average Seebeck coefficient of 182 µV/°C. The probe-type thin film temperature sensor prepared using IZO/In2O3 can work continuously for more than 5 h at 1200 °C [29]. The TFTC prepared from ITO/In2O3 can be measured at 1250 °C [30], with a sensitivity of 131.7 µV/°C. When the upper-temperature limit of TFTCs composed of (10–90) vol% MoSi2–Al2O3 and (10–90) vol% WSi2–Al2O3 reaches 1350 °C [31], the maximum output thermoelectric potential is 19.3 mV. TFTCs with 95 wt% W–5 wt% rhenium (W–5Re) and 74 wt% W–26 wt% rhenium (W–26Re) based on a silicon carbide substrate and a thermally sensitive component coated with an aluminum oxide (Al2O3) protective layer were prepared via radio frequency magnetron sputtering and other processes [32]. The maximum measurable temperature was 1420 °C, and the sensitivity was 28.63 μV/°C. ITO/In2O3 TFTCs with a thickness of 1 μm were prepared on Al2O3 ceramic substrates via processes such as radio frequency magnetron sputtering [33]; the maximum measurable temperature was 1450 °C, and the sensitivity was 129.26 μV/°C. Pt Rh10/Pt TFTCs prepared by screen-printing technology have a maximum measurement temperature of 1500 °C and a sensitivity of 10.7 μV/°C [34]. The main reasons for the failure of TFTCs currently include thermal oxidation failure of sensitive material and thermal stress mismatch caused by differences in the thermal expansion coefficients of multilayer heterogeneous thin films at high temperatures, resulting in film cracking, detachment, or delamination. The high-temperature stability of sensitive material is insufficient, resulting in unstable performance. In addition, element diffusion occurs between multilayer films, affecting their thermoelectric performance and even making it impossible to maintain a stable crystal structure or chemical composition at high temperatures, while the insulation layer may also fail.

Research on TFTCs focuses on structural design and exploration of new sensitive material, aiming to develop semiconductor thermoelectric material systems with higher Seebeck coefficients to surpass traditional metal K-type, R-type, and S-type thermoelectric material. Research has successfully achieved good thermoelectric performance under high-temperature conditions and has a higher sensitivity than traditional metal material. However, the current upper limit of TFTCs falls short of the 1800 °C required by advanced engine designs, underscoring the urgent need to push the limits of TFTC technology. Research into ultrahigh-temperature TFTCs is crucial for high-temperature sensor technology, optimizing the performance of aerospace engines and gas turbines and ensuring operational efficiency and safety [27,33,35−37].

This research addresses these challenges by proposing a novel In2O3 yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ)/In2O3 ultrahigh-temperature TFTC sensor. Previous research on YSZ has focused mainly on its mechanical and thermal insulation properties as ceramic material, its application as a protective layer and thermal barrier coating, or investigations of YSZ preparation methods [38−45]. However, there has been no research on blending YSZ with thermoelectric material to enhance their performance [46−48]. This study designs TFTCs on the basis of their temperature measurement principles, first principles, and simulations. Moreover, neural network technology can be used to combine deep learning algorithms with experimental data from TFTCs. The experimental data are trained and learned to establish a nonlinear mapping between error signals and real signals, improving the repeatability accuracy of TFTCs. Finally, an In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3 ultrahigh-temperature TFTC based on neural network technology regulation is obtained, providing a practical and feasible solution for 1800 °C ultrahigh-temperature measurements.

### 2.1 Simulation

First, the carrier mobility, Seebeck coefficient, and conductivity of the two electrode materials were calculated via first-principles simulations. Second, on the basis of these material parameters, a multiphysics simulation of the thermoelectric properties was then performed to establish a theoretical basis for subsequent experiments. The Seebeck coefficient is defined as the average entropy carried by charge carriers divided by the amount of charge at thermal equilibrium [49]. Equation (1) is derived from degenerate statistics and the Bethe–Sommerfeld expansion of the Mott relation for single-band conduction [50]:

(1) S=π23.KB2Te(N(E)n(E)+1μ(E).dμ(E)dE)|E=Ef

where *S* is the Seebeck coefficient, *N*(*E*) is the electronic density of states, *e* is the carrier charge, *n*(*E*) is the energy-dependent number of states, *K*B is the Boltzmann constant, *μ*(*E*) is the carrier mobility, and *E*f  is the Fermi level.

The conductivity can be expressed in terms of the relaxation time [51] (Eq. (2)):

(2) σ=τ⋅K=22πCβ3Dℏ4K3(KBTm∗)32Eβ2

where σ is the conductivity, *τ* is the relaxation time, i.e., the mean free range between two collisions of carriers, * Cβ3D is the elastic constant,  ℏ is Planck’s constant, K* is the calculation constant, *T* is the temperature, *E**β* is the deformation potential energy, and *m** is the effective mass.

In2O3(YSZ) and In2O3 were selected as the positive and negative electrodes, respectively. Using first principles, the thermoelectric parameters of each material were examined, and simulations were conducted to quantify the relationships among the Seebeck coefficient, conductivity, temperature, and carrier concentration. Additionally, a multiphysics coupling simulation was conducted on the TFTCs formed by combining the material, enabling the investigation of both the static and transient thermoelectric characteristics of the In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3 TFTCs. The model structures of In2O3 and In2O3(YSZ) are depicted in Figs. 1(a) and 1(b). The space group of In2O3 is *IA** 3¯, with a total of 80 atoms in the model. The space group of In2O3(YSZ) is P*1, with a total of 80 atoms in the model. It is composed of indium oxide doped with 10 Zr atoms and 1 Y atom. The mass fraction ratio of In2O3 to YSZ is approximately 9 : 1. The CASTEP module in Materials Studio software was used to perform band structure calculations, and the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional from generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was selected to describe the exchange-correlation potential between electrons, effectively balancing computational efficiency and prediction accuracy. To analyze their electronic structural characteristics, the energy band structures and partial wave density of states were examined through simulations, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). As a semiconductor compound, In2O3 has an undoped energy band gap of 2.50 eV. Remarkably, the slope of the state density at the upper edge of the In2O3 valence band is considerably steeper than that at the lower edge of the conduction band.

The energy band along the G-X direction was fitted to a quadratic curve to calculate the effective mass in the (100) direction on the basis of its curvature. The calculated effective mass of In2O3 is 0.230 me, whereas that of In2O3(YSZ) is 0.258 me.

The results indicate that as the temperature increases, material exhibits similar carrier mobility trends, with a noticeable decline, suggesting that intensified lattice vibrations at elevated temperatures lead to increased scattering, which in turn diminishes the carrier migration ability. At room temperature, the carrier mobilities of In2O3 and In2O3(YSZ) were measured at 252.32 and 96.63 cm²/(V·s), respectively. According to Eq. (1), the primary parameters influencing the Seebeck coefficient include the effective mass of electrons and the band gap. The effective mass of electrons significantly affects the sensitivity and migration behavior of carriers as they respond to temperature changes. Additionally, wide bandgap semiconductors tend to exhibit a higher Seebeck coefficient, as a larger band gap enhances temperature-induced changes in carrier distribution, resulting in a greater thermoelectric response under a temperature gradient.

To assess the thermoelectric-related parameters of the material, the previously calculated band structure data were used as input for BoltzTrap-1.2.5 software [53]. The Seebeck coefficients for In2O3 and In2O3(YSZ) were calculated independently across a temperature range from 300 to 2100 K (27–1827 °C). The relationships between the Seebeck coefficient and changes in both the carrier concentration and temperature are depicted in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b). For In2O3, at a fixed temperature, the absolute value of the Seebeck coefficient decreases as the carrier concentration increases. Conversely, when the carrier concentration is constant, the Seebeck coefficient stabilizes at approximately −147 μV/°C as the temperature increases. For In2O3(YSZ), the Seebeck coefficient initially increases and then decreases with increasing carrier concentration at a constant temperature. When the carrier concentration is fixed, the Seebeck coefficient stabilizes at approximately −40 μV/°C as the temperature increases. Given the significant difference in Seebeck coefficients between In2O3(YSZ) and In2O3, it can be preliminarily inferred that the material may serve as positive and negative electrode material for TFTCs, generating a thermoelectric potential due to temperature variations. According to Eq. (2), conductivity-related parameters, such as the electron effective mass, deformation potential, and elastic constant, are crucial. The electron effective mass influences electron transport within material, whereas changes in the deformation potential affect the electron distribution and mobility. The elastic constant affects the relaxation time, stability of the crystal structure, and subsequent modifications in the electronic structure, thereby affecting the material conductivity.

### 2.2 Design and fabrication

The TFTC temperature sensor developed in this study utilizes a customized alumina ceramic as the substrate. The properties of thermoelectric material determine the upper limits of temperature measurement, sensitivity, and conductivity. Low-cost p-type oxide semiconductors, such as NiO, CoO, Cu2O, and FeO, exhibit poor stability in ultrahigh-temperature air atmospheres, thereby compromising their long-term performance and reliability. The valence band structure of these p-type oxides is influenced by the strong localization of oxygen 2p orbitals, resulting in a flatter valence band top and reduced hole mobility. This often requires doping to enhance conductivity. Owing to their temperature sensitivity, p-type oxides have limited applicability as positive electrode material for ultrahigh-temperature thermocouples. This study uses n-type oxide semiconductors for both the positive and negative electrodes of TFTCs. The high melting point of the material is essential for operation at temperatures up to 1800 °C. Analysis revealed that materials such as ITO and indium–tin–zinc–oxide (ITZO) are prone to failure under such conditions, resulting in unstable thermoelectric performance. In contrast, In2O3 and YSZ possess high melting points and can function effectively at ultrahigh temperatures; however, YSZ has very low conductivity, making it difficult to generate a detectable signal. This study presents an innovative approach by doping In2O3 with YSZ material to create In2O3(YSZ), a material capable of stable performance at ultrahigh temperatures. This doped In2O3(YSZ) is then combined with In2O3 to form the positive and negative electrodes of the TFTCs.

For In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3 TFTCs, YSZ stands for yttria-stabilized zirconia, which serves as a doping material. In this study, three commonly used doping ratios of ZrO2 and Y2O3 were selected: ZrO2 (92 wt%)–Y2O3 (8 wt%), ZrO2 (95 wt%)–Y2O3 (5 wt%), and ZrO2 (97 wt%)–Y2O3 (3 wt%), referred to as YSZ92, YSZ95, and YSZ97, respectively. On the basis of the simulation results, In2O3 and YSZ were combined at four different weight ratios (In2O3 : YSZ in wt%): 8 : 2, 9 : 1, and 98 : 2. This approach resulted in a total of 9 different powder sample ratios, as shown in Table 1.

To prepare the In2O3 and YSZ powder samples for the TFTCs, a ball mill was used to uniformly mix the powder at a specific ratio. The speed was set to 300 r/min, and the ball milling time was 12 h. After wet milling, the powder was dried on a heating table at 60 °C and then screened via a vibrating screen machine (ZFJ-II, Shaoxing Bowei Instrument Equipment Co., Ltd, China) to strictly control the powder particle size, resulting in powder samples suitable for subsequent screen printing. The obtained powder, ethyl cellulose, glass powder, and terpineol were mixed at a mass fraction ratio of 10 : 1 : 1 : 2 to prepare a slurry for screen printing. After each electrode was printed, the sample was placed on a heating table at 120 °C for 20 min to ensure that the previous layer of film material solidified and adhered to the substrate, after which the other electrode was printed. Finally, the printed sample was placed in a muffle furnace (ZH-1600MFL, Henan Zhonghuang Furnace Industry Co., Ltd., China) and gradually heated to 1200 °C for 30 min, allowing the film to undergo necessary physical and chemical reactions in a high-temperature environment, thereby optimizing its microstructure and properties.

TFTCs can operate in extremely high-temperature and high-pressure environments, where sensitive material may volatilize due to these harsh conditions, potentially reducing signal repeatability over repeated use. To address this, a protective layer was applied to prevent material volatilization and enhance the performance of TFTCs under extreme conditions. Alumina was selected as the protective layer material because of its high melting point and excellent compatibility with thermal stresses. Additionally, screen printing was used to fabricate a protective layer with sufficient thickness to ensure adequate protection. Once the protective layer was applied, the thin-film thermocouple underwent high-temperature heat treatment. Finally, the cold end of the thin-film thermocouple was bonded to the lead wire via conductive silver paste (DS3120), and its ultrahigh-temperature thermoelectric properties were evaluated. The prepared In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3 TFTCs can be used in extremely harsh, ultrahigh-temperature environments and effectively capture and convert thermal signals into electrical signals at elevated temperatures for thermal parameter monitoring. However, owing to factors such as thermal stress, oxidative corrosion, and material fatigue inherent in ultrahigh-temperature conditions, TFTCs may experience certain degradation during use, manifested as increased repeatability errors and reduced durability. At present, repeatability errors are generally reduced by improving the level of sensor preparation and standardizing experimental procedures. No compensation algorithm has been developed to improve the repeatability accuracy of high-temperature thin-film temperature sensors. To address this, neural network technology was employed to model and compensate for these errors. Using deep learning algorithms, experimental TFTC data were used to train the model, establishing a nonlinear mapping between the error signal and the actual signal. Once trained, the neural network model can perform corrections in new measurement tasks by simply inputting relevant environmental conditions and the thermocouples’ usage histories. This compensation enhanced the accuracy, consistency, and repeatability of the TFTC measurements.

This study involves four neural network algorithms, namely, the back propagation (BP) neural network, the particle swarm optimization-BP (PSO-BP) neural network, the convolutional neural network (CNN), and the CNN-long short-term memory-attention (CNN-LSTM-attention) neural network. The BP neural network algorithm can approximate complex nonlinear relationships because of its simple hierarchical structure, good nonlinear mapping ability, and strong self-learning optimization. The PSO-BP algorithm can mitigate the BP network’s tendency to fall into local minima during training. CNN is mainly used to predict the values of continuous variables in regression tasks, effectively modeling spatial and temporal structures in input data through convolutional operations for feature extraction. In the CNN-LSTM-attention algorithm, the CNN typically acts as the front-end processor to extract significant local features from sequence data.

In this study, BP, PSO-BP, CNN, and CNN-LSTM-attention neural networks were applied to compensate for the repeatability error of thin-film thermocouples, allowing a comparison of the performance of each network in terms of error correction. The experiments were conducted via MATLAB R2021b (MathWorks, USA).

### 2.3 Tests and calibration

During the sensor preparation process, the doping ratios of the sensitive layer material significantly affect the sensor performance. To identify the optimal doping ratio parameters, the microstructure, composition, and uniformity of the films were analyzed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Gemini Sigma 300, ZEISS, Germany). The films with different doping ratios were also examined via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, USA) with a peak position accuracy of 2*θ* ≤ 0.01°) to assess the impact of these ratios on the films’ crystal composition. Furthermore, an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; ESCALAB Xi+, Thermo Fisher, UK) was used to characterize the elemental composition and binding energy of In2O3(YSZ) films with varying YSZ doping ratios.

To evaluate the thermoelectric performance of In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3 TFTCs under ultrahigh-temperature conditions, an experimental platform for static testing at extreme temperatures was constructed, as shown in Fig. 5(a). The platform utilized an induction heating system (heating range: 0–2000 °C) with an infrared thermometer (M316, Sensortherm, Germany; range: 400–2500 °C) as the temperature control feedback unit. The induction heating furnace (TH-30KVA, Xi’an Tianshuo Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., China) heats the graphite chamber to a set temperature through induction heating and then conducts heat through the graphite chamber. It does not directly heat TFTCs and does not generate an induced electromotive force in TFTCs. The temperatures at the hot end and the cold end were measured via a standard B-type thermocouple and a K-type thermocouple, respectively. A multichannel data collector recorded the temperature difference between the hot and cold ends of TFTCs, along with the corresponding variations in the output electromotive force.

### 3.2 Static test

To determine the optimal YSZ doping ratio, the effects of different YSZ doping levels on the thermoelectric properties of In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3(TFTCs) were evaluated, with the test results presented in Fig. 9. Notably, when the hot end of the TFTCs was subjected to an ultrahigh temperature of 1800 °C, the temperature at the cold end varied with that at the hot end, yielding a maximum sensor output of 200.8 mV thermoelectric potential, which aligned closely with the simulation and experimental results. In contrast, doping with YSZ97 produced lower thermoelectric outputs across all doping ratios than did doping with YSZ92 and YSZ95, with a maximum Seebeck coefficient of only 74.1 μV/°C (the sample 3-c). The overall doping performance of YSZ92 was superior, as evidenced by its relatively high Seebeck coefficient. Furthermore, for YSZ97 doping, the output was significantly reduced, likely due to poor crystallization of the film grains, which hindered effective thermoelectric output at this specific ratio.

### 3.3 Dynamic test

A dynamic response test platform was developed to assess the dynamic temperature response capability of the In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3(TFTC) sensor. A schematic diagram of the test platform is shown in Fig. 10. Initially, the lumped heat capacity method was used for evaluation. The results in Fig. 10(a) indicate that the response time of the fabricated In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3(TFTCs) was 2.8 ms, demonstrating the ability of the sensor to quickly return to the original thermoelectric potential state following temperature fluctuations. This rapid response was attributed to the small volume and approximately two-dimensional structure of the film. The low thermal inertia of the flat film facilitated the rapid evaporation of alcohol. Furthermore, the calculated time constant of In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3(TFTCs) was 1.78 ms. The experimental results demonstrated that the prepared In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3(TFTCs) exhibited excellent responsiveness in a liquid physical environment.

The sensor was able to withstand vibrations with a maximum acceleration of 21.17*g* and impacts up to 100*g*.

In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3(TFTCs) developed in this study were evaluated via a small ATR air turbine rocket engine. A schematic of the test site is shown in Fig. 10(e), while the results of the experiment are illustrated in Fig. 10(f). The entire heating and cooling process lasted approximately 540 s, with a maximum recorded temperature of 1090 °C in the high-temperature gas environment. At this temperature, the In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3 film thermocouple achieved a peak thermoelectric output of 168 mV. Notably, the voltage increase curve of TFTCs closely matched the temperature increase curve of the standard K-type thermocouple. These findings demonstrate that In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3(TFTCs) can function effectively in a high-temperature gas environment at the outlet of an air turbine rocket engine at 1090 °C.

To further improve the accuracy of sensors, this study introduces neural networks and optimizes the data obtained from testing through neural network optimization to increase the repeatability accuracy of sensors.

### 3.4 Performance testing of neural network algorithm optimization

To address the increased repeatability error and reduced durability of TFTCs caused by factors such as thermal stress, oxidative corrosion, and material fatigue in ultrahigh-temperature environments, we propose the use of neural network technology. A neural network model was developed to integrate deep learning algorithms with experimental data from TFTCs.

This model was trained to establish a nonlinear relationship between the error signal and the actual signal, thus compensating for the repeatability error via a BP neural network. The input parameters for the model included the hot end temperature, cold end temperature, and error voltage recorded during testing, while the first thermoelectric potential served as the output. Using the constructed neural network, we corrected the repeatability error in subsequent uses of TFTCs. After correction by the trained BP neural network, the experimental data were compared. Figure 11(a) presents the variation in the root mean square error (RMSE) during the training process of the BP neural network and the corrected repeatability experimental results by the neural network. The optimal RMSE is 2.288×10−5 in the 12th round of training. Multiple experimental data of In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3 thin film thermocouples were used as test data. After the trained BP neural network was calibrated, the experimental data were compared, revealing a range of 12.03 mV and a standard deviation of 5.16 mV. The calculated repeatability error after correction was 2.24%, indicating a repeatability rate of 97.76%. This demonstrates an improvement in repeatability compared with TFTCs without a protective layer.

The PSO-BP neural network model was employed to further compensate for the repeatability error of the TFTCs. In this model, the fitness function of the PSO optimization algorithm was used to optimize the weights and biases of the BP neural network. The parameters for training iterations, learning rate, and training error were consistent with those of the BP neural network. Figure 11(b) shows the repeatability error after the PSO-BP neural network correction and the RMSE variation during the training process. Test data from multiple repeated experiments involving In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3(TFTCs) were used. After correction via the trained PSO-BP neural network, the experimental data were analyzed, revealing a range of 10.18 mV and a standard deviation of 4.37 mV. The repeatability error after correction was 1.88%, indicating a repeatability rate of 98.12%. This represents a marginal improvement over the BP neural network alone, suggesting that the PSO algorithm effectively mitigates issues such as local minima in the BP neural network. However, significant errors remained within the temperature range of 600–1400 °C.

To construct a CNN model, the initial step involves implementing a two-layer convolutional structure. The convolution kernels were configured with a size of 1 × 1 to prevent overfitting and enhance training speed. A pooling layer was then added to downsample the feature space while preserving essential information, thereby improving the model’s generalization ability. A fully connected layer subsequently integrates global features to create a unified feature representation, which facilitates regression predictions. Finally, a regression layer produces the quantitative prediction results. The dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 8 : 1 : 1, normalized, and flattened into a one-dimensional format to meet the input requirements of the neural network. The model underwent 400 training iterations, with RMSE as the loss function. Figure 11(c) shows the RMSE change curve throughout the CNN training process, with RMSE of 9.97499×10−4 recorded after 400 training iterations. Figure 11(c) also presents the experimental results after CNN correction, showing a range of 4.51 mV and a standard deviation of 1.94 mV. The repeatability error after correction was 0.83%, indicating a repeatability rate of 99.17%.

The CNN-LSTM-attention neural network model was employed to further mitigate the repeatability error of the TFTCs. The model structure consists of two convolutional layers, each with a kernel size of 1 × 1, followed by one LSTM layer. The LSTM unit includes input, forget, and output gates, along with a cell state, and is paired with an attention layer. The data of the thin-film thermocouples are divided into a training set, a validation set, and a test set at a ratio of 8 : 1 : 1. The data are normalized and flattened into a one-dimensional format to meet the input requirements of neural networks. At each temperature step, the LSTM unit updates its internal state on the basis of the characteristics of the current temperature and the hidden state of the previous temperature, generating the hidden state at the current time. The entire LSTM layer processes the input sequence sequentially, constructing a hidden state sequence that captures global contextual information. The output layer consists of a fully connected layer and a regression layer, which map the context vector to the desired predicted value. In this process, the model adjusts the parameters of all the layers via backpropagation and optimization algorithms to reduce the root mean square error between the predicted and actual target values. The model has been trained 400 times.

Figure 11(d) shows the variation in RMSE during the training process. Upon completion of model training, the RMSE was recorded as 5.629×10−4. Compared with the CNN model, the CNN-LSTM-attention model exhibited a more consistent decline in RMSE, converging after 100 iterations. Conversely, the CNN model converged after 150 iterations and showed fluctuations in RMSE during training. Test data derived from multiple repeated experiments involving In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3(TFTCs). Following correction by the trained CNN-LSTM-attention neural network, multiple datasets were compared. Figure 11(d) also presents the repeatability experimental results following correction by the neural network, showing a range of 2.54 mV and standard deviation of 1.09 mV. The repeatability error after correction was 0.47%, indicating a repeatability rate of 99.53%, a significant improvement. This enhancement was attributed to the LSTM layer’s ability to capture dependencies in long sequences and model the dynamic evolution between data points. Additionally, the attention layer improved the sensitivity to critical information within the input sequence, enhancing the model’s ability to learn complex patterns and thereby improving generalizability and accuracy.

The output voltage was obtained after five static tests were conducted on TFTCs. Additionally, each output voltage was compensated via the CNN-LSTM-attention neural network. Following error correction, the fitted quadratic polynomial representing the voltage was converted into temperature. The hot end temperature was then compared with that measured by a standard B-type thermocouple, as shown in Fig. 12(a). The calculations revealed that the temperature measurement error of the calibrated TFTCs was 13.1 °C when 100 °C was measured, with a full-scale error of ±0.73% FS.

## 4 Discussion

In the future, we will conduct long-term stability testing and high-temperature destructive experiments on sensors to further investigate their performance under different working hours and extreme conditions. This will involve analyzing material aging and sensor failure mechanisms, as well as proposing potential solutions. At the same time, we will continue to explore the application of this sensor in fields such as the petrochemical industry, where it can monitor equipment performance under extreme temperatures and pressures to prevent accidents. In mechanical processing and automotive manufacturing, these sensors can be used to monitor the temperature of critical components, ensuring their proper operation and extending their service life. In addition, we will continue to assess the applicability and stability of neural network models in field applications, evaluate their accuracy and reliability in practical use, and explore the use of deep learning algorithms for real-time processing and correction of thermocouple output signals to improve their signal stability and accuracy under extreme temperatures. Moreover, deep learning algorithms are also considered for predicting the performance degradation of thermocouples to perform maintenance and replacement in advance and ensure their long-term reliability. For example, by analyzing the output data of thermocouples at different temperatures and times, training a neural network model can predict their performance changes at extreme temperatures.

## 5 Conclusions

This research addresses the challenge of ultrahigh-temperature measurement in extreme environments, specifically by targeting the temperature measurement needs of combustion chambers in next-generation aerospace engines, which can reach temperatures up to 1800 °C. To meet these demands, we developed an In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3 ultrahigh-temperature TFTC sensor. The feasibility of doping In2O3 with YSZ was initially assessed via first principles and multiphysics simulations. YSZ was subsequently doped with In2O3 through wet ball milling to create the novel thermoelectric material, In2O3(YSZ), with In2O3 serving as the sensitive material for both electrodes and the alumina ceramic used as the substrate. A TFTC temperature sensor was fabricated via a screen-printing technique, and the effects of the In2O3 and YSZ doping ratios were analyzed to determine the optimal configuration.

The developed In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3 ultrahigh-temperature film thermocouple temperature sensor underwent several tests, including an ultrahigh temperature test at 1800 °C, a dynamic response test, a thermal shock test, and a vibration shock test. A protective layer was applied via screen printing to increase the repeatability of TFTCs. The test results indicate that the maximum working temperature is 1850 °C. In experiments with different doping ratios at 1800 °C, the maximum thermoelectric output was 258.5 mV, and the maximum Seebeck coefficient was 180.9 μV/°C with a doping ratio of In2O3 : YSZ92 = 9 : 1. Without a protective layer, TFTCs achieved a repeatability rate of 97.13%. After a screen-printed aluminum oxide protective layer was applied, the repeatability improved to 99.29%, although the improvement was associated with a reduction in the thermoelectric output. In the dynamic test experiment, the sensor demonstrated a response time of 2.8 ms and a time constant of 1.78 ms, demonstrating accurate temperature measurement performance under extreme instantaneous temperature changes of 1200 °C. During the vibration impact test, which involved a vibration acceleration of 21.17*g* and a maximum impact of 100*g*, the resistance of the In2O3(YSZ)/In2O3 film thermocouple remained stable before and after the test, and no voltage was observed due to vibration and impact. In an application test conducted on an air turbine rocket engine, TFTCs demonstrated effective operation in a high-temperature gas environment of 1090 °C.

To further enhance the repeatability and assess the performance, various neural network models, including BP, PSO-BP, CNN, and CNN-LSTM-attention, have been employed. Among these models, the CNN-LSTM-attention model exhibited the best performance, achieving a repeatability rate of 99.53% after error correction. Compared with a B-type thermocouple, the calculated full-scale error was ±0.73% FS.

Overall, this research offers a practical approach for measuring ultrahigh temperatures.
