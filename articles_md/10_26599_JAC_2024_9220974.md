## 1 Introduction

In recent years, in-depth exploration of biomaterials has provided new perspectives and possibilities in the field of structural materials. Biomaterials typically exhibit hierarchical structures that have evolved over extended periods—such as the oriented arrangement of collagen fibers in bone tissues—demonstrating distinct textures. Complex bone tissues further feature hierarchical arrangements, leveraging the microstructural morphology and interactions between different materials at the interfaces to achieve exceptional performance. Inspired by these specific biomaterial structures, numerous studies have been dedicated to bioinspired anisotropic materials with layered architectures [1−3].

Anisotropic ceramics typically feature special structural designs that can result in improved compressive creep resistance, bending strength, and increased hardness [4,5]. Additionally, the literature suggests that biomimetic anisotropic designs can facilitate crack deflection, crack bridging, and grain pulling, thereby enhancing the material toughness. Moreover, the synergistic enhancement of these properties can contribute to the increased effectiveness of materials in specific directions [6]. Anisotropic ceramics also exhibit exceptional functional characteristics in terms of their structural features, distinguishing them from traditional ceramics [7−9].

However, anisotropic ceramics do not have a single point of origin or starting point; rather, this concept has gradually evolved and matured with the advances in ceramic science and engineering technologies and manufacturing techniques. As early as the latter half of the 20th century, various scholars employed different methods to create ceramics exhibiting anisotropic properties [10,11] and studied the effects of heterogeneous microstructures on properties such as their dielectric, piezoelectric, thermal, and electrical conductivity properties [11]. Concurrently, researchers have begun exploring the influence of complex biological structures—such as the hierarchical organization of bone, the layered structure of nacre, and the cellular architecture of plant tissues—on the functional effectiveness of various organisms, ushering in a new era of bioinspired ceramics. By the present century, with a deep understanding of anisotropic ceramics, their preparation has become increasingly systematic and diverse.

Consequently, there has been a proliferation of research articles on heterostructured materials (Fig. 1), demonstrating meticulous control over their crystal structure, microstructural features, and processing techniques [1,12−14]. This has enabled the design and production of ceramics and their composites with specific directional properties, demonstrating their enormous potential in semiconductor, electronics, optics, and aerospace applications.

Carbides are binary compounds composed of carbon and other elements with low or similar electronegativities (excluding hydrogen). Among advanced structural and functional ceramics, carbide ceramics exhibit superior properties—including high modulus, high-temperature resistance, and wear resistance properties—making them ideal materials. They are extensively used in the aerospace, manufacturing, and chemical sectors, among others. Structural ceramics such as silicon carbide (SiC) possess anisotropic crystal structure characteristics. Treating these materials with biomimetic texturing can further enhance their performance in specific orientations. Typically, different types of biomimetic-textured carbide ceramics or carbide ceramic composites can be fabricated by applying external physical conditions—such as anisotropic force fields, electric fields, and magnetic fields—or by inducing specific anisotropy through internal factors—such as chemical driving forces and interfacial energies [9−11]. These different types of anisotropic carbide materials usually exhibit enhanced physicochemical properties along specific orientations, making them suitable for use in severe environments [15,16].

A typical preparation process for anisotropic carbide ceramic composites involves several steps, including raw material preparation, formation, drying, degassing, sintering, and post-treatment. Among these steps, the formation process plays a crucial role in creating anisotropy. Common methods for fabricating anisotropic carbide materials include the use of strong external forces (such as pressure) [4], templating techniques [17], self-assembly [18], three-dimensional (3D) printing [19], and other processing methods [3] to induce anisotropy. For example, Azuma et al*. [5] fabricated a textured B4C-based ceramic with a *c*-axis orientation using strong-magnetic-field-assisted colloidal processing, ethanol pretreatment, and spark-plasma sintering. The resulting ceramic exhibited a relative density of 91.3%. The samples were subjected to three-point bending tests, which revealed a bending strength of 554±98 MPa along the *c*-axis of the textured porous B4C-based ceramic. Moreover, the bending strength perpendicular to the *c*-axis in the force direction was measured to be 459±39 MPa. The strength of the material was more than twice that of B4C with the same porosity. The measured strength of the textured ceramic was comparable to that of conventionally press-molded B4C, with a relative density of 94%–98%, demonstrating excellent performance.

In 2019, Zhang *et al*. [20] provided a comprehensive review of textured ceramics, covering the concepts, characterization of textured structures, fabrication methods, and typical textured ceramics. However, the concept of “biomimicry” remains vague and focuses more on the materials themselves rather than the relationship between biomimicry and texture. Additionally, there has been limited discussion on anisotropic carbide ceramics and their fabrication methods.

This review provides the first comprehensive examination of biomimetic-textured carbide ceramic composites. It focuses on the structural characteristics of biomimetic materials and fabrication methods of anisotropic carbide ceramic composites (Fig. 2) [21−25]. This discussion covers several common biomimetic materials and highlights their key properties and promising structural features. Additionally, it summarizes the development of biomimetic anisotropic carbide ceramics or ceramic composites over the past decade, illustrating the connection between biomimetics and carbide ceramics. This review also emphasizes the common experimental methods used to fabricate these ceramics or composites—such as the introduction of a strong external field, templating methods, and 3D printing technology—along with the latest research advances. Moreover, this review compares the advantages and disadvantages of different fabrication methods, identifies the ongoing challenges in producing biomimetic-textured carbide ceramic composites, and outlines their application prospects.

## 2 Biological structural materials

Carbide ceramics typically possess specific advantages, such as high modulus, high strength, chemical inertness, light weight, and high toughness. Tailoring the structure of these materials can considerably improve their capabilities, making them more suitable for applications in extreme environments [4,29]. Biological materials exhibit outstanding properties because of their complex internal ordered structures. For example, the layered structure of pearls can effectively disperse and absorb external stresses, thereby enhancing their toughness and tensile strength. Needle- and plate-like crystal arrangements in eggshells provide rigidity and strength, enabling them to withstand external pressure and impact.

Figure 3 compares the specific strengths, stiffnesses, Young’s moduli, and fracture toughnesses of various biological and synthetic materials [30]. The results show that many biological materials demonstrate superior mechanical performance compared with synthetic materials. Therefore, employing biomimetic structural design for carbon-based ceramics has the potential to markedly improve the material capabilities.

Two common design methods for biomimetic carbide-based composite materials are as follows: First, biological materials can be used as templates to fabricate carbide-based composites, which retain the original porous structure and texture of biological materials [31]. For example, Zhou et al.* [26] used wood as a template to obtain bioporous SiC with a unidirectional pore structure through carbothermal reduction and silicon infiltration, achieving an anisotropic thermal conductivity (TC) coefficient of 5.79. Second, biomimetic structural designs can be directly applied to carbide-based composite materials, reflecting more autonomous design principles [32]. Wang *et al.* [33] employed an ice-template method to fabricate layered porous SiC ceramics by adjusting the polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) content and solid-state sintering temperature. Jia *et al.* [34] revealed the relationship between the stress modulation functionality and anisotropy of bioinspired materials through computational simulations, achieving material design and preparation by adopting 3D printing stereolithography (SLA) techniques.

A thorough understanding of the structure of biological materials can aid in designing biomimetic textures for carbide-based composite materials. In the following sections, we outline several common biological-structure materials, including nacreous-structured, hierarchical bone-structured, and wood-structured materials, and analyze the structural components responsible for their outstanding performance, with the aim of broadening their application in the structural design of carbide-based composite materials.

### 2.1 Nacreous-structured materials

Nacreous hierarchical structures—such as those of gastropods and bivalves—are commonly found in mollusk shells. Nacre comprises 95 vol% aragonite (CaCO3) and 5 vol% softer organic material composed of chitin and proteins, which form brick-and-mortar-like arrangements (Figs. 4(a) and 4(b)) [35]. The aragonite layers are closely stacked in a parallel direction with diameters and thicknesses of approximately 5–8 and 0.5 μm, respectively. The organic substance, 20–30 nm in size, fills the gaps between the ceramic tiles, creating a laminated structure, as shown in Figs. 4(c) and 4(e). The hardness and stiffness of aragonite contribute to its excellent compressive strength and rigidity. Moreover, the flexibility of the organic material endows nacre with high toughness. The unique brick-and-mortar hierarchical structure renders remarkable strength and toughness to nacre, surpassing that of aragonite by three orders of magnitude in terms of toughness [36].

The outstanding toughness of nacre can be attributed to a microscale mechanism whereby shear yielding occurs at the interfaces between the aragonite layers, enabling them to slide against each other and undergo localized plastic deformation without compromising the integrity of the hard constituents. Material failure occurs only once the potential sliding sites are exhausted and the layers are dislodged.

It is now well-acknowledged that gradual crack propagation results in an increasing crack resistance curve (R*-curve) for crack extension [37]. Unique interfacial interactions between the layers can strongly influence the ability of nacre, particularly in identifying the mechanisms responsible for its hardening and shear resistance. Furthermore, the unique interfacial interactions between the layers strongly influence the behavior of nacre, particularly in identifying the mechanisms responsible for its hardening and shear resistance (Figs. 4(f)‒4(h)). Compared with that of pure calcium carbonate, the layered structure of nacre can withstand stable crack propagation, with cracks prone to form, thus increasing the energy during the crack extension. When hard layers are combined with relatively softer layers, deformation is localized at the compliant interfaces ahead of the crack tip, preventing the coalescence of cracks.

### 2.2 Hierarchical bone-structured materials

Despite being organized into various shapes and configurations suited to their functions, different types of bones typically have fundamentally similar structures, with bone tissue composed of osteocytes, bone matrix, and bone marrow. The bone matrix comprises inorganic minerals and organic substances that provide strength and hardness. Inorganic minerals—primarily calcium phosphate salts such as hydroxyapatite—provide bone rigidity and compressive strength, whereas the organic matrix is primarily collagen—a fibrous protein that forms long chains or bundles—which is similar to the collagen found in ligaments, tendons, and skin. Additionally, small amounts of proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans, which are chemically associated with proteins, are dispersed within and around the collagen fiber bundles. Together, these organic substances endow the bone with resilience and toughness [39,40]. The inorganic mineral nanocrystals are arranged parallel to the *c*-axis of the collagen fibers and exhibit a periodically staggered arrangement along the fiber length, as shown in Fig. 5(a) [41]. This structure results in multifunctional materials that are tough, lightweight, adaptable, and self-healing. As shown in Fig. 5(a), bones exhibit hierarchical multiscale structures that provide excellent fracture resistance through complex deformation and toughening mechanisms at different scales [42]. The fracture resistance of healthy human cortical bone is derived primarily from internal mechanisms that promote ductility and external mechanisms that “shield” growing cracks (Fig. 5(b)) [42]. Small-scale internal and large-scale external processes are coupled with external toughening mechanisms that contribute more to bone fracture resistance. Internal mechanisms act at the nanoscale, improving material plasticity through fiber sliding and enhancing the inherent toughness of the material (Fig. 5(c)).

External toughening, which is most relevant for brittle materials, typically initiates on the 10–100 μm scale. Mechanisms within the microscopic structure that suppress further cracking are activated as the cracks begin to grow, with crack bridging and deflection being two prominent toughening mechanisms [44]. Collagen fibers in structures such as bone trabeculae and plates can bridge cracks and prevent further crack extension when the bone is subjected to external forces, resulting in the appearance of small cracks (Fig. 5(c)). Moreover, a crack can change direction when it encounters these structures and moves along them rather than propagating in a straight line. This crack deflection lengthens the crack path, consumes more energy, hinders further crack propagation, and increases the toughness and fracture resistance of bones.

### 2.3 Wood-structured materials

Wood is composed of tiny units referred to as “cells”, with basic cell types including tracheids, vessel members, fibers, and parenchyma cells. Wood has an ideal layered structure with alternating bundles of fibers and channel-like porous regions, making it the preferred material for developing new bone-substituted biomaterials [45]. Unlike nacre and bone, the main chemical components of wood are organic materials, such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. Wood is commonly used as a template for preparing anisotropic carbide materials in which the wood structure can serve as a carbon template during pyrolysis in a high-temperature argon atmosphere. Moreover, bioinspired SiC (bio-SiC) can serve as a bioinert bone scaffold (prepared by reacting a carbon template with Si vapor or molten Si) [17].

### 2.4 Other special biological structures

Other biological structures also exhibit outstanding performance [1,13]. For example, the longitudinal distribution of fibers provides bamboo with high strength and stiffness, allowing it to withstand tensile and compressive loads [46,47]. Dentin in teeth exhibits a fibrous structure aligned along the curvature of the tooth, resulting in dentin-direction-dependent physical properties. In the fiber direction, dentin has a relatively high compressive strength and elastic modulus, enabling it to withstand the pressure and stress generated during the chewing process [48].

The intricate structural designs of biological materials endow them with excellent mechanical properties. For example, alternating soft and hard layers and interfacial interaction mechanisms in the nacre structure considerably limit crack propagation, contributing to an improvement in toughness. The unique structure of bone also features crack bridging and deflection characteristics, providing superior fracture resistance. The complex porous structure within wood can be used as a carbon template to retain the structural design for bone-substituting applications. Consequently, the complex structures of biological materials and their superior mechanical and chemical properties have sparked widespread interest in bioinspired composite materials. Many studies have shown that bioinspired structural designs can considerably enhance the performance of carbide materials. In Section 3, we discuss recent examples of bioinspired carbide materials to elaborate on common fabrication techniques for anisotropic carbide ceramics and carbide ceramic composites.

## 3 Processing techniques for anisotropic carbide ceramic materials

Researchers have aimed to achieve composite materials with considerably enhanced properties compared with their constituent components by mimicking the intricate microstructures of biological materials. However, creating anisotropic biomimetic composite materials with complex structures remains challenging, as it requires the design of specific microstructures and the implementation of processing methods or procedures to realize these designs. In this section, we introduce the use of strong external forces, templating technology, 3D printing, and other processing methods that induce anisotropy during the fabrication of biomimetic carbide ceramic materials with anisotropic properties and analyze and compare the research findings of various researchers.

### 3.1 Anisotropy induced by strong external forces

Biological materials such as wood and bone possess highly oriented fiber arrangements that endow them with excellent mechanical properties [1]. Inspired by the textured arrangement of biological materials, material texturing or orientation processing has been employed to enhance mechanical performance. Studies have shown that applying strong external forces to materials can produce carbide ceramics with textured reinforced structures [4,5].

The term “strong external forces” refers to the application of high-intensity forces to materials or objects during the shaping and sintering processes to alter their shape, structure, or performance. This can be achieved using tensile and compressive forces and various external fields. Anisotropic carbide ceramic structures can be produced by adjusting the magnitude and direction of these applied forces or external fields. Common methods for preparing anisotropic carbide ceramics using strong external forces include hot working under uniaxial stress and high-pressure magnetic casting.

#### 3.1.1 Hot working under uniaxial pressure

Hot working is a method of processing materials that involves the application of uniaxial pressure during high-temperature sintering or heat treatment. This induces an oriented grain arrangement that forms a textured microstructure [49]. By reducing the flow stress of materials through heating, hot working facilitates plastic deformation. Moreover, parameters such as heating temperature, heating time, pressure magnitude, and cooling method can be controlled to regulate and optimize the structure and properties of the material. For ceramic materials, external pressure can promote densification and lower the sintering temperature to some extent [50,51]. The primary mechanisms controlling the development of anisotropic polycrystalline carbide ceramics during hot working include grain rotation and preferential grain growth [52]. The grains of carbide ceramic materials with hexagonal crystal lattices (α-SiC) usually exhibit plate-like or rod-like morphologies, and these grains can be prone to rotation and texture formation during hot working [53,54].

Hot working includes hot pressing, hot forging, and sinter-forging [20]. Among these methods, research on the sinter-forging preparation of anisotropic ceramics has focused predominantly on Si3N4 ceramics and Si3N4 ceramic-based composites [12,55]. For example, Kondo *et al.* [55] synthesized and sinter-forged Si3N4–SiC ceramics using a reactive synthesis-superplastic sinter-forging method, achieving anisotropic properties in which rod-shaped, micrometer-scale Si3N4 particles tended to align along the flow direction of their long axes. Submicrometer SiC particles were located at the Si3N4 grain boundaries and within the Si3N4 grains, thereby reinforcing the material. Fan *et al.* [56] prepared B4C ceramics with exceptionally high mechanical properties, possibly owing to the rhombohedral structure of B4C; however, no substantial material anisotropy was evident. Currently, there are no reports of interforging being used in the preparation of anisotropic carbide ceramics.

Lee *et al*. [4] fabricated plate-like SiC with preferential grain orientation by hot-forging hot-pressed SiC with an equiaxed microstructure. Anisotropic growth occurred during hot forging, and most of the SiC particles developed into spindle-shaped particles. The crystal phase transformed from 3C (β-SiC) to 4H (α-SiC), which promoted the directional organization of SiC. Owing to its microstructural anisotropy, the material was harder and stronger perpendicular to the pressing direction and tougher parallel to the pressing direction. The hot-forged SiC exhibited a flexural strength of 1150±190 MPa, hardness of 26.8±1.5 GPa, and fracture toughness of 4.4±0.3 MPa·m1/2 perpendicular to the pressing direction, whereas a flexural strength of 810±80 MPa, hardness of 24.4±1.1 GPa, and fracture toughness of 5.9±0.6 MPa·m1/2 were recorded parallel to the pressing direction.

To analyze and compare the effects of SiC raw materials with different crystal structures on the subsequent hot working process, Xie *et al*. [57] subjected SiC raw materials of different compositions to hot forging or annealing processes after hot pressing and analyzed the textural differences. For SiC materials annealed after the hot-pressing process, the mechanism responsible for texture development involved the preferential grain growth of the oriented α-SiC obtained during the hot-pressing process. However, for SiC materials hot-forged after the hot pressing process, SiC grain growth occurred in an equiaxed form, and the degree of textural orientation was dependent on the grain rotation. Regardless of whether the samples were annealed or hotged after the hot pressing process, the degree of textural orientation tended to increase with increasing α-phase content in the initial powder. To investigate the frictional performance of carbon ceramic composites, An *et al*. [58] assembled ceramic films spirally in graphite molds and hot-pressed them at 1500 °C and 30 MPa for 1 h to obtain Bouligand-structured carbon fibers (Cf)/ZrB2–SiC (B–Cf/ZS) ceramics with a ZrB2/SiC mass ratio of 1 : 2, as shown in Fig. 6(a). The influence of the carbon-fiber content on the anisotropic frictional performance of the ceramic-matrix composite was examined by adjusting the amount of carbon fibers. Owing to the different arrangements of carbon fibers in different directions, the Cf/ZrB2–SiC composite material exhibited different friction properties in the *X–Y* and *X–Z* planes [58] (Figs. 6(b)‒6(d)).

Spark plasma sintering (SPS) and flash spark plasma sintering (FSPS) have also been considered as processing methods for anisotropic ceramics. SPS involves the application of an external electric current and is akin to hot pressing [20]. A common method for texture preparation after SPS involves additional hot forging to achieve texturization of the material [21]. The strong unidirectional direct current (DC) electric field of FSPS induces notable thermal gradients within the material, resulting in the formation of textured microstructures. Unlike the textures produced by traditional thermal processing, those formed by FSPS are oriented parallel to the pressing direction. Grasso et al.* [59] used FSPS to fabricate SiC ceramics with textured structures. They applied an exceptionally high current (5–10 kW power) externally [60], causing rapid sublimation of SiC and transformingα-SiC precursor into β-SiC, while extending the dwell time to facilitate complete physical vapor transport. Under the influence of thermal gradients, SiC grains assumed a plate-like morphology (illustrated by the white arrows and insets in Figs. 7(a) and 7(b)), with crystal planes oriented parallel to the (0001) basal plane. These plates exhibited a preferred orientation, with their *c*-axes orthogonal to the direction of the applied load, forming an internal textured structure (region II in Fig. 7). Additionally, the introduction of the B4C sintering aid promoted densification during the sintering process without altering the morphology of SiC ceramics.

#### 3.1.2 Magnetically aligned casting technology

Magnetic slip casting refers to the application of an external magnetic field during the casting process to orient the preferred crystalline axes of the ceramic grains when the slurry is a dispersed suspension [61,62]. A strong magnetic field is applied during solidification to stabilize the particles in the suspension, causing particles with anisotropic magnetic susceptibility to align under the influence of magnetic field [63,64], forming a textured structure.

Apart from ferrites and spinels, most ceramic phases are diamagnetic. The application of an external magnetic field to a diamagnetic material induces an oppositely oriented internal magnetic field, resulting in repulsion. Typically, a high-intensity magnetic field is required to induce an internal magnetic field, owing to the weak magnetization susceptibility of diamagnetic materials. When a crystal is placed in a magnetic field, the principle of crystal orientation arises from the interaction between the applied magnetic field and magnetization intensity of the crystal. This leads to a magnetic torque that causes the crystal to rotate and adopt a stable crystal orientation while minimizing the magnetization energy [65]. For ceramics with magnetically induced anisotropies such as tetragonal and hexagonal structures, the magnetization susceptibility is crystal axis-dependent, and the preferred crystal orientation is determined by the magnitude of the magnetization energy (which is dependent on the magnetization susceptibility of each crystal axis). The magnetization energy (*U*) can be calculated by Eq. (1):

(1) U=−χ2μ0(1+Nχ)2B2

where *N* denotes the demagnetization factor; *μ*0 denotes the vacuum permeability; *B* denotes the magnetic induction intensity; *χ* denotes the magnetic susceptibility.

The magnetic susceptibility along the *a-* or *b-*axis differs from that along the *c*-axis in a crystal with a hexagonal structure. The magnetization energy along the *c-*axis (*Uc*) is less than that along the *a-* or *b-*axis (*Uc* *a*,*b*) when the magnetization susceptibility along the *c-*axis (*χc*) is greater than that along the *a-* or *b*-axis (*χc* > *χ**a*,*b*). Consequently, the *c*-axis of the crystal rotates to align with the preferred direction of the magnetic field [64,65]. The magnetization susceptibility along the *c*-axis of the α-SiC crystal (with its hexagonal structure) is greater than that along the *a-* and *b*-axes; consequently, the *c*-axis of the SiC crystal is aligned in the direction parallel to the magnetic field. It is possible to introduce an additional magnetic field during the sintering process. For example, in the induction heating process, a sample is placed in an oscillating magnetic field produced by an electromagnet [63]. However, this additional magnetic field is typically used as a heating method to promote densification of the sample during sintering and is not related to the orientation structure. To date, there have been no reports on the application of an additional magnetic field during the sintering process [66−69]. If no sintering aids are incorporated during the sintering process, the SiC grains grow slowly and experience almost no rotation, maintaining the degree of texture but resulting in poor densification [68,69]. In liquid-phase sintering, these large non-oriented particles consume the surrounding relatively small oriented particles, leading to grain growth through dissolution and reprecipitation, thereby reducing the degree of texture of the sintered SiC ceramics. Yang et al.* [69] adjusted the sintering aid content to minimize the decrease in the texture structure during the sintering process, aiming for coordinated improvements in the densification, texturing, and mechanical properties. With the addition of 10 wt% sintering aids, α-SiC achieved optimal performance, decreasing the texture degree from 0.83 to 0.76 and exhibiting maximum flexural strengths of 445.58±24.87 and 372.21±19.98 MPa under loads applied parallel and perpendicular to the magnetic field, respectively. In comparison, the nontextured sample exhibited a flexural strength of 339.89±27.58 MPa.

Yang *et al.* [24] used relatively low magnetic field (6 T)-assisted gel casting to form *c*-axis textured α-SiC ceramics. After magnetic gel casting and sintering at 1950 °C for 4 h, randomly oriented small grains in the material were completely consumed, resulting in elongated SiC grains oriented along the *c*-axis and aligned in the direction of the magnetic field. The material exhibited the maximum degree of texture. Further extension of the sintering time improved the density and flexural strength of the material while maintaining a texture degree of 0.84.

Grasso *et al*. [5] achieved pronounced anisotropy in textured hexagonal B4C using magnetic slip casting at 12 T combined with spark-plasma sintering and compared the anisotropic differences in various physical properties of the textured side surface (TSS) and textured top surface (TTS) of a highly textured polycrystalline B4C material and single-crystal B4C. The anisotropy in electrical conductivity was attributed primarily to crystal orientation effects, with a similar behavior evident for TC. TTS and TSS exhibited hardness values of 38.86±2.13 and 31.31±0.79 GPa, respectively, which were high values consistent with those of the B4.38C single-crystal phase.

Suzuki *et al.* [68] found that the shape of α-SiC particles after the sintering process was nearly isotropic. Compared with pressureless sintering, hot-press sintering considerably reduced the texture of the material in the presence of Al2O3–Y2O3 sintering aids. This reduction may be attributed to the preoriented SiC grains in the magnetic field (which undergo further deflection under pressure) and the influence of sintering aids affecting the textural structure of SiC. Lee *et al.* [66] used Al3BC3 as a sintering aid, which resulted in plate-like SiC grains. Hot pressing further enhanced the grain alignment, with ratios of the unit-length grain numbers perpendicular and parallel to the magnetic field direction of 2.58 and 2.67 under hot pressing and pressureless sintering, respectively, whereas the contrast samples without magnetic field orientation did not exhibit anisotropy, as shown in Figs. 9(a)–9(d).

### 3.2 Anisotropy induced by templating technologies

The templating method can be used to effectively fabricate anisotropic structures, as it enables precise control over the material morphology, pore structure, and crystal orientation through the judicious choice of template, thereby regulating the functionality and characteristics of materials [72]. Templating methods are widely applicable to the preparation of various functional materials because they effectively optimize the structure and properties of a material [73] and enhance the performance indicators in specific directions. Common templating methods used to prepare anisotropic carbide ceramic composites include biological and ice templating methods.

#### 3.2.1 Bio-templating

Biological templating is a commonly used method to prepare biomimetic anisotropic ceramic composites. Natural wood has an ideal bioinspired hierarchical structure. Moreover, bio-SiC, with its large surface area and porous geometry, can be obtained by infiltrating molten Si into a carbonized wood template [74]. The alternating arrangement of fiber bundles and channel-like porous regions makes wood an ideal choice as a new bone-substituted biomaterial template. In recent years, Si/SiC wood-derived structures have been studied extensively as bioinert bone scaffolds [45].

Lelli *et al*. [17] fabricated a biomorphic ceramic structure—similar in internal morphology and tissue structure to that of a biological body—using a biological templating method. Subsequently, they prepared a biomimetic nanostructure coating on the surface of the resulting SiC ceramic structure using electrochemical deposition to improve the interface between the material and bone tissue and increase the biocompatibility, bone conduction, and bone integration of the composite material while maintaining its mechanical properties for better use as a bone substitute and bone-tissue-engineering scaffold. To explore the effects of various wood precursors on the performance of biologically inert bone scaffolds, Gryshkov *et al*. [75] studied the surface morphologies, porosities, and pore size distributions of bio-SiC ceramic composites derived from different wood precursors, namely, hornbeam, sapele, tilia, and pear. The results revealed that by selecting appropriate initial wood templates, bio-SiC ceramic composites could be excellent candidates for replacing dense and less porous cortical bone (porosity: 5%–30%).

The highly dense, elongated 3D multilevel and multiporous microchannel structure of a wood template is suitable for constructing thermally conductive “highways” in a composite material to improve its TC [26]. Zhou *et al*. [76] used natural poplar wood as a template to manufacture epoxy-resin (EP) composite materials (bio-SiC/EP) with 3D bio-SiC skeletons and vertically arranged densely interconnected channels. Owing to their unique structures, these materials exhibited different TCs in the axial and radial directions; a high TC coefficient of 10.27 W/(m·K) was obtained along the axial direction at a relatively low bio-SiC loading (21 vol%). Compared with that of pure EP, a TC coefficient enhancement efficiency of 259 was recorded for each 1 vol% increase in the bio-SiC content. The material also exhibited a highly anisotropic TC ratio of 5.77. However, poplar wood contains heterogeneously shaped pores (~50 μm) that inevitably lead to inferior performance.

Researchers have determined that a 3D bio-SiC network derived from spruce wood could be easily recovered from an epoxy-resin composite material for multifunctional reuse. Zhou *et al*. [26] introduced a 3D biomorphic SiC skeleton using spruce wood as a template, followed by impregnation with an epoxy resin, to obtain bio-SiC/EP composite materials with lattice structures in the axial direction and long-aligned channels in the radial sections, as shown in Figs. 10(a)–10(m). A high TC coefficient of 10.4 W/(m·K) was obtained along the axial direction when ~22 vol% bio-SiC was included, with a corresponding enhancement efficiency for the TC coefficient of 253 recorded for each 1 vol% increase in bio-SiC content (which is far higher than that recorded for other materials). Moreover, the bio-SiC/EP composite material exhibited a low linear expansion coefficient, good flame retardancy, and high thermal stability owing to the barrier effect of the 3D bio-SiC network. Additionally, the 3D bio-SiC network considerably enhanced the mechanical properties of the composite material, with an ultrahigh bending strength of 188±11 MPa recorded for the 19% SiC network.

#### 3.2.2 Ice templating

Ice templating is a common method of preparing porous materials [80] that involves placing an appropriate concentration of an aqueous solution at the top of a copper column and freezing the column in liquid nitrogen. Ice crystals nucleate at the bottom of the slurry and grow along a temperature gradient to form an array of ice columns with a layered structure. The filler is simultaneously expelled into the gaps between adjacent ice columns and gradually templated. The ice crystals are removed by freeze drying to afford a 3D ceramic scaffold with vertically aligned, ordered, continuous channels. The infiltration of the second phase during the preparation of a 3D porous ceramic scaffold using the ice templating method can result in an alternately arranged layered composite material, which appreciably enhances the mechanical properties of the composite. However, high-pressure infiltration under relatively high scaffold strength is required during the infiltration process; otherwise, the applied high pressure could cause SiC layers in the scaffold to fracture [32], which is particularly serious for SiC scaffolds with low solid loadings. However, pressureless infiltration is simple, cost-effective, and more suitable for low-strength ceramic scaffolds, as it is associated with almost no risk of damage or shape restrictions. In contrast, pressure infiltration requires special equipment and expensive tools, resulting in high production costs. Accordingly, Shaga et al*. [29] used ice templating and pressureless infiltration methods to fabricate layered interpenetrating Al–Si–Mg/SiC composite materials. A clear layered structure was evident in the SiC scaffolds with solid contents of 20 and 30 vol%, whereas this structure was less pronounced in the 40 vol% SiC scaffold. In the longitudinal direction, the sample containing 30 vol% SiC exhibited the highest compressive strength (722±35 MPa), approximately 180 times and twice that of the porous SiC scaffold and alloy matrix, respectively.

Furthermore, the wettability between the second phase and the matrix material is an important factor affecting the permeability and interfacial bonding quality of a composite material, which can directly affect its performance. Guo *et al*. [81] investigated the wettability of molten ZL107 (Al–7Si–5Cu) in layered TiC scaffolds and found that Reaction (2) occurred over a prolonged period:

(2) 4Al+3TiC↔3[Ti]+Al4C3

The wetting behavior of the alloy on the TiC matrix changes from non-wetting to wetting, which highlights the rationality and feasibility of material selection during the preparation of this composite material. However, excessive formation of the brittle and hydrolyzable Al4C3 phase can lead to a composite material with inferior mechanical properties. Consequently, pressure infiltration is typically used to reduce the interaction time between the liquid and solid phases.

Additionally, a study conducted by Guo *et al*. [27] found that dendritic features 1–5 μm high were formed on one side of each sheet, as shown in Figs. 13(i)–13(n). The formation of these surface dendrites can be attributed to ceramic particles trapped in the channels between the converging secondary fronts of the ice pocket. The formation of these surface dendrites appears to depend only slightly on the initial solid loading. More TiC particles are pushed into regions between the ice cell branches in slurries with high solid loadings to form ceramic bridges that connect adjacent ceramic sheets. The formation of these surface dendrites and ceramic bridges not only increases the interfacial bonding area but also enhances the sliding resistance between adjacent sheets after the metal fills the pores, thereby playing an important role in reinforcing the material [82].

#### 3.2.3 Other templating methods

In addition to biological and ice templating methods, several scholars have used other templating methods to fabricate anisotropic carbide ceramics. For example, Sacks *et al*. [83] used the template grain growth method and added low concentrations of α-SiC thin slices to induce preferentially oriented SiC growth. They successfully synthesized bulk SiC materials with highly anisotropic properties, as shown in Figs. 15(a)–15(d). The grains almost grew along the (006) crystal face at a heat-treatment temperature of 2250 °C, with the peak intensity ratio of (006)/(110) crystalface determined to be 18.6 at this point. Remyamol *et al*. [84] first fabricated graphite/SiC composites of the required sizes and shapes by hot pressing and then prepared porous SiC materials with anisotropic properties by controlled heat treatment to remove the graphite template. The materials exhibited superior mechanical properties parallel to the pressing direction, and the sample with a porosity of 42% exhibited flexural and compressive strengths of 76–80 and 271–275 MPa, respectively; however, the material exhibited an inferior TC, with a value of 16 W/(m·K) determined at 30 °C. Poor mechanical properties were evident perpendicular to the pressing direction, with flexural and compressive strengths of 17–18 and 82–86 MPa, respectively; however, its TC was high, with a value of 29–30 W/(m·K) determined at 30 °C. Additionally, the thermal shock resistance of the material was analyzed in two directions at different water-quenching temperatures. The strength reduction after thermal cycling was greater in the direction parallel to the pressing direction, owing to the higher thermal stress resulting from the lower TC parallel to the pressing direction. The specimens had fewer pores infiltrated by the quenching medium in a direction perpendicular to the pressing direction, which could have led to more cracks and reduced strength. The quenched specimens did not exhibit any notable change in strength with increasing number of quenching cycles, demonstrating the potential of porous SiC for use in high-temperature cycling applications.

Different templating methods have their advantages; however, they can also have drawbacks. For example, wood templates exhibit excellent cell structures with a wide range of pore sizes. However, cumbersome pyrolysis treatment, complex and time-consuming precursor synthesis, and resulting environmental pollution have driven researchers to seek alternative materials and manufacturing methods that are cost-effective, energy-efficient, and environmentally friendly [29]. Ice templating is a simple, effective, and environmentally friendly method for fabricating porous materials. However, the anisotropy factor of a 3D filler network fabricated using the ice templating method can be limited, as the pore channels exhibit poor long-range continuity and are bridged by numerous horizontal branches in the vertical direction. Moreover, forming dense channel walls through physical compression can be difficult, often resulting in structural defects that inevitably weaken the TC [85].

### 3.3 Anisotropy induced by 3D printing

3D printing technology is a manufacturing method based on digital models that allows the creation of complex geometric shapes and internal structures using the layer-by-layer stacking of materials. It encompasses various techniques, such as digital light processing (DLP) [87−89], direct ink writing (DIW) [19], laminated object manufacturing (LOM) [22], selective laser sintering (SLS) [90], laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) [91,92], and fused deposition modeling (FDM) techniques, among others. 3D printing technology enables the design and direct fabrication of complex parts [93], providing considerable advantages in the rapid construction of intricate 3D structures and shapes. Bioinspired structures are artificial structures designed to mimic and apply the extraordinary performance of biological forms, structures, and functions evident in nature [94].

Consequently, 3D printing technology allows customized design and manufacturing that is consistent with the concept of bioinspired design. Using it, we can simulate and achieve the structural and morphological features of biological organisms, thereby realizing similar functionality and capabilities [28]. For example, by controlling the distribution and arrangement of materials during the printing process, the strength and lightweight properties similar to those of skeletal structures can be achieved. Additionally, by controlling the porosity and pore distribution during the printing process, excellent energy absorption and toughness can be achieved, similar to those of skeletal structures [95]. This combination can be used in various fields, such as medical devices, aerospace, architecture, and material science applications, to provide innovative and efficient solutions.

The connection between 3D printing technology and bioinspired structures lies in the flexibility and freedom afforded by 3D printing technology, which enables the design and fabrication of bioinspired structures with exceptional functionality and capabilities similar to those of biological organisms, making 3D printing one of the most promising design and construction methods [13,96]. However, owing to its unique manufacturing process, 3D printing can introduce differences in the mechanical properties of printed parts along the *Z*-axis and *Y*-plane. This inherent anisotropy can occur in the 3D printing of isotropic materials and should be avoided during the fabrication process. Therefore, this section focuses on the anisotropic characteristics caused by the process or the experimental design itself rather than discussing the inherent anisotropy.

#### 3.3.1 DIW

Under the action of the shear force at the nozzle tip, the fibers in the slurry are not randomly oriented but are aligned along the extrusion direction, as shown in Figs. 16(a)–16(e) [19,97,98]. Similar to the oriented arrangement of fibers in wood, this high-aspect-ratio filler alignment in the extrusion direction can effectively enhance the mechanical performance along a specific direction and improve the toughness. Hmeidat *et al.* [97] investigated the influence of filler morphology, nozzle size, and printing speed on the mechanical anisotropy of printed products. They found that the mechanical properties of composite materials with anisotropic fillers varied inversely with the nozzle size and directly with the printing speed. Using nanoclay (NC) platelets as an anisotropic filler, SiC/NC-doped epoxy-resin composites printed with a 609-μm nozzle size at 40 mm/s exhibited bending strengths of 215.7±3.13 MPa in the printing direction and 93.3±4.84 MPa vertically. This demonstrated the potential of DIW processes to manufacture structures of high toughness and strength, attributable to the fiber alignment facilitated by DIW technology

Kemp et al.* [99] pointed out that the short carbon-fiber content could affect the degree of fiber orientation in the ink. As the fiber content in the ink increased, the interfiber interactions increased, which weakened the preferential alignment of the fibers along the printing direction. When the fiber content increased from 5 to 10 wt%, the degree of orientation in the printing direction decreased by 40%. To improve the mechanical performance of one-dimensional nanomaterials, Zhu *et al.* [100] used DIW to fabricate SiC nanofiber scaffolds. By optimizing the nozzle size, SiC nanofibers were uniformly distributed in the scaffold, with a Lotgering orientation factor of 0.956 along the *f*(111) direction.

In addition to short carbon fibers, continuous carbon fibers have been used for DIW printing. For example, Li *et al.* [101] used DIW techniques combined with precursor infiltration and pyrolysis to fabricate continuous carbon fiber-reinforced SiC-based composites and investigated the influence of various parameters on the quality of continuous fiber-reinforced SiC preforms. The results showed that the addition of short carbon fibers to the SiC slurry further improved the strength and toughness of the continuous-fiber-reinforced SiC composites. Through precursor infiltration and pyrolysis, the interfiber spacing was reduced, achieving a bending strength of 146±10 MPa and a fracture toughness of 3.77 MPa·m1/2.

There is ongoing research on the development of 3D printed human tissue structures [102,103], although the relatively poor interlayer strength of 3D printing has limited its further use. Recently, new 3D printing systems based on industrial robotic systems have been developed by integrating a print head with a robotic arm to overcome the limitations of the layer-based printing process. Theoretically, these robot-based 3D printing systems can freely deposit materials in 3D space, avoiding interlayer degradation and leveraging the superior functionality of carbon fiber-reinforced polymer composites along the fiber direction. This method shows enormous promise for the fabrication of bioinspired human tissue structures with integrated shapes and functionality.

#### 3.3.2 FDM

Studies on FDM techniques for fabricating bioinspired anisotropic materials have been reported; however, the materials selected are mostly organic polymers, and very few studies have focused on fabricating bioinspired anisotropic ceramic materials using the FDM method. Here, we introduce the design concepts and technical methods for 3D printing of bioinspired anisotropic materials using examples of other material systems.

Similar to DIW, the nozzle-based structure of FDM has been widely applied in the material design of bioinspired structures. For example, Sai *et al.* [104] developed a bioinspired continuous-fiber-reinforced SiC-based composite using a novel dual-material 3D printing method (Figs. 17(a) and 17(b)), in which two inks were extruded alternately through a *Y*-shaped coaxial nozzle (Fig. 17(c)). This enabled controllable variation in the SiC solid content, which promoted a bioinspired gradient change of the continuous fibers within the matrix. This specific structural design enhanced the bending strength and fracture toughness by increasing the crack propagation resistance and path, resulting in a stepwise fracture pattern. Moreover, this bionic configuration improved the dielectric loss and electrical conductivity loss, with a minimum reflection loss of −24.3 dB at an effective absorption band of 3.89 GHz.

Ufodike et al.* [105] proposed a parameterized design that mimicked the graded cellular structure of bamboo. Compared with traditional honeycomb structures, FDM-fabricated bamboo-inspired structures could absorb four times more impact energy. The proposed design was inspired by the multilayered composite structure of the turtle shell, with its functionally graded porosity, which helps to absorb mechanical impacts and serves as a lightweight armor for the turtle. Naik *et al.* [106] adopted FDM technology to fabricate samples with multiple infill patterns, with 20% infill density samples printed in the edge direction exhibiting the highest impact strength and impact strength/weight ratio.

Moreover, researchers have introduced aligned short Cf into materials to markedly improve their performance. For example, Liao *et al.* [107] added 10 wt% aligned short Cf to polyamide 12 (PA12), which increased its tensile and flexural strengths by 102.2% and 251.1%, respectively. Tekinalp *et al.* [108] incorporated short fibers into acrylonitrile–butadiene–styrene composites, and the nozzle effect led to a highly oriented fiber distribution (91.5%), which improved the tensile modulus and strength by ~115% and ~700%, respectively.

#### 3.3.3 SLA

SLA refers to the selective curing of a liquid composed of light-polymerizable prepolymers (oligomers), light-polymerizable monomers, and photoinitiators using a light source of a certain wavelength (usually ultraviolet light). Through layer-by-layer curing, a 3D part is constructed. When particles with an extremely high response to the internal magnetic field of a reactive resin are used, the orientation of the particles can be manipulated by controlling the magnetic field [13,109,110]. Consequently, complex bioinspired materials with controllable reinforcement orientations can be manufactured using SLA.

Martin *et al.* [111] combined real-time colloidal assembly with existing additive manufacturing technologies to create highly programmable discontinuous fiber composites. Different bioinspired microstructures were created by dividing the voxels in each printed layer into groups with similar reinforcement directions and subjecting them to magnetic field alignment before photocuring (Figs. 18(a)–18(i)).

Moreover, the combination of SLA with machine learning and computational simulations is a commonly used method for fabricating fine bioinspired structures. Jia et al.* [34] revealed the relationship between the stress modulation function and irregularity of bioinspired building materials using a generative computational framework. This framework assembled irregular materials with heterogeneous, disordered microstructures by optimizing the spatial distribution of a set of basic building blocks and exhibited spatially varying characteristics that could precisely modulate the stress distribution in various control regions and load conditions, echoing the powerful stress modulation capability of natural materials.

Currently, there are no reports on the SLA-based fabrication of bioinspired anisotropic carbide-based composites. However, Tang *et al.* [115] coated Cf, used Cf, SiC, and photosensitive resin to fabricate Cf/SiC green bodies, and through silicon infiltration, obtained a composite material with a strength of 250.71 MPa and a toughness of 3.46 MPa·m1/2, where Cf was randomly distributed. By magnetically modifying Cf and exerting an external magnetic field during the SLA process, directional control of Cf/SiC could be achieved, further improving the performance of the material.

#### 3.3.4 Other 3D printing techniques

In addition to DIW, FDM, and SLA techniques commonly used to fabricate bioinspired anisotropic materials, researchers have employed other 3D printing methods. Le Ferrand *et al.* [116] used a combination of water-based slip casting and magnetic alignment to create oriented alumina-based bioinspired heterogeneous composites. They used a magnetic field to control the direction and distribution of the anisotropic building blocks, gradually changing the angle between the applied magnetic field and the mold to fabricate a cake with the desired particle orientation. Remarkably, the pitch of the resulting architectures closely followed the values predicted by correlating the periodic change in the magnetic field direction with the growth kinetics of the jamming front. The periodic structure conceptually resembled the fiber plywood architecture found in several biological materials, including fish scales, plant cell walls, lobster exoskeletons, and stomatopod dactyl clubs. Polozov *et al*. [117] prepared samples via selective laser melting (SLM) using Ti–22Al–25Nb/10 vol% SiC as a raw material. Different regions of the samples were processed using SLM at varying energy densities. A laser energy density of 56 J/mm3 was applied to the bottom region, whereas 370 J/mm3 was applied to the top region. The resulting sample is shown in Fig. 19(a).

Zhang et al*. The 3D printing process is shown in Fig. 19(c), and the silicon infiltration process is shown in Fig. 19(d). The short-cut carbon fibers in the slurry were preferentially oriented along the *Y* direction during extrusion because of the influence of shear forces, endowing the material with anisotropic properties. The material exhibited different mechanical properties when a load was applied parallel or perpendicular to the *Y* direction, exhibiting a maximum flexural strength of 300 MPa at a solid loading rate of 34.68 vol% with a shrinkage rate of less than 2%, thereby achieving a nearly net-shaped SiC ceramic component.

Although 3D printing technology has been used to fabricate bioinspired anisotropic carbide-based composite materials, some problems remain to be addressed.

(1) The application of 3D printing technology in the manufacturing of ceramic materials is limited, and many traditional ceramic raw materials cannot be printed directly [28].

(2) Polymers are currently irreplaceable raw materials for bioinspired anisotropic 3D printing, and 3D printing of anisotropic ceramic composites—particularly carbide ceramic composites—requires further development [119].

(3) Current 3D-printed products have poor surface quality and accuracy. Post-processing after printing and high-temperature sintering increases the overall production costs [19].

(4) Specialized structural designs may be constrained by 3D printing equipment, and modifications to it may be necessary to conduct experiments [111].

Overall, owing to its unique fabrication method, 3D printing allows control of the spatial deposition of materials, adjusting the local chemical composition, and constructing complex microstructures in the preparation of bioinspired anisotropic materials [13]. Currently, research on bioinspired anisotropic carbide ceramics is relatively limited, and 3D printing of bioinspired anisotropic carbide ceramic composites is still in its infancy. However, certain 3D printing techniques have been used to manufacture highly precise and customizable complex structures of anisotropic carbide ceramic composites [104,120]. With the continuous advances in materials science and manufacturing technology, it is believed that the 3D printing of bioinspired anisotropic carbide ceramic materials will mature.

### 3.4 Other processing methods that induce anisotropy

In addition to the commonly used methods for preparing anisotropic carbide ceramic composites, alternative approaches have been explored. Li *et al*. [3] adopted a top-down fabrication method in which SiC ceramics were pulse-laser-processed after hot-press sintering to fabricate biomimetic vein-fractal-branching structures. Based on the different microchannel spacings, this design was categorized into sparse (left of Fig. 20(a)) and dense branching (right of Fig. 20(a)) fractal structures. The fractal-branching structure considerably enhanced the grinding performance of the material. Moreover, the influence of the fractal-branching structure on the grinding force became more pronounced with decreasing microchannel spacing, resulting in materials with improved grinding performance.

Various methods can be used to prepare anisotropic carbide-based composites. Owing to differences in the preparation conditions and process parameters, the performance of anisotropic carbide ceramic materials produced by different methods varies. Nonetheless, the structures and properties of these materials can be regulated by controlling the preparation process and material parameters. These anisotropic carbide ceramic materials exhibit outstanding performance and offer new potential applications in many fields. With the continued development of preparation technologies, anisotropic carbide ceramic materials can be expected to become widely used to achieve further breakthroughs.

## 4 Current challenges and future development

This review starts with the introduction of several natural biomaterials that exhibit anisotropic properties and complex microstructures [35,36]. Compared with individual components alone, combinations of different components and the unique layered structures of these biological materials endow them with superior performance in specific directions [124,125]. Consequently, we consider various biomimetic anisotropic carbide ceramics and carbide ceramic composites developed by researchers in recent years as examples, shifting the focus toward the preparation of anisotropic carbide ceramics and carbide ceramic composites with detailed experimental principles and recent research progress using strong external forces, templating methods, and 3D printing technology. Various methods have been discussed, including hot working under uniaxial pressure, magnetic alignment-based casting technologies, biotemplating and ice templating, DIW, FDM, and SLA methods, among other preparation techniques. (1) The mechanisms associated with some common biological materials, such as interfacial interactions between pearl material layers, remain unclear [28−31].

(2) The use of strong external forces to fabricate anisotropic carbide ceramics requires strict and demanding external conditions, which can lead to high production costs [5,24].

(3) Template-based methods for preparing anisotropic carbide ceramic composites are limited by template selectivity. For example, the choice of biological template used during biotemplating can appreciably affect material performance. Additionally, methods such as biotemplating and ice templating are commonly used at the laboratory scale but face scale-up challenges in mass production [75].

(4) 3D printing technologies for anisotropic carbide ceramic composites are expensive and limited in terms of achieving high precision, along with the complexities associated with the use of preceramics or polymer-derived ceramics [22,120].

(5) Despite the excellent prospects of anisotropic carbide ceramic materials for practical applications, engineering considerations must address complex forces and structural issues. Moreover, the long-term stability and durability of these materials under various environmental conditions must be considered [126].

In the near future, bioinspired anisotropic materials can be expected to become mainstream options for the development of anisotropic carbide-based composite materials, with continuous progress being made in machine learning applications. By tailoring the structural units of metamaterial designs, such as tetrahedrons and octahedrons, and adjusting the size, orientation, and combination of each unit, such methods can be used to precisely control the mechanical properties of materials [34]. Furthermore, as the understanding of biological structures progresses, direct 3D printing of biomimetic structures through structural simulation and 3D slicing also promises to be an exciting trend for bioinspired anisotropic carbide-based composite materials. Additionally, with the development of the four-dimensional (4D) printing of complex ceramic structures, 4D printing for preparing biomimetic materials with excellent performance also holds enormous potential for future development [130].

As a promising class of advanced materials, bioinspired anisotropic carbide ceramics continue to shed new light on scientific research and engineering applications. This not only reflects the performance optimization of materials but also includes innovations in manufacturing processes, structural design, and bionic simulation.

## 5 Conclusions

Biological materials with unique microstructures exhibit properties that far exceed those of their traditional counterparts. Anisotropic carbide ceramic composites with excellent properties can be fabricated by designing textured structures inspired by biological materials. This review introduces the common techniques for manufacturing anisotropic carbide ceramic composites, including densification under strong external fields, template-based approaches, and 3D printing technologies.

Among these fabrication methods, 3D printing is one of the most promising methods for producing bioinspired materials, as it allows for precise structural design and compositional control. Although research on the 3D printing of bioinspired polymeric materials has been conducted, the development of 3D-printed anisotropic carbide ceramic composites is still in its infancy. Anisotropic carbide ceramic composites show enormous potential for future applications, which merits further investigation. Moreover, the assistance of computational simulations alongside innovative manufacturing technologies should facilitate rapid breakthroughs toward strategic industrial and military purposes.
