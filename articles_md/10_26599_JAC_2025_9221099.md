## 1 Introduction

Since global climate change emerged as a critical concern for the international community, significant efforts have been directed toward achieving a sustainable and low-carbon world [1−6]. Innovative power generation systems utilizing cleaner energy resources, such as hydrogen and solar energy, are increasingly gaining widespread attention. Among them, gas turbine technology, which has been widely adopted because of its high efficiency, reliability, and flexibility, is now being explored for further environmental benefits. Specifically, replacing traditional fuels such as natural gas with hydrogen-rich alternatives (e.g., methane blends, syngas, or even pure hydrogen) represents a promising strategy to significantly reduce and even eliminate CO2 emissions [7−9]. However, during the operation of hydrogen-fueled gas turbines, the introduction of hydrogen and the consequent increase in steam content pose significant challenges for the material design of gas turbines, particularly for thermal barrier coating (TBC) systems. TBCs are typically applied to the surface of Ni-based superalloys to shield these hot-section components from intense heat [10−12]. A standard TBC system comprises three layers: a ceramic top coat, a thermally grown oxide layer (TGO), and a bond coat [12,13]. For hydrogen-fueled gas turbines, the surface temperatures of turbine blades are expected to rise significantly, necessitating the development of advanced TBCs. This is primarily due to the higher flame temperature of hydrogen than that of conventional hydrocarbon fuels (e.g., CH4) and the increased steam concentration in the exhaust gas, which increases the heat transfer coefficient between the hot gas and the components [14,15]. In recent years, great efforts have been made to develop advanced TBCs with new ceramic top coats that are capable of withstanding higher operation temperatures, including the renovation of ZrO2-based materials and identification of alternative materials, such as pyrochlores, perovskite oxides, yttrium aluminum garnets, and rare-earth aluminates [16−22].

However, another critical issue appears to be neglected. The increased content of hydrogen in fuels and the consequently higher water content in the exhaust gas can both facilitate hydrogen dissolution into the ceramic top coat. Wagner [23] investigated the relationship between hydrogen solubility in zirconia–yttria solid solutions and water partial pressure and reported that the former was proportional to the square root of the latter. There are two possible reactions that describe dissolution (Reactions (1) and (2)) [24]:

(1) H2(g)+2OOX=2OHO⋅+2e′

(2) H2O(g)+VO⋅⋅+OOX=2OHO⋅

The dissolved hydrogen has been suggested to have a negative effect on the microstructure and phase stability of oxides [25−28]. Moreover, hydrogen is likely to reach the metallic bond coat, where hydrogen embrittlement may occur [29,30]. A study on the oxide behavior of MCrAlY coatings (M = Ni, Co) revealed that the presence of both hydrogen and water vapor in the atmosphere can lead to severe degradation under high-temperature conditions [31]. This report indicates that the co-existence of hydrogen and water is devastating to the bond coat of TBCs. Therefore, the ceramic top coat in TBCs for hydrogen-fueled gas turbines must possess specific hydrogen permeation resistance. Investigating the hydrogen migration behavior in existing TBCs is essential for understanding and mitigating TBC degradation, as well as guiding the design of next-generation TBCs for hydrogen-fueled gas turbines.

Yttria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia (T-YSZ) remains the most widely applied ceramic top coat because of its high melting temperature, low thermal conductivity and good mechanical properties [32,33]. Additionally, in pyrochlore/YSZ multilayer TBCs, YSZ serves as a diffusion barrier between TGO and rare-earth zirconate pyrochlores, which confront the problem of poor chemical stability when encountering Al2O3 from TGO [34,35]. Owing to the dominance of T-YSZ in TBCs, it is necessary to systemically investigate the evolution process and assess its mechanical properties, thermal properties, phase stability, and hydrogen permeation resistance in the complex hydrogen and steam environments of hydrogen-fueled gas turbines. Understanding the local configuration and diffusion mechanism of H defects in TBCs is the first and fundamental step for the above evaluation. Previous theoretical calculations have studied the configurations, energies, and migration of H defects (H0, H−, and particularly H+) in ZrO2-based materials [36−42]. Marinopoulos *et al.* [39] investigated the incorporation of isolated hydrogen in 10.3 mol% YSZ using *ab* *initio* calculations and suggested that hydrogen was an amphoteric impurity with equilibrium charge-transition levels. Haurat *et al.* [40] focused on the interactions of hydrogen with some point defects in monoclinic ZrO2 and calculated the concentrations of various H defects by a thermodynamical model. Nevertheless, these studies are related mainly to solid oxide fuel cells and zirconium alloy corrosion, with a focus on the cubic and monoclinic phases. To the best of our knowledge, studies on H defects in the tetragonal phase, which is the focus of TBCs, are rare [43]. The unique behavior of hydrogen in the tetragonal phase has not yet been studied, and the underlying mechanism remains to be revealed.

In this work, atom-scale modeling of H defects in T-ZrO2 and T-YSZ using first-principles calculations is performed. A vivid picture about the behavior of H defects has been gained through the calculation of the configuration, formation energy, and migration. The roles of lattice tetragonality and Y–oxygen vacancy (VO)–Y triple in the proton configuration and migration are revealed. This work is expected to shed light on the mutual interactions between hydrogen and TBCs and offers atom-scale insight into the application of YSZ in hydrogen-fueled gas turbines.

## 2 Computational details

DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) code within the generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) and the projector augmented wave (PAW) methods [44,45]. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof for solids (PBEsol) potential was employed for the description of the exchange-correlation functional [46]. A cut-off energy of 480 eV for the plane wave basis set and a Monkhorst–Park 2 × 2 × 2 *k*-mesh for the Brillouin-zone integration were used. Our tests for the cut-off energy indicate that the adopted cut-off energy was sufficient. A further increase in the cut-off energy led to an energy change of less than 0.001 eV/atom. A denser 5 × 5 × 5 *k*-mesh was used for the electronic structure calculations. The minimum energy structure, p-band center, and electron locational function (ELF) were obtained through these calculations. Semicore states (4s and 4p electrons) were included as valence electrons for Y and Zr atoms. Structural optimization was pursued until the force acting on each atom was less than 1×10−2 eV/Å and the energy difference was within 1×10−6 eV/atom. To calculate the migration energies and the associated minimum energy pathway (MEP), the climbing image-nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method as implemented in VASP was applied [47]. Convergence criteria were set as a total energy of 1.0×10−6 eV/atom and a Hellmann–Fynman force of 0.1 eV/Å.

The defect formation energy is an important parameter that expresses the stability of defects. In this work, the H defects with different charge states (H−, H0, and H+) were studied. The defect formation energy *E*f(Hq) is a function of the charge and electron chemical potential according to Eq. (3):

(3) Ef(Hq)=Etot(Hq−bulk)−Etot(bulk)−μH+q(EF+EVBM)+Ecorrect

where *E*tot(Hq-bulk*)* is the total energy of the supercell containing a H atom and *E*tot(bulk) is the total energy of the bulk-crystal supercell; *µ*H is the chemical potential of hydrogen and is taken as half the total energy of the hydrogen molecule; *E*VBM is the energy level corresponding to the valence band maximum (VBM; *E*F is the electron chemical potential (also called the Fermi level); *E*correct is the first-order correction to the energy caused by an excess charge [48]. The thermodynamic transition level between charge states q and q' is also called the charge transition. *E*(q/q') is the value of the electron chemical potential, which makes the formation energies of the two charge states q and q' equal.

Based on the bond valence theory, the bond valence sum of the oxygen atoms was calculated via the python package Python Materials Genomics (pymatgen) [49]. The p-band center (*ε*p) of oxygen was calculated via Eq. (4):

(4) εp=∫−∞+∞EPDOS(E)dE∫−∞+∞PDOS(E)dE

where PDOS is the projected density of states obtained by the VASPKIT code [50].

### 3.1 Modeling of crystal structures

The tetragonal zirconia (T-ZrO2) lattice is in *P*42/*nmc* space group and is generally assumed to be a distorted version of the CaF2-type face-centered cubic (fcc) lattice [51]. As shown in Fig. 1(a), a 2 × 2 × 2 fcc-like supercell containing 96 atoms was used in the T-ZrO2 calculations. For the T-ZrO2 structure, there are three structural parameters, i.e., *a* (the lattice size in the *x* and *y* directions), *c* (the lattice size in the *z* direction), and *d**z* (*d**z* is the internal parameter that describes the displacement of oxygen atoms from the ideal CaF2 lattice, *d**z* = Δ*c*/*c*, where Δ*c* is the displacement of the oxygen atom in the unit cell of T-ZrO2). The calculated structural parameters together with the experimental data of T-ZrO2 are all listed in Table 1. The calculated lattice constants are in good agreement with the experimental values, with the deviation of 1.4% for *a* and 1.8% for *c*. The parameter *d**z* is relatively smaller than the experimental value, which is also observed in other calculations for T-ZrO2. In the relaxed T-ZrO2 supercell, two Zr atoms are replaced by Y atoms, while one O atom is removed to maintain electrical neutrality and form an oxygen vacancy (VO), as shown in Fig. 1(b). The relative position between the Y atom and VO has been controversial for a long time. In this work, Y atoms are placed at the position of the second nearest neighbors of VO according to Refs. [52,53]. To consider the doping of Y atoms, the supercell volume of T-YSZ and the lattice parameters are refined by fitting the free energy–volume (*F*–*V*) curve with the third-order Birch–Murnaghan equation of states (EOS) [54]. Moreover, as displayed in Figs. 1(c)–1(f), the T-ZrO2 structure can also be seen as the periodic repetition of a cage-like structure, [Zr6O8], containing six Zr atoms and eight O atoms. The [Zr6O8] cage is composed of two distinct surfaces, namely, surface A and surface B. In T-ZrO2, the O atom is fourfold coordinated, and the lengths of long and short Zr–O bonds are 2.36 and 2.08 Å, respectively.

To explore the configuration spaces in T-ZrO2 and T-YSZ sufficiently, the initial position of the H atom must be carefully considered. Barbour *et al*. [37] placed initial H atoms in T-ZrO2 and T-YSZ structures according to a mesh with regularly spaced possible sites. Mantz and Gemmen [41] took another method, in which the initial H atoms were placed along O–O vectors, to explore the potential proton configurations. These O–O vectors begin at the O atoms that are expected to bond to H atoms and end at surrounding O atoms within 3.83 Å. The initial O–H bond length is chosen to be 1.00 Å. The configurations involved in the previous studies focused on H defects in the monoclinic and cubic ZrO2 ceramics can be divided into two categories: (1) the interstitial configuration and (2) the bond configuration [42,43,57]. The two configurations have been modeled by using muonium as a pseudoisotope in muon-spin spectroscopy experiments [58]. In this work, a hydrogen atom is directly placed at the center of the [Zr6O8] cage to obtain interstitial configurations. For the bond configurations, we adopt the method described by Mantz and Gemmen [41].

### 3.2 Configurations and formation energies of H defects in T-ZrO<sub>2</sub>

Configurations and formation energies are essential for understanding the behavior of H defects in ZrO2 ceramics. The H defects with three charge states, i.e., H0, H−, and H+, in T-ZrO2 are all considered. H− and H0 can stably occupy the interstitial position of the Zr octahedron, namely, the center of the [Zr6O8] cage, while H+ and H0 combine with neighboring O to form bond configurations, as displayed in Fig. 2. The Zr–O bond lengths (the four Zr–O bonds around each O atom are considered), O–H distances, and displacements of O atoms are listed in Table 2. When H− and H0 interstitial defects are located in the center of [Zr6O8] cage, the eight O atoms will differentiate into two categories. Among them, the four O atoms (O1, O2, O5, and O6) initially near the center would move away from the center of the [Zr6O8] cage by approximately 0.1 Å, while the other four O atoms (O3, O4, O7, and O8) initially far from the center and the six Zr atoms remain nearly constant. The interstitial configuration of T-ZrO2+H− is similar to that of T-ZrO2+H0, but the impact of H− on the crystal lattice is slightly more significant. When H0 or H+ bonds to an O atom, the bond configuration forms, and the eight O atoms in the [Zr6O8] cage will differentiate into six categories. As shown in Fig. 2(b), O1, the bonded O atom, has the largest displacement (approximately 0.58 Å) moving close to the center and becomes away from one Zr atom, which potentially leads to the breaking of this long Zr–O bond. O1 and the other three Zr atoms are roughly in the same plane, which is perpendicular to the O–H bond. Thus, O1 can be seen as a 3-coordinated O atom, and a pyramid-like local configuration forms, as shown in the close-up of Fig. 2(b). This phenomenon has also been reported in previous theoretical studies [59,60] concerning tetragonal SnO2 and TiO2. Moreover, in the bond configuration, H0 or H+ always appears along the direction of long Zr–O bond vectors. It is at a distance of approximately 1 Å from the bonded O atom. The Zr–O bond length seems to be the vital factor determining the orientation of O–H bond, namely, the bonding site. Here, a “long bond” criterion is proposed to describe the feature of the orientation of the O–H bond in T-ZrO2. The definition of the “long bond” criterion is that the long Zr–O bond is easier to stretch or break than the short one, and O atom tends to relax along long Zr–O bonds, ultimately forming the bond configuration. This feature consequently leads to the H atom only choosing to bond to the four O atoms (O1, O2, O5, and O6 in Fig. 2(b)) near the center of the [Zr6O8] cage but not to bond to the four O atoms (O3, O4, O7, and O8) far from the center when the H atom is located in this [Zr6O8] cage. This is because only the bonding sites of the former are located in this cage.

To determine the thermodynamic stability of H defects in T-ZrO2, their formation energies as a function of electron chemical potential were calculated, as shown in Fig. 3. When the electron chemical potential moves through the band gap, the stable charge state changes directly from positive to negative, and the neutral state is never the lowest-energy state. Such behavior can be described by the effective *U* parameter, which is defined as *U = E*f(H+) *+ E* (H−) *−* 2*E*f(H0) [61]. In this work, the energy diagram shows that H defects in T-ZrO2 exhibit a negative-*U* characteristic, with a *U* value of −2.42 eV. This finding reveals that H0 is not thermodynamically stable in T-ZrO2. As an amphoteric defect, hydrogen can exist as H− and H+, depending on the value of electron chemical potential. This behavior of hydrogen defects has also been observed in cubic YSZ and monoclinic ZrO2 [37,39]. Our calculated thermodynamic transition level (+/−) for hydrogen defects is 2.72 eV, which is consistent with the results reported by Barbour *et al*. [37] and Youssef and Yildiz [43].

In the actual environment, the hydrogen partial pressure also affects the formation energy. According to the following thermodynamic equation (Eq. (5)), the formation energies (μ*(*T*, *P*)) at different temperatures (*T*) and partial pressures (*P*) can be calculated:

(5) μ(T,P)=μo+Δμ(T,Po)+kBTln⁡(PPo)

where *µ*o is the chemical potential of the hydrogen molecule at 0 K; *P*o is the total pressure (usually taken as 1 atm); *k*B is the Boltzmann constant. Combining Eqs. (3) and (5), the temperature- and hydrogen partial pressure-dependent formation energies of H defects in T-ZrO2 can be calculated, as shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The relevant thermodynamic quantities used in the calculation come from NIST–JANAF thermochemical tables [62]. As shown in Fig. S1 in the ESM, as the temperature increases, the formation energy increases and becomes more sensitive to the hydrogen partial pressure.

### 3.3 Configurations and formation energies of H defects in T-YSZ

The configurations and formation energies of H0, H−, and H+ defects in T-YSZ are further explored. The [Zr6O8] cages may no longer be perfect because of the existence of Y atoms and VO, and for simplicity, this name is retained. The centers (C) of 32 [Zr6O8] cages in the T-YSZ supercell can be roughly divided into four categories according to their distances to VO, namely, four C1NNs, twelve C2NNs, twelve C3NNs, and four C4NNs (1NN, 2NN, 3NN, and 4NN represent the first, second, third, and fourth nearest neighbors of VO, respectively). One single H atom is placed at the positions of VO and all the [Zr6O8] cage centers to explore the potential interstitial configuration of H defects. According to the results of structural optimization, these positions are proven not to be occupied by H+. In other words, H+ will not form the interstitial configuration in T-YSZ. H0 and H− can stay at the positions of VO, C2NN, C3NN, and C4NN and are found to eventually move into VO when initially placed at C1NN. This result suggests that VO strongly attracts H0 and H− defects. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the formation energies of H0 and H− interstitial rconfigurations in the T-YSZ supercell are calculated. The interstitial configurations of H0 or H− defects located in the VO both have the lowest formation energies, meaning that the VO attract H0 and H−. As the distance from H0 and H− to VO increases, their formation energies gradually increase until they reach a certain value. The reason is that the local environment of farther cage centers in T-YSZ is similar to that of T-ZrO2, and the formation energies of H0 and H+ in these centers should be similar to those in the pure phase.

The study on bond configurations in T-YSZ is never trivial because O atoms are not equivalent. In fact, the Y–VO–Y triple has the most important influence on the lattice distortion, which changes the local environment of the oxygen atoms surrounding it. The saturation of the oxygen valence state, which is influenced by the coordination environment, is calculated using bond valence theory to estimate its proton affinity [63,64]. As shown in Fig. 5, the six O1NNs (the first nearest O atoms to Vo) show the obvious undersaturation of the valence (|bond valence sum (BVS)| 2 supercell containing one VO, there are three kinds of O1NN, containing four A-type O1NNs, one B1-type O1NN, and one B2-type O1NN, due to the characteristics of tetragonal phase, as displayed in Fig. 6(a). A-type O1NN should be located at the same oxygen layer in cubic ZrO2 and separate slightly because of tetragonal lattice distortion. B1-type O1NN, B2-type O1NN, and VO have the same coordinates of (x*, *y*), so they are at a line along the *c*-axis. From VO to B1-type O1NN, it is necessary to pass through a Zr atomic layer and an O atomic layer in sequence. In contrast, from VO to B2-type O1NN, an O atomic layer and a Zr atomic layer need to go through in turn. The difference between B1-type and B2-type O1NN is also reflected in the variation in the Zr–O bond length. For B1-type O1NN, the long Zr–O bond shrinks, and the short Zr–O bond elongates due to the attraction of VO to O1NN. In the case of B2-type O1NN, such a situation is the opposite.

### 3.4 Oxygen p-band center analysis and ELF analysis of T-ZrO<sub>2</sub> and T-YSZ

Because H defects are more likely to enter the YSZ lattice in the form of protons (H+) [24], subsequent research will focus on protons. In this section, we calculate the p-band centers of O atoms to explore the dependence of the proton formation energy on p-band centers, while ELF analysis gives an explanation for the trapping effect of VO on protons from the perspective of electrons. Refs. [65,66] have reported that the p-band center of O atoms relative to the valence band maximum (VBM) is a sensitive and reliable descriptor that can represent the proton preference for certain O atoms in proton-conducting materials such as BaFeO3 and BaZrO3. The p-band centers of 63 O atoms in T-YSZ supercell are calculated, and the correlation coefficient, *R* = 0.78, between the p-band center energy and the proton formation energy is obtained through linear fitting, as shown in Fig. 8. These results indicate that the p-band centers of the six O1NNs with relatively low proton formation energies are closer to the VBM and the correlation coefficient between the p-band center energy and the proton formation energy is high. Therefore, the p-band center of a specific O atom is an important local characteristic affecting the proton formation energy.

Although the bond configuration of ZrO2+H+ has been reported [37,39−41], there is still a lack of explanation for the underlying mechanism of O atom displacement during the O–H bond formation and the trapping effect of VO on protons. Since positions with high electron density are preferred by protons, the electron redistribution following the displacement of O atoms may be the real factor determining the change in bonding site [67]. The results of ELF calculation are shown in Fig. 9. As shown in Fig. 9(a), the Zr–O bonds show the mixed bond characteristics with ionic and covalent bonds. Specifically, the electrons are neither completely transferred from zirconium to oxygen nor completely localized between the Zr–O bonds. Compared with the long Zr–O bond, the lowest ELF value in the area of the short Zr–O bond is greater, which indicates the greater covalency of the short Zr–O bond and thus the difficulty of stretching. When a proton bonds to an O atom in T-ZrO2, as shown in Fig. 9(b), the corresponding long Zr–O bond is stretched. Owing to the existence of protons in Fig. 9(b), it is hard to perceive the effect of large displacement of O atom on its surrounding electron distribution. We perform an extra ELF calculation using the structure obtained by deleting the H atom in Fig. 9(b) and fixing remaining atoms. The results are shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. The large displacement of O atoms can effectively redistribute the electron density. More electrons are localized at the bonding site, and a local environment, which is preferred by protons, is formed accompanying the formation of 3-coordinated O atoms. VO can also cause obvious displacement of A-type O1NN, further inducing electron redistribution. As shown in Figs. 9(c) and 9(d), protons are found to only occupy the bonding sites with high electron density. Now, two explanations for the trapping effect of VO on protons are reached: (1) the existence of VO leads to O1NN’s p-band center closer to the VBM and thus a lower formation energy; (2) the existence of VO leads to the electron redistribution and thus a decrease in the number of bonding sites, which will have a deep impact on the proton migration and will be studied in Section 3.5.

### 3.5 Proton migration behavior in T-ZrO<sub>2</sub> and T-YSZ

A well-known classification method for proton migration in oxides has been described in Ref. [68]. If a proton hops from an initially bonded O atom to a neighboring O atom, forming a new O–H bond, it is referred to as a hop. If only the reorientation of O–H bond occurs and the proton still bonds to the same O atom after migration, it is referred to as reorientation. However, this general classification of oxides fails to capture the characteristics of the crystal structure. Unlike previous articles which took this classification [37,42], the migration paths in this work are divided into two categories (intracage-type and intercage-type) based on whether a proton passes through the surface of the [Zr6O8] cage to understand the role of lattice tetragonality in proton migration.

Four migration paths have been carefully determined as the basic paths for proton migration in T-ZrO2, and all other paths can be seen as the combinations of these paths. Next, the role of isolated Y atom and Y–Vo–Y triple is investigated. As shown in Figs. 10(a) and 10(b), the energy profiles of the S1 and S2 paths (intracage-type migration) have similar unimodal shape, but there is a significant difference (approximately 0.22 eV) in the numerical values. Another significant difference between the S1 and S2 paths is the relative positions of Oi and Of. In the S1 path, Oi and Of are at the same O layer, while they are at different O layers in the S2 path. As shown in Figs. 10(c) and 10(d), protons migrate between [Zr6O8] cages through surface A in the S3 path and through surface B in the S4 path. Regardless of the intercage-type migration path, the energies of the transition states decrease and reach a local minimum value as the proton gradually approaches the surface. When a proton leaves the surface, the energies of the transition states increase briefly. This phenomenon is more significant in the S3 path; thus, the double peaks are more obvious in the S3 path. Notably, for the S3 path, the energy of the transition state is very low and close to the energy of the lowest-energy bond configuration when the proton is precisely located on the surface. This special configuration is also observed in some relaxed structures during the process of searching for a bond configuration. In this configuration, the proton bonds to the other O atom on surface A, the original O–H bond is broken, and the newly formed O–H ion is along the [001] direction. As shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM, the charge density distribution of the saddle point structure confirms the formation of new O–H bonds. This transient configuration can be seen as an action of collaboration between the two O atoms on surface A to allow proton passage. Because it is not the lowest-energy configuration, this second-lowest-energy configuration is taken as a transition state that occurs in the S3 path instead of another stable bond configuration.

In YSZ, the Y atom always accompanies the existence of VO. The effect of Y atom alone on proton migration is difficult to demonstrate by experiments. Therefore, an isolated Y atom was first introduced near the four migration paths to investigate proton migration without the cooperation of VO. For the S1 and S2 paths, the effect of isolated Y atoms on the energy barriers is almost negligible except for the situation of S2–Y–OYO, whose energy barrier increases by approximately 0.1 eV. For the S3 and S4 paths, a relatively large change is observed halfway through the energy profile. From the perspective of energy, if a Y atom appears on surface A, it would impede the proton migration. However, the Y atom would promote the proton migration if it appears on surface B.

The proton migration in the T-YSZ supercell was subsequently investigated. To study the migration behavior of protons near the VO, three representative migration paths are chosen and referred to as near-vacancy paths, as shown in Fig. 11(a) (N1: proton hopping from A-type O1NN to A-type O1NN; N2: proton hopping from A-type O1NN to B2-type O1NN; N3: the reorientation of protons in B2-type O1NN). We find that the energy barrier of N3 is much larger than those of N1 and N2. As mentioned above, when a proton reorients from one bonding site of a specific O atom to another, i.e., crossing surface A, it needs the collaboration of another O atom on surface A. However, no other O atom but a VO exists in N3. The electrostatic repulsion between protons and VO may also explain the increase in the energy barrier. According to the type of O1NN (A-type O1NN or B2-type O1NN), three or two representative paths are chosen to study the migration behavior of protons away from the VO and are referred to as escape–vacancy paths, as shown in Figs. 11(b) and 11(c) (these more distant O atoms in E1, E2, E3, E4, and E5 follow a selection rule: having a bonding site which is as close as possible to the bonding site of O1NN). Notably, the difference between the E2 path and the E3 path results from O–H bond reorientation. Their common initially bonded O atom is an A-type O1NN, which has only one bonding site located in the left cage in Fig. 11(b). Therefore, in the E2 path, the proton moves in the left [Zr6O8] cage, while in the E3 path, the proton first moves from the left cage to the right cage and then moves in the right cage. From the curves of NEB, it is clear that protons tend to move close to VO rather than leave VO because of the asymmetry of energy barriers. E1, E3, and E5, as intercage-type paths, are the most sensitive to the existence of VO. Compared with those of the corresponding original paths, their energy barriers are greater for T-ZrO2 (E1 and E5 vs. S4, E3 vs. S3+S2). E2 and E4, as intracage-type paths, are affected to a limited degree by VO (E2 vs. S1, and E4 vs. S2).

As shown in Fig. 12(a), the formation energy and migration energy of point defects in different oxides are collected for comparison [69−71]. Clearly, compared with those of oxygen-related defects, the formation energy and migration energy of protons are both lower in T-ZrO2, indicating greater activity of the latter with a lattice and even comparable to those of the proton-conducting oxide BaZrO3. The introduction of the Y–Vo–Y triple can increase the migration energy but lower the formation energy. This enhances the hydrogen permeation resistance but has a trapping effect on protons, which may cause potential local hydrogen accumulation around the Y–Vo–Y triple. The schematic movement modes of the protons are shown in Figs. 12(b) and 12(c), and the proton migration anisotropy behavior in T-ZrO2 is predicted. At low temperatures, paths with low energy barriers (S2 and S3 paths) are preferentially activated. Notably, based solely on these two paths, protons can shuttle only between [Zr6O8] cages, which have the same coordinates along the c* axis. The proton is confined to two O atom layers perpendicular to the *c* axis and hops back and forth. Only at high temperatures can the energy barrier of S4 path be overcome, and with the combination of S2 and S4 paths, the macroscopic diffusion of protons along the *c* axis can be realized. Therefore, the proton migration along the *c* axis is more difficult than that along the *ab* plane, and the origin of the proton migration anisotropy can be explained by the four proposed basic migration paths and be attributed to lattice tetragonality. To the best of our knowledge, no relevant experiments have reported proton migration anisotropy in tetragonal ZrO2 ceramics. Youn’s experiment [72] may be referable, which was designed to capture the proton migration anisotropy in hexagonal perovskite-related oxides and was further explained using CI-NEB methods. In T-YSZ, an isolated Y atom has a limited influence on proton migration, and the influence is highly dependent on the path and position of Y atom. However, the Y–Vo–Y triple can effectively impede proton migration. For example, some paths closely related to the macroscopic diffusion of protons along the *c*-axis are more sensitive to VO, and their energy barriers are greatly increased. As the concentration of VO increases, the proton migration anisotropy in the tetragonal phase may be enhanced.

## 4 Conclusions

The formation energies and configurations of mono-hydrogen defects and proton migration in T-ZrO2 and T-YSZ were systematically studied via first-principle calculations. The conclusions are as follows.

(1) Hydrogen is an amphoteric defect in T-ZrO2. H− or H+ alternately becomes thermodynamically stable when the electron chemical potential changes. Depending on the charge, H defects form interstitial configurations (H− and H0) or bond configurations (H0 and H+).

(2) The structural origin of the O–H bond orientation in the bond configuration is attributed to the lattice tetragonality, which causes the differentiation of the Zr–O bond length and further influences the electron distribution surrounding O atoms.

(3) The crystal structure of T-ZrO2 is divided into [Zr6O8] cages. Two intracage-type and two intercage-type proton migration paths are studied. Proton migration along the *ab*-plane is easier than that along the *c*-axis.

(4) The existence of the Y–Vo–Y triple elevates the p-band center of the O atoms neighboring it, and a higher p-band center of the O atom tends to lead to lower proton formation energy and greater proton affinity.

(5) A single Y atom has a very limited effect on proton migration, while the Y–VO–Y triple can effectively impede proton migration. The local lattice distortion induced by VO intensifies the O–H bond orientation of A-type O1NN, resulting in the disappearance of bonding sites and hindering proton migration.

Two strategies are expected to enhance the hydrogen permeation resistance of T-YSZ: (1) suitably increasing the concentration of VO to increase the trapping effect of VO on hydrogen and (2) leveraging the proton migration anisotropy by regulating the overall grain orientation. However, it should be noted with caution that there is still a gap between its proton migration energy and that of Al2O3 (a common hydrogen permeation barrier material) when YSZ is applied in hydrogen-fueled gas turbines. This investigation not only offers a deeper understanding of H-defect behavior in T-ZrO2 and T-YSZ but also may shed much light on further optimization of TBCs for hydrogen-fueled gas turbines.
