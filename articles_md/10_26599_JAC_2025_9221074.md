## 1 Introduction

ZrB2 is recognized as a promising material for ultrahigh-temperature structural components used in hypersonic vehicles, atmospheric re-entry, and rocket propulsion [1−4]. To satisfy the demands for load-bearing and reliability in these applications, ZrB2-based ceramics must have excellent mechanical properties, especially strength and toughness [5,6]. Owing to their high sensitivity to defects, ZrB2-based ceramics need to be prepared with low porosity to ensure these mechanical properties. However, high temperatures and long sintering time are usually required to densify ZrB2-based ceramics, which can cause significant grain coarsening and thus deteriorate their mechanical properties.

Suppression of grain coarsening during the densification process is crucial for improving the mechanical properties of ceramics. Hot pressing (HP) is a widely used technique for the densification of ZrB2-based ceramics [7]. During the HP process, high temperature and applied pressure help rearrange the ceramic particles and reduce porosity, which enables the preparation of fully dense ceramics [8]. However, the HP densification process typically involves ultra-high sintering temperatures and long processing time, which cause significant grain growth and compromised mechanical properties. For example, ZrB2-based ceramics prepared by HP at 2100 °C have a grain size of 35.5 μm, a flexural strength of 337 MPa, and a fracture toughness of 2.7 MPa·m1/2 [9]. In contrast, the application of spark plasma sintering (SPS), along with the addition of ZrC and SiC, allows for the preparation of ZrB2-based ceramics with a smaller grain size of 2 μm at 2100 °C. Under these conditions, the flexural strength and fracture toughness can reach 723 MPa and 3.5 MPa·m1/2, respectively [10]. In addition, the use of sintering techniques with ultrafast heating rates can help inhibit grain growth during high-temperature sintering [11,12]. The overall sintering time can be significantly reduced, which facilitates the production of fine grains [13,14]. Flash sintering (FS) and ultrafast high-temperature sintering (UHS) can achieve heating rates of ~102–104 °C/min [15−20], which allows ceramics to be sintered in just a few minutes [18−20]. However, the effectiveness of flash sintering depends on the specific properties of the materials used [13,14]. Notably, ZrB2 ceramics exhibit a high current/voltage threshold, which presents challenges for successful flash sintering. Recently, UHS has been used to prepare various ZrB2-based ceramics, including ZrB2–WC, ZrB2–B4C, and ZrC–B4C [11,21−23]. In the UHS process, a green ceramic body is positioned between two carbon strips or graphite felts to facilitate rapid heating through direct current Joule heating. However, these flexible carbon strips and graphite felts cannot provide pressure to the sample, which means that the sintering of ceramics is primarily conducted without pressure. The densification of ceramics during the UHS process primarily depends on mass transfer and grain coarsening, which often results in closed pores within the final material. Therefore, the ultrafast preparation of dense ZrB2-based ceramics remains a challenge.

Texturized microstructures also help improve the mechanical properties of ceramics. The strength of ZrB2 is orientation dependent, with the highest theoretical strength occurring along the *a*-axis [24]. Therefore, the mechanical properties of ceramics can be enhanced by aligning ZrB2 grains. For example, the *c*-axis of ZrB2 grains in ZrB2-based ceramics can be aligned parallel to the pressure direction by hot forging techniques [25]. This alignment results in an increase in the overall strength of the ceramics by 30% in the plane perpendicular to the pressure direction and 15% in the plane parallel to the pressure direction. Additionally, ZrB2 grains can also be aligned using strong magnetic fields. *c*-axis-aligned ZrB2-based ceramics can be prepared by slip casting of the raw powder in a strong magnetic field [26]. The results indicate that the hardness is greater on the surface perpendicular to the grain alignment. Furthermore, studies have shown that optimally oriented ZrB2 grains can form a self-toughening structure, which further enhances both the strength and toughness [27−29]. However, the preparation of textured ceramics often involves complex procedures and long processing time.

Electric fields have been shown to modify the microstructure of materials [30]. In metallic materials, grains have been observed to be optimally aligned under the influence of a direct current (DC) electric field and Joule heating, facilitating the formation of textured microstructures, particularly in regions of high current density [31−35]. Specifically, DC can induce a transition of grain boundaries from disordered to ordered [30]. During the SPS process, the applied pulsed DC enhances mass transport by changing the defect concentration and improving the mobility of the defects, which allows for rapid densification of materials. However, the direction of the imposed current has a weak effect on the growth of materials [36]. Reports indicate that even at temperatures as high as 2100 °C, conventionally, SPS-sintered ZrB2-based ceramics show low levels of texturization [37−42]. This limitation can be attributed to the relatively low current density and the intermittent nature of the pulsed current. The intermittent nature of the pulsed current alters the electric field, temperature field, and kinetic conditions of atomic diffusion, thereby weakening the driving force for grain orientation growth and alignment. In contrast, the use of heavy continuous DC, which provides a stable driving force involving electromigration effects in addition to Joule heating, may enable ultrafast sintering of textured ZrB2-based ceramics.

In this study, we introduce a sintering route that combines heavy continuous DC Joule heating with pressing for the ultrafast fabrication of ZrB2-based ceramics. As designed, heavy continuous DC Joule heating can achieve an ultrafast heating rate (with a maximum heating rate exceeding 1000 °C/min), while pressing helps rearrange ceramic powder and reduce porosity. Consequently, dense ZrB2-based ceramics can be successfully sintered at 1500–1900 °C within just a few minutes. Moreover, the ZrB2 grains can align optimally to create a bonded network, which effectively promotes the synergistic strengthening and toughening of the ceramics. The methodology outlined in this study is expected to be applied to other ultrahigh-temperature ceramics and promote the development of high-performance materials.

## 2 Materials and methods

ZrB2 (purity > 99%, particle size 200–500 nm, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China), ZrC (purity > 98.5%, particle size 200–400 nm, Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China), and SiC (> 99.9%, 200 nm, Beijing Huawei Ruike Chemical Co., Ltd., China) were used as the raw materials. The raw materials were uniformly mixed in the specified proportions (80 vol% ZrB2–10 vol% ZrC–10 vol% SiC) using a ball milling process (XQM-1, Changsha Tianchuang Powder Technology Co., Ltd., China). The milling employed a WC–Co jar with WC–Co balls, operating at 250 r/min for 12 h. The weight ratio of ball to powder was 10 : 1. Anhydrous ethanol served as the milling media. The mixed powder slurry was dried at 60 °C for 6 h under vacuum condition. The resulting dried agglomerated powder and WC–Co balls were then vibrated on a shaking table in air for 20 min for pre-oxidation. This pre-oxidation treatment allows the nanopowder to carry oxygen, which contributes to the formation of low melting point phases during the subsequent sintering process. Finally, the crushed powder was sieved through a 200-mesh screen.

The powder mixtures were placed in a graphite mold and densified through heavy continuous DC Joule heating and pressing (HDCP) under vacuum condition (10−1 Pa). Figure 1(a) shows the schematic of HDCP. The graphite mold is composed of three parts: the outermost graphite mold, which supports the sample during pressurized sintering; the middle BN liner, which prevents the electric current from passing through the outer graphite mold; the inner graphite liner, which enables rapid heating of the sample under heavy continuous DC. The pressure and continuous DC were supplied to the samples via two graphite rods, where the graphite rods were connected to a pressure system and a DC power supply (6000 A, 12 V). The temperature of the sample was monitored using a two-color infrared thermometer (STRONG-SR-7025WL, 700–2500 °C, Changzhou Sijie Optoelectronics Technology Co. Ltd., China) through a channel created by drilling into the sintering mold. The distance between the location of the temperature measurement point and the sample is ~2 mm.

*
Fig. 1(a) Schematic of heavy continuous DC Joule heating and pressing; (b, c) typical sintering profiles; (d) heating and cooling rates.

During the HDCP process, initially, a pressure of 50 MPa was applied and maintained throughout the sintering process. Subsequently, a continuous DC with a current density of 1250 A/cm2 was applied for rapid heating. Upon reaching the target temperature, the current was manually adjusted to maintain the target temperature for 3 min. Following the holding period, the DC power was shut off, and the pressure was released. The sample was then cooled down naturally to room temperature. Figures 1(b) and 1(c) illustrate typical sintering profiles throughout the HDCP process, and Fig. 1(d) depicts the heating and cooling rates. The HDCP facilitates a maximum heating rate exceeding 1000 °C/min, enabling a sample to reach 1900 °C within less than 2 min. At the end of the holding period, the applied pressure was removed. Note that unloading-related deviations in the temperature measurement position and manual relocation of the temperature measurement position lead to sudden changes in the measured temperature values. ZrB2-based ceramics sintered at 1500, 1600, 1800, and 1900 °C for 3 min via HDCP were thereafter labeled HDCP-1500, HDCP-1600, HDCP-1800, and HDCP-1900, respectively. For comparison, ZrB2-based ceramics were sintered via conventional HP at 1500 °C for 15 min and at 1500, 1800, and 1900 °C for 60 min and labeled HP-1500, HP-1500-1h, HP-1800-1h and HP-1900-1h, respectively. In both the HDCP and HP processes, identical molds and an equal amount of ceramic powder were utilized.

The oxygen content of the powder was detected by an oxygen analyzer (ONH836, LECO, USA). The bulk density and open porosity of the as-sintered ceramics were measured by Archimedes’ method. An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Ultima IV, Rigaku Corporation, Japan) was used to analyze the phase composition at a 2θ* scanning rate of 3 (°)/min. For microstructure characterization, the samples were polished to mirror-finished surfaces with 0.5 μm diamond particles. The surface topography and microstructure were analyzed via a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; JSM-7610F Plus, JEOL, Japan) equipped with an energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS; Ultim Max, Oxford, UK). The fracture morphology of a 45° sample was characterized. Further microstructure characterization and phase composition analysis were conducted on a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200X G2, FEI, USA). The samples for TEM observation were prepared using a focused ion beam (SEM-FIB; Helios G4 CX, FEI, USA).

Flexural strength and fracture toughness tests were carried out on a mechanical testing system (WDW-20S, Shandong Lisu Test Equipment Co., China) at room temperature. Test bars were cut from sintered bulk ceramics using electrical discharge machining (EDM), followed by grinding and polishing to a mirror finish using diamond sandpaper. The four long edges of each test sample were chamfered. The machining direction was aligned with the long axis of the test samples. Each test was repeated at least 3 times. Flexural strength testing was performed using 3-pt bending on samples that were nominally 20 mm × 2.5 mm × 2 mm in size at a speed of 0.5 mm/min over an 18 mm span. Fracture toughness testing was performed by the single-edge notched beam (SENB) method on samples that were nominally 20 mm × 2 mm × 4 mm in size with a 2 mm-deep notch at a speed of 0.05 mm/min over a 16 mm span.

### 3.2 Phase composition and textural characteristics of ZrB2-based ceramics via HDCP

Figure 3 shows the XRD results of ZrB2-based ceramics sintered via HDCP at various temperatures. Figures 3(a) and 3(b) show the XRD patterns detected on the top surface (T plane) and side surface (S plane) of the as-sintered samples, respectively. The T plane and S plane are perpendicular and parallel to the pressing direction, respectively. The XRD patterns of the ball-milled mixed powder are presented for comparison. Figure 3(a) shows that the diffraction peaks corresponding to ZrB2 (PDF#04-002-1160), ZrC (PDF#04-004-2559), and SiC (PDF#97-016-4972) phases are observed in ball-milled mixed powder. Upon high-temperature sintering (Figs. 3(a) and 3(b)), most of the obvious diffraction peaks correspond to ZrB2. The diffraction peaks of ZrC phases completely disappear, and the intensity of the peaks corresponding to SiC decreases. At sintering temperatures of 1800 and 1900 °C, some small diffraction peaks corresponding to ZrO2 and SiO2 phases were detected (Figs. 3(c) and 3(d)). The complex oxidation products may exist in an amorphous state at moderate temperatures but can crystallize at higher temperatures.

A comparison of the relative peak intensity with the standard PDF card of ZrB2 (PDF#04-002-1160) reveals that there was no preferred orientation for the ZrB2 phase within the mixed powder. However, for the as-sintered bulk ceramics, the XRD peaks corresponding to the (001) and (101) planes of ZrB2 on the top surface exhibit a reduction in intensity, while the diffraction peaks of the (100) planes of ZrB2 display an increase in intensity. Conversely, the diffraction peaks of the (100) planes of ZrB2 on the side surface exhibit relatively weak intensities. These findings indicate that the ZrB2 phases within the sintered samples exhibit a preferred orientation, specifically with the [001] direction preferentially aligned perpendicular to the pressing direction. Additionally, this preferred alignment is enhanced as the sintering temperature increases. The texture coefficient (TC) can be used to quantify this preferred orientation, as expressed by Eq. (1) [43]:

(1) TC(hkl)=Ihkl/Irhkl1N∑(Ihkl/Irhkl)

where  Ihkl is the peak intensity corresponding to the (hkl) plane of the ZrB2 phase within the as-sintered sample;  Irhkl denotes the peak intensity corresponding to the (hkl) plane on the standard PDF card for ZrB2; N* is the number of diffraction peaks considered. The calculated results are shown in Figs. 3(e) and 3(f). The TC(hkl) value of 1 represents the non-preferred orientation for the (hkl) plane. A TC(hkl) value larger than 1 indicates a preferred orientation. Conversely, a TC(hkl) value less than 1 indicates an opposite or less preferred orientation for the (hkl) plane.

Figures 3(e) and 3(f) clearly shows that the TC(hkl) values for the mixed powder are close to 1, suggesting a non-preferred orientation. After HDCP, the ZrB2 phase within the sintered samples exhibits a significant texture. For the T plane of the sample (Fig. 3(e)), the TC(100), TC(110), and TC(200) values are all greater than 1, and those values continue to increase with increasing sintering temperature. Notably, the TC(100) value is quite large (1.76) for the sample sintered at 1900 °C. Conversely, the TC(001), TC(101), and TC(002) values are smaller than 1, with particularly low values of 0.2, 0.65, and 0.23, respectively, for the 1900 °C sintered sample, indicating strongly less preferred orientation for the (001), (101), and (002) planes. The TC(hkl) values for the S plane of the sample reveal an opposite trend (Fig. 3(f)). These results suggest that the *c*-axis of ZrB2 tends to be perpendicular to the pressing direction (which is also the current direction) and the texturization of ZrB2 increases with increasing sintering temperature. The schematic of ZrB2 growth direction is illustrated at the bottom of the XRD pattern.

### 3.3 Mcrostructure of ZrB2-based ceramics via HDCP

Figure 4 shows SEM images of the top surfaces (T plane) of the as-sintered ZrB2-based ceramics. Figures 5(a)–5(h) show backscatter electron images (BSEs) of the top surfaces (T plane) of samples. The SEM and BSE images of the side surfaces (S plane) are not present, as no significant difference seems to be observed between the morphological characteristics of the T plane and S plane. Previously reported results also revealed this phenomenon [26]. This phenomenon can be attributed to the lack of remarkable elongation of the ZrB2 grains. From the polished surfaces and fracture surfaces of HDCP**-**sintered samples in the temperature range of 1500–1900 °C (Figs. 4(a)–4(l)), a few nanoscale pores can be observed in Figs. 4(j) and 4(l). The porosities of the samples were measured using a graphical method based on SEM and BSE images, as shown in Table 2. Specifically, the porosity of the HDCP-1500 sample was measured at 2.95%. Notably, at temperatures of 1600 °C and above, the porosity of the samples decreased to less than 1%.

During conventional hot pressing, it is generally accepted that oxides on the particle surface limit the densification of ZrB2-based ceramics. The slow heating process allows for the sufficient removal of gaseous species, which is crucial for effective densification [55]. As the temperature increases and oxides are removed, the densification of HP-sintered ZrB2-based ceramics improves. Figures 9(a)–9(d) show SEM images of HP-sintered ZrB2-based ceramics. The porosities of HP-1500-1h and HP-1900-1h samples are 16.89% and 5.37%, respectively. B2O3 is believed to promote grain coarsening in ZrB2-based ceramics through evaporation–condensation kinetics. The trade-off between grain coarsening and densification leads to pore residues.

Faster heating rates may shift the evaporation temperature upward, thereby delaying the oxide elimination process [57]. Note that B2O3, SiO2, and ZrO2 would form a ternary glassy phase [44]. Hu et al*. [58] reported that a transient liquid phase would commonly present in the sintering process to promote the phase formation, grain growth, and densification processes. In HDCP-sintered ceramics, electronically transparent phases (EP phases) containing B, Si, Zr, and O elements coexist with carbon. As shown in Fig. 7(e), these EP phases fill the ZrB2 grains of HDCP-1500. The EP phases contained stripe-shaped species, as indicated by the dashed ellipses. By etching these EP phases with NaOH, the striped-shaped species were exposed, as shown in Figs. 9(f) and 9(g). EDS analysis (Fig. 9(h)) indicated that these strip-shaped species primarily consisted of carbon, potentially containing small amounts of ZrC. As the temperature increases to 1800 °C, it is anticipated that B2O3, Si, and SiO evaporate. ZrO2 is expected to precipitate from the EP phase, whereas Si-containing species preferentially react with C to form SiC, as illustrated in Figs. 9(i) and 9(j). Upon increasing the sintering temperature to 1900 °C, the majority of the low melting point species were consumed, whereas the precipitation of ZrO2 and the production of SiC increased, as shown in Figs. 9(k) and 9(l).

In conventional sintering processes, carbon is typically added to convert ZrO2 to ZrC. The reaction between ZrO2 and carbon is particularly thermodynamically favorable above 1650 °C. The coexistence of carbon and EP phases and ZrO2 in the rapid sintering process may result from the reaction not being fully completed within the shorter processing time. Similar findings were reported in SPS-sintered ZrB2–ZrC–SiC samples [37]. Figure 10 shows the TEM microstructure of HDCP-1900, confirming that the ceramic sintered at 1900 °C is characterized by a primary composition of ZrB2, ZrO2, and SiC with some residual carbon. Based on the phase composition measured using the imaging method (Table 2), the theoretical density of the ceramics is calculated to be 5.65 g/cm3 according to the rule of mixtures. Given a bulk density of 5.62 g/cm3 measured using Archimedes’ method, the relative density of HDCP-1900 is found to be 99.4%.

In summary, rapid sintering can delay the oxide elimination process, and oxides can form transition liquid phases to promote densification. #### 3.4.2 Texturization mechanism

Figure 9 shows the XRD results for the ZrB2-based ceramics sintered via HP at various temperatures. No significant difference in phase species is observed between ceramics sintered by HP and those sintered by HDCP. However, a notable difference in the diffraction intensity of the ZrB2 crystal planes is observed, indicating a change in the ZrB2 grain orientation. The TC parameters on both the T plane and S plane of the hot-pressed samples are approximately 1, similar to those of the mixed powder (Figs. 11(c) and 11(d)). SEM images (Figs. 9(a)–9(d)) reveal equiaxed ZrB2 grains. This lack of texturization in ZrB2 has also been reported in other studies involving hot-pressed ZrB2–ZrC–SiC ceramics [37,44,60−62]. It appears that higher sintering temperatures, exceeding 2000 °C, are necessary to promote the texturing of ZrB2 [61]. These results are in sharp contrast to those of the samples sintered via DC Joule heating, which exhibit strong deviations in TC(001) and TC(100).

*
Fig. 11XRD patterns of bulk ceramics sintered by HP at different temperatures: (a) top surface (T plane) and (b) side surface (S plane). Different sintering temperatures are marked. Texture coefficients of each crystal plane of ZrB2 in ceramics: (c) T plane and (d) S plane.

Numerous studies have reported current-induced texturization of microstructures [31−35]. Specifically, the grains tend to be optimally aligned in a direction that minimizes energy, such as toward the direction of reduced electrical resistance [34]. For example, in body-centered tetragonal (t) Sn, the resistivity is lower in the [100] and [010] directions than in the [001] direction. Consequently, the [001] direction tends to be perpendicular to the direction of the current [32]. Similarly, for hexagonal crystals, the c*-axis is expected to be perpendicular to the current, as the resistivity along the *a*- or *b*-axis is lower than that along the *c*-axis. Deng *et al*. [63] and Zhang *et al*. [43] reported that hexagonal crystals preferentially grew in the direction of high conductivity (*a*- or *b*-axis) under the influence of a current, while the *c*-axis aligned perpendicular to the current direction. In contrast, the texturization of ZrB2 was not significant in ZrB2–ZrC–SiC samples sintered by SPS with pulsed current, even at temperatures up to 2100 °C [37−42]. This lack of texturization can be attributed to the relatively low current density and the intermittent nature of the pulsed current, which was inadequate to drive the optimal growth and alignment of ZrB2 grains. Munir *et al*. [36] reported that the direction of pulsed DC had no effect on the growth of intermetallics, in contrast to expectations based on electromigration theory. In contrast, for continuous DC, a steady driving force involving electromigration effects is provided, which may continuously drive atomic migration and thus promote texturization of the material [31−35]. Therefore, we suggest that the texturization of ZrB2 in HDCP is related to heavy continuous DC, and the detailed mechanism requires further investigation.

### 3.5 Mechanical properties

The flexural strength and fracture toughness on the T plane and S plane were measured by means of the 3-pt bending and SENB testing, respectively. Figure 12 shows the mechanical properties on the T plane. Figure 12(a) shows the typical load–displacement curves of the SENB test for samples sintered at different temperatures. Figures 12(b) and 12(c) show SEM images of the samples after the SENB test. From Fig. 12(a), it is apparent that both samples sintered at 1500 and 1600 °C exhibit brittle fracture, accompanied by the rapid load dropping to zero after reaching the peak value. As shown in Fig. 12(b), the samples tend to break into two parts after the SENB test. For samples sintered at and above 1800 °C, the loads decrease slowly after dropping to approximately 10 N, indicating a trailing phenomenon (marked by the red dashed rectangle). This tailing phenomenon was caused by mechanical meshing and friction between the fractured surfaces. Such behavior is commonly observed in fiber-reinforced composites, and mechanisms such as fiber pull-out and fiber bridging are activated to dissipate energy, leading to a reduction in the crack propagation rate [64]. This phenomenon appears to be unusual for intrinsically brittle monolithic ceramics. As shown in Fig. 12(c), the samples do not fracture into two pieces after the SENB test, and the crack tips blunt and blurd, which might be attributed to the ZrB2-bonded network. The toughening mechanism is discussed in the next section.

*
Fig. 12Mechanical properties on T plane: (a) Load–displacement curves obtained from SENB tests, (b, c) SEM images showing fracture behavior of SENB bars, (d) flexural strength and fracture toughness, and (e) data for ZrB2-based ceramics [9,10,40,66−69,71,72].

Figure 12(d) shows the flexural strength and fracture toughness on the T plane with the tensile surface of the test bars perpendicular to the pressing direction. The flexural strength and fracture toughness exhibit the same trend as the sintering temperature increases from 1500 to 1900 °C. The flexural strength and fracture toughness on the T plane are 605±87 MPa and 4.49±0.26 MPa·m1/2, respectively, at sintering temperature of 1500 °C. The mechanical properties decrease at a sintering temperature of 1600 °C, which might be attributed to the balance between the growth of grains and the increase in texturization. As the sintering temperature increases from 1600 to 1900 °C, the flexural strength and fracture toughness on the T plane gradually increase to 773±114 MPa and 5.88±0.08 MPa·m1/2, respectively.

The mechanical properties on the S plane were also evaluated. The flexural strength and fracture toughness on the S plane are 570±10 MPa and 4.41±0.56 MPa·m1/2 for HDCP-1500 and 528±53 MPa and 5.25±0.07 MPa·m1/2 for HDCP-1900, respectively. Microcantilever bending experiments indicate that the arrangement orientation of the ZrB2 grains has an important effect on the relaxation of the concentrated stress at the crack tip [65]. The relatively lower mechanical properties of the sample on the S plane could be related to the texturised microstructure [29].

For ZrB2-based ceramics sintered by HP, the flexural strength and fracture toughness on the S plane are ~320 MPa and ~2.7 MPa·m1/2 for HP-1500-1h, and ~590 MPa and ~4 MPa·m1/2 for HP-1900-1h, respectively. These values were lower than those of the samples prepared using HDCP (Fig. 11(d)).

Figure 12(e) shows a comparison on the mechanical properties of our samples and those of previously reported ZrB2-based ceramics fabricated by HP, SPS and reactive spark plasma sintering (RSPS) [9,10,40,66−70]. The fracture toughness and bending strength of monolithic ZrB2 ceramics prepared via the HP process are ~350 MPa and ~3 MPa·m1/2 [9]. The use of SPS or RSPS to shorten the sintering time and the addition of second phases can inhibit the grain growth to obtain fine grains, resulting in improved mechanical properties [10,40,66,67,70].

ZrO2 has been reported to enhance the properties of ZrB2 through the t→m phase transformation [71,72]. For example, the flexural strength and fracture toughness of ZrB2–10 vol% SiC–10 vol% ZrO2 can reach 771 MPa and 6.4 MPa·m1/2, respectively. However, as the sintering temperature increases from 1750 to 1950 °C, the amount of t-ZrO2 retained at room temperature decreases significantly. During fracture, the content of the t→m phase transformation decreases to 1.35% [72]. Consequently, the flexural strength and fracture toughness of the ceramics decline to 532 MPa and 4.5 MPa·m1/2, respectively. In this study, the volume fraction of m-ZrO2 in HDCP-1900 is determined to be 94% of the total volume of ZrO2 by applying the formula of Zhang et al*. [72] based on the diffraction intensities of m-ZrO2 on the ( 1¯11) and (111) crystal planes and t-ZrO2 on the (101) crystal plane observed in the XRD results (Fig. 3(d)). As shown in Table 2, the total volume fraction of ZrO2 in HDCP-1900 is 8.01%. Therefore, the content of t-ZrO2 in HDCP-1900 is approximately 0.5%. This low content of t-ZrO2 has a limited contribution to the mechanical properties. Thus, we propose that the improved mechanical properties of HDCP-1900 are attributed primarily to the formation of a unique microstructure resulting from the texturized polycrystalline ZrB2. Additionally, laminated ZrB2–SiC ceramics exhibit excellent mechanical properties, with a strength and toughness of up to 960 MPa and 8.8 MPa·m1/2, respectively [73]. It is possible to combine the methods employed in this work with other techniques for rapid preparation of high performance ceramics.

As shown in Figs. 13(a) and 13(b), the presence of multiple complex alternating fracture planes is evident in the fracture surface of HDCP-1500. These fracture surfaces give rise to the formation of distinctive features, including mountains, cliffs, and basins. In Fig. 13(c), the terraced fracture surfaces is notablely observed. The terrace area is predominantly composed of ZrB2 grains (Fig. 13(d)). For HDCP-1900, the angle of inclination of the entire fracture plane is greater than that of the loading plane (Fig. 13(e)). To examine the crack extension characteristics, the samples were damaged using the indentation method. A high indentation load of 100 N was selected to highlight the damage resistance of the sample (Fig. 13(i)). As illustrated in Figs. 13(j) and 13(k), ZrB2-bonded networks can induce interlocking effects. ZrB2 grains and their bonded longitudinal species can also bridge cracks. These interlocking and bridging effects were found to be helpful in blunting the main cracks [25,74]. The main crack could propagate in a zig-zag path, and complex cracks could be induced, as presented in Figs. 13(n)–13(p). As a result, multi-stage fracture surfaces could be formed during the failure process. This series of processes is accompanied by the dissipation of a large amount of fracture energy, which ultimately contributes to the improvement in the mechanical properties.

## 4 Conclusions

ZrB2-based ceramics were ultrafastly fabricated by heavy continuous DC Joule heating and pressing route with a maximum heating rate exceeding 1000 °C/min. The microstructure and mechanical properties were investigated. The following conclusions can be drawn:

(1) Ultrafast densification of designed ZrB2-based ceramics was successfully achieved. The densification rate peaked at 1218 °C, and the densification process was nearly complete at a low temperature of 1500 °C. Dense ceramics could be fabricated within short processing time of less than 5 min.

(2) ZrB2 grains underwent selective growth during the sintering process, and textured ceramics were obtained. The degree of texturization increased with increasing temperature. Optimally aligned ZrB2 grains tended to couple together to form ZrB2-bonded networks.

(3) The enhanced densification and texturization of ZrB2-based ceramics are attributed mainly to the applied heavy continuous direct current.

(4) The flexural strength and fracture toughness of ZrB2-based ceramics sintered at 1900 °C were 773±114 MPa and 5.88±0.08 MPa·m1/2, respectively, which were 124% and 103% greater than those obtained through conventional hot pressing at 2200 °C. The synergistic strengthening and toughening of the ceramics can be attributed to the ZrB2-bonded network, which effectively induces multi-stage fracture surfaces during failure.
