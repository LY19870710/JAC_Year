## 1 Introduction

A solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) is a highly efficient and clean advanced energy conversion technology that converts chemical energy directly into electrical energy through electrochemical reactions and can be widely used for distributed and stationary power generation [1,2]. In the practical application process, it is necessary to consider the user’s operational requirements and maintenance demand. This may subject the device to conditions where temperature fluctuations are frequent and pronounced. For example, in residential applications, the SOFC system may undergo frequent cycles of activation and deactivation in accordance with the homeowner’s requirements [3,4]. Furthermore, the temperature of SOFCs may change during operation. For instance, when harnessing electricity from waste heat generated by industrial processes and thermal power plants, SOFC may experience an unstable heat supply. Additionally, substantial diurnal temperature variations in the SOFC operational region or severe meteorological conditions, such as extreme winds and snowfall, can lead to significant temperature fluctuations [5,6]. The thermal expansion coefficient (TEC) of the common air electrode La0.6Sr0.4CoO3−*δ* is approximately 23×10−6 K−1 [7], while that of the common electrolyte 8 mol% yttria stabilized zirconia (YSZ) is approximately 10.9×10−6 K−1 [8]. A mismatch in TEC of different SOFC components can lead to the generation of thermal stresses during temperature fluctuations. These stresses may compromise the integrity of the interfaces between components, thereby degrading the SOFC’s power output [9,10]. Consequently, ensuring thermal cycle stability is a critical challenge that must be addressed to achieve successful commercialization of SOFC technology [11,12].

Ruddlesden‒Popper (R‒P) structured materials have the chemical formula A*n*+1B*n*O3*n*+1, where the A-site is typically occupied by rare-earth or alkaline-earth metals, and the B-site is occupied by transition metals such as Ni, Mn, Cu, and Fe [13]. These materials consist of alternating layers of perovskite layers (ABO3) and rock-salt layers (AO) along the *c*-axis [14,15]. Compared to single perovskite materials, R‒P structured materials can accommodate additional interstitial oxygen (Oi) between the AO rock salt layer and the ABO3 perovskite layer, in addition to the intrinsic lattice oxygen present in the crystal structure [16]. This feature enhances the material’s oxygen surface exchange capacity and reduces its TEC [17,18]. TEC of R‒P structured materials is comparable to that of common electrolytes used in SOFCs, making them promising candidates for SOFC applications [19, 20].

Kharton *et* *al*. [21] investigated TEC of La2Ni1−*x*M*x*O4+*δ* (M = Fe, Co, and Cu; *x* = 0–0.2) under various oxygen partial pressures. The findings indicated that the TEC values for these materials were within the range of 14.4×10−6 to 15.1×10−6 K−1 between 650 and 950 °C. The TEC values are comparable to those of the electrolytes commonly utilized in SOFCs. However, R–P perovskite materials exhibit two-dimensional conduction [22]. They demonstrate significant anisotropy in the diffusion of oxygen ions and electrons, with transport predominantly occurring within the *a*–*b* plane and minimal movement along the *c*-axis [23]. Consequently, there is a need to modify R–P structured materials to enhance their charge transfer capability, thereby increasing their catalytic activity without sacrificing their stability for application in SOFCs.

The most common strategy for increasing the catalytic activity of R–P oxides is introducing a secondary phase [24]. Mechanical mixing is a relatively straightforward method for the introduction of a secondary phase. Sharma *et* *al*. [25] observed a significant reduction in the electrode polarization resistance (*R*p) from 1.00 to 0.03 Ω·cm2 at 700 °C by adding 50 wt% Gd0.1Ce0.9O2−*δ* (GDC) to La3Ni2O7−*δ*. Shirani-Faradonbeh *et* *al*. [26] mixed (Nd0.9La0.1)1.6Sr0.4Ni0.75Cu0.25O4+*δ* (NLSNC4) with Sm0.2Ce0.8O2−*δ* (SDC), and the composite with 50 wt% SDC exhibited the lowest *R*p of 0.12 Ω·cm2. While mechanical mixing can enhance electrode performance to a certain degree, it struggles with achieving a homogeneous distribution of the phases, which in turn restricts the interfacial contact between them. Infiltration is another alternative for introducing second-phase materials. Liu *et* *al*. [27] impregnated La2NiO4+*δ* into a PrBaCo2O5+*δ* skeleton and reduced *R*p from 0.148 to 0.061 Ω·cm2 at 750 °C. Han *et* *al*. [28] infiltrated SDC into a La1.2Sr0.8NiO4+*δ* electrode and reduced *R*p to 0.049 Ω·cm2 at 800 °C. However, this process is cumbersome and time-consuming and requires multiple steps. Moreover, in contrast to doping and mixing techniques, the infiltration method does not allow for direct control over the quantity of the second phase material [29,30]. The one-pot synthesis technique for fabricating composite materials is capable of yielding thermodynamically stable and homogeneously dispersed dual-phase structures in a single, streamlined operation. By merely adjusting the ratios of the starting materials, the incorporation of the second phase can be finely tuned. Furthermore, this one-pot approach holds significant promise for creating numerous heterogeneous structural interfaces within composite air electrodes, which can significantly boost the kinetics of the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) [31,32]. Additionally, this method has the potential to greatly enhance the performance of composite air electrodes by optimizing the ORR process [33,34].

In this work, with the aim of enhancing the catalytic activity of R–P structured oxides while simultaneously preserving their stability, a single perovskite oxide-decorated R–P structured oxide was synthesized by a one-pot method. Precise control over the dual-phase ratio is accomplished by adjusting the initial materials. The single perovskite oxide (La,Sr)FeO3−*δ* (LSF-P), which has a cubic structure and high catalytic activity, was introduced to facilitate charge transport across the R–P-structured oxide (La,Sr)2FeO4+*δ* (LSF-RP) in various orientations. This approach overcomes the inherent structural anisotropy and concurrently enhances the catalytic activity of the composite electrode. The intimate heterointerfaces that may form *in* *situ* between LSF-RP and LSF-P particles are anticipated to expedite the charge transfer process, thereby enhancing the ORR kinetics. We present the influence of the LSF-P content in the dual phase on the phase structure, TEC, electrode reaction kinetics, and single-cell performance under thermal cycling and reversible conditions in detail. The obtained results indicate that the incorporation of LSF-P improves the oxygen surface exchange kinetics, reduces *R*p, and significantly enhances the single-cell performance without sacrificing the stability of the composite electrode. The prepared LSF-RP and LSF-P dual phases show considerable potential as competitive air electrodes for SOFCs.

### 2.1 First-principles calculations

The crystal structures and electronic density of states of LSF-RP and LSF-P were calculated based on density functional theory using CASTEP (Cambridge Sequential Total Energy Package) module in Materials Studio software. In the calculations, the generalized gradient approximation functions generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) and parametric empirical Bayes (PEB) are used, the pseudopotential approach is norm-conserving, the cutoff voltage is set to 330.0 eV, and the electronic minimizers are set to the “All bands/EDFT” type.

### 2.2 Powder synthesis and characterizations

LSF-RP and LSF-P composite powders were synthesized by a citrate‒nitrate combustion process employing ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) and citric acid (CA) as complexing agents. The LSF-RP samples with different molar percentages of LSF-P were designated as La0.8Sr1.2FeO4+*δ*–*x*La0.4Sr0.6FeO3−*δ* (LSF-dual phase-*x* (LSF-DP*x*)), where *x* = 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 mol%. All the nitrates were obtained from Aladdin, China, while the other chemicals were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, China. All the lanthanide oxides used were of 4 N purity, and all other reagents were of analytical reagent (AR) grade.

For the one-pot synthesis of the LSF-DP*x* composite, stoichiometric amounts of La2O3, Sr(NO3)3, and Fe(NO3)3·9H2O were dissolved in dilute nitric acid (approximately 18 wt% HNO3). Subsequently, EDTA and CA were added at molar ratios of Sr : EDTA = 1 : 1 and (La + Fe) : CA = 1 : 2, respectively. The pH of the solution was adjusted to neutral (~7) using ammonium hydroxide. The complex solution was heated at 80 °C to form a gel, which was then heated at 250 °C to initiate self-combustionin a drying oven (DHG-9055A, Shanghai Huitai Instrument Manufacturing Co., Ltd., China). The obtained black precursors were then ground and calcined in a muffle furnace (YFX12/13Q-YC, Shanghai Y-feng Electrical Furnace Co., Ltd., China) at 1100 °C for 10 h to obtain the LSF-DP*x* composite powder.

The crystal structure of the LSF-DP*x* composites was investigated at room temperature (RT) by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan) equipped with Cu Kα1 radiation and a 2*θ* angular range of 5°–90°. The identification of the crystal phase was performed with Jade6.5 and GSAS/EXPGUI software (created by Brain Toby, Argonne National Laboratory, USA). To assess the specific surface area of the LSF-DP*x* composite powders, a fully automated specific surface and porosity analyzer (ASAP 2460, Micromeritics, USA) was employed. The micromorphology was observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Sigma 300, ZEISS, Germany). Furthermore, to probe the atomic-scale structure of the LSF-DP*x* composite, a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HR-TEM; JEM-F200, JEOL, Japan) was used.

TEC of the LSF-DP*x* composites over the temperature range from RT to 900 °C was measured by a thermal dilatometer (DIL402C, Netzsch, Germany) at a controlled heating rate of 5 °C·min−1.

### 2.3 Electrical conductivity measurements

To produce high-density bars, the LSF-DP*x* composite powders were mixed with 8 wt% polyvinyl alcohol solution as a binder and then cold-pressed under a uniaxial pressure of 180 MPa to form green bars. These bars were subsequently sintered at 1300 °C for 2 h. The relative density of the samples was ascertained using the Archimedes drainage method, a well-established technique for determining the volume and density of objects. The sintered LSF-DP*x* dense bars were prepared for the assessment of their electrical conductivity. The electrical conductivity within the temperature range of 200–900 °C was measured using the four-probe method, facilitated by an electrochemical workstation (IVIUM, the Netherlands).

To investigate the oxygen surface exchange kinetics, the electrical conductivity relaxation (ECR) method was employed. The surface exchange coefficient (*k*chem) values, which are indicative of the reaction kinetics, were determined by fitting the normalized electrical conductivity during the transition of the oxygen partial pressure from 0.21 to 1.0 atm. To record the resistance of the dense bars, a digital multimeter (Keithley2100, Tektronix, USA) was utilized, and the four-probe method was applied for accuracy. The specific process used to conduct the ECR measurements was described in our previous work [35].

### 2.4 Cell fabrication and electrochemical characterizations

The ink vehicle for the composite air electrode was formulated by blending a solvent (terpineol, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), a dispersant (Solsperse 28000, Lubrizol, USA), and a binder (ethylcellulose, M70, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China). The composite air electrode slurry was then prepared by mixing the electrode powders with the ink vehicle and a pore former (glutinous rice flour, Changzhou Jintan Maoshan Milling Co., Ltd., China) at a ratio of 100 : 50 : 5.

The GDC slurry (Zhejiang H2-Bank Technology Co., Ltd., China) was applied via screen-printing on the surface of the YSZ electrolyte and sintered at 1300 °C for 5 h. Then, the air electrode slurry was screen-printed on the GDC layer and sintered at 1150 °C for 2 h. This procedure resulted in the creation of symmetrical cells with a structure of LSF-DP*x*/GDC/YSZ/GDC/LSF-DP*x*. To enhance the current collection, a uniform layer of silver grid made of silver paste (Ag, DAD-87, Shanghai Plastics Research Institute Co., Ltd., China) was applied to both sides of the electrodes.

Using an IVIUM electrochemical workstation, *R*p was measured across a temperature range of 600–850 °C and varying oxygen partial pressures from 0.045 to 0.345 atm, with a frequency sweep from 0.1 Hz to 100 kHz. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data were analyzed and fitted using ZView software (Version 3.1). The distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis of the impedance spectra was carried out with DRTtools to distinguish the involved reaction processes [36,37].

For the anode-supported cell, the air electrode slurry was screen printed onto half button cells (Zhejiang H2-Bank Technology Co., Ltd., China) and sintered at 1150 °C for 2 h. These button cells featured a structure of Ni-YSZ/YSZ/GDC/LSF-DP*x*, with an electrolyte thickness of approximately 10 µm and an effective area of the air electrode measuring 0.50 cm2.

### 3.1 DFT calculations

The R‒P structure has the chemical formula A*n*+1B*n*O3*n*+1, while the perovskite structure follows the formula A*n*B*n*O3*n*, where A represents a rare earth or alkaline earth metal, and B denotes a transition metal. The distinct cation ratios in the R‒P and perovskite structures facilitate the spontaneous formation of each phase. The electronic structures of LSF-RP and LSF-P were calculated theoretically. The computational models for the calculations are shown in Fig. 1(a). The density of states (DOS) plots comparing the O 2p orbitals with the Fe 3d orbitals for LSF-RP and LSF-P are depicted in Fig. 1(b). The orbital center positions were determined by integrating the orbital curves, and the results are summarized in Table 1. The catalytic activity of perovskite oxide is intricately related to the distance between the orbital center of O 2p and the Fermi surface [38,39]. The closer the O 2p orbital center is to the Fermi surface, the better its catalytic activity, a phenomenon attributed to the charge transfer between neighboring oxygen atoms within the perovskite structure. Specifically, the O 2p orbital centers for LSF-RP and LSF-P are calculated to be −3.0019 and −2.4354 eV, respectively. It is reasonable to expect that LSF-P will exhibit superior catalytic activity.

In addition, the electrical conductivity of perovskite oxides, governed by the small polaron transport mechanism—hopping through the –O–B–O– pathway—can be deduced from the distance between the O 2p and Fe 3d orbital centers. An increase in orbital overlap due to shorter distances facilitates more effective electron transfer [40,41]. The calculated distances between the O 2p and Fe 3d orbital centers for LSF-RP and LSF-P are 1.1976 and 0.8297 eV, respectively, indicating that LSF-P possesses a higher electrical conductivity than LSF-RP.

The binding energy, defined as the difference between the total energy of the lattice and the sum of the energies of the constituent isolated atoms, can be mathematically represented by Eq. (1) [42,43]:

(1) EB=mEiso(La)+nEiso(Sr)+pEiso(Fe)+qEiso(O)−ET

where E*B, *E*T, and *E*iso denote the binding energy, the total energy, and the energy of the isolated atom, respectively, while *m*, *n*, *p*, and *q* represent the number of each type of atom in the computational model. The LSF-RP system has a higher binding energy than the LSF-P oxide, implying that LSF-RP has better structural stability (Fig. 1(c)).

Considering the theoretical calculation results, it is plausible to hypothesize that incorporating LSF-P into LSF-RP could boost the catalytic activity and electrical conductivity of the composite, thereby improving its output performance. However, this enhancement comes at the cost of reduced stability. Therefore, there exists an optimal threshold for the incorporation of LSF-P to achieve a balanced enhancement in performance without compromising structural stability.

### 3.2 Structure and micromorphology

The LSF-DP*x* dual-phase composite was successfully synthesized using a one-pot method, as indicated by the XRD patterns (Fig. 2(a)). The diffraction patterns are consistent with those of standard JCPDS Cards No. 34-0641 (SrFeO3−*x*) and No. 29-1305 (LaSrFeO4). Notably, the peak at 34°, which is the characteristic peak (the main peak) of the perovskite phase, overlaps with the second strongest peak characteristic of the R–P structure. As the content of the perovskite phase increases, the prominence of the 34° peak becomes increasingly pronounced, culminating in the LSF-DP40 sample exhibiting a significantly higher peak compared to the other samples (Fig. 2(b)).

To distinguish the dual-phase materials, the XRD patterns were refined by GSAS software. Since lanthanum has a smaller ionic radius than strontium, an increase in the perovskite content leads to a reduction in the volume of the R‒P phase. Additionally, due to lanthanum having a greater relative atomic mass than strontium, the molar percentage of the R–P phase was found to be lower than that of the initially designed phase. The microstructures of LSF-RP and LSF-DP*x* (*x* = 10, 20, and 30 mol%) were observed by SEM, as shown in Figs. 3(a)–3(d). The composite air electrode predominantly exhibited two distinct microstructures: flake-like and granular. Notably, with an increase in the LSF-P phase content, the granular structures become more prevalent in the SEM images. Hence, the particles are most likely in the LSF-P phase. To differentiate between the microstructures of the two phases, SEM energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) line scanning was conducted. The flakes were found to have a greater content of Sr and O than the granules, as shown in Figs. 3(e) and 3(f). This elevated Sr and O ratio in the flake structures is attributed to the presence of SrO rock-salt layers within the LSF-RP phase, which is greater than that observed in the LSF-P phase. Based on these observations, it is reasonable to propose that the flake-like structures and granular structures observed in the SEM images correspond to the LSF-RP and LSF-P phases, respectively.

### 3.3 Thermal expansion

To elucidate the influence of the single perovskite phase content on the thermal expansion characteristics of the dual-phase air electrode material, thermal dilatometric measurements were conducted. The relative density of the obtained samples was greater than 99%. The cross-sectional views and relative densities of the samples are displayed in Fig. S2 in the ESM. The thermal expansion curves are shown in Fig. 5**,** and the calculated TEC values are listed in Table 3. TEC of LSF-RP over RT–1000 °C range is 13.73×10−6 K−1. TECs of LSF-P from RT to 600 °C and from 600 to 1000 °C were 13.23×10−6 and 28.31×10−6 K−1, respectively. Obviously, TEC of LSF-RP is significantly lower than that of LSF-P. The transition of the expansion curve is caused by the change in the spin state of the Fe ion, which is also called chemical expansion. The relatively more relaxed R‒P structure allows for the intercalation of interstitial oxygen between the perovskite layer and rock-salt layer, thereby reducing the chemical expansion associated with the spin state of iron ions through a synergistic effect between these two layers.

### 3.4 Electrical conductivity and oxygen surface exchange

For the electrical conductivity study, the sample bars were densified at 1300 °C in air. The resulting samples exhibit an exceptionally dense microstructure (Fig. S2 in the ESM). The XRD pattern of LSF-DP10 after sintering at 1300 °C is shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. Notably, no extraneous reflections attributable to any additional crystalline phase were detected, indicating that the dual-phase material maintains its stability upon sintering at 1300 °C. Figure 6(a) shows the variation in electrical conductivity with temperature for the LSF-RP, LSF-DP10, and LSF-P samples in air. The electrical conductivity of LSF-P is evidently superior to that of LSF-RP, which is consistent with the above calculation results. For example, at 600 °C, the electrical conductivities of LSF-RP, LSF-DP10, and LSF-P are 17.67, 23.91, and 88.11 S·cm−1, respectively. The addition of 10 mol% LSF-P led to an increase in the electrical conductivity of the composite material, as expected.

In addition, the incorporation of 10 mol% LSF-P also reduced the activation energy (E*a) from 0.215 to 0.094 eV (Fig. S4 in the ESM). Given that electron transport along the *c*-axis is obstructed by the rock-salt layer, the electron transport in LSF-RP exhibits two-dimensional characteristics. Combined with the decrease in *E*a, it can be reasonably inferred that the introduction of LSF-P expands the electron transport pathways and promotes three-dimensional electron transport.

To elucidate the impact of surface decoration on oxygen surface exchange kinetics, the ECR technique was employed. This methodology involves recording the transient conductivity at a fixed temperature following a sudden alteration in oxygen partial pressure. The normalized conductivity relaxation curves can be fitted to derive the surface exchange coefficient (*k*chem) based on Fick’s second law [46]. Figure 6(b) presents the typical normalized conductivity relaxation profiles. At 800 °C, the relaxation times for LSF-RP, LSF-DP10, and LSF-DP50 are approximately 44,000, 20,000, and 4000 s, respectively. The derived *k*chem of the material significantly increased from 7.20×10−6 to 1.03×10−5 cm·s−1 after the addition of 10 mol% LSF-P at 800 °C. The shortened relaxation time and increased *k*chem demonstrated enhanced surface reaction kinetics. Due to the fixed valence of La/Sr, the transport of oxygen ions perpendicular to the A–O rock-salt layer direction is significantly hindered. In contrast, LSF-P exhibits three-dimensional characteristics of oxygen ion transport. Thus, the incorporation of the LSF-P phase expands the oxygen ion pathways and increases *k*chem.

Due to the increase in the conductivity and *k*chem, Fig. 6(c) provides a schematic illustration of the charge transport pathways within the LSF-DP*x* dual-phase composite. The decoration of the LSF-P phase serves to expand the charge transport path, thereby facilitating not only electrons but also oxygen ions. This strengthening of the transport pathways is pivotal for optimizing the composite’s performance.

### 3.5 Oxygen reduction mechanism

Due to the fast oxygen exchange process, EIS was employed to investigate the catalytic activity of the LSF-DP*x* composite electrode in air, utilizing a symmetrical cell configuration. The LSF-DP*x* phases have good chemical compatibility with GDC, yet they are prone to react with YSZ, particularly the LSF-P phase (Fig. S5 in the ESM). Therefore, it is imperative to interpose a GDC buffer layer between the YSZ electrolyte and LSF-DP*x* electrode. The Nyquist plots of the symmetrical cell with the LSF-DP*x* electrode at different temperatures are shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM, and the corresponding *R*p values are shown in Fig. 7(a). Due to the nonspontaneous nature of the half-cell reaction, *R*p of a symmetrical half-cell cannot be directly compared to that of the full single-cell. Meaningful comparisons are confined to symmetrical half-cells themselves. *R*p of the electrodes generally decreases with increasing LSF-P content in LSF-RP, as expected from the above calculation results. For example, at 800 °C, the *R*p values of LSF-RP, LSF-DP10, LSF-DP20, LSF-DP30, LSF-DP40, and LSF-DP50 are 0.352, 0.180, 0.248, 0.160, 0.149, and 0.108 Ω·cm2, respectively. Interestingly, the *R*p value of LSF-DP10 is lower than those of the LSF-RP and LSF-DP20 samples, which is comparable to that of samples with the addition of 30 mol% or more LSF-P. The unexpectedly lower *R*p value could be ascribed to the higher specific surface area of the LSF-DP10 composite powder sample (Fig. 7(b)), which is beneficial to the electrode catalytic reaction.

*

To investigate the detailed kinetics of the LSF-DP*x* composite air electrode, EIS based on the oxygen partial pressure (* PO2) was performed. R*p in relation to * PO2 adheres to the equation R*P = *k*(* PO2)−m*, where *k* is a constant, and parameter *m* distinguishes the underlying subreactions [47,48]. The overall ORR can be described by the equation * O2+2VO⋅⋅+4e′→2OO× [49]. However, this process is intricate and comprises multiple substeps, each characterized by distinct m* values [50,51].

The initial step denotes the adsorption of molecular oxygen onto the electrode surface: O2,gas → O2,ad, with *m* = 1. The second reaction represents the dissociation of gaseous oxygen on the air electrode surface, O2,ad → 2Oad, *m* = 1/2. The third step involves the reduction of oxygen atoms capturing an electron: * Oad+e′→Oad′, m* = 3/8. The fourth step represents the further reduction of partially reduced oxygen atoms: * Oad+e′→Oad″, m* = 1/8. The fifth step involves the combination of adsorbed oxygen ions with oxygen vacancies in the lattice: * Oad″+VO⋅⋅→OO×, m* = 0. The complete reduction of oxygen atoms, encompassing the charge transfer process, equates to an *m* value of 1/4, which is derived from averaging the above steps. The final step pertains to the transport of oxygen ions at the electrode–electrolyte interface: * OO×(electrode)→OO×(electrolyte), m* = 0.

The EIS of the LSF-DP50 electrode under various * PO2 concentrations was assessed at 750 °C, with select data (0.045, 0.068, 0.153, and 0.345 atm) presented for clarity (Fig. 8(a)). Evidently, R*p decreases with increasing oxygen partial pressure. However, the ambiguity of the Nyquist plots makes it challenging to discern the individual subreactions. To address this, the EIS data were then deconvoluted using the DRT method, which accounts for the differing relaxation times inherent to each step (Fig. 8(b)). DRT analysis revealed three distinct peaks, labeled P1, P2, and P3, representing processes from high to low frequencies.

*
Fig. 8(a) Nyquist plots of symmetrical cell with an LSF-DP50 electrode measured under different oxygen partial pressures at 750 °C and (b) corresponding DRT spectra with fitting curves of each peak. (c) R*p as a function of oxygen partial pressure at different temperatures for LSF-DP50 sample. (d) DRT spectra of LSF-RP and LSF-DP10 composite electrodes.

The integral area of a specific peak is proportional to *R*p of a specific elementary process. The relationship between *R*P and * PO2 is illustrated in Fig. 8(c), with the corresponding reaction orders m* detailed in the inset. These *m* values are derived from the slopes of the plotted data. For P1, the *m* value is nearly zero, suggesting that it represents oxygen ion transport across the electrode–electrolyte interface. The *m* value for P2 is 0.247, nearly 0.25, indicating that it is likely associated with the charge transfer process. The *m* value for P3, which is approximately 0.53, closely matches the expected value of 0.5, indicative of the oxygen dissociation process. It is evident that the rate-limiting step of the composite electrode is the dissociation process of adsorbed oxygen (P3), even in an environment where the  PO2 (0.345 atm) exceeds atmospheric levels (0.21 atm). With an understanding of the meaning of each peak in the DRT spectra, it can be observed that the addition of LSF-P into the composite electrode enhances both the charge transfer and the oxygen dissociation process.

### 3.6 Single-cell performance

Compared with the LSF-RP air electrode, the LSF-DP10 air electrode demonstrates significantly superior output performance, which is attributed to its lower *R*p and enhanced oxygen surface exchange capacity. Figures 9(a) and 9(b) illustrate the *I*–*V* and power density curves for fuel electrode-supported cells in the temperature range of 650–850 °C. The cell with the LSF-RP air electrode achieved maximum power densities of 579, 404, 226, 157, and 88 mW·cm−2 at 850, 800, 750, 700, and 650 °C, respectively. The cell with the LSF-DP10 air electrode reached maximum power densities of 890, 753, 583, 402, and 231 mW·cm−2 at 850, 800, 750, 700, and 650 °C, respectively. Notably, the incorporation of 10 mol% LSF-P substantially boosted the power density of the cell, particularly at intermediate temperatures, with an increase of approximately 158% at 750 °C. It is worth noting that at 850 °C, the *I*–*V* curve of the LSF-DP10 cell exhibits a curvature at a high current density, indicating the presence of concentration polarization. This phenomenon suggests that the cell is experiencing an inadequate supply of fuel or air for the high-efficiency reaction. This issue is likely attributable to the insufficient porosity of the cell, which hampers the effective transport of reactants.

### 3.7 Robustness

The thermal cycling process involved rapid heating and cooling between 700 and 850 °C, with a limited heating rate of 10 °C·min−1. The cell with the LSF-P air electrode exhibits noticeable performance degradation throughout 11 thermal cycles, while the cell with the LSF-DP10 air electrode shows excellent stability (Fig. 10(a)).

To simulate a sudden power outage, i.e., the cessation of heat supply caused by the cessation of gas supply, during system operation, the open-circuit voltages (OCVs) of the cells with LSF-DP10 and LSF-P air electrodes were monitored under the same accelerated thermal cycling conditions, with the temperature range extending from 200 to 800 °C. A robust relationship exists between OCV and the operating temperature. Within the typical stability range for operation (≥ 600 °C), which is the temperature at which YSZ can efficiently transport oxygen ions, OCV decreases as the temperature increases, in accordance with the Nernst equation. At reduced temperatures, OCV decreases due to relatively sluggish electrode reaction kinetics. It is observable that following 20 thermal cycles, the cell with the LSF-P air electrode exhibited a marked reduction in OCV at 200 °C compared to its initial state, and OCV became unstable starting from the 7th cycle, which may be related to the worsened contact of the internal components of the single cell (Fig. 10(b)). However, the cell with the LSF-DP10 air electrode showed dramatic stability with negligible degradation in OCV after the same number of thermal cycles.

To determine the reasons behind cell degradation, SEM was utilized to examine the cross-sectional microstructure of cells with LSF-DP10 and LSF-P air electrodes following thermal cycling tests (Figs. 10(c) and 10(d)). After thermal cycling, the electrolyte in the cell featuring LSF-DP10 as the air electrode maintained robust contact with the GDC barrier layer. Conversely, in the cell with LSF-P as the air electrode, noticeable cracks or delamination were observed at the interface between the air electrode and the GDC barrier layer. The OCV measurement requires effective connections between cell components. Hence, the decrease in OCV of the cell under similar conditions is mainly attributed to the significant difference between the air electrode and the buffer layer. The results of these two thermal cycle tests collectively suggest that the LSF-DP10 composite air electrode is well matched to the electrolyte, substantially enhancing the resilience of the single cell to thermal stress.

Chemical expansion can also cause interfacial mismatch between the electrode and electrolyte, and the cycle of charge and discharge processes can induce a redox cycle in the electrode, essentially a chemical expansion process [52]. This also simulates the operation scenario in which the user alternates between charge (SOEC mode) and discharge (SOFC mode) on an hourly or daily basis, depending on the availability of the power supply and the fluctuating demand, when coupled with renewable energy sources. To investigate the durability of a single cell under charge/discharge cycling, cells with different air electrodes were subjected to continuous transitions between current densities of −200 and 200 mA·cm−2 at 750 °C. Unfortunately, the electrolysis voltage of the single cell with the LSF-P air electrode increased significantly after 21 charge/discharge cycles, indicating substantial degradation in the cell performance (Fig. 11(a)). Nevertheless, the single cell with the LSF-DP10 air electrode maintained stable performance throughout the charge/discharge cycles, with negligible performance degradation observed even after 25 cycles. This enhanced stability may be attributed to the superior thermal expansion characteristics of the LSF-DP10 electrode compared to those of the LSF-P electrode. These findings collectively suggest that the LSF-DP10 electrode is a promising candidate for reversible SOFCs.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, LSF-RP and LSF-P dual-phases were simultaneously synthesized by a one-pot method. DFT calculations indicate that LSF-P possesses superior catalytic activity and electrical conductivity, while LSF-RP has robust stability. The TEC of LSF-RP was 13.73×10−6 K−1, while the introduction of 10 mol% LSF-P increased the TEC to 13.95×10−6 K−1, ensuring expansion compatibility with the common electrolyte. The cubic LSF-P phase decorated onto the tetragonal LSF-RP system expands the charge transport pathway, not only for electrons but also for oxygen ions, leading to enhanced catalytic activity. Electrochemically, the incorporation of 10 mol% LSF-P at 800 °C resulted in a 49% reduction in the *R*p (0.352 Ω·cm2 for LSF-RP and 0.180 Ω·cm2 for LSF-DP10), an 86% increase in the maximum power density under SOFC mode (404 mW·cm−2 for LSF-RP and 753 mW·cm−2 for LSF-DP10), and a 33% increase in the electrolysis current density under SOEC mode (529 mA·cm−2 for LSF-RP and 705 mA·cm−2 for LSF-DP10 at 1.3 V). Furthermore, the LSF-P-modified LSF-RP composite also exhibited thermal and chemical stability, showing negligible performance degradation under both thermal cycling and charge/discharge cycling conditions.

Additionally, no significant loss in cell voltage was observed during short-term operation with the LSF-DP10 electrode. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of the win–win strategy of self-assembly decoration, where a single perovskite oxide is used to modify the R–P oxide, thereby enhancing the electrochemical performance without compromising the stability of the composite electrode.
