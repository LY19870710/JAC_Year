## 1 Introduction

Compared with traditional lighting structures, fourth-generation solid-state lighting sources (LEDs/LDs) have become the most promising lighting technology because of their high efficiency, good stability, long working life, environmental friendliness, and other advantages [1−5]. Currently, the mainstream technical solution for white light generation is to combine blue LEDs/LDs with phosphor conversion materials, and these new conversion materials mainly include phosphor ceramics (PCs), single crystals (SCs), and phosphor-in-glass (PIG) [6−10]. Among them, the preparation process for SCs is complex and costly, making them difficult to produce on a large scale. Although PIG can achieve good luminous performance, its low thermal conductivity and low saturation threshold decrease its luminous stability [11−13]. In contrast, PCs possess high thermal conductivity and a high saturation threshold, which is conducive to the realization of high-power lighting. Ce:Y3Al5O12 (Ce:YAG) PCs are considered to be potential conversion materials. Nevertheless, white LEDs/LDs structured by traditional Ce:YAG PCs still suffer from a low color rendering index (CRI  7500 K) due to the lack of spectral components in both the red and cyan regions [1,14−19]. Many optimized PCs usually have the problem of luminous decline under high-power-density excitation. This limitation hinders the application of PCs in the field of high-power lighting [20−26].

To increase CRI of Ce:YAG PC, spectral regulation by ion substitution was carried out to control the crystal field of Ce3+. Ce:GdYAG PCs were prepared, and CRI improved to 67.1, which was due to the redshift and expansion of the Ce3+ emission peak by Gd3+ substitution. However, the thermal properties were greatly reduced by 30% at 150 °C [21]. Moreover, the Mg2+–Si4+ ion pair was introduced into Ce:YAG to replace the Al3+–Al3+ ion pair, and a large redshift of the Ce3+ emission peak from 533 to 598 nm was achieved. However, the luminous efficiency (LE) of the corresponding PC decreased by 25.2% [27]. To obtain better thermal stability and luminescence properties, Ma *et al.* [28] prepared Ce,Sc:YAG PCs by incorporating Sc3+ ions into Ce:YAG, the emission of Ce3+ ions was blueshifted by 21 nm, and the full width at half maximum (FWHM) increased from 80.3 to 88.5 nm; moreover, 94% of the emission intensity was retained at 150 °C. Therefore, upon further introduction of Sc3+ ions into Ce:(Lu,Y)3(Al,Sc)2Al3O12 PCs, FWHM of Ce3+ emission increased by 10.2%, and 89.9% of room temperature (RT) emission intensity of the ceramic was retained at 200 °C; CRI improved from 55.9 to 61.7 [29].

On the other hand, red-light-emitting activator ions, such as Pr3+, Eu3+, Sm3+, Cr3+, and Mn2+/Mn4+ [30−34], are always added as a promising strategy to compensate for the red component in Ce:YAG PCs. By introducing Pr3+ into Ce:YAG, the Commission Internationale de l'éclairage (CIE) coordinates of the Ce,Pr:YAG ceramic changed from (0.44, 0.51) to (0.33, 0.32), and CRI increased from 50 to 72. Moreover, the further introduction of Cr3+ increased CRI to 78, and the corresponding CIE coordinates were (0.35, 0.40) [31]. In addition, high-quality single-structure Ce,Pr,Mn:YAG PCs were designed to compensate for red light emission [34]. Compared with the emission of Ce,Pr:YAG PCs, FWHM at 545 nm of Ce,Pr,Mn:YAG ceramics was enlarged from 94.2 to 102.2 nm by the simultaneous doping of Mn2+ ions. Due to this spectral broadening, CRI of the Ce,Pr,Mn:YAG ceramic-based white LED device reached 84.8. However, CRI was only 70 under blue LD excitation. Sm3+ ions were also incorporated into Ce:LuAG ceramics, and multiple narrow and strong light emissions of Sm3+ at 569, 618, and 667 nm were observed. Upon further introduction of Mn2+ into Ce,Sm:LuAG, the resulting white LD device obtained the highest CRI of 78.5 [35]. Mn2+ ions, which are effective transition metal luminescent ions in terms of increasing red light components and improving white light sources, are the most widely reported [36−39]. In brief, introducing Sc3+ ions could effectively expand FWHM and regulate the spectral shift of active ions, and doping with Mn2+ ions could effectively supply red emission. Therefore, realizing full-spectrum light by codoping Sc3+ and Mn2+ into Ce:YAG PCs is desirable.

In this work, high-quality Ce,Mn:Y3(Al,Sc)5O12 PCs (Ce,Mn:YSAG) with excellent CRI and luminous stability were fabricated via solid-state reaction sintering. The phase structure and optical properties were studied. To further enhance the red-light emission, PCs doped with different concentrations of Ce and Mn ions were studied. White LED/LD devices were constructed by combining the prepared PCs with blue LED chips/blue lasers, and their luminescence performances were verified. This study demonstrates that Ce,Mn:YSAG has a promising application as a phosphor conversion material for high-CRI white LED/laser lighting.

### 2.1 Ceramic fabrication

Commercial powders of Y2O3 (99.99%, Alfa Aesar, USA), Al2O3 (99.999%, Alfa Aesar, USA), Sc2O3 (99.99%, Shandong Xiya, China), CeO2 (99.99%, Shandong Xiya, China), and MnCO3 (99.99%, Shandong Xiya, China) were used as starting materials to fabricate Ce,Mn:YSAG PCs. The detailed compositions are as follows: (a) (Y0.997Ce0.003)3(All−*x*−0.004Sc*x*Mn0.004)5O12 (*x* = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4) and (b) (Y0.997Ce*z*)3(All−0.4−*y*Sc0.4Mn*y*)5O12 (*z* = 0.004, 0.005; *y* = 0.006, 0.008). Tetraethyl orthosilicate (0.5 wt%, 99.99%, Alfa Aesar, USA) and MgO (0.1 wt%, 99.999%, Alfa Aesar, USA) were chosen as the sintering additives. Organic polyethyleneimine (PEI) was used as a dispersing agent. After being stoichiometrically weighed, raw materials were mixed in anhydrous ethyl alcohol in a ball milling jar, planetary ball milled for 15 h, and then dried in an oven at 55 °C for 24 h. Then, the dried powders were pulverized and sieved 3 times using 100 mesh and calcined at 800 °C for 6 h in a muffle furnace (MF1200C-L, Anhui Beiyike Equipment Technology Co., Ltd., China). For the fabrication of PCs, the sieved powder mixture was pressed into a stainless-steel mold of *ϕ*22 mm wafers and cold isostatically pressed (CIPed) under 200 MPa for 200 s. Then, the samples were sintered at 1740 °C for 8 h in a vacuum furnace (712T, Thermal Technology LLC, USA) with 10–5 Torr. To remove the internal stress and eliminate oxygen vacancies, the ceramics were annealed at 1450 °C for 10 h in air. All the ceramics were polished to 1.0 mm on both sides and denoted as Sc10Ce03Mn04, Sc20Ce03Mn04, Sc30Ce03Mn04, Sc40Ce03Mn04, Sc40Ce03Mn06, Sc40Ce04Mn06, Sc40Ce04Mn08, Sc40Ce05Mn08, and Sc40Ce05Mn10. ### 2.2 Characterizations

The phase compositions of all the samples were characterized using an X-ray diffraction (XRD) device with a copper target X-ray tube (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) with a scanning range of 10°–80° and dwelling time of 0.02 s per step. The microstructures of the TCs were determined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-6510, JEOL, Japan) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) system. The photoluminescence (PL), photoluminescence excitation (PLE), fluorescence decay spectra, and temperature-dependent PL spectra of the PCs were measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (OmniFluo 900, Zolix, China) equipped with a scintillating xenon lamp and a temperature control system. A quantum efficiency spectrometer (C11347-11, Hamamatsu, Japan) was used for measuring the quantum efficiency of the prepared PCs. The temperature distribution of the ceramic surfaces was measured using an infrared camera (Fotric 225s, Fotric, USA). The electroluminescence (EL) spectra, CRI, CCT, and CIE coordinates of the as-fabricated white LD devices were recorded using an integrating sphere spectroradiometer system (HASS-2000, Everfine, China) excited by a 460 nm blue LED chip as well as a 455 nm laser LD source. A fiber-coupled blue LD with a maximum output power of 4.54 W (4 A) and a numerical aperture (NA) of 0.22 was used. The core diameter was 200 μw. After calculation, the laser-irradiated spot was approximately 0.0985 mm2 circular with a diameter of 0.314 mm at a distance of 0.1 mm.

### 3.1 Phase structure and microstructure

To verify the phase compositions and structural parameters of Ce,Mn:YSAG PCs, the XRD patterns of (Y1−*z*Ce*z*)3(Al1−*x*−*y*Sc*x*Mn*y*)5O12 (*x* = 10–40 at%, *y* = 0.4–1.0 at%, and *z* = 0.3–0.5 at%) PCs are exhibited in Fig. 1(a). The crystalline phase of all the samples was completely consistent with the cubic YAG phase, and no secondary phase was observed [40,41]. This indicated that the Sc3+ ions could completely replace the octahedral Al3+ ions, and all prepared Ce,Mn:YSAG PCs were still in the pure phase. In addition, as shown in Fig. 1(b), all the diffraction peaks at approximately 33.5° shifted to lower angles when the Sc3+ ion content increased from 0 to 40 at%. This demonstrated that the preferred substitution of Al3+ (CN = 6, *r* = 0.053 nm) by Sc3+ (CN = 6, *r* = 0.075 nm) induced lattice distortion. This could be proven by the Rietveld structural refinement of Ce:Y3(Al,Sc)5O12 (Ce:YASG) ceramics prepared in our previous work [28,42]. In addition, the diffraction peaks shifted to lower angles with increasing Ce3+ and Mn2+ doping concentrations. This is due to the larger ionic radius of Mn2+ (CN = 6, *r* = 0.067 nm) than that of Al3+ (CN = 6, *r* = 0.053 nm) and the larger ionic radius of Ce3+ (CN = 8, *r* = 0.118 nm) than that of Y3+ (CN = 8, *r* = 0.102 nm). With the doping of Sc, Mn, and Ce ions, although the XRD diffraction peak of PCs shifted to a small angle, all PCs still maintained a pure phase. The detailed schematic crystal structure concerning the ion substitution process in Ce,Mn:YSAG PC is shown in Fig. 1(c). The crystal structure of Ce,Mn:YSAG PCs included [YO8/CeO8] dodecahedrons, [AlO6/ScO6/MnO6] octahedrons, and [AlO4] tetrahedrons.

### 3.2 Optical properties

The appearance and in-line transmission spectra of the as-fabricated ceramics are shown in Fig. 3. Specifically, images of Ce,Mn:YSAG PCs under daylight and UV light are presented in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b). All the samples exhibited transparent appearance, and the words behind them could be clearly visible. The transmittance of Ce:YAG PC reached 78.1%, as shown in Fig. 3(c), and then decreased to 50.3% for Sc40Ce03Mn04 with increasing Sc3+, Ce3+, and Mn2+ concentrations. The results showed that codoping of Sc3+, Mn2+, and Ce3+ had a great influence on the transmittance of YAG ceramics [43,44]. Remarkably, the doping content of the Sc10Ce03Mn04 ceramic was the smallest, and its transmittance was also the lowest. The reason behind this phenomenon was that ion doping affects the optimum sintering temperature of ceramics. Considering that the doping of Mn ions could easily reduce the optimum sintering temperature of ceramics, the sintering temperature of Ce,Mn:YSAG PCs was set at 1740 °C. Nevertheless, this sintering temperature was not optimal for Sc10Ce03Mn04, which affected the densification behavior of the ceramics during sintering.

To further explore the optimal doping concentration of Sc3+ in Ce,Mn:YSAG PCs, the normalized PLE and PL spectra of ceramics doped with different concentrations of Sc3+ ions are shown in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b). The excitation bands centered at 350 and 460 nm were caused by the 4f→5d2 and 4f→5d1 transitions of Ce3+ ions, respectively. With an increase in the concentration of doped Sc3+ ions from 10 to 40 at%, the emission peak of Ce3+ ions blueshifted by 15 nm. This was ascribed to the decrease in the distortion ratio of the [CeO8] dodecahedron caused by the increase in the concentration of doped Sc3+. Specifically, the small lattice distortion caused the 5d level of Ce3+ ions to shift to a higher energy level, resulting in a blueshift of the 5d→4f emission [28]. The detailed peak positions, spectral intensity, and FWHM of the Ce3+ emissions are shown in Fig. 4(c). With the introduction of Sc3+ ions, the emission peak blueshifted from 533.2 to 519.6 nm, and FWHM gradually increased from 90.68 to 100.3 nm. However, its emission intensity gradually decreased. With increasing Sc3+ doping concentration, the emission intensity of the ceramics decreased. The obtained PL spectra of Sc40Ce03Mn04 could be decomposed into three Gaussian profiles with Ce3+ and Mn2+ ion peaks centered at 521 and 570 nm, respectively. Compared with the emission intensity of Ce,Mn:YAG (Ce3+: 533 nm, Mn2+: 590 nm) [32], the luminescence centers of the Ce3+ ions were blueshifted. Therefore, the results showed that the luminescence properties of Ce,Mn:YSAG could be effectively regulated when the amount of doped Sc3+ ions reached 40%.

To further increase the red-light component and achieve better luminescence, the doping concentrations of Ce and Mn ions were optimized. PLE (λ*em = 530 nm) spectra of Ce,Mn:YSAG PCs are shown in Fig. 5(a). The excitation bands centered at 350 and 460 nm were attributed to 4f→5d2 and 4f→5d1 transitions of the Ce3+ ion [45]. Figure 5(b) shows the PL (*λ*ex = 460 nm) spectra of these PCs. With the increase in the amount of doped Ce3+ and Mn2+ ions, the intensity of the excitation peak gradually decreased. This indicated that the intensity was affected by ion doping. Obviously, the emission intensity of Sc40Ce03Mn06 was the highest, and further increasing the Ce3+ and Mn2+ doping concentrations decreased the emission intensity of PC due to the concentration quenching effect. In addition, all ceramics had two distinct emission peaks centered at 530 nm for Ce3+ and 580 nm for Mn2+. The main emission peak gradually changed from Ce3+ to Mn2+, especially for Sc40Ce05Mn08 and Sc40Ce05Mn10 ceramics. The emission peak of Ce3+ decreased faster, which was ascribed to energy transfer (ET) from Ce3+ to Mn2+ [46]. A corresponding schematic diagram of the Ce3+→Mn2+ ET process is shown in Fig. 5(c). Because the 5d energy level of the Ce3+ ion is slightly greater than the 4T/4E energy level of the Mn2+ ion, the energy transfer process easily occurs from Ce3+ to neighboring Mn2+ via a nonradiative transition. Under excitation at 460 nm, the electrons in the ground energy level of Ce3+ are excited to the upper 5d1 energy level. Then, some of the electrons relax to lower energy levels, and a rapid electron relaxation process occurs from the 5d1 energy level to the 2F7/2 energy level, which emits broad yellow–green light at 530 nm. Other electrons are transferred to the 4T1 energy level of Mn2+, and then, the 4T1 excited electrons are transferred back to the 6A1 ground state, which emits red light at 580 nm [47,48].

The decay curves of Ce3+ ions were surveyed under 460 nm excitation and monitored at 550 nm, as shown in Fig. 5(d). With increasing concentrations of Sc3+ and Mn2+, the fluorescence lifetime of the Ce3+ ions decreased. This was ascribed to the enhancement of the nonradiative transition by ion doping. In addition, the increase in the Mn2+-doping concentration allowed some of the Ce3+ electrons to relax to the Mn2+ ions and reduced the fluorescence lifetime of the Ce3+ ions. This phenomenon revealed the mechanism of ET between Ce3+ and Mn2+. The ET efficiency from Ce3+ to Mn2+ in Ce,Mn:YSAG PCs was acquired using Eq. (1) [46]:

(1) η=(1−τsτ0)×100%

where τ*s is the fluorescence lifetime when Ce3+–Mn2+ is codoped, and *τ*0 is the fluorescence lifetime when Ce3+ is single doped (*τ*0 =89.54). After calculation, in the sample Sc40Ce05Mn10, the ET efficiency of Ce3+–Mn2+ was 34.98%. This indicated that the fluorescence lifetime of Ce3+ decreased, and the luminescence intensity of Mn2+ increased.

Considering that the operating temperature of white LEDs/LDs can reach 150 °C or higher under high power density, the variation trend of the peak position and intensity of ceramics under increased temperature should be evaluated. Figure 6 shows the temperature-dependent PL spectra of the Ce,Mn:YSAG ceramics under 460 nm excitation in the temperature range of RT–200 °C. The emission intensity of Ce3+ decreased from 87.7% to 73.12% at 150 °C (the ratio of the emission maximum at 423 K to the emission intensity at room temperature) when the doping concentration of Sc3+ increased from 0% to 40%. As reported by Denault *et al.* [15], the strong structural rigidity of the compound was beneficial for thermal stability. Doping ions in YAG leads to lattice distortions and a decrease in thermal stability [49]. However, compared with reported Ce3+-doped garnet phosphor materials, such as Ce(Y,Gd)3Al5O12 [50] and Ce(Lu3(Mg,Al)3(Al,Si)2O12 [51], Ce,Mn:YSAG PCs exhibited better thermal stability.

### 3.3 Luminescence performance of white LED/LD devices

Ce,Mn:YSAG-based white LED devices were constructed using the remote excitation mode [35,52]. The EL electroluminescent spectra, chromaticity parameters, CIE chromaticity diagram, and lighting image of Ce,Mn:YSAG PC under 1.0 W blue LED excitation are shown in Fig. 7. For detailed analysis of the electroluminescence properties, Figs. 7(a)–7(e) display the EL spectra of ceramics with different doping concentrations of Sc3+ ions (0.3% Ce3+ and 0.4% Mn2+). CRIs of Ce:YAG PCs increased from 56.4 to 76.4 with increasing concentrations of Sc3+ ions. This was mainly due to the gradual increase in the cyan-green component (caused by Sc3+ ions), and the significant red component (caused by Mn2+) achieved full-spectrum luminescence. The incorporation of Sc3+ ions caused lattice distortion of the garnet matrix and effectively adjusted the luminous color ratio of the emission spectra. This approach is useful for increasing CRI and achieving suitable CCT (6002 K).

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, Ce,Mn:YSAG PCs with different doping concentrations of Sc, Ce, and Mn were successfully fabricated by vacuum sintering technology. The emission peak of Ce3+ was obviously blueshifted by 15 nm, and the orange-red emission at approximately 580 nm significantly increased. Impressively, the Sc40Ce05Mn08 sample matched well with the solar spectrum (CCT ≈ 6000 K), and a high recorded CRI of 85.8 was obtained under high-power LED excitation. Moreover, the operating temperature remained stable at approximately 50 °C for long working time, and there was little CIE drift. Subsequently, the CRI of the Ce,Mn:YSAG PCs under high-power LD excitation improved to 80.9. These results suggest that Ce,Mn:YSAG PC is a potential light convertor with excellent CRI performance for high-power white LEDs/LDs.
