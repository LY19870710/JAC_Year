## 1 Introduction

The importance of optical communication in the modern world cannot be overstated, as it serves as the foundation of our global information infrastructure [1,2]. The rapid growth of optical communication demands advanced materials with superior electro–optic (EO) properties for ultrafast and power-efficient light modulation [3]. Lithium niobate (LiNbO3), known for its first-order EO coefficient tensor (*r*42) of ~31 pm/V at the GHz modulation frequency [4], stands out as a typical commercial EO modulator, setting benchmarks for efficiency and reliability. However, as we look toward next-generation EO modulators, there is an increasing demand for advanced materials with higher EO coefficients, such as PbZr0.52Ti0.48O3 (PZT) and BaTiO3 (BTO), to meet the requirements for higher efficiency, lower power consumption, and device miniaturization [5]. Recent advancements in research involving EO ferroelectric materials and devices, particularly those focused on PZT and BTO, have been significant [6−19]. For example, BTO is theoretically predicted to have an EO coefficient (*r*42) of ~42.5 pm/V [20], and experimentally, its maximum effective EO coefficient (*r*eff). has been measured at an impressive value of ~286 pm/V via the spatial transmission geometry method [13]. In comparison, the largest theoretical and experimental values of the linear EO coefficient for PZT with the MPB (morphotropic phase boundary) composition PbZr0.52Ti0.48O3 are ~13 pm/V [20] and ~219.6 pm/V [15], respectively. Although the EO coefficient of PZT is generally lower than that of BTO, it has the advantage of a higher Curie temperature (~360 °C) [21]. The gap between the theoretical and experimental EO coefficient values is often significant. This difference, along with the pronounced EO effect, can be understood by considering the interplay between the orientation-dependent distribution, spontaneous polarization, epitaxial strain, domain configuration, and electron‒optical phonon coupling [5,22−24]. Furthermore, the in-plane polarization switching in EO modulators, upon coplanar electrodes and an in-plane electric field, typically occurs at lower fields owing to the rapid domain wall switch, as multiple superdomains switch simultaneously, forming a connected band [25]. The domain switching behavior under an in-plane electric field is fundamentally different from the conventional domain nucleation process under an out-of-plane electric field [26]. Therefore, clarifying and understanding the link between EO effects and ferroelectric polarization dynamics, along with manipulating the degree of freedom of polar domains and the switching barrier for the transition of polarization, remains a formidable challenge.

Generally, single-orientation or epitaxial PZT thin films can be closely linked with physical models to explain their ferroelectric properties. However, achieving a breakthrough in performance has been elusive. For example, in epitaxial thin films, irreversible domain wall motion is highly affected by the strong clamping effect [27,28]. This difficulty stems from the intricacy of precisely controlling the various functional elements that influence ferroelectric polarization changes, such as domains, domain walls, and phase transitions [29]. These factors collectively present critical bottlenecks in enhancing the EO effect [13,30]. In this work, we synthesized PZT thin films with an MPB composition of PbZr0.52Ti0.48O3 via a sol‒gel method on a fused quartz glass substrate [31,32], aiming to reduce the stress constraints imposed by the substrate and allow greater degrees of freedom for polarization tuning. Upon the application of an in-plane electric field, the polarization variation encompasses both polarization switching (upward and downward) and phase transition, which significantly maximize the degree of freedom of polarization for refractive index modulation. This process enables more effective polarization manipulation and a significantly enhanced EO effect, surpassing the limitations inherent to traditional polarization switching. The phase transition, facilitated by the highly relaxed domain structure in the PZT characterized by a local polymorphism structure and disordered nanosized domain orientation, is confirmed by both experimental results and theoretical calculations.

### 2.1 Experimental process

PZT film preparation: The PbZr0.52Ti0.48O3 (PZT) film was prepared on a La-doped PZT buffer layer via a sol–gel process following our previous method [31]. First, a 0.3 M/L PZT solution was prepared according to the stoichiometric ratio of the elements. After the solution was spin coated on the substrate surface, the spin-coated substrate was first dried on a 300 °C hot plate for 5 min and then transferred to a 550 °C hot plate for pyrolysis for 30 min. A nominal thickness was obtained by repeating the spin coating and annealing process in a sophisticated manner.

Photolithography and electrode preparation: First, we layout the pattern, and the designed interdigital electrode pattern is shown in Fig. 1(d). The designed interdigital pattern was subsequently transferred to the surface of the PZT thin film via photolithography, followed by electron beam evaporation to deposit Cr/Au metal layers with thicknesses of 10 nm/200 nm. Finally, the required interdigital electrode is obtained through the lift-off process.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The phase structures of the PZT films were characterized by an X-ray diffractometer (X’pert3 Powder, PANalytical B.V., the Netherlands). The microstructures were observed via a scanning electron microscope (SEM; GeminiSEM300, Zeiss, Germany). Hysteresis loops of polarization–electric fields *P*–*E* were characterized at room temperature on a ferroelectric test system (Precision RT66C, Radiant Technologies, USA). Ferroelectric domain reversal and polarization amplitudes were investigated via a piezoelectric force microscope (PFM; Dimension ICON, Bruker, USA). EO coefficients were measured by a self-built testing system using a 780 nm laser as the incident light (Fig. S5 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). The refractive index (*n*) and extinction coefficient (*k*) were measured with an ellipsometer (RC2 XI, J.A. WOOLLAM, USA). Atomic images were obtained via a spherical aberration transmission electron microscope (TEM; Spectra 300, Thermo Fisher, USA).

### 2.3 Theoretical calculation and simulation

Density functional theory calculations: All the first-principles results are calculated via the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) with the projection enhanced wave (PAW) method [33] to address the electron‒ion interaction, and the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof generalized gradient‒approximation (GGA–PBE) is adopted to address the exchange‒correlation interaction [34]. In the calculations, we first consider the two different phases of *P*4*mm* and *R*3*m* based on a 1 × 1 × 1 super cell combined with PZO and PTO to maintain the concentration of Ti and Zr at 50%. The 5 × 5 × 5 *k*-points are adopted during relaxation. The cutoff energy of 500 eV is used throughout the entire calculation process, and the free energy fluctuation converges to within 10−6 eV per atom. The forces per atom were less than 0.01 eV/Å. The lattice parameters of fully relaxed PZT are *a* = *b* = 5.86624 Å, and *c* = 8.76895 Å. The static dielectric constant and frequency-dependent dielectric constant are simulated to study the properties of the refractive index [35]. To treat the optical properties of polycrystalline PZT films along different axes equally, the average refractive indices of *x*, *y*, and *z* are adopted. To analyze the effect of the domain wall concentration on the refractive index, we further constructed several 1 × 12 × 1 supercells. We then generate 180° domain walls in the fully relaxed supercells by changing the polarization directions (pointing up or down along the *Z* axis) of some unit cells. These supercells correspond to four different domain wall concentrations (single domain, 2 domains, 4 domains, and 6 domains), with four corresponding numbers of domain walls (*N*DW) per supercell (0, 2, 4, and 6), as shown in Fig. 4(b). All the structures are fully relaxed to calculate the refractive index.

Phase field modeling: The theoretical model consists of two parts to investigate the domain structure and calculate the dielectric response: the traditional ferroelectric phase field model and the ultrafast ferroelectric phase field model. The traditional phase-field model adopts space-dependent spontaneous polarization ( P) and a displacement field ( u) as the field variables. The temporal evolution of the polarization is characterized by the time-dependent Ginzburg–Landau (TDGL) equation and the stress equilibrium equation [36]. ### 3.1 Structural and ferroelectric characterizations

The quality and orientation of the PZT thin film were characterized via X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Figure 1(a) shows a dominant (001) peak with a secondary peak near 22° to be indexed to (100). This indicates that the anisotropy of the crystal structure is lower than that of epitaxial films with designed singular orientations and high anisotropy. Additionally, a strong peak near 45° represents a mixture of (002) and (200) orientations, whereas a weaker peak near 31° corresponds to (110). The SEM energy dispersive spectra indicate a uniform distribution of elements (Fig. S1 in the ESM). The film thickness was approximately 968 nm (Fig. 1(b)). PFM images, as shown in Fig. 1(c), reveal that unpoled regions demonstrate the same phase contrast as positively poled regions when an in-plane electric field is applied via interdigital electrodes (Fig. 1(d) and Fig. S2 in the ESM). The poling process is achieved by applying a voltage to the tip, with one of the interdigital electrodes grounded (Fig. S3 in the ESM). The domain contrast in the PFM images indicates that the PZT films exhibit spontaneous polarization in both the in-plane and out-of-plane directions. In addition to phase contrast imaging, by applying an in-plane electric field, Fig. 1(e) shows a typical moderate polarization charge (*Q*) hysteresis loop. Polarization charge hysteresis demonstrates high-quality polarization switching under an in-plane electric field, with the hysteresis behavior closely resembling that of thin films subjected to an out-of-plane electric field.

Surprisingly, the coercive field for in-plane domain switching (*E*c-in-plan*e*) was found to be ~4.3 kV/cm, which is significantly smaller by at least an order of magnitude than the reported typical out-of-plane coercive field (*E*c-out-of-plane) in PZT thin films (> 100 kV/cm) [31,37]. This value is even less than half of that reported for PZT ceramics (> 10 kV/cm) [38,39]. This relatively low coercive field can be attributed to several factors. Initially, the lattice-matched growth of the PZT film on a La-doped PZT-buffered quartz glass substrate significantly mitigated the clamping effects from lattice mismatches. Both theoretical analyses and experimental results suggest that the in-plane electric field is notably amplified at the electrode edges [40]. This heightened field concentration promotes domain nucleation at the electrodes before the coercive field is attained, aligning with our simulation results, where the electric field is predominantly localized near the electrode periphery. Compared with traditional metal/ferroelectric/metal structures, other factors, such as the weak effect of the dead layer due to the insulating nature of the substrate, may also contribute to the low coercive electric field [41]. The low coercive field observed in this study aligns with the greater tunability observed in Sr-doped BaTiO3 systems, which feature a relaxor-type nanodomplex structure under a moderate in-plane electric field, in comparison to classical ferroelectric BaTiO3 [42].

### 3.2 EO measurement and properties

The low in-plane coercive field suggests a low polarization reversal barrier in the prepared PZT films, which is favorable for dielectric tunability and EO modulation. We first analyzed the refractive index *n* and extinction coefficient *k* of the thin film (Fig. 2(a)). The value of *n* gradually decreases from 2.40 to 2.37 as the wavelength increases from 780 to 1550 nm, whereas the value of *k* decreases from 5×10−5 to a level well below 1×10−6. A small *k* value is beneficial for both high transmittance and EO modulation. A custom-designed EO characterization system in reflection mode (Fig. 2(b)), which is based on Stokes vectors [45], was used to measure the phase difference *δ* under different applied DC electric fields via an electrode-guided wired sample (Figs. S4 and S5 in the ESM). A LiNbO3 single-crystal plate with a thickness of ~0.5 mm was used to calibrate the system, yielding an EO coefficient of ~29.8 pm/V by applying a DC electric field parallel to the  direction (Fig. S6 in the ESM), which is consistent with the reported value (~31 pm/V) [4], confirming the stability and accuracy of the system. A typical phase difference hysteresis was obtained, which aligns with the polarization hysteresis (Fig. 2(c)). The change in the refractive index (Δ*n*) as a function of the applied electric field (*E*) was derived from *δ* according to the Stokes vector, which encapsulates the phase information of light (Section II in the ESM). The electric field-dependent (Δ*n*) shows typical hysteresis behavior (Fig. 2(d)). The first-order Pockels and second-order Kerr EO coefficients (*r*1st, *r*2nd) were determined through piecewise polynomial fitting of Δ*n* vs *E* at 780 nm (Fig. 2(e)). This analysis established the functional relationship between the change in refractive index (∆*n*) and the electric field (*E*). The calculated *r*1st and *r*2nd values were ~233.5 pm/V and ~16.3×10-16 m2/V2, respectively.

In light of recent studies, we compiled a comprehensive summary of the EO coefficient values for PZT films measured via a spatial method by applying an in-plane electric field with a similar coplanar electrode, as shown in Fig. 2(f) [6−18] The extracted *r*1st is at least seven times greater than that of the traditional EO material LiNbO3 (~31 pm/V) [4] and at least one order of magnitude greater than the theoretically predicted value (~13 pm/V) [20]. Given the enormous Pockels coefficient, PZT films deposited via a wafer-scale, CMOS-compatible method [31] are anticipated to deliver outstanding half-wave voltage performance, along with the superior high-temperature stability of the PZT system. This is reflected in the relatively stable capacitance temperature spectrum above 200 °C, with a noticeable capacitance peak at approximately 347 °C, corresponding to the Curie temperature (*T*C). This advancement significantly strengthens the potential of PZT as a competitive EO active material for next-generation silicon-integrated photonics.

Despite the varying EO coefficients obtained in ferroelectric materials, a clear mechanism to explain the high EO coefficient remains elusive. The EO effect is a coupling effect of an external electric field, an optical field, and electric polarization, in which the EO coefficient is affected mainly by electronic, ionic or lattice polarization and the converse piezoelectric effect [22]. This combination of multiscale components and the complexity of optoelectronic coupling make the study of the EO mechanism challenging. From a structural point of view, factors such as the phase, domain structure, and domain walls play critical roles in affecting both the polarization and refractive index [29,46,47]. Therefore, understanding the evolution behavior of these structural elements and Δ*n* under an in-plane electric field is vital to clarify the relationship between polarization and the EO effect.

### 3.3 Microstructural and domain dynamics

In this work, the evolution of the domain and phase structure was investigated via *in situ* high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images. Figures 3(a)–3(c) show the in-plane ionic displacement measured at 0, 500, and 1000 mV in-plane voltages, respectively. Obvious signatures of nanosized domains and even polar nanoclusters measuring several unit cells can be discerned from polarization mapping. The nanosized domains show disordered polarization directions. However, some areas show no clear ionic displacement, and the polar vector in these areas may extend out of the plane, suggesting a random distribution of the domain structure. For the fresh sample (Fig. 3(d)), the as-prepared PZT has a mixed structure with a T phase (purple) and R phase (blue). The green areas, although not representing the cubic phase, do not exhibit magnitude in the 2D projection and are referred to as the cubic-like (C-like) phase for convenience. Similarly, C-like phases have also been reported in relaxor ferroelectric ceramics, which exhibit low anisotropy and random domain orientation [48]. The ratio of the T, R, and C-like phases is approximately 50% : 48% : 2% at zero-electric field. The as-prepared PZT with low anisotropy exhibited nanosized domains and nanoclusters, indicating a highly relaxed state with reduced internal stress and a more uniform nanoscale domain distribution. This structure differs from typical epitaxial or sol-gel MPB PZT thin films constrained by the clamp effect, where distinct R and T phases are clearly visible in the 2D projection [49,50]. As the electric field increases, no significant domain structure nucleation or growth is observed within the surface of the studied PZT. At 500 mV (2.5 kV/cm), the domain size and phase distribution noticeably change. At 1000 mV (5.0 kV/cm), the polarization direction of the nanodomains continued to evolve, as there was a visible change in the color distribution, corresponding to the R, T, and C-like phase structures. The large observable color patterns generally decrease compared with those in the cases at 0 and 500 mV, which suggests domain polarization switching and phase evolution followed by variation in the domain walls. The strain associated with this phase evolution process is further confirmed by *in situ* XRD results, where the 2*θ* values of the diffraction peaks (001) and (100) experience hysteresis strain variations in response to electric field cycles (Fig. S7 in the ESM). Therefore, the phase evolution, domain switching and domain wall variations enabled by the highly relaxed PZT significantly unleash the degree of freedom of polarization for large refractive index modulation, which is expected to contribute to a high EO coefficient.

### 3.4 Multiscale structural EO mechanism analysis

To further confirm the contributions of the phase evolution and domain switching followed by the variation in the domain wall concentration to Δ*n*, we first calculated the average refractive index (*n*average) of the R and T phases on the basis of a 1 × 1 × 1 supercell after the PbZr0.5Ti0.5O3 structure was relaxed. The *n* values of the R phase and T phase are ~2.64 and ~2.54 at a wavelength of 780 nm, respectively, corresponding to Δ*n* of 0.1 (Fig. 4(a)). Therefore, the variation in the phase significantly changes the refractive index and enhances the EO effect. Next, to determine the effect of variation in the domain wall concentration on the refractive index, we set the 180o domain wall as an example, which allows us to easily build and be stable in the model. A 1 × 12 × 1 supercell with four different polarization states with a single domain, 2 domains, 4 domains, and 6 domains was constructed after structural relaxation, corresponding to a number of domain walls per supercell (*N*DW/supercell) of 0, 2, 4, and 6, respectively (Fig. 4(b)). As shown in Fig. 4(c), the refractive indices at a wavelength of 780 nm along the *X*, *Y*, and *Z* axes increase in magnitude from 10−2 as the *N*DW/supercell increases. Notably, significant alterations are observed along the *X*- and *Y*-axis, whereas the *Z*-axis exhibits minimal variation. Consequently, for films possessing a singular (001) orientation, the refractive index along the *Z* axis undergoes only minor changes as the NDW/supercell increases. However, a mixed orientation comprising both (001) and (100) is assumed to have a larger refractive index and can have a more pronounced Δ*n*, which makes the large EO coefficient in the as-prepared PZT thin films reasonable. Notably, compared with the magnitude of Δ*n* (~10−4) of single-crystal PZT upon normal domain polarization switching [16], the magnitude of Δ*n* induced by phase (~10−1) and domain wall variation (~10−2) are two orders of magnitude greater, highlighting the effective enhancement in the EO coefficient. Furthermore, we quantified the impact of domain wall migration (i.e., a constant number of domain walls with variable cell occupancy within the domain) on the refractive index. Our analysis reveals that this effect is negligible, further supporting the conclusion that the substantial variation in the refractive index is predominantly attributed to domain switching, phase evolution and variation in the domain wall concentration.

Phase-field modeling was carried out to confirm the domain structure evolution and the dielectric response at 384.3 THz (780 nm wavelength) (Section IV in the ESM). The structural model of the highly relaxed MPB PZT with low anisotropy is composed of fluctuating R and T phases with a C-like region, following the initial phase ratio observed in Fig. 3(d). The simulation results suggest that the dielectric constant (*ε*r) decreases and that the change in the dielectric constant (Δ*ε*r) increases as *E* increases (Fig. 4(d)). Compared with the zero-field condition, the highly relaxed MPB PZT has a significantly larger Δ*ε**r*, and it increases dramatically as a function of the electric field. The magnitude of the decrease is significantly greater than that of a normal MPB PZT without considering the phase ratio and lower anisotropy.

Figure 4(e) shows the two simulated domain structures corresponding to the zero-electric field and high electric field (8 kV/cm). The domain structure changes significantly with increasing electric field. The highly relaxed MPB PZT shows nanoscale regions of color and domains with disordered orientations under zero-electric field conditions, which is consistent with the signature of the highly relaxed structure observed in the TEM results. As an in-plane electric field is applied along the *x* direction, the majority of the domain structures align in this direction. The phase ratio for the highly relaxed PZT is quantitatively calculated, which is believed to be the best way to quantitatively evaluate their respective contributions (Fig. S8 in the ESM). The increasing trend of the R phase and decreasing trend of the T phase are consistent with the TEM results. The substantial phase change leads to a pronounced polarization response, which aligns with experimental observations. In contrast, the normal MPB PZT shows much larger domain structures and no obvious phase evolution (Fig. 4(e)), suggesting that the change in the refractive index is attributed mainly to domain switching. Therefore, compared with normal MPB PZT, where phase evolution is rarely observed under an in-plane electric field, highly relaxed MPB PZT with lower anisotropy is more susceptible to phase evolution in addition to domain switching under an in-plane electric field. This is believed to explain why the EO coefficient in epitaxially grown single-crystal PZT is less than 30 pm/V. The results are believed to unlock the electro–optic potential of ferroelectrics via advanced domain and phase manipulation. The multiscale manipulation of the domain structure, phase, and domain wall concentrations is considered to be the reason for the large gap between the theoretical predictions and experimental results, as well as the significant differences among the reported experimental results.

To this end, the high EO coefficient is mainly attributed to the peculiarity of our highly relaxed MPB PZT, which provides more flexibility for multiscale structural manipulation (including intrinsic lattice, domain, and phase). It is not simply a "relaxor" but an MPB system where the intrinsic phase instability has been maximized by largely weakened external constraints. This creates a disordered, multiphase, low-anisotropy nanocomposite that exists in a high-entropy, low-energy state. This state includes structural traits (mixed orientation, nanoscale polymorphism), predicted behavior (dominant phase and domain evolution mechanism from modeling), and functional properties (ultralow *E*c enabling phase transition). These features are distinct from and synergistic beyond those of conventional relaxors or clamped MPB films. This specific combination of traits unlocks the electro-optic potential by leveraging phase transition, in addition to domain switching, as a powerful new mechanism for refractive index modulation.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, we present a facile yet effective method to realize a giant effective EO coefficient (~233.5 pm/V) in PZT films, which is seven times larger than that of the traditional EO material LiNbO3 (~31 pm/V) and at least one order of magnitude larger than the theoretically predicted value (~13 pm/V). This demonstrates great potential for practical applications, especially given its high Curie temperature. State-of-the-art *in situ* TEM analysis revealed that, compared with normal MPB PZT, the as-prepared MPB PZT film with mixed (001) and (100) crystal orientations has a highly relaxed structure comprising nanosized domains and mixed R, T, and C-like phases. These structural features facilitate both phase transition and domain switching under an in-plane electric field. Multiscale theoretical calculations, including DFT and phase field modeling, confirm that, in addition to domain switching, the phase transition and induced variation in domain wall concentration are key contributors to the observed changes in the refractive index and dielectric constant, which results in a significantly greater EO effect than in previous reports. Our preliminary studies indicate that similarly high EO coefficients can be achieved on various insulating substrates. Our findings are believed to bridge the gap between theory and experiments, offering a promising pathway for designing high-performance EO materials and devices. This work demonstrates the substantial potential of PZT films for practical applications, particularly given their high Curie temperature and compatibility with established fabrication techniques. The enhanced freedom of polarization modulation demonstrated here opens new avenues for optimizing ferroelectric properties, such as electrocaloric and pyroelectric effects, which are crucial for advancing integrated photonics and next-generation chip-scale ferroelectric devices.
