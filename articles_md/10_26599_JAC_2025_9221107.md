## 1 Introduction

The global energy consumption is continuously increasing, driven by factors such as population growth, economic development, and industrialization. This escalating energy demand has led to a significant reliance on fossil fuels, which in turn contributes to global warming. The combustion of these fuels releases large amounts of greenhouse gases, such as carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide, into the atmosphere, causing adverse climate effects, including rising temperatures, extreme weather events, and sea-level rise [1−3]. Among the various energy-consuming sectors, traditional refrigeration systems account for a substantial proportion, with estimates suggesting that they consume approximately 10% of the global electricity supply [4]. Moreover, the leakage of refrigerants from these systems, which often have high global warming potentials, further exacerbates the greenhouse effect [5,6].

Interfacial engineering is an effective method for regulating the surface properties of a substrate [7,8]. Among them, passive daytime radiative cooling technology is an effective choice that can alleviate the high energy consumption and heavy pollution of traditional refrigeration technology. This technology can reflect a significant portion of solar radiation, reducing the heat absorption of buildings and objects. Additionally, the passive daytime radiative cooling technology can emit heat in the form of longwave infrared radiation through the atmospheric transparency window, enabling efficient heat dissipation without the need for external energy input. Passive daytime radiative cooling technology has a wide range of applications, including building envelopes, solar panels, automotive cooling systems, food preservation, and electronic device cooling, making it a viable solution for reducing energy consumption and mitigating the impacts of global warming [9−13].

The core principle of passive daytime radiative cooling relies on achieving spectrally selective radiative properties: high solar reflectance across 0.3–2.5 μm to minimize solar heat gain, coupled with strong thermal emissivity within the 8–13 μm atmospheric window to maximize heat dissipation [14,15]. Material performance is governed by intricate interactions between intrinsic optical properties and engineered micro/nanostructures. Key determinants include electronic bandgap characteristics for ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) reflection or the micro/nanostructure of UV–Vis scattering, lattice vibration modes or specific high-infrared absorption functional groups for mid-infrared emission, and photon localization effects through photonic crystals or phodesigns [16−21].

Current passive daytime radiative cooling material systems can be categorized into four primary groups: polymer-based composites [22−26], multilayer thin-film architectures [27−29], bio-inspired photonic structures [30,31], photonic crystals or metamaterials [20,21,32,33], and inorganic particulate coatings [34−36]. Polymer matrices (e.g., polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA)) demonstrate excellent processability and low-cost fabrication but suffer from UV degradation and limited thermal stability [19,37,38]. Multilayer dielectric stacks achieve near-ideal spectral selectivity through interference effects, yet their complex vacuum deposition processes hinder scalability. Biomimetic photonic crystals replicate the natural cooling mechanisms observed in Saharan silver ants but require precise nanostructural control. Photonic crystals and metamaterials, as passive daytime radiative cooling materials, face certain limitations, primarily due to their complex fabrication processes and high costs. The intricate manufacturing techniques required for these materials often involve multiple steps and specialized equipment, which can significantly increase the production cost and limit their large-scale application. Additionally, the multi-layered and periodic structures of photonic crystals and metamaterials can be fragile and difficult to integrate with other components, further restricting their practical use. Inorganic oxides represent a particularly promising class due to their inherent advantages: high refractive indices enabling strong Mie scattering, stable phonon–polariton resonances in the infrared region, and exceptional weathering resistance. Materials such as TiO2, SiO2, and Al2O3 demonstrate synergistic performance when formulated into hierarchically porous coatings, combining efficient photon management with decades-long durability [39−41]. Owing to their abundance, non-toxicity, and compatibility with industrial coating technologies, inorganic oxides are ideal candidates for large-scale passive daytime radiative cooling implementation.

The development of high-performance inorganic oxide-based passive daytime radiative cooling materials addresses critical limitations in current technologies while unlocking novel applications. Unlike organic composites vulnerable to photodegradation, oxide ceramics maintain their optical properties under prolonged UV exposure and extreme temperature cycling [42,43]. Their refractory nature enables integration with high-temperature industrial equipment and fire-resistant building envelopes. Recent advances in solution-processed nanoparticle coatings have further bridged the gap between laboratory-scale achievements and commercial viability [4]. By engineering particle morphology and packing density, researchers have demonstrated daytime cooling powers exceeding 60 W·m2 with sub-ambient temperature drops of 3.5 °C under peak solar irradiance. Such progress not only advances the fundamental understanding of photon–thermal interactions in disordered media but also provides practical solutions for global cooling challenges. The exploration of novel oxide compositions promises to push the boundaries of radiative cooling efficiency while maintaining essential criteria for real-world deployment, such as scalability, affordability, and long-term reliability.

SrZrO3, one kind of perovskite ceramics with a wide bandgap (~5.6 eV), has a high emissivity (*ε* ≥ 0.85) within the 8–14 μm atmospheric transparency window arising from strong phonon–polariton resonances associated with Zr–O bond vibrations, enabling efficient heat dissipation to outer space [44]. The material’s wide electronic band gap indicates high UV–vis reflectivity, which minimizes the material’s absorption of sunlight in direct sunlight. The intrinsic properties indicate that SrZrO3 is a promising candidate for passive daytime radiative cooling applications. Furthermore, SrZrO3 demonstrates exceptional UV resistance and chemical inertness, maintaining optical stability under prolonged environmental exposure, which is critical for outdoor applications. These inherent characteristics, combined with their high melting point (> 2600 °C) and thermal shock resistance, suggest robust compatibility with large-scale coating processes and harsh operating conditions, addressing the durability challenges faced by organic-based passive daytime radiative cooling materials.

Despite these advantages, SrZrO3 has the potential to improve both its solar reflectivity and its emissivity in the 8–13 μm window. These limitations are due to the small refractive index caused by the wide band gap and the single and localized phonon vibration modes. Strategic ion doping at Zr sites offers a viable pathway to refine spectral selectivity [45]. Zn is a frequently utilized doping element in ionic modification strategies [46−50]. The incorporation of Zn2+ ions enables the regulation of material properties, including antibacterial activity, dielectric behavior, photoluminescence, magnetic characteristics, photocatalytic performance, and electrical properties, primarily through Zn2+-mediated modulation of the host crystal’s bandgap width [51−59]. Literature analysis has revealed that Zn2+ doping has distinct regulatory effects on the bandgaps of host crystals. For materials with narrow intrinsic bandgaps, Zn2+ doping enhances the bandgap width, as exemplified by increases from 2.51 to 2.60 eV in CdO, 2.07 to 2.60 eV in Mn3O4, 1.14 to 1.74 eV in PbS, 1.35 to 1.65 eV in AgO2, and 3.22 to 3.99 eV in CeO2 [51−55]. Conversely, for materials with a wider initial bandgap, Zn2+ doping induces a slight reduction in the bandgap width, such as a contraction from 3.76 to 3.41 eV in CuO, from approximately 4.25 to 4.10 eV in MgO, and a minor narrowing in the bandgap of TiO2 [56−59]. Density functional theory (DFT) confirms that Zn doping can only slightly reduce the band gap of SrZrO3 [60] so that the refractive index of the crystal can be adjusted on the basis of the low intrinsic absorption of UV–vis light. Moreover, the difference in ionic radius between Zn2+ (0.74 Å) and Zr4+ (0.72 Å), as well as the difference in the valence state could broaden the phonon vibration pattern [61]. Thus, Zn2+ emerges as a preferred dopant. This dual optimization—boosting solar reflection via crystal optical property modulation engineering and amplifying thermal emission through phonon engineering—enables the development of SrZrO3-based ceramics with balanced passive daytime radiative cooling performance.

In this work, Zn2+ was chosen as the dopant, while SrZrO3 served as the matrix material. The substitution of Zr4+ with Zn2+ aims to regulate the band gap and refractive index, induce lattice distortion, and thereby enhance the passive daytime radiative cooling performance of SrZrO3. A series of Zn-doped SrZrO3 (abbreviated as SZZO) ceramics were fabricated using a sol–gel method combined with solid-phase synthesis. The impact of Zn2+ doping on passive daytime radiative cooling properties was systematically investigated, and the mechanisms underlying the influence of various Zn2+ doping concentrations on these properties were elucidated through both experimental and theoretical approaches.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

The ceramic components prepared in this experiment were SrZr0.9375Zn0.0625O2.9375, SrZr0.875Zn0.125O2.875, SrZr0.8125Zn0.1875O2.8125, and SrZr0.75Zn0.25O2.75, designated as SZZO-6.25, SZZO-12.5, SZZO-18.75, and SZZO-25, respectively, and were uniformly referred to as SZZO. The ceramic powders were prepared by a sol–gel method combined with a solid-phase synthesis method. The specific process flow is shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The raw materials for preparing the samples were Zr(C2H3O2)4 (AR, Grirem Advanced Materials Co. Ltd., China), Sr(NO3)2 (AR, Grirem Advanced Materials Co. Ltd., China), Zn(C2H3O2)2 (Xiya Reagent Co., Ltd., China), citric acid (Kemao Chemical Reagent Co., China), dilute ammonia water (Fuchen Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), and deionized water (lab-made). The settling time was 12 h. For comparison, SrZrO3 (abbreviated as SZO) ceramic powder was prepared using the same process.

To evaluate the passive daytime radiative cooling performance of the SZZO ceramics, uniform ground radiating refrigerant SZZO ceramic powder was prepared as a slurry with adhesive polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China) and solvent N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China). The mass ratio of SZZO ceramic powder to PVDF was 10 : 1, and the ratio of PVDF to NMP was 1 g : 15 mL. The wet film was obtained by scraping the slurry onto a bare aluminum sheet (purchased from the Taobao platform) with a film applicator; the dimensions of length, width and thickness were 150, 100, and 0.48 mm, respectively, and the surface of the aluminum sheet had not undergone mirror polishing processing. Photos of the aluminum sheets are shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. The wet film was then dried to obtain the coating. The specific process flow is shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. The preparation methods for SZZO coatings with different thicknesses of 70/100/130 μm are given in the ESM.

### 2.2 Structural characterizations

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Bruker D8 Advance, Bruker AXS Inc., Germany) with Cu Kα radiation with *λ* = 1.5406 Å was used to determine the crystal structure of the SZO and SZZO ceramic powders. A field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; FEI-Apreo 2S HiVac, Thermo Fisher, USA) with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS; ULTIM MAX40, Oxford Instruments, UK) was used to determine the morphology and elemental distribution of the SZO and SZZO ceramic powders. All powder samples for SEM were subjected to strictly identical preparation protocols: ultrasonic dispersion (30 min in anhydrous ethanol with fixed powder-to-solvent ratios), drop-casting onto glass dishes, drying at 80 °C, grinding in an agate mortar (15 min per sample), and standardized SEM mounting (gentle compaction, airflow removal of loose particles, and gold sputtering). This uniformity ensures comparability across SEM images. The grain size data of SZO and SZZO ceramics were processed and extracted by using Nano Measurer 1.2 software. A Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba Scientific, Japan) with an excitation laser wavelength of 514 nm was employed to confirm the vibrational peaks. An electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectrometer (A300-10/12, Bruker, Germany) was used to measure the quantified oxygen vacancy concentrations of SZO and SZZO.

### 2.3 Performance testing

The infrared emissivities of the SZZO and SZO powders and coatings were measured using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Nicolet iS50; Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) at room temperature. The infrared emissivity of the bare Al sheet in the 8–14 μm waveband at room temperature was measured using an infrared emissometer (IR-2, Shanghai Chengbo Hightech Co., Ltd., China), following the test standard GJB-2502 [62]. The ultraviolet–visible–near infrared (UV–vis–NIR) reflectance spectra of the SZZO and SZO powders and coatings were measured at room temperature in the range of 0.25–2.5 μm via a spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Lambda 950; PerkinElmer, Inc., USA). The preparation process of the samples used for infrared emissivity and UV–vis–NIR reflectance spectra tests is as follows: (i) The powder was dried at 120 °C for 2 h to eliminate moisture; (ii) the dried powder was ground in an agate mortar for 15 min to ensure uniform particle dispersion; (iii) homogenized powders were compacted into bulks (1 mm thickness, 15 MPa pressure) using a hydraulic press; (iv) the UV–Vis–NIR and infrared instruments were calibrated with standard references prior to measurements. Triplicate tests were conducted to verify reproducibility. The reflectivity of the material at 0.3–2.5 μm and the emissivity at 8–13 μm are calculated via the equations shown in the ESM. The band gap widths (*E*g) of all the ceramic powders were calculated from the UV–Vis–NIR reflectance spectra using the Kubelka–Munk equation [63]. The equation is as follows: (*F*(*R*)*hυ*)*α* = *B*(*hυ* − *E*g), where *F*(*R*) is the Kubelka–Munk function; *h* is Planck’s constant; *υ* is the frequency of the photon; *B* is a constant. The value of *α* is 2 for the direct bandgap and 1/2 for the indirect bandgap.

Self-built test equipment was used to test the passive daytime radiative cooling performance of the coating samples. A photograph of the equipment is shown in Fig. S4(a) in the ESM. The equipment included a portable automatic weather station (PC-6, Jinzhou Sunshine Technology Co., Ltd., China) to obtain weather information such as solar irradiance, ambient temperature, and wind speed on the test day. The cuboid test box is made of a high-density foam board with a thickness of 5 cm. In the middle of the test box, there is a sample table made of high-density foam board. The height of the sample table is lower than the height of the test box. A cavity with a length and width of 2.5 cm and a depth of 1 cm is provided under the position where the sample is placed on the sample table. All bare foam panels and cavities are wrapped in aluminum foil with high electromagnetic reflectivity to avoid test interference caused by the absorption of solar radiant energy by the test box or test bench [64,65]. A four-probe TA612C thermocouple thermometer was used to measure the temperature in the space covered by the bare aluminum sheet, the space covered by the sample, and the test box. A laptop was used to read and record the temperature data. In addition, an infrared thermal imager was used to take pictures of the samples under test.

During the test, the probe of the thermocouple thermometer was first fixed in the cavity near the sample, and all the probes were of the same height, as shown in Figs. S4(b) and S4(c) in the ESM. Then, the sample was cut into a square of 3 cm side length (coated side up) and fixed above the cavity on the sample table, and the cavity was completely covered. Finally, a polyethylene (PE) film (PE film has high transmittance of sunlight and atmospheric window band photons) was used to seal the upper opening of the test box, aiming to create a test space for the sample with only radiative heat conduction to obtain more accurate test results and ensure the reliability and accuracy of the test results [27]. The performance test location is on the roof of Building A, Engineering Technology Building, New Town Campus, Inner Mongolia University of Technology, with a longitude of 111.678955°E, latitude of 40.74702°N, and altitude of 1057.6 m. The test time was mainly distributed from mid-September to mid-October 2024.

To further evaluate the passive daytime radiant cooling performance of the coating, the net radiant cooling power and maximum cooling temperature of the coating under different heat conduction coefficients were calculated, and the calculation equations are shown in the ESM [28].

### 2.4 First-principles calculations

The electronic structures of SZO and SZCO-*x* orthorhombic perovskites, including the band structure and density of states, were calculated from first principles using the CASTEP code in Materials Studio. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out with plane-wave ultra-soft pseudo-potentials using the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with Perdew Burke Ehrenzorf (GGA-PBE) functional. The cut-off energy of the plane wave was set to 340 eV. The energy convergence criterion for each atom was set to 1.0×10−6 eV with self-consistent accuracy. The maximum force acting on each atom was limited to 0.03 eV·Å, and the maximum internal stress was limited to 0.05 GPa. Brillouin zone integration was performed using a 4 × 4 × 3 *k*-point mesh, and crystal model optimization was performed using the Monkhorst–Pack scheme.

### 3.1 Structural compositions of Cu-doped SrZrO3 perovskite powders

Figure 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of the SZZO and SZO ceramic powders, revealing the crystal structure characteristics of the prepared ceramic samples. It is evident from the figure that all the prepared ceramic samples exhibit a consistent crystal structure. This result confirms that the orthorhombic perovskite structure of SrZrO3 (JCPDS No. 44-0161) was maintained during the Zn doping process, with no secondary phases observed, which is crucial for the material’s stability and consistency.

*
Fig. 1(a) XRD patterns of SZZO and SZO ceramic powders at 2θ* diffraction angles ranging from 20° to 80°. (b) Characteristic peak between 30.2° and 31°.

Further analysis of the characteristic peak positions shown in Fig. 1(b) reveals a shift in the crystal’s characteristic peaks toward lower angles as Zn is doped. This shift phenomenon gradually increases with increasing Zn doping concentration, providing important information about the changes in the crystal structure. In conjunction with Bragg’s Law, it can be inferred that the low-angle shift of the characteristic peaks indicates an increase in the unit cell volume; that is, the doping has successfully caused lattice expansion. This lattice expansion effect is attributed mainly to the difference in ionic radii between Zr2+ and Zn4+ ions.

Through analysis of the XRD patterns, the effects of Zn doping on the crystal structure and lattice changes of SrZrO3 were thoroughly explored, further confirming the orthorhombic perovskite structure and lattice distortion phenomena of the crystals. However, Zn doping not only affects the lattice parameters but also changes the micro-defect distribution of the material by introducing oxygen vacancies, which profoundly affects the infrared radiative performance of SrZrO3. To further investigate this effect, the oxygen vacancies in the Zn-doped SrZrO3 ceramics were measured and analyzed by EPR spectroscopy.

As shown in Fig. 2, the EPR spectrum exhibits a signal with a *g* value of 2.003—indicative of unpaired electrons interacting with their local chemical environment. Based on this feature, Zn doping is found to introduce oxygen vacancies into the crystal, as represented by the defect equation (Reaction (1)) [66]. The presence of oxygen vacancies disrupts the local symmetry within the crystal, causing distortions or local defects in the crystal lattice, which in turn changes the phonon (lattice vibration) pattern. Eventually, new phonon patterns are introduced, or the intensity of existing phonon patterns is enhanced, thereby enhancing the infrared absorption associated with lattice vibrations. In addition, a comparison of the peak intensities of the EPR spectra reveals that increasing Zn doping significantly increases the concentration of oxygen vacancies in the crystal, which further indicates the regulatory effect of doping on the microstructure of defects.

where ΓRaman is sum of all Raman-active vibrational modes of the crystal under the given symmetry, indicating the types and quantities of phonon modes that appear in the Raman spectrum. Ag is fully symmetric Raman-active modes, invariant under all symmetry operations, corresponding to symmetric stretching or bending vibrations of bonded atoms in the lattice. B1g, B2g, and B3g are non-degenerate Raman-active modes, each associated with vibrations along different principal crystallographic axes, exhibiting antisymmetry under certain mirror or rotational operations. Based on the above-mentioned Raman vibration model of the Pnma structure, 12 different vibration peaks were observed in the SZO and SZZO samples, which proves that the perovskite SZO and SZZO ceramics have ordered lattices. The Raman peak positions of SZO in the range of 700–800 cm−1 disappeared with the doping of Zn ions (Fig. 3(a)), which was attributed to the oxygen vacancies introduced by Zn doping disrupting the long-range order of the oxygen octahedron, resulting in a decrease in lattice symmetry [68]. With increasing doping amount, the Raman peak at 550 cm−1 gradually redshifts (Fig. 3(b)), which is related to the lattice expansion caused by the doping of Zn ions. Lattice expansion causes an increase in the bond length of Zr–O. According to the simple harmonic vibration model, an increase in the bond length will reduce the interatomic force constant, resulting in a decrease in the vibration frequency. Therefore, the Raman results not only confirm that the doping of Zn ions causes the expansion of the SrZrO3 lattice, which is consistent with the results obtained by XRD but also prove that the introduction of oxygen vacancies reduces the lattice symmetry. Lattice expansion and the decline of lattice symmetry significantly affect the optical properties of materials [69].

The microstructure of crystals was further observed by SEM. Figure 4 shows the powder particle morphology characteristics of the ceramic crystals. The particle morphology of the undoped SZO ceramics is irregular; the average grain size is 0.215 μm, and the particles exist in an independent state. With increasing doping amount, the particle morphology gradually changed from irregular particles to rod-like particles and flaky particles, and the particle size gradually increased with increasing doping amount and was distributed in the range of 0.35–1.6 μm. This phenomenon can be attributed to the effect of ZnO produced by the complete decomposition of raw material Zn(C2H3O2)2 above 400 °C [70]. As a typical sintering assistant, ZnO does not completely form a liquid phase at 1200 °C, but it significantly accelerates the adhesion and growth of grains by promoting diffusion and grain boundary migration [71]. Therefore, compared with undoped SrZrO3, Zn doping results in the growth of the crystal particles into rod and flake shapes, and the particle size increases significantly with increasing doping amount.

The elemental composition and atomic ratio of SZZO crystals were further observed and analyzed by EDS. Figure 5 shows the results of an energy spectrum scan of the SZZO-25 crystal. Zn, Sr, Zr and O elements are evenly distributed in the ceramic grains, and the atomic proportions of each element are basically consistent with the expected composition of the chemical formula. Through analysis, it is concluded that there are two factors that determine the enhancement of the solar reflectivity of SrZrO3 crystals by Zn doping. The first factor is the relationship between the absorption coefficient and the reflectance of the material in the 0.3–2.5 μm band with the extinction coefficient (*k*) and the refractive index (*n*).

The refractive index and extinction coefficient of the material in the corresponding band are of great significance when studying the solar reflection performance of the material. These parameters directly affect the interaction between the material and the photon, and thus affect the spectral radiative performance of the material. The refractive index is a physical quantity that describes the influence of a material on the propagation speed of light waves, specifically the ratio of the propagation speed (*c*) of light in a vacuum to the propagation speed (*v*) in a certain medium, as shown in Eq. (3).

(3) n=c/v

The relationship between refractive index and reflectivity is shown in Eq. (4):

(4) R=|n1−n2n1+n2|2

where *R* is the reflectivity; *n*1 is the refractive index of the incident medium, and the refractive index of air is approximately equal to 1; *n*2 is the refractive index of the medium material. From the above equation, it can be concluded that if the refractive index of the medium material is significantly higher or lower than the refractive index of air, its surface will have high reflectivity, thus achieving higher sunlight reflection performance.

In addition, the extinction coefficient indicates the degree to which light is absorbed or attenuated as it travels through the medium. A large *k* value of a material in a specific band usually indicates that the material has a strong absorption capacity for the photons in that band, and this relationship can be reflected by Eq. (5):

(5) k=αλ4π

where *λ* is the wavelength and *α* is the absorption coefficient, which is a local quantity that describes the light absorption capacity of the material and represents the degree of attenuation of light intensity per unit length.

Therefore, the effect of Zn doping on the *n* and *k* of SrZrO3 crystals can be studied to investigate the change in the reflection performance of SZZO ceramics in the solar irradiation band. For this reason, the *n* and *k* values of SrZrO3 crystals before and after Zn doping are calculated by first principles.

Figure 10 shows the *n* and *k* values of the SZZO and SZO crystals calculated from first principles in the 0–2.5 μm band. The results show that the *n* increases gradually with increasing Zn doping amount. According to the Moss relation shown in Eq. (6) [74], the band gap of the crystal is inversely proportional to its refractive index, while the band gap of SZZO decreases with increasing Zn doping amount. Therefore, the refractive index of SZZO increases with increasing Zn doping.

*

(6) n4Eg=0.95eV

According to Eq. (4), the greater the difference in the refractive index between the material and the air, the greater the reflectivity of the material. Therefore, it can be judged from the change in the refractive index that the reflectivity of the SZZO crystal increases in the 0.3–2.5 μm band.

Moreover, in the same band, the *k* value of the SZZO crystals is close to 0, which is consistent with the characteristics of typical passive daytime radiative cooling materials (Al2O3, Si3N4, SiO2, etc.) with near zero extinction coefficients in this band [27]. Combined with Eq. (5), the absorption coefficient *α* of the material in the 0.3–2.5 μm band is very small.

The second factor by which Zn doping enhances the solar reflectance of SrZrO3 crystals is related to the change in grain morphology after Zn doping. According to Fig. 4, after Zn doping, the micro-grain morphology of the doped material changes due to the sintering aid of ZnO. From the existence state between the grains and adjacent grains, the grains of undoped SrZrO3 exist in an independent state, and the interface between independent particles may cause light to be scattered from the surface of the material; however, at the same time, it will also increase the absorption of some light, as shown in Fig. 11(a). After Zn is doped, the grains bond with adjacent grains, result in rod-like and flaky grain structures, both of which increase the continuity of the grain surface, helping to reduce the absorption path of light and increasing specular reflection in a specific direction, thereby improving the overall reflectivity, as shown in Fig. 11(b). Moreover, the adhesion of the grains leads to an increase in the density of the material, which reduces the chance of light being scattered and absorbed. Under the combined action of the above two factors, the solar reflectivity of SZZO is greater than that of SrZrO3.

*

In addition, the results in Table 1 also show that the emissivity of SZO in the 8–13 μm band is 0.922. With the doping of Zn and a further increase in the doping amount, the emissivity of the SZZO crystal gradually increases from 0.929 for SZCO-6.25 to 0.954 for SZCO-25. The addition of Zn can increase the emissivity of the SrZrO3 crystal in the infrared window, which is attributed to the lattice distortion caused by the difference in the radii of Zn2+ and Zr4+ and the decrease in the lattice symmetry caused by the oxygen vacancy introduced by the valence difference. The enhancement effect of these two lattice changes on the infrared radiative performance increases with increasing amount of Zn.

An analysis on the data in Fig. 9(b) reveals that the emissivity of the SZZO crystal gradually increases with increasing Zn doping amount, especially in the 9–13 μm band. As mentioned earlier, the absorption of infrared photons in the 8–13 μm band by crystals is achieved mainly through lattice vibration, which is closely related to phonon behavior. Debye model, as a theoretical model describing phonon behavior in solids, can provide a theoretical basis for analyzing the specific effects of Zn doping on the infrared photon absorption of SrZrO3 crystals [75]. The Debye temperature is a characteristic temperature related to phonon properties. Therefore, the effect of Zn doping on the infrared photon absorption of SrZrO3 crystals can be analyzed from the change in the Debye temperature (Θ*D), which is defined as Eq. (7) [76]:

(7) ΘD=ℏυDkB(6π2NV)1/3

where * ℏ is the reduced Planck constant; υ*D is the Debye phonon velocity; *k*B is the Boltzmann constant; *N* is the number of atoms; *V* is the volume of the cell. After the incorporation of Zn into SrZrO3, the generation of oxygen vacancies in the crystal and the increase in the number of electrons enhance phonon scattering in the crystal and reduce the mean free path of phonons, resulting in a decrease in *υ*D. Moreover, Zn doping can reduce the number of atoms in the cell and increase the volume of the cell. Combining these factors, according to Eq. (7), it is concluded that Zn doping can lead to a decrease in the Debye temperature of the SrZrO3 crystal. A reduced Debye temperature means that the energy of the phonons in the material decreases and the number of low-frequency phonons increases. These low-frequency phonons interact more easily with low-energy (long-wavelength) infrared photons, thereby increasing the absorption intensity of the crystal for long-band infrared photons. Therefore, with increasing Zn doping amount, the radiative intensity of the SZZO crystal in the 9–13 μm band gradually increases, as shown in Fig. 9(b).

Figure 15 shows a picture of the sample obtained via an infrared thermal imager during the passive daytime radiative cooling performance test. To analyze the figure, it is necessary to understand the test principle of the C200 infrared thermal imager. The infrared thermal imager used in the experiment needs to set a calibration emissivity before the test, and then the instrument receives the infrared radiative signal from the sample during the test and calculates the infrared radiative intensity value P* of the sample according to the intensity of the signal. Finally, the radiative intensity is substituted into the equation of the Stefan–Boltzmann law (* T∝(P/ϵ)1/4) to calculate the surface temperature T* of the sample. Since there are certain differences in the infrared emissivities of the bare aluminum sheet, SZO coating, and SZZO-25 coating, the calibration emissivity is set to a value close to that of the SZZO-25 coating, that is, 0.95. The test results indicate that the surface temperature of the bare aluminum sheet is the lowest, while the surface temperature of the SZZO-25 coating is the highest. This is because the low emissivity of the bare aluminum sheet itself leads to the low radiative energy released during the test process, so the radiative intensity received by the infrared thermal imager from the bare aluminum sheet is small. When the smaller infrared radiative intensity value matches the high calibration emissivity of 0.95, the surface temperature value of the bare aluminum sheet is lower. The surface of the SZZO-25 coating emits more radiative energy during the test, so its surface temperature is also higher in the test results. The thickness of the passive daytime radiative cooling coating has a certain influence on its radiative cooling performance. If the coating is too thin, the radiative cooling particles in the coating cannot form an effective multistage scattering network, resulting in reduced solar reflectivity, and even the substrate may interfere with the radiative cooling performance. If the coating is too thick, it may not improve the radiative cooling performance but may increase the amount of coating material, and may also cause the coating to crack or flake because of uneven solvent volatilization. Therefore, SZZO-25 coatings with different thicknesses of 70/100/130 μm were prepared, and the SEM images of the coating interface are shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM. The passive daytime radiative cooling performance of SZZO-25 coatings of different thicknesses was investigated, and the test results are shown in Fig. 16. As shown in Fig. 16(b), the radiant cooling temperature of the 70 μm thick SZZO-25 coating is approximately 9.1 °C atsolar radiative intensity of approximately 600 W·m−2. When the coating thickness is increased to 100 μm, the radiant cooling temperature can reach approximately 9.8 °C. When the coating thickness is increased to 130 μm, the radiative cooling temperature is the same as that of the 100 μm coating. Therefore, when the thickness of the SZZO-25 coating is approximately 100 μm, the coating can achieve the best radiative cooling performance, and a continuous increase in thickness does not improve the performance but increases the coating cost.

### 3.5 Performance stability of SZZO coating after prolonged outdoor exposure

Because passive daytime radiation-cooled coatings need to be in a state of long-term outdoor exposure, the performance stability of the SZZO-25 coating needs to be examined. For this purpose, two SZZO-25 coating samples with a thickness of 100 μm were fixed to a tilted concrete column on the roof of a building by adhesive backing (Fig. 20(a)), so that the coating was subjected to outdoor exposure for a period of 21 d, and the surface state of the coating was photographed and recorded, as shown in Figs. 20(b)–20(j). It rained on the fourth day of exposure, so grit spattered onto the surface of the coating, as shown in the photographs of the coating recorded on the fifth day. With increasing exposure time, the grit on the coating surface gradually decreased and disappeared due to wind erosion. In addition, the hydrophobicity of the coatings before and after exposure was also examined, as shown in Figs. 20(k) and 20(j). The results showed that the unexposed coating had a contact angle of 142.7° and exhibited high hydrophobicity. However, the hydrophobicity of the coating after 21 d of exposure decreased to 90°. It is believed that the high hydrophobicity of the original coating originated from the PVDF on the surface of the coating [81], but long-term exposure of the coating to ultraviolet light may have triggered the breakage of the C–F bond, generating degradation products (e.g., –OH) containing hydrophilic groups, which reduced the hydrophobicity of the coating. In addition, atmospheric PM2.5 (particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter ≤ 2.5 μm, such as sulfates and nitrates) deposits on the coating surface and penetrates its pores by capillary action. These hydrophilic substances may also diminish the coating’s hydrophobicity.

In addition to being prepared for coating applications by combining with adhesives, Zn-modified SrZrO3-based crystals also have a broader range of service scenarios. For example, a photovoltaic power station is equipped with containers containing inverters. The ambient temperature at noon in summer is above 37 °C. From June to August, during the period from 11:00 to 14:30 every day, the operating temperature of the insulated-gate bipolar transistor (IGBT) module of the inverter during operation can reach above 95 °C, and the inverters of the power stations are generally in a high-temperature operating state. As key equipment for power generation in power stations, inverters will trigger self-protection mechanisms in high-temperature environments, leading to reduced load operation and a significant decrease in power generation. Zn-doped modified SrZrO3-based materials, with their high melting point and excellent chemical inertness, demonstrate engineering adaptability; single-component radiant cooling coatings can be directly prepared on the surface of metal containers through plasma spraying or flame spraying processes. This material does not undergo phase change or decomposition during the spraying process and can form strong bonds with the substrate, ensuring mechanical stability for long-term outdoor service. The inherent corrosion resistance of SrZrO3-based ceramics can withstand the erosion of harsh environments such as acid rain and salt spray. The high emissivity in the 8–13 μm band combined with high solar reflectivity enables the coating to reduce the surface temperature of the box under AM1.5 irradiation, thereby reducing the temperature rise loss of equipment such as photovoltaic inverters. Unlike traditional polymer-based composite coatings, this inorganic ceramic coating does not require organic adhesives, thus avoiding performance degradation caused by ultraviolet aging. It has significant application advantages in high-temperature and high-humidity scenarios such as desert power stations and coastal photovoltaic farms.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, Zn-doped SrZrO3 powders were prepared by the sol–gel method combined with solid-phase synthesis method. The phase structure, micromorphology, oxygen vacancy changes, and spectral radiative properties of Zn-doped SrZrO3 crystal ceramics, including the reflection properties of the 0.3–2.5 μm band and the radiative properties of the 8–13 μm band, were studied. The effects of changes in the electronic structure on the optical properties of doped crystals were also discussed. The results showed that Zn2+ in all the samples completely entered the SrZrO3 lattice to replace Zr4+, and all the elements were evenly distributed in the crystal; and the final product maintained the original ortho-phase perovskite structure of the crystal. In addition, with increasing doping amount of Zn, the microstructure of the grains is significantly changed by the sintering effect of ZnO, from independent grains to fine rod, coarse rod, small sheet, and large sheet grains. The change in the grain microstructure improves the specular reflectance of the crystals to 0.3–2.5 μm band photons. Moreover, although the band gap of the Zn-doped SrZrO3 crystals is reduced, it is still greater than 5.5 eV, maintaining the characteristics of the wide-band gap of SrZrO3 crystals. The slightly reduced band gap effectively improves the refractive index of the crystals in the sunlight band, thus effectively improving the reflectivity of the crystals to the photons in the 0.3–2.5 μm band. In addition, the lattice distortion caused by Zn doping and the decrease in lattice symmetry caused by oxygen vacancies have an enhanced effect on the crystal emissivity in the 8–13 μm band. The spectral radiative performance test shows that the reflectance of the Zn-doped SrZrO3 crystals in the 0.3–2.5 μm band and the emissivity in the 8–13 μm band improved. The solar reflectance of the SrZr0.75Zn0.25O2.75 crystal is 0.892, whereas the atmospheric window emissivity reaches 0.954.

Passive daytime radiative cooling coatings using SrZr0.75Zn0.25O2.75 and SrZrO3 ceramic powder as radiating refrigerants were prepared by the scraping coating method. The passive daytime radiative cooling properties of the coatings were investigated. Under solar irradiation at 503 W·m−2, the SrZr0.75Zn0.25O2.75 coating can achieve a cooling temperature of 9.1 °C, which is higher than the radiative cooling temperature of the SrZrO3 coating (7.8 °C). Under 600 W·m−2 solar irradiation, the radiative cooling performance of the coating with a thickness of 100 μm (9.8 °C) is greater than that of the coating with a thickness of 70 μm (9.1 °C), and the radiative cooling performance does not change significantly when the coating thickness continues to increase to 130 μm. Under 654 W·m−2 solar irradiation, the maximum radiant cooling temperature of the SrZr0.75Zn0.25O2.75 coating with a thickness of 100 μm reaches 15.3 °C, and the maximum net radiant cooling power reaches 64.7 W·m−2. The results show that the SrZr0.75Zn0.25O2.75 coating has excellent passive daytime radiative cooling performance. Moreover, the SZZO-25 coating maintains stable passive daytime radiative cooling performance after 21 d of weathering.
