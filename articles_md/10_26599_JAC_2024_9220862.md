## 1 Introduction

Materials scientists have been enchanted by the search for new materials that exceed the temperature capability of Ni-based superalloys for many decades [1]. High melting point and solidus temperatures and elevated temperature strength endow refractory metals (RM) and their alloys materials of choice for this purpose [2]. However, poor oxidation resistance is the main drawback that impedes their applications in oxygen containing environments due to the formation of porous and fasting growing oxides or volatile oxides [3]. Recently introduced new design paradigm of high-entropy alloys has enabled the overcome of such a shortcoming possible due to the vast opened composition space [4,5]. In line with this new design paradigm, Senkov *et al*. [6−8] have shown that refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) exhibit outstanding mechanical properties beyond 1200 ℃, which are promising for high temperature extreme environment applications such as in gas turbines. It is noteworthy that some RHEAs exhibit good oxidation resistance through the formation of either well-known protective scale such as Al2O3 [9,10] or rarely encountered complex oxides such as CrTaO4 and CrNbO4 [11−13]. Most particularly, the protective CrTaO4 scale formed on RHEAs displays a unique combination of fascinating properties including: (1) easy formation in a wide temperature range from 500 to 1000 ℃, (2) good adherence to the RHEA matrix after cooling, (3) slow growth rate, (4) effective prevention of the outward diffusion of metal cations, and (5) wide range of composition stability that suppresses the formation of non-protective oxides. In addition, the oxidation kinetics of CrTaO4 forming RHEAs, calculated by Gorr *et al*. [12] using the kinetic data of the equiatomic alloy TaMoCrTiAl at 1000 ℃, seems to approach the oxidation resistant Cr2O3 and Al2O3 forming Ni-based superalloys. This surprising discovery confirms that CrTaO4 plays a decisive role in protecting the oxidation of Cr and Ta containing RHEAs at high temperatures.

Despite the vast reports on the oxidation behavior of RHEAs, the microstructure, elastic/mechanical and thermal properties of the protective complex oxide CrTaO4 have yet to be studied, and filling this research gap is imperative since this complex oxide has been overlooked for decades. As such, the leitmotif of this study is to explore the untapped elastic/mechanical and thermal properties of CrTaO4. In addition, the unique combination of the properties of this complex oxide also inspires us to explore the lucrative possibility of leveraging it as a new thermal barrier material. So the other goal of this work is to foreshadow the new application of CrTaO4 as a thermal barrier material with good high temperature stability and low thermal conductivity [14]. In line with these goals, this study is structured as follows. Section 2 devotes the experimental methods. In Section 3, synthesis of powder and bulk of CrTaO4 as well as microstructure and mechanical properties including elastic modulus, hardness, strength, fracture toughness, and thermal-physical properties including melting point, thermal expansion coefficient, and thermal conductivity are described. In Section 4, the possibility of using this material as a new thermal barrier material is discussed. Finally, conclusion remarks are given in Section 5.

### 2.1 Preparation of CrTaO<sub>4 </sub>powders and dense bulk

The CrTaO4 powders were prepared by solid-state reaction method using Cr2O3 and Ta2O5 as starting materials. Firstly, near-stoichiometric Cr2O3 and Ta2O5 powders were mixed in anhydrous ethanol and ball milled with ZrO2 balls as milling medium. After mixing at 80−100 r/min for 8 h, the uniformly mixed slurry was obtained. The slurry was then dried in an oven at 80 ℃ for 12 h and the mixed powders were obtained by sieving through 400 mesh. Secondly, solid state reaction was conducted according to Reaction (1). To optimize the reaction temperature and reaction time, a coupled thermal gravimetric (TG) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis was conducted from which the reaction temperature for the formation of CrTaO4 was found.

(1) Cr2O3+Ta2O5→2CrTaO4

Based on the TG-DSC analysis and phase composition of the reaction product examined by XRD, solid state reaction synthesis of CrTaO4 was conducted by placing the mixed Cr2O3 and Ta2O5 powders in an alumina crucible and heated to 1120 ℃ in air at a rate of 5 ℃/min and held for 2 h. The phase composition of the as-synthesized CrTaO4 powders was examined in an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Rigaku Ultima IV, Japan) and the microstructure and particle size distribution were analyzed in a scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM-7001F, Japan).

Bulk CrTaO4 was prepared by hot-press sintering the CrTaO4 powders in flowing argon at 1400 ℃ under 30 MPa for 40 min. The heating rate was 10 ℃/min and the hot pressing mold was graphite. After cooling down to room temperature, the surface contaminants were removed using grinding mills. The density of bulk CrTaO4 was measured by the Archimedes method. The phase composition of the bulk CrTaO4 was analyzed by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Rigaku Ultima IV, Japan). The scanning speed was 1 (°)/min with a step size of 0.02°. In addition, the lattice parameters, peak positions (2*θ*), and peak intensities for (*hkl*) reflections of CrTaO4 were refined by using the Reflex code in Materials Studio software suit (Accelrys Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The microstructures of the samples were observed in a JEOL field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM-7001F, Japan). For detailed microstructure analysis, transmission electron microscopic (TEM) analysis was conducted in an FEI Tecnai F30 and atomic-resolution high-annular dark field (HAADF) and the annular bright field (ABF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) was performed in a Thermofisher Spectra 300 aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope.

### 2.2 Elastic/mechanical properties of bulk CrTaO<sub>4</sub>

The Young’s modulus, shear modulus, and Poisson’s ratio of CrTaO4 were evaluated by the impulse excitation technique [15]. The rectangular beam-like sample with dimensions of 3 mm ×15 mm × 40 mm was used. The Young’s modulus and shear modulus were calculated from the flexural resonate frequency and the torsional resonate frequency, respectively, according to ASTM E1876-97.

(2) E=0.9465(mfF2w)(L3δ3)C1

(3) G=4LmfT2wδ(X1+Y)

where *E* and *G* are the Young’s modulus and shear modulus in Pa, respectively; *m* is the mass of rectangular bar in kg; *f*F and *f*T are the fundamental flexural resonant frequency and torsional resonate frequency in Hz, respectively; *w*, *L*, and *δ* are the width, length, and thickness of the rectangular bar in m, respectively; *C*1 is a correction factor that depends on the thickness/length ratio *δ*/*L* and Poisson’s ratio; and *X* and *Y* are two correction factors that depend on the width/thickness ratio *w*/*δ*. Using the measured *E* and *G*, the Poisson's ratio (*v*) and bulk modulus (*B*) were calculated according to Eqs. (4) and (5) [16,17]:

(4) v=E2G−1

(5) B=EG3(3G−E)

The Vickers hardness (*H*V) was measured by a microhardness tester (YZHV-1000P, Shanghai Yizong, China). The load applied on the polished sample was 0.49, 0.98, 1.96, 2.94, 4.90, and 9.80 N, and the dwell time was 15 s. The *H*V was calculated according to Eq. (6) [18]:

(6) HV=PS=2Psin(θ2)d2=1854.4Pd2

where *P* is the load, *S* is the area of the indentation, *θ* is the angle between opposite face of the intender, and *d* refers to the diagonal length of the indent.

Fracture toughness and flexural strength were measured in a universal testing machine (MTS-CriterioC45.105, USA). The flexural strength was determined via a three-point bending test using sample with the dimensions of 3 mm × 4 mm × 40 mm. The span of the test was 30 mm. The flexural strength was calculated by Eq. (7) [19]:

(7) σf=3Fl2bh2

where *F* is the load, *l* is the length of the span, *b* is the width, and *h* is the thickness of the sample. The fracture toughness was measured using the single edge V-notched beam (SEVNB) specimens with the dimension of 3 mm × 4 mm × 40 mm at room temperature. The V-notches with a sharp tip radius of *ca*. 8 μm and notch depth of *ca*. 810 μm were cut at the center of the 3 mm × 40 mm surfaces of the rectangular samples by automated means with a razor blade coated with diamond paste before testing. The fracture toughness of the material was calculated by Eq. (8) [20]:

(8) KIvnb=Y3Pmax(S0−Si)α2wh1.5(1−α)1.5

(9) Y=1.9887−1.326α−(3.49−0.68α+1.35α2)α(1−α)(1+α)−2

where *Y* is a stress intensity shape factor; *P*max is the maximum force at which fracture occurred; *S*0 and *S*i are the outer and inner span, respectively; *w* and *h* represent the width and height of the specimen, respectively; and * α equals to d*V/*h* (*d*V is the V-notches depth).

### 2.3 Thermal properties of CrTaO<sub>4</sub>

The melting point of CrTaO4 was measured by direct observing the shape and volume change during the laser heating process in a system established at Shanghai Institute of Ceramics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The bulk sample was put in a graphite crucible, which was placed in a vacuum chamber. The graphite crucible was heated by four laser beams arranged symmetrically and the temperature was measured by an infrared pyrometer. The volume and shape changes were monitored by an optical system and the melting point was obtained at the temperature when the solid sample was observed collapse.

The average linear TEC of bulk CrTaO4 was obtained from the temperature dependent length changes from 298 to 1473 K in air by using an optical dilatometer (Misura ODHT1600-50, Expert System Solutions, Italy). The sample size was 3 mm × 4 mm × 15 mm with a 45° chamfer cutting at one end of the sample. The average linear TEC of CrTaO4 was calculated according to Eq. (10):

(10) α=L−L298K(T−298)L298K

The anisotropic thermal expansion coefficients of CrTaO4 were determined from the high-temperature XRD data. The powder sample was heated stepwise from room temperature to 1473 K at a rate of 10 K/min in a heating plate of a high temperature X-ray diffractometer (Xper't Pro MPD; PANalytical). To achieve temperature equilibrium, XRD data were not collected until the temperature reached the pre-set value for 5 min.

The thermal conductivity of CrTaO4 was determined from the thermal diffusivity *D*th, heat capacity, and density. The thermal diffusivity (*D*th) was measured by the laser flash method (LFA457, NETZSCH, Germany) from 298 to 1473 K. Referring to the procedure described in ASTM E1461-01, the heat pulse was sprayed on one side of the sample and the temperature rise was monitored on the other side. Before the measurement, the sample was sprayed with a thin layer of graphite to prevent the laser beam from direct transmission through the sample. The heat capacity (*c**p*) was calculated based on the data of its constituent oxides according to the Neumann–Kopp rule [21,22]. The thermal conductivity (*κ*) was calculated from the relationship between thermal diffusivity (*D*th), heat capacity (*c**p*) and density (*ρ*) as Eq. (11) [23]:

(11) κ(T)=Dth(T)cp(T)ρ(T)

Among them, the variation of density with temperature can be calculated using the volumetric TEC.

### 3.1 Synthesis and characterization of CrTaO<sub>4</sub>

Since the synthesis of a phase-pure material is the first step for investigating its properties, solid-state reaction synthesis of CrTaO4 from Cr2O3 and Ta2O5 was conducted firstly. To achieve such a goal, a coupled TG-DSC experiment was performed, which is instructive to optimize the synthesis parameters. Figure 1(a) exhibits the TG-DSC curves of the Cr2O3/Ta2O5 powders heated up to 1400 ℃ at a rate of 10 ℃/min in flowing air. From Fig. 1(a), one can see that the hallmark in the TG curve is a mass loss of about 0.358%, which can be attributed to the evaporation of Cr2O3 at high temperatures [24]. In the DSC curve, there is a wide exothermic peak in the temperature range of 1030−1110 ℃. The presence of this hump is an indication that the reaction between Cr2O3 and Ta2O5 powders starts at 1030 ℃ and ends at 1110 ℃. Furthermore, in the temperature range of 1150−1400 ℃ there is no discernible presence of endothermic or exothermic peak in the DCS curve and weight loss in the TG curve, indicating that CrTaO4 has good thermal stability.

Having understood the reaction temperature from the coupled TG-DSC curves, we next set forth to explore the synthesis parameters of CrTaO4. Based on the temperature range alluded to above, we select 1120 ℃ as the temperature to synthesize CrTaO4 powders, in agreement with the previous work of Androš Dubraja et al*. [25] who used oxalate to synthesize CrTaO4. In addition, to compensate the weight loss of Cr2O3 during the heating process, a small amount of excess Cr2O3 was added. As such, the mole ratio of the starting materials was set Cr2O3 : Ta2O5 = 1.01 : 1. Phase composition analysis by XRD indicates that after reaction at 1120 ℃ for shorter time such as 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 h, residual germane Cr2O3 and Ta2O5 can be detected indicating incompletion of the solid-state reaction. As the reaction goes to completion at 1120 ℃ for 2 h, only CrTaO4 can be detected and there is no discernible presence of either Cr2O3 or Ta2O5, which is a clear indication that phase-pure CrTaO4 powders are obtained. Figure 1(b) compares the experimental XRD pattern of CrTaO4 synthesized at 1120 ℃ for 2 h with the simulated XRD pattern obtained from the Reflex code in the Materials Studio software suit (Accelrys Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). One can see that the experimental XRD pattern is consistent with the simulated one in both peak position and intensity, without any detectable impurity phases, denoting that the product prepared at 1120 ℃ for 2 h is phase-pure CrTaO4. On the ground of the above results, the optimized synthesis parameters were selected as 1120 ℃ for 2 h for the solid-state reaction synthesis of CrTaO4, which is almost coincided with that reported in Ref. [14]. Figure 1(c) shows the SEM image of CrTaO4 powders synthesized at 1120 ℃ for 2 h. As shown in Fig. 1(d), the particle size has a narrow distribution with an average size of 0.55 μm determined by using Image J software. Such a small and uniform particle size is favorable for the consolidation to prepare bulk material.

To further confirm the powders synthesized at 1120 ℃ for 2 h are CrTaO4, Rietveld refinement was conducted. CrTaO4 crystallizes in rutile structure with tetragonal symmetry and a space group *P*42/*mnm* (No. 136) [26]. The crystal structure of CrTaO4 is shown as an inset in Fig. 2. The Ta5+/Cr3+ cations occupy one half of octahedral sites and are randomly distributed. The TaO₆/CrO6 octahedra are edge-shared in *c* direction forming infinite chains and each edge-shared octahedral is corner-shared by oxygen. Because this rutile-structured CrTaO4 went overlooked since its discovery, giving a complete set of XRD data is of paramount significance for further study of this intriguing material. ### 3.2 Microstructure of bulk CrTaO<sub>4 </sub>

With the availability of dense bulk, investigation on the microstructure becomes imminent to shed light on the structure−property relationship of CrTaO4. In this work, bulk CrTaO4 was prepared by hot-press sintering at 1400 ℃ under 30 MPa for 40 min, which was optimized by monitoring the shrinkage versus temperature curve during hot-pressing process. The density measured by the Archimedes method is 7.43 g/cm3, which is 98% of the theoretical value (7.58 g/cm3). Figure 3(a) shows the XRD pattern collected from the surface of the bulk CrTaO4 sample. No other phase but CrTaO4 was observable. Careful comparison in XRD pattern between the bulk CrTaO4 prepared in this work with the simulated one demonstrates that the CrTaO4 is phase-pure and isotropic. Figure 3(b) shows an SEM image taken from the fracture surface of bulk CrTaO4, where neither micropores nor microcracks are visible within the field of view, indicating that the bulk CrTaO4 is near fully dense, being consistent with the measured density of 98%. Figure 3(c) shows the gain size distribution collected from Fig. 3(b) using the Image J software. It is seen that the grains are small with a narrow size distribution. The average gain size is statistically determined to be 2.07±0.73 μm. Figure 3(d) is a high magnification SEM image, showing that the equiaxed gains are highly crystallized with well-defined faceted shapes. These unique microstructure characteristics are strongly related to the crystal structure of CrTaO4. According to Donnay and Harker [29], the morphology of a crystal depends on the relative growth rate of different crystallographic planes. For rutile-structured CrTaO4, the morphology is determined by the relative growth rate of (110) and (101) planes. Using the Bravais–Friedel−Donnay–Harker method, the equilibrium morphology was generated through Morphology code in Materials Studio software suit (Accelrys Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). In Fig. 3(d), the simulated morphology intuitively shows the crystallite shape and the faceted faces corresponding to (110) and (101) planes.

To get a better insight into the microstructure−property relationship, atomic-resolution imaging and chemical mapping were performed. Figures 5(a) and 5(b) are high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) and annular bright-filed (ABF)-scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images of rutile-structured CrTaO4 in [001] zone axis and Figs. 5(c) and 5(d) are the HAADF and ABF-STEM images of rutile-structured CrTaO4 in [100] zone axis. The atomistic structures are overlaid on Figs. 5(b) and 5(d). In HAADF images (Figs. 5(a) and 5(c)), atomic columns appear as peaks, the brightness of which scales strongly with the atomic number of the atoms in the column; while in the annular bright-field (ABF) images (Figs. 5(b) and 5(d)), the atomic columns appear as dark troughs: The locations of O columns are evident. The HAADF and ABF images in Fig. 5(a) and 5(b) are very similar to those of TiO2 [001], confirming the rutile structure of CrTaO4 [30]. From Fig. 5(b), it can be seen the TaO₆/CrO6 octahedra are corner-shared by oxygen. From Figs. 5(c) and 5(d), alternative stacking of TaO₆/CrO6 octahedra in [001] direction can be observed, i.e., only one half of the octahetra are occupied by Ta/Cr cations. Two types of Cr/Ta–O bond lengths can be distinguished, which are 1.968 and 2.002 Å, respectively (see the overlaid atomistic structure in Fig. 5(d). Intriguingly, from the intensity (brightness) of atomic columns in Fig. 5(c), one may speculate that Ta and Cr atoms are not random distributed. In other words, there might be short-range order (SRO) in CrTaO4 since it can be written as (Cr0.5Ta0.5O)2 and is actually a solid solution with the (0,0,0) site equally shared by Ta and Cr in the rutile structure. To reveal the SRO, further STEM analysis were conducted. Figure 5(e) shows an HAADF lattice image of CrTaO4 in [001] zone axis and the inset is the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern at the [001] zone axis. Extra reflections (circled in red) halfway between the transmission spot (000) and {110} spot can be clearly observed, indicating the presence of SRO. To verify this hypothesis, we take a closer look at the cracks produced at the corners of the indent produced under a 9.80 N load in SEM. One can see from Fig. 8(b) that the cracks produced at the four corners of the indent are asymmetrical with not only different crack lengths but also different crack openings. In addition, zigzag crack propagation paths are obvious and crack bridging can also be observed, revealing that CrTaO4 is a damage tolerant ceramic. To unravel the mechanism of the crack propagation behavior, high magnification SEM images are vital. In Figs. 8(c) and 8(d), crack branching and crack deflection are evident, which are hallmarks of damage tolerant ceramics. Of particular importance, the cracks propagate along the grain boundaries, evident by the crack paths along the grain boundaries (Fig. 8(c)) and pull-out of some small grains on the crack paths (Fig. 8(d)). On the fracture surface shown in Fig. 3(d), many convex and concave features are present, which also indicate that some crack deflection and grain pull-out have been occurred. Thus, the damage tolerance of CrTaO4 can be attributed to interwind effects of faceted grains and weak grain boundaries on the propagating cracks in accordance with the intergranular fracture behavior.

### 3.4 Thermal properties of CrTaO<sub>4</sub>

Exploring the thermal properties of CrTaO4 is instructive not only to the understanding of the protective role for its formation on RHEAs but also to elucidate the possibility of leveraging CrTaO4 as a new thermal barrier material. With these goals in mind, the thermal properties of CrTaO4, such as melting point (m.p.), thermal expansion coefficient (TEC), and thermal conductivity (*κ*) are studied herein. Furthermore, fundamental properties such as Debye temperature (*θ*D), heat capacity (*c**p*) as well as sound velocities are provided, which are instructive for the further investigation and application of this unknown material.

It can be seen that the peak positions continuously shift to low angle directions with temperature increasing due to the lattice expansion. Since the thermal conductivity of most oxides is almost inversely proportional to temperature, least squares fit of the data yields Eq. (23):

(23) κ=0.501+230/T

The *R*2 value of the fitted curve is 0.989. The thermal conductivity of CrTaO4 at room temperature is 1.31 W·m−1·K−1, which is much lower than other reported TBC materials, such as YSZ [48] (3.06 W·m−1·K−1), Yb3Al5O12 [35] (4.67 W·m−1·K−1), and γ-Y2Si2O7 [49] (4.91 W·m−1·K−1). As the temperature increases, the thermal conductivity of CrTaO4 decreases slowly and reaches 0.66 W·m−1·K−1 at 1473 K.

Debye uses the analogy of gas dynamics theory to derive the lattice thermal conductivity of dielectric solids, which can be expressed as [50]:

(24) κ=13Cvλ

where *C* is the heat capacity, *v* is the speed of sound, and *λ* is the phonon mean free path. Above the Debye temperature, the heat capacity and sound velocity are approximately constant, while the average phonon free path of the crystalline material is nearly proportional to 1/*T* [51]. In this case, the thermal conductivity of CrTaO4 can be fitted by a 1/*T* equation in the temperature range of 560–1473 K. At temperatures above 1473 K, the thermal conductivity should yield a platform and become independent of temperature. For most materials, such a plateau value of thermal conductivity reaches the minimum thermal conductivity *κ*min, which can be obtained using Clarke’s model [53]:

(25) κmin→0.87kBNA2/3m2/3ρ1/6E1/2M2/3

where *κ*B is the Boltzmann constant, *N*A is Avogadro’s constant, *m* is the number of atoms in the primitive cell, *ρ* is the density, *E* is the Young’s modulus, and *M* is the molecular mass. In order to accurately estimate the lower limit of the intrinsic *κ*min of CrTaO4, the modified Clarke model reported by Liu *et al.* [53] is used here, which can be expressed as

(26) κmin→kBvm(MnρNA)−23

For CrTaO4, the estimated *κ*min using the modified Clarke’s model of Liu *et al.* [53], as illustrated by Eq. (26), is 0.55 W·m−1·K−1. At 1473 K, the experimentally measured value of *κ* is 0.66 W·m−1·K−1, which is slightly higher than the estimated *κ*min value but still close to it. The measured *κ*min is higher than the evaluated one is common for most oxides. The reason may be that the testing temperature (1473 K) is relatively low and does not approach the value that thermal conductivity saturates to *κ*min.

## 4 Discussion

The high melting point, good high temperature stability, low thermal conductivity, moderate TEC, and similar mechanical properties to YSZ allowed detailed discussions on the mechanisms that underpin these properties. These discussions not only are instructive in understanding the protectiveness displayed by CrTaO4 scales formed on RHEAs but also inspire us to further explore a scenario of utilizing it as a new thermal barrier material.

Trying to extent this line of research, we embark on the study of matched TEC of CrTaO4 with the candidate high-temperature materials. To achieve such a goal, Fig. 11(a) compiles the TEC and Young’s modulus of some current investigated TBC materials, refractory metals, typical RHEAs, and ultrahigh temperature ceramics (UHTCs). TEC is one of the key parameters of TBCs since the matched TEC with the substrate material can effectively alleviate the thermal stress and improve the cycle life of the coating. From Fig. 11(a), it is noteworthy that the TEC of CrTaO4 is much lower than that of currently investigated TBC materials [42,54−56] on superalloys, such as YSZ (10.5×10−6 K−1), Tm4Hf3O12 (9.57×10−6 K−1), La2Hf2O7 (8.76×10−6 K−1), Yb3Al5O12 (8.22×10−6 K−1), Y4Al2O9 (7.51×10−6 K−1), La2Zr2O7 (9×10−6 K−1) and BaZrO3 (7.72×10−6 K−1) but higher than that of SiC (4.94×10−6 K−1) [36], mullite (5.18×10−6 K−1) [57], β-Y2Si2O7 (4.9×10−6 K−1) [58], Y2Si2O7 (3.9×10−6 K−1) [59], and other rare earth silicates [60]. Of particular interest, the TEC of CrTaO4 is close to some refractory alloys such as Ta (6.3×10−6 K−1) and Nb (7.3×10−6 K−1) [2] and UHTCs such as ZrB2 (6.8×10−6 K−1), HfB2 (6.3×10−6 K−1) [61], ZrC (6.9×10−6 K−1), NbC (6.8×10−6 K−1), and TaC (6.6×10−6 K−1) [62]. Therefore, from the perspective of matched TECs, CrTaO4 is promising as protective and TBC for refractory metals such as Ta and Nb and their alloys as well as UHTCs such as TaC, NbC, ZrC, ZrB2, and HfB2. It has come to light that the protectiveness and stability of CrTaO4 formed on RHEAs is attributed to the similar growth kinetics of CrTaO4 to that of Al2O3 and Cr2O3 forming Ni-based superalloys and the effective prevention of the outward diffusion of metal cations through the CrTaO4 scale. The comparison on TEC and Young’s modulus indicates that the closed TEC of CrTaO4 to refractory metals is another indispensable factor to cater the stability of the CrTaO4 scale on RHEAs. The above results are appealing because it not only explains the stability and protectiveness that displayed by the CrTaO4 scale formed on RHEAs but also outreach the possible application of this complex oxide.

For thermal barrier applications, low thermal conductivity in the application temperatures is another important gauge to judge the suitability of a candidate material. To achieve effective thermal insulating, there is a perpetual need for materials with low thermal conductivities. Depending on the matched TEC with the substrate materials, a variety of eligible low thermal conducting materials including rare earth zirconates, fluorite derivate pyrochlore, perovskites, rare earth silicates, and rare earth aluminates have been developed other than the widely used YSZ [63−66]. As shown in Fig. 11(b), the thermal conductivity of CrTaO4 is lower than that of currently well-known thermal barrier materials. The low thermal conductivity at low temperatures can be understood from the crystal structure. On the one hand, in the crystal structure of rutile-type CrTaO4, only one half of the octahedral sites in every layer along the c* direction are filled with Cr3+/Ta5+ cations. On the other hand, for the solid solution Cr0.5Ta0.5O2, there is significant difference in the mass of Ta and Cr atoms (Ta5+, cation radius 0.640 Å, mass 181 amu; Cr3+, cation radius 0.615 Å, mass 52 amu) [67] although their ionic radiuses are similar. Both partial occupation of the octahedral sites and the big mass difference endow CrTaO4 with low thermal conductivity due to their scattering of transport phonons.

As we have stated in Section 1, one of the leitmotivs of this work is to explore the lucrative possibility of using CrTaO4 as a new thermal barrier material. The non-trivial low thermal conductivity and closed mechanical properties to YSZ explicitly portray CrTaO4 a character of a TBC since the thermal conductivity of CrTaO4 is much lower than that of common TBCs such as YSZ [68], Yb3Al5O12 [35], β-Lu2Si2O7 [69], γ-Y2Si2O7 [49], Yb2SiO5 [70], and La2Zr2O7 [71] as shown in Fig. 11(b). Low thermal conductivity of CrTaO4 can effectively reduce the surface temperature of the substrate material and protect the substrate. Another advantage of low thermal conductivity material is that under the same temperature gradient, the thickness of the coating can be reduced, the weight of the hot structural components can be reduced, and the thermal engine efficiency can be improved [72]. For practical application of a TBC, its weight increases linearly with the increase of thickness and density. Therefore, another important factor affecting the design of TBCs is the specific thermal conductivity (*S*), which is the product of thermal conductivity and density. However, we must admit that more works are still needed before the final application of this new TBC. Further directions may be pursued include the test of the water vapor resistance, molten CMAS (CaO–MgO–Al2O3–SiO2) resistance, coating methods, stability during the coating process, and service life during performance.

## 5 Conclusions

In summary, we report herein the synthesis, consolidation, microstructure, mechanical, and thermal properties of a rarely encountered rutile-type complex oxide CrTaO4 (or Cr0.5Ta0.5O2 solid solution). Firstly, phase-pure CrTaO4 powders with an average particle size of 0.55 μm was synthesized by the solid-state reaction between Cr2O3 and Ta2O5 at 1120 ℃ for 2 h. Then, near fully dense bulk CrTaO4 was prepared by hot-press sintering the CrTaO4 powders at 1400 ℃ for 40 min. The grains are well crystallized with average equiaxed grain size of 2.07±0.73 μm and well faceted shapes determined by the relative growth rate of (110) and (101) planes. Using HAADF and ABF-STEM techniques, the rutile crystal structure of CrTaO4 was confirmed and short-range order was directly observed. In addition, Ta and Cr segregate at the grain boundaries but O is lacking, resulting weak grain boundary. The Young’s modulus *E*, shear modulus *G*, and bulk modulus *B* of CrTaO4 are 268, 107, and 181 GPa, respectively; while the Vickers hardness (*H*V), flexural strength (*σ*f), and fracture toughness (*K*IC) of CrTaO4 are 12.2±0.4 GPa, 142±14 MPa, 1.87±0.07 MPa·m1/2, respectively. Judged from the low Pugh’s ratio and high Poisson’s ratio, CrTaO4 belongs to the family of damage tolerant ceramics and the mechanisms responsible for the damage tolerance are crack branching, crack deflection, crack bridging, and grain pull-out in accordance with the intergranular fracture features. Intriguingly, the mechanical properties of CrTaO4 are close to those of YSZ, which give us a hint that CrTaO4 is a new promising thermal barrier material. CrTaO4 has a high melting point of 2103±20 K. The anisotropic TECs measured by high temperature XRD are *α*a = (5.68±0.10)×10−6 K−1, *α*c = (7.81±0.11) ×10−6 K−1, from Debye temperature (560 K) to 1473 K. The volume TEC is *α*V = (19.17±0.33)×10−6 K−1 with the average TEC of (6.39±0.11)×10−6 K−1, which is much lower than that of YSZ. The room temperature thermal conductivity of CrTaO4 is 1.31 W·m−1·K−1 and declines to 0.66 W·m−1·K−1at 1473 K, which endows it a new thermal barrier material. From room temperature to 1473 K, the thermal conductivity follows *κ* = 0.501 + 230/*T* and in the whole temperature range, the thermal conductivity of CrTaO4 is lower than most of the currently well-known thermal insulating materials such as YSZ, rare earth silicates, rare earth aluminates, and rare earth zirconates. On the ground of matched TEC, CrTaO4 pertains to an eligible thermal barrier material on top of refractory metals and their alloys, RHEAs, UHTCs. However, more delicate research works are still needed to explore the suitability of CrTaO4 as a new thermal barrier material. Hence, five major development directions may be pursued in future works including the CMAS resistance, water vapor resistance, coating deposition methods, stability during plasma deposition, and thermal/structural stability of the coatings during performance.
