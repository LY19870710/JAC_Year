## 1 Introduction

Near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy presents significant application potential in the fields of food detection, agricultural production, biological imaging, etc., due to its fast response, high efficiency, and nondestructive penetration [1−6]. The traditional tungsten filament and halogen lamp can generate broadband NIR emission. However, disadvantages such as large volume, incompactness, and low efficiency limit their applications [7,8]. The above disadvantages can be overcome by applying NIR light-emitting diode (LED) chips, and their full width at half maximum (FWHM) is narrow (commonly less than 50 nm) [9,10]. Inspired by remote exciting white LEDs/LDs, novel NIR phosphor-converted LEDs/laser diodes (LDs) are recognized as potential candidate for NIR lighting sources [11−14]. Therefore, discovering NIR luminescence materials with excellent photothermal performance is crucial for NIR spectroscopic applications.

Among the activation ions for NIR phosphor materials, trivalent Cr ion has wide-ranged absorption bands that couple well with the commercial InGaN blue LED chips and LD sources (~460 nm). The luminescence characteristics of Cr3+ doped NIR phosphor materials can be simply modified by optimizing matrix species, crystal field strength, and doping ions [15−17]. For example, Ca2LuZr2Al3O12:Cr3+ has a wide emission band located within 650–870 nm, and its emission intensity at 423 K maintains 51.22% of that at room temperature (RT) [18]. The Ca2LuScGa2Ge2O12:Cr3+ NIR phosphor with an output power of 1.213 mW@100 mA was synthesized by Bai *et al.* [19], and the obtained emission intensity at 423 K was 59% of that at RT. By altering the effective ionic radius of octahedral and tetrahedral sites of the Ca2LuScAl2Si2O12:Cr3+ NIR phosphor, high internal quantum efficiency (IQE) of 73.7% and emission intensity of 76% at 423 K compared with that at RT are realized, but its NIR light conversion efficiency was only 4.64%@350 mA [20]. By altering the ionic substitution of Cr3+ among dodecahedral, octahedral, and tetrahedral sites in the (Y,Lu)3(Mg,Al)2(Al,Si)3O12:Cr3+ NIR phosphor, high thermal stability (15.8% luminescent intensity loss at 150 ℃) with IQE of 64% were obtained by Jiang *et al*. [21]. Detailed luminescent performances of the recently reported NIR garnet phosphor materials are shown in Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) [20−27]. Unfortunately, the photoelectricity efficiencies and thermal stability of the above NIR phosphors are relatively low and could not meet the application standard. Therefore, it is urgent to discover high-performance NIR luminescence materials with high photo-electric efficiency and outstanding thermal stability.

Recently, Zhong *et al.* [28] detected a Y3In2Ga3O12:Cr3+ NIR phosphor of zero-thermal-quenching. Under a 150 mA driving current, its NIR output power and photoelectric efficiency were 68.4 mW and 15.9%, respectively [28]. It demonstrates the feasibility of achieving a Cr3+ doped garnet matrix with high thermal stability through cation substitution. Given that the ionic radius between Sc3+ (0.0745 nm, CN = 6) and In3+ (0.08 nm, CN = 6), as well as that between Y3+ ion (0.1019 nm, CN = 8) and Lu3+ ion (0.0977 nm, CN = 8) are similar. It is speculated that a new matching relationship of the average effective ionic radius between the [Lu/YO8] dodecahedral and [Sc/AlO6] octahedral sites of NIR garnet materials can be established. Inspired by the above analysis, by doping Lu3+ and Y3+ ions into the dodecahedral site, while doping the biuniqueness Sc3+ ion (i.e., Sc3+ ion presents the strongest electronegativity and the minimum ionic radius among rare earth ions) into the octahedral site of garnet, an efficient regulation of luminescence performances in the garnet matrix can be expected.

It is generally recognized that phosphor ceramics (PCs) have superior thermal shock resistance and luminescence stability compared to that of phosphor powders and glasses. Currently, PCs as luminescence materials for visible lighting and displaying have become a research hotspot, and abundant relevant research achievements have been realized [29−34]. However, very few research conducted systematic research on the preparation of NIR-PC with high thermal stability, as well as the application explorations toward NIR PC-based LEDs/LDs.

In this work, a series of (Lu,Y)3(Al,Sc,Cr)2Al3O12 (LuYScCr) NIR-PCs with high thermal stability were fabricated by vacuum sintering. Microstructural, optical, and luminescence properties of LuYScCr NIR-PCs, as well as the output power, photoelectric efficiency, and working temperature of PC-based NIR LED/LD prototypes, were systematically investigated. Furthermore, interconnections between thermal quenching and electron–phonon coupling of LuYScCr NIR-PCs were theoretically discussed. The application effect of the encapsulated NIR-PC prototypes in food detection (wheat and fruit) was also demonstrated. This study provides a promising phosphor ceramic for NIR detection.

### 2.1 Raw materials and synthesis method

In this work, commercial powders of α-Al2O3 (4N, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, China), Y2O3 (4N, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, China), Lu2O3 (4N, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, China), Sc2O3 (3N, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, China), and Cr2O3 (3N, Aladdin, China) were selected as starting materials to fabricate (Lu,Y)3(Al,Sc,Cr)2Al3O12 (abbreviated as LuYScCr) NIR-PCs. They were weighted precisely according to the formula (Lu0.5Y0.5)3(Al0.25−*x*Sc0.75Cr*x*)2Al3O12 (*x* = 0.001, 0.002, 0.003, 0.004, 0.005, and 0.006, respectively) and were denoted as LuYSc01Cr, LuYSc02Cr, LuYSc03Cr, LuYSc04Cr, LuYSc05Cr, and LuYSc06Cr, respectively. In addition, Lu3(Al0.998Cr0.002)5O12 (Lu02Cr) and Y3(Al0.998Cr0.002)5O12 (Y02Cr) PCs were fabricated as references. The detailed composition design is listed in Table 1. 0.5 wt% tetraethyl orthosilicate (4N, Alfa Aesar, USA) was chosen as a charge compensator to adjust the valence state of Cr ions. Fabrication procedures, characterization details, and preparation environments of NIR-PCs are described in Fig. 1, and the detailed description can be found in the ESM.

### 2.2 Characterization methods

X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra of NIR-PCs were performed on an X-ray diffractometer (D2 Puaser, Bruker, Germany) equipped with Cu Kα radiation operated at 40 mA and 40 kV in the 2*θ* range of 10°–80°. The diffuse reflection spectra (DRS) of the samples were observed by an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (Lambda 950, Perkin Elmer, USA) connected to an integrating sphere. Morphologies of NIR-PCs were recorded using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Su8100, Hitachi, Japan). Elemental composition and elemental mapping of the specimens were conducted on an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDS; Element, EDAX, USA). The photoluminescence (PL), photoluminescence excitation (PLE), fluorescence decay, and temperature-dependent PL spectra were collected on a spectrometer (FLS920P, Edinburgh, UK) equipped with a scintillating xenon lamp and a temperature control system. A quantum efficiency spectrometer (FLS1000, Edinburgh, UK) was used for the quantum efficiency measurement. Raman spectra of NIR-PCs were tested by a Raman spectrometer (LabRAM Odyssey, HORIBA, France) using the 785 nm laser as the excitation source. The electroluminescence spectra of the fabricated NIR PC-based LED/LD were obtained by a photoelectric measuring system (OHSP-350M, HOPOOCOLOR, China). The working temperature of NIR-PC based LED/LD devices was measured by an infrared camera (VT08, FLUKE, China). The laser spot diameter of LD was 1.6 mm, and the luminous surface size of the applied LED source was 8 mm × 8 mm.

## 3 Results and discussion

Appearances of LuYScCr NIR-PCs with different Cr3+ doping concentrations under daylight and UV light radiation are shown in Fig. 2(a). All the samples exhibited a uniform red color under UV radiation, and this tint was gradually deepened as the rising Cr3+ concentration. Appearances of Lu02Cr and Y02Cr NIR-PCs under daylight and UV light radiation are shown in Fig. S1 in the ESM. Diffuse reflectance spectra of LuYScCr NIR-PCs are illustrated in Fig. 2(b), in which the absorption bands of 430–460 and 610–640 nm were corresponded to the 4A2 → 4T1 and 4A2 → 4T2 transitions of Cr3+, respectively. These absorptions were enhanced by elevating Cr3+ contents from 0.1 to 0.6 at%. Besides, there was no absorption derived from Cr4+ ions ranging from 900 to 1300 nm observed. XRD patterns of LuYScCr NIR-PCs with different Cr3+ contents are plotted in Fig. 2(c). The diffraction peaks of all LuYScCr NIR-PCs matched well with the garnet structure (PDF#97-037-4607) without any impurity phase detected, indicating that Lu3+, Sc3+, and Cr3+ ions were completely dissolved into the matrix lattice [35,36]. In general, Lu3+ ions would substitute the dodecahedral Y3+ site, while Sc3+ and Cr3+ ions would occupy the octahedral Al3+ (0.054 nm, CN = 6) site in the garnet matrix. It has been demonstrated that a pure garnet phase was still maintained, even when Lu3+ and Sc3+ ions concentrations reached 50 and 30 at%, respectively, attributing to the effective ion radius matching effect. It is calculated that the average effective ionic radius of the dodecahedral and octahedral sites in LuYScCr NIR-PCs was approximately 0.0998 nm and 0.069375 nm, respectively [37]. The detailed schematic crystal structure sketch illustrating the ion substitution process in LuYScCr NIR-PCs is exhibited in Fig. 2(d). It is obvious that Lu3+/Y3+, Sc3+/Cr3+/Al13+, and Al23+ occupied the dodecahedral, octahedral, and tetrahedral coordination of garnet host, respectively.

To analyze the influence of Lu3+ and Sc3+ ion doping on the structural parameters of Y02Cr NIR-PCs, the crystal structure refinement was conducted via Rietveld method using the General Structure Analysis System (GSAS) software. According to Figs. 2(e) and 2(f), all the refined diffraction peaks were in consistent with that obtained from the XRD measurement, and the R*wp, *R*p, and *χ*2 values of Y02Cr were 7.66%, 8.49%, and 1.556, whereas that of LuYSc02Cr were 5.58%, 6.41%, and 1.802, respectively, demonstrating that the pure garnet phase NIR-PC could be obtained, even when the Lu3+ and Sc3+ ion doping concentrations were 50 and 30 at%, respectively.

Fracture surfaces of the prepared NIR-PCs are displayed in Fig. 3. All samples exhibited a uniform microstructure without any secondary phase observed, and their facture mode was transformed from intergranular to transgranular with increasing the Cr concentration. Residual pores of submicron size could be observed from the Lu02Cr sample (Fig. 3(a)), which indicated that the doping Lu3+ and Sc3+ ions influenced the sintering behavior of NIR-PCs (e.g., sintering temperature and densification process) [38−41]. Actually, Lu3+ and Sc3+ ions doping not only changed the melting point and refractive index of a garnet bulk, but also influenced its densification behavior, resulting in the microstructure differences between ceramics.

The PLE and normalized PLE spectra of NIR-PCs are depicted in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b), respectively. Monitoring at 710 nm, two excitation bands located within 350–500 and 530–680 nm could be observed, attributing to the spin-allowed 4A2→4T1 and 4A2→4T2 transitions of Cr3+ ion, respectively [42−44]. The PL and normalized PL spectra of LuYScCr NIR-PCs with different Cr3+ doping concentrations are presented in Figs. 4(c) and 4(d). An unilatered broadening phenomenon was observed from the PL spectra of the samples. Generally, the local crystal environment around Cr3+ ions was altered by doping Lu3+ and Sc3+ ions into the [YO8] dodecahedron and [AlO6] octahedron. However, this incorporation hardly altered the symmetry of the tetrahedron [AlO4] of the garnet matrix [39,45,46]. Emission intensities of NIR-PCs were gradually strengthened with increasing the Cr3+ doping content, and no sign of luminescence quenching was detected even at the Cr doping concentration of 0.6 at%. Considering Cr4+ ions would be presented if further increasing the Cr concentration, it is necessary to explore regulating mechanism of the Cr valence state in the (Lu,Y)3(Al,Sc)2Al3O12 ceramic matrix [47]. Besides, a broad emission band ranging from 650 to 850 nm covering the orange-red and red regions could be observed, corresponding to the spin-allowed 4T2 → 4A2 transition of Cr3+ ions. A sharp R line (zero-phonon line) due to the spin-forbidden 2E → 4A2 transition was detected at 686 nm. A similar phenomenon was observed by Xu et al*. [48] in YAG:Cr3+. Jiang *et al*. [21] reported a broad R line ranging from 680 to 690 nm in (Y,Lu)3(Mg,Al)2(Al,Si)3O12:Cr3+ phosphor powders. The PL spectra of Y02Cr and Lu02Cr PCs are shown in Fig. 4(e), and the emission intensities of all samples are displayed in Fig. 4(f). It was worth noting that the emission intensity of Y02Cr was stronger than that of Lu02Cr, which means that the crystal field strength had a significant influence on the luminescence of Cr3+ ion. Additionally, a schematic diagram of Cr3+ energy levels in the (Lu,Y)3(Al,Sc)2Al3O12 host is displayed in Fig. S3 in the ESM.

Thermal stability is a crucial indicator for a light conversion phosphor, because the electro–optical conversion efficiency of the excitation source is universally lower than 20%, and thus a large amount of waste heat would be generated [49]. Temperature-dependent photoluminescence properties of Y02Cr NIR-PCs are reflected in Fig. 5. It could be found that the emission intensity of Y02Cr NIR-PCs was gradually decreased with the rising temperature, attributing to the increased nonradiative transition probability at an elevated temperature (Figs. 5(a) and 5(b)). As portrayed in Fig. 5(c), the emission intensity of Y02Cr NIR-PCs was maintained 85.23% at 423 K (150 ℃) of that at room temperature. Generally, the activation energy (Δ*E*) is used to evaluate thermal quenching properties of phosphor materials, which could be calculated by Arrhenius equation, as described in Eq. (S1) in the ESM [50,51]. Figure 5(d) depicts the linear fitting ln(*I*0 /*I*T) versus 1/(*kT*) of Y02Cr NIR-PCs where *I*0 and *I*T are the emission intensity at RT and at operating temperature, respectively, and the calculated Δ*E* was 0.26 eV.

Temperature-dependent PL spectra of NIR-PCs under 460 nm excitation in the temperature range of 298–473 K are displayed in Fig. 6. The PL intensity of LuYSc01Cr NIR-PCs was initially increased and then declined, whereas those of the rest samples were gradually decreased, as rising the temperature from 298 to 473 K. The redshift phenomenon could be observed from the PL spectra, attributing to the decreased crystal field intensity caused by the expansion of the crystal lattice with increasing temperature [21,52]. Normalized temperature-dependent PL intensities of prepared NIR-PCs and their thermal quenching behaviors were presented in Figs. 6(i)–6(l). The relative emission intensities of LuYSc01Cr, LuYSc02Cr, LuYSc04Cr LuYSc05Cr, and LuYSc06Cr were 95.0%, 94.2%, 84.1%, 83.5%, and 76.9% at 423 K (150 ℃), respectively, compared with that at 298 K (25 ℃). The observed thermal quenching could be attributed to the structural rigidity of garnet [53,54]. Their activation energy was calculated using Arrhenius equation, and the results are shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. The Δ*E* of LuYSc02Cr, LuYSc04Cr, LuYSc05Cr, and LuYSc06Cr were calculated to be 0.28, 0.21, 0.20, and 0.16 eV, respectively. Therefore, the degradation of the thermal stability from LuYSc02Cr PCs to LuYSc06Cr PCs resulted in smaller Δ*E* (from 0.28 to 0.16 eV). Surprisingly, the PL intensity of LuYSc01Cr at 423 K was maintained at 95% compared to that at RT, which was far superior to that of Y02Cr. It should be attributed to the “abnormal thermal quenching effect” that took place in LuYSc01Cr NIR-PCs at ~348 K. Considering all NIR-PCs in this work were prepared by vacuum sintering (weak reducing atmosphere), it can be inferred that these traps should be regarded as oxygen vacancies. Besides, the luminescence intensity of LuYSc02Cr could maintain 94.2% at 423 K compared to that at RT. This phenomenon might be ascribed to a new matching relationship of the average effective ionic radius between the [Lu/YO8] and [Al/ScO6] sites was established, and higher structural rigidity of the host was obtained [35,36]. The above results demonstrated that prepared LuYScCr NIR-PCs presented superior thermal stability compared with that the reported garnet NIR phosphors, such as Y3Ga3MgSiO12:Cr3+ (~20%) [16], Ca2LuScAl2Si2O12:Cr3+ (~24%) [17], Gd2.4Lu0.6Ga4AlO12:Cr3+(~25%) [18], and LiInSi2O6:Cr3+(~23%) [55].

Generally, the obtained high thermal stability should be associated with the electron–phonon coupling (EPC) effect, due to that the thermal quenching process is essentially the excited electrons go through the cross point between excited states and ground states, backing to the ground states in the nonradiative pathway. The effects of EPC can be represented by the Huang–Rhys factor (S*), which is obtained through fitting Eq. (1) [56]:

(1) FWHM=2.36Sℏωcoth(ℏω2kT)

where *ħω*, *k*, and *T* represent the phonon frequency, the Boltzmann constant (8.6×10−5 eV/K), and the room temperature, respectively. *S* is the Huang–Rhys factor. Since the order of magnitude estimation of *ħω*/(*kT*) is 10−3, it can be approximated as Eq. (2):

(2) eℏω2kT−1≈ℏω2kT

Above equations can be rewritten as Eq. (3):

(3) y=a+bx

where *y* = FWHM2, *x =* 1/(2*kT*), *a =* 5.57× *S *× (*ħω*)2, and *b =* 5.57 × *S *× *ħω*. Based on the temperature-dependent emission spectra, the fitting results of *y* as a function of temperature are described in Fig. S4 in the ESM. Obviously, the Huang–Rhys factor (*S*) of LuYSc01Cr, LuYSc02Cr, LuYSc04Cr, LuYSc05Cr, and LuYSc06Cr NIR-PCs were calculated to be 3.868, 4.625 4.523, 4.751, and 6.311, respectively. The smaller *S* value revealed a weak electron–phonon coupling, which was responsible for luminescence of NIR-PCs.

Fluorescence decay curves of LuYScCr NIR-PCs monitored at 710 nm are plotted in Fig. 7(a) under 460 nm excitation. The fitted lifetime of PCs was gradually declined from 618 μs (LuYSc01Cr) to 434 μs (LuYSc06Cr), indicating an effective energy transfer between Cr3+ ions. As is unveiled in Fig. 7(b), IQE of NIR-PCs was initially increased and then declined with rising Cr3+ doping contents, and the maximum value reached 60.68%. It was related to the crystal structure and the intrinsic characteristics of the luminescent center of NIR-PCs. To further verify the structural development of NIR-PCs with Lu3+ and Sc3+ doping, Raman spectra of Lu02Cr, Y02Cr, LuYSc01Cr, LuYSc02Cr, LuYSc03Cr, and LuYSc04Cr NIR-PCs within the frequency range of 100–1000 cm−1 are measured and displayed in Figs. 7(c) and 7(d). It was obvious that the vibration of Raman peaks was altered by incorporating Lu3+ and Sc3+ ions into NIR-PCs, illustrating the structural symmetry of the matrix was changed. A slight enhancement of the Raman peaks of LuYScCr NIR-PCs was also observed, with increasing Cr3+ doping contents [57,58].

To further investigate feasibility of the prepared LuYScCr PCs, an NIR LED prototype was encapsulated by combining LuYSc06Cr NIR-PCs with a commercial 460 nm InGaN chip. Electroluminescence (EL) spectra of the NIR prototype at different driving currents are shown in Fig. 8(a), and the EL intensity continually ascended without any sign of saturation with increasing the driving current. As is provided in Fig. 8(b), the NIR output power was gradually increased to 447 mW, and the photoelectric conversion efficiency presented an increasing trend (18.6%@180 mA) with increasing the input current from 20 to 300 mA. Obviously, both output power and light conversion efficiency of the LuYSc06Cr NIR-PC-based LED prototype were superior to that of the reported garnet phosphors (see Table 2). Driven by a 160 mA current, the emission intensities of NIR-PCs were promoted with rising the Cr3+ doping content (Fig. 8(c) and Fig. S5 in the ESM). Furthermore, the thermographs of PCs at different driving currents are displayed in Fig. 8(d). ## 4 Conclusions

In this paper, LuYScCr NIR-PCs with excellent thermal stability and broad NIR emission were fabricated to evaluate their application potentials in NIR detection. The emission intensity of the prepared NIR-PCs at 150 ℃ was 95% higher that of at RT, ascribing to the weak electron–phonon coupling effect with a small Huang–Rhys factor. Besides, the prepared NIR-PCs presented a broad emission band ranging from 650 to 800 nm with high IQE of 60.68%. An LED device was encapsulated based on LuYScCr NIR-PCs and a blue LED chip, showing NIR output power of 447 mW with a photoelectric efficiency of 18.6% at 180 mA. By coupling NIR-PCs with a blue LD chip, the obtained output power was 814 mW at 2.5 A. Moreover, the working temperature of the NIR-LED device was increased from 33.5 to 70.8 ℃ with rising the driving current from 50 to 300 mA, whereas that of the NIR-LD device was 40.8–102.8 ℃ (0.5–3 A), demonstrating their excellent heat dissipation performances. This work provides a new perspective on thermal stability optimization by the cationic radius matching effect in LuYScCr NIR-PCs, and demonstrates their promising application prospects in the field of the NIR detection.
