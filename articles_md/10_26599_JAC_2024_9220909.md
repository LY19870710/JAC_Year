## 1 Introduction

Hypersonic aircraft requires a thermal protection system (TPS) that can withstand the intense and harsh thermal environment caused by aerodynamic heating [1−3]. Among the various options available, porous ultrahigh temperature ceramics (UHTCs) have emerged as promising candidates for TPS due to their inherent characteristics, such as high melting point, excellent high-temperature stability, low density, and superior thermal insulation properties [4−6]. In particular, high-entropy ceramics are known for their low thermal conductivity (*κ*T) due to the scattering of phonons by multiple components and distorted lattices [7,8]. The distorted lattices of these ceramics also lead to sluggish diffusion, resulting in excellent oxidation resistance [9]. Therefore, porous high-entropy ceramics can achieve the integration of thermal insulation, load-bearing, and high-temperature resistance, making them promising thermal insulation materials for ultrahigh-temperature applications [10,11]. Generally, porous high-entropy carbide ceramics have been fabricated using various methods, such as templates [12], direct foaming [13], and partial sintering [14]. To synthesize these ceramics, metal carbides or oxides are commonly used as starting materials by solid-state methods [7]. This requires extensive milling to disperse the various components and extremely high processing temperatures to accelerate the diffusion of atoms. However, this approach is energy-intensive and introduces impurities during the ball milling process, which destroys the stoichiometry and affects the configurational entropy [15]. Additionally, high-temperature sintering results in grain growth and the elimination of nanosized pores, making it difficult to regulate the porosity and pore structure of porous high-entropy (Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2)C carbide (PHEC) ceramics [16]. Moreover, the molding and processing of high-entropy carbides are limited by this solid-state method [17].

Regulated and diversified processing strategies are essential for fabricating porous ceramics. One innovative approach is the self-foaming of polymers, which allows for the fabrication of ceramics with isotropic and/or graded porosity in a single step [18]. This behavior is caused by condensation reactions between small amounts of hydroxyl and ethoxy functionalities within the precursor. They lead to the formation of water/ethanol (C2H5OH), which acts as an *in situ* foaming agent in viscous polymers [19,20]. Recently, silicon-based porous ceramics, including SiOC foams [21,22], porous Si3N4 ceramics [23], and SiCN ceramic aerogels [24,25], have been fabricated using this method. Compared to conventional sintering methods, the fabrication of porous ceramics through self-foaming using polymeric precursors requires lower temperatures and can be achieved via various plastic-forming techniques, such as injection molding [26], gel casting [27], melt spinning [28], blowing [29], and three-dimensional (3D) printing [30,31]. Furthermore, the porous structure of ceramics can be conveniently tailored by changing the temperature [32], introducing particles [21], and changing the molecular structure of the polymer precursor from linear to branched [24]. Polymer precursors are effective in the synthesis of high-entropy carbides due to their molecular-level homogeneous elements and adjustable compositions [32]. These precursors allow for easier atomic migration and promote the formation of high-entropy carbides at relatively low temperatures. Recently, various high-entropy carbide materials, such as (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C powder [17], nanowires [33], (Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2W0.2)C powder [34], and (Zr1/8Ta1/8Nb1/8Ti1/8W1/8Hf1/8V1/8Mo1/8)C powder [35], have been successfully synthesized via the polymer-derived ceramic route. However, there have been no reports of porous high-entropy carbide ceramics produced by the self-foaming method using polymer precursors.

Furfuryl alcohol (FA) has been successfully employed as a carbon source in the synthesis of high-entropy carbide ceramics [16,36]. FA is widely used in the production of polymer foams by a self-foaming method [37]. When inorganic acids or Lewis acids are used, the self-condensation of FA results in an exothermic reaction and causes self-foaming [38]. Inspired by the self-blowing behavior of FA, polymer foams that contain elements such as Ti, Zr, Ta, Nb, and Hf can be produced by the self-condensation of FA using metal chlorides as catalysts. The polymer foam transformed into porous ceramics through pyrolysis and carbothermal reduction processes. Thus, a novel self-foaming method for the fabrication of PHEC ceramics was introduced in this study. The structural evolution and formation mechanism of the PHEC ceramics are discussed in detail. The prepared PHEC ceramics have a hierarchical structure and exhibit excellent compressive strength and low thermal conductivity. This innovative self-foaming strategy holds significant potential for enabling the cost-effective and large-scale production of porous high-entropy carbide ceramics.

## 2 Materials and methods

Tantalum chloride (TaCl5, purity 99.8%), niobium chloride (NbCl5, purity 99.9%), titanium chloride (TiCl4, purity 99.9%), zirconium chloride (ZrCl4, purity 99.9%), hafnium chloride (HfCl4, purity 99.5%), and furfuryl alcohol (AR 98%) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd., China. Ethanol (99.9%) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China. FA and ethanol were used as the carbon source and solvent, respectively. To prepare the PHEC ceramics, the transition metal chlorides were equimolarly weighed and dissolved in ethanol to form a metal-alcohol salt solution. The concentration of the salt solution was set at 1.5 mol·L−1. The metal−alcohol salt solution was mixed with FA at a volume ratio of 4 : 1 to form the precursor liquid. FA in the precursor spontaneously polymerized and released gas at room temperature, resulting in the formation of a porous green body. The green body was dried in an oven (DZF-6050, MTI Group, China) at 80 °C for 24 h and then heat-treated at 1800 °C in a vacuum sintering furnace (GSL-2000X-HV, MTI Group, China) 2 h under an argon atmosphere to obtain the PHEC ceramics.

The chemical evolution of the green body was analyzed by a combination of thermogravimetric and mass analyzer (TG-DTG/MS; Thermo Plus EVO2/Thermo Mass Photo, Rigaku, Japan) at 25–1350 °C in an Ar atmosphere with a heating rate of 10 °C·min−1. The as-prepared samples were analyzed by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Smart Lab, Rigaku, Japan) using Cu Κα radiation (*λ* = 1.540593 Å). The morphology of the porous ceramics was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Regulus 8100, Hitachi, Japan) equipped with an energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) system. The microstructure of the samples was investigated using a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Themis Z, FEI, USA) equipped with EDS. The pore size distributions (PSD) and porosities were analyzed by mercury porosimetry (AutoPore IV 9500, Micromeritics, USA). *κ*T was calculated according to Eq. (1) [39]:

(1) κT=α×ρ×cp

where *α* is the thermal diffusivity, *ρ* is the density of the PHEC ceramics, and *c**p* is the specific heat capacity. *α* was measured using a laser thermal conductivity testing instrument (LFA 467 HT, NETZSCH, Germany). The size of the samples for thermal conductivity testing was *ϕ*12.5 × 2.0 mm. *c**p* of the samples was measured by a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC STA 449F3, NETZSCH, Germany). The compressive strength was characterized by using a universal testing machine (CMT6103, MTS, USA). Five samples with a size of *ϕ*15 × 30 mm were employed to obtain the average value of the compressive strength.

### 3.1 Microstructural evolution of PHEC

The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectrum in Fig. 1(a) provides insight into the molecular structure of the PHEC precursor. The observed peaks at 1710, 1455, 1365, and 1204 cm−1 indicate the presence of C=O, C–C, C–H, and C–O–C groups, respectively, which suggest the formation of ester intermediates [40]. Additionally, the bands at 1610, 850, and 1180 cm–1 correspond to C=C, C–H, and C–O bonds, respectively, indicating the polymerization of FA [41]. The vibrations at 1021 and 1100 cm–1 can be attributed to the stretching vibrations of M–O–C chains, while the peak at 636 cm–1 indicates the presence of M–O in the M–O–M' chain (M = Ta, Nb, Ti, Zr, and Hf) [41]. Transition metal chlorides act as Lewis acid catalysts and initiate the polymerization and cross-linking reactions of FA [42]. As a result of polymer network formation, the mobility of transition metal ions is impeded, leading to their progressive and homogeneous distribution throughout the precursor [43].

The pyrolysis behavior of the high-entropy UHTC precursor was studied during heat treatment using TG-DTG and TG-MS. According to the TG-DTG curves (Fig. 1(b)), the pyrolysis process can be divided into three stages as the temperature increases from 25 to 1400 °C. A weight loss of approximately 15.3% occurs below 200 °C (DTG peak at 93 °C), mainly due to the dehydration of hydroxyl groups and the release of H2O (*m*/*z* = 18, Fig. 1(c)). The second stage of organic−inorganic transformation occurs between approximately 200 and 600 °C, with a weight loss of approximately 24.3%. This stage results in the release of H2O (*m*/*z* =18, Fig. 1(c)), CH4 (*m*/*z* =15, Fig. 1(d)), CO2 (*m*/*z* = 44, Fig. 1(e)), and CO (*m*/*z* = 28, Fig. 1(f)) due to the rearrangement of ligands and the breakage of the molecular carbon chain [44]. Finally, the carbothermal reduction process occurs between 800 and 1400 °C, leading to a weight loss of 16.6% and the release of CO (*m*/*z* = 28, Fig. 1(f)).

Figure 2 shows the high-resolution XPS spectra of the PHEC ceramic green body. The curves in the spectra correspond to the C 1s, O 1s, Ta 4f, Nb 3d, Ti 2p, Zr 3d, and Hf 4f bands (Fig. 2(a)). The C 1s spectra (Fig. 2(b)) have three peaks at 287.8, 285.2, and 283.9 eV, which correspond to O–C=O, C–O–C, and C–C bonds, respectively [45]. The O 1s binding energy (Fig. 2(c)) contains peaks at 533.2, 531.9, and 530.1 eV, which correspond to M–O, C–O, and C=O bonds, respectively [46]. The high-resolution Ta 4f XPS spectra (Fig. 2(d)) has two peaks at 27.0 and 25.2 eV, while the Nb 3d spectra (Fig. 2(e)) has three peaks at 209.7, 205.9, and 203.2 eV. The Ti 2p XPS spectrum (Fig. 2(f)) has two peaks at 460.5 and 454.5 eV, while the Zr 3d spectra (Fig. 2(g)) has two peaks at 181.3 and 178.7 eV. The Hf 4f spectra (Fig. 2(h)) exhibits two peaks at 18.2 and 16.5 eV. The transition metal peaks are attributed to the M–O bond (M = Ta, Nb, Ti, Zr, and Hf) based on the C 1s and O 1s spectra [47]. However, these M–O bonds do not match well with those of the corresponding oxides. The shift in these transition metal peaks to lower binding energies is due to the formation of the coordination complex of the transition metal [48,49]. The XPS results confirmed the formation of polymeric precursors composed of transition metals (Ta, Nb, Ti, Zr, and Hf). These polymeric precursors are preferred for ceramic conversion at low temperatures and for producing nanostructured ceramics [50].

To study the phase evolution of the PHEC ceramics, samples pyrolyzed at 600, 800, 1200, 1600, and 1800 °C were selected (Fig. 3). The XRD results reveal that the precursor transforms into amorphous carbon at 600 °C, which corresponds to the organic-to-inorganic conversion stage. At 800 °C, m-ZrO2, HfO2, and ZrTiO2 are formed along with TaC and NbC. The preferential formation of TaC and NbC at relatively low pyrolysis temperatures agrees with previous studies on (Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2)C ceramics [12]. When the temperature increases to 1200 °C, TiC appears and forms a binary carbide solid solution (NbTiC2) with NbC. As the temperature increases to 1600 °C, most of the oxides are converted into carbides, while a solid-solution carbide phase can be found at approximately θ* = 35°. After the heat treatment at 1800 °C, the (Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2)C ceramics are formed with a typical solid-solution structure. The lattice parameter is calculated to be 4.520 Å, which is very close to the theoretical value calculated from first principles (4.528±0.005 Å) [51].

Figure 4 shows the Raman spectra of the PHEC ceramics prepared at various temperatures. The D peak at 1350 cm−1 represents the vibration of sp3 hybridized carbon, indicating a defective and disordered carbon structure. The G peak at 1580 cm−1 represents the in-plane stretching vibration from the rings of the graphitic domain. At 800 °C, the broad and syncretic peaks of the D and G bands are due to the presence of structural defects in the carbon matrix [52]. However, as the pyrolysis temperature increases, these two peaks separate. The intensity of the G peak increases, suggesting the graphitization transition of the carbon materials. The intensity ratio between the D band and the G band (R* *=* *I*D/*I*G) is an effective way to assess the degree of disorder in carbon samples. A lower *R* value indicates a relatively better graphitic structure with fewer defective regions. The *R* values of the samples change from 2.32 to 0.37 as the temperature increases from 800 to 1800 °C. Moreover, in the sample pyrolyzed at 1800 °C, a strong peak at approximately 2700 cm−1, called the 2D band, can be observed. This peak reflects the extent of the multilayer graphene [53]. The appearance of the 2D band is due to the generation of graphitic domains induced by the carbothermal reaction between the carbon matrix and the nanosized oxides [53,54]. The Raman results indicate that the residual carbon in the PHEC ceramics is mainly in the form of a graphitic structure. The carbon content in the PHEC ceramics was determined by the thermal gravimetric (TG) method. The oxidation reactions in the PHEC ceramics are shown in Eqs. (2) and (3). The mass gain can be attributed to the oxidation of the PHEC ceramics, while the mass loss can be attributed to the release of CO2 by the oxidation of carbon atoms. Two equations can be derived from the TG curve, as shown in Eqs. (4) and (5). The variables *m*C, *m*HEC, and *m*oxides represent the normalized masses of the carbon phase, HEC phase, and oxides, respectively. *M*HEC and *M*oxides are the molar weights of the HEC (130.3) and oxides (153.5) produced by the oxidation of the HEC phase. Figure 5 displays the morphology of the green body of the PHEC ceramics before and after treatment at 800 and 1800 °C. In Fig. 5(a), the morphology of the green body shows the agglomeration of microspheres ranging from 1.0 to 2.0 μm in diameter. After the green body was pyrolyzed at 800 °C, a 3D porous network was formed by the interconnection of the microspheres due to the pyrolysis of the precursor and the release of CH4, CO, and CO2. In TEM analysis, the microstructure of the PHEC ceramics can be determined by examining the size of the grains in the neck and body areas of the microspheres. Figure 7 displays the microstructure of the PHEC ceramics in the necking area. It can be inferred that the black regions represent high-entropy carbide grains, while the gray regions may contain carbon substrates, as shown in Fig. 7(a). Carbon materials form interconnections between high-entropy UHTC grains. Most of the high-entropy carbide grains are coated with a carbon layer (Fig. 7(b)), which is confirmed by the elemental mapping of the high-entropy carbide grains (Fig. 7(c)). Figure 7(d) shows the HRTEM image of the high-entropy carbide grain, which clearly depicts a periodic lattice structure with a set of fringes of 0.27 nm, corresponding to the (111) planes of the carbides [41]. The lattice parameter can be calculated to be 4.521 Å, which is close to the XRD result (4.520 Å). The SAED pattern along the [011] plane is shown in Fig. 7(f), indicating a typical face-centered cubic (fcc) crystal structure. The HRTEM image of the high-entropy carbide grain (Fig. 7(d)) also reveals that the grains are covered by a graphitic carbon layer with a lattice of 0.34 nm. Based on the results of the mercury porosimetry test, it was discovered that the PHEC ceramics have a porosity of 91.3% with an average pore size of 208 nm. Additionally, it has a low bulk density of 0.55 g·cm−3 and a high BET of 230 m2·g−1, which indicates its high porosity and low density. Figure 9 illustrates the PSD of the PHEC ceramics and the degree of porosity. The PHEC ceramics have a dual-porosity structure, with a primary porosity ranging from 1 to 10 µm and nanosized pores ranging from 9 to 12 nm (Fig. 9(a)). These micron-sized pores are formed during the polymerization of the precursor and pyrolysis of the green body, as shown in Fig. 9(b). During the polymerization process, water is produced, which acts as a foaming agent within the viscous precursor. Moreover, the release of CH4, CO, and CO2 during the pyrolysis process forms pores between the microspheres. Microsized pores are formed by the interconnected microspheres, whereas nanosized pores are mainly attributed to the pores present in the microspheres. The microspheres themselves are composed of nanosized particles, as shown in the SEM and TEM images. During the carbothermal reduction process, carbon is consumed within the microspheres, resulting in the release of CO and the formation of nanosized HEC grains. The aggregation of these grains and the consumption of carbon materials lead to the formation of nanosized pores within the microspheres.

### 3.2 Properties of PHEC ceramics

The compressive properties of the fabricated PHEC ceramics at room temperature are shown in Fig. 10(a). As the strain increases, the mechanical strength quickly increases to 28.1±2 MPa before decreasing significantly. PHEC ceramics have significantly greater mechanical strength than porous UHTCs [64−66], making them capable of bearing heavy loads. In addition to its excellent mechanical properties, the PHEC ceramics have a low thermal conductivity (*κ*T) of 0.046 W·m−1·K−1 at room temperature (Fig. 10(b)). When tested at 1000 °C, *κ*T increases to 20 W·m−1·K−1, while *α* increases from 0.60 to 2.7 mm2·s−1. The increase in thermal conductivity with temperature is caused by increased thermal radiation heat transfer at elevated temperatures [68].

Compared to other reported porous UHTCs [12,14,57−67], the obtained PHEC ceramics show remarkable mechanical and thermal properties (Fig. 10(c)). This makes them highly suitable for thermal insulation applications. The unique hierarchical structure of the PHEC ceramics, as shown in the schematic diagram in Fig. 11, explains its excellent properties. On the macroscale, the ceramic microspheres are firmly connected by sintered necks with high-quality and strong interfaces. This allows the loading to transfer quickly along the frame through the well-connected necks, leading to a homogeneous stress distribution and avoiding stress concentration [68]. The interconnected microspheres construct micron-sized pores (1–10 µm) in the ceramics. These micron-sized pores can depress the heat conduction of gas by restricting the movement and collision of gas molecules, according to the Knudsen effect [69]. The high porosity and interconnected nature of the microspheres effectively reduce the conduction area and sharply extend the conduction path during solid conduction. This can effectively reduce the thermal conductivity of porous ceramics. On the microscale, these ceramic microspheres are composed of high-entropy grains with a size of approximately 20 nm and carbon materials with graphitic sheets. The unique nanostructured microspheres provide abundant interfaces and nanosized pores, which block crack propagation and result in a significant increase in compressive strength. Concerning solid heat conduction, the abundant interface and nanosized pores restrain solid heat conduction by phonon scattering [8,67]. On the atomic scale, the severe lattice distortion of high-entropy carbides increases the resistance of dislocation motion, making it difficult to carry out slip, thus increasing the strength and hardness of high-entropy ceramics [70,71] and leading to enhanced stiffness and strength. The severe mass ﬂuctuations and lattice distortion of high-entropy ceramics promote phonon scattering and decrease the thermal conductivity [66]. In summary, PHEC ceramics possess interconnected microspheres, abundant interfaces between the microspheres, and severe lattice distortion, leading to excellent mechanical and thermal insulation properties.

## 4 Conclusions

We have successfully fabricated PHEC ceramics using metal chloride and FA as precursors by employing a self-foaming method. The PHEC ceramics comprise interconnected microspheres with a size of 2 µm, which consist of high-entropy carbide grains of 20 nm and graphitic carbon. The PHEC ceramics exhibit a hierarchical porous structure that comprises micron-sized pores ranging from 1 to 10 µm and nanosized pores ranging from 9 to 12 nm. The micron-sized pores are formed by the interconnected microspheres, while the nanosized pores result from the carbothermal reduction process within these microspheres. On the macroscale, the interconnected structure of the PHEC ceramics results in a high porosity of 91.3% and a low density of 0.55 g·cm−3. On the microscale, the nanosized high-entropy carbide grains and graphitic carbon lead to numerous interfaces and nanosized pores in the microspheres. On the atomic scale, the high-entropy effect results in severe lattice distortion in the high-entropy carbide grains. The unique hierarchical structure of the PHEC ceramics results in its low thermal conductivity of 0.046 W·m−1·K−1 and superior compressive strength of 28.1±2.0 MPa. This self-foaming method is a low-cost and facile strategy for the fabrication of porous high-entropy carbide ceramics.
