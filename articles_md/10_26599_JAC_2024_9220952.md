## 1 Introduction

Piezoelectric materials can be reversibly deformed when exposed to an alternating current (AC) electric field, which is essential to the modern automobile industry and electric accessories, enabling a wide range of sonars, transducers, sensors, actuators, MEMS, and energy harvesting [1]. In recent years, there has been a growing demand for the use of these materials in harsh environments and at high temperatures, such as for structural health monitoring of hot components in aircraft engines, gas turbines, nuclear power plants, and petroleum refineries [2]. There is an urgent demand for the development of high-temperature piezoelectric materials. Unfortunately, conventional piezoelectric materials such as lead zirconate titanate (PZT) are limited to operating temperatures below 300 °C because of their limited Curie temperatures (*T*C ≤ 380 °C) [3].

Owing to their high *T*C (≥ 500 °C), low dielectric permittivity and aging rate, and good resistance to fatigue [4−6], bismuth layer-structured ferroelectrics (BLSFs, also called Aurivillius-type compounds) are suitable for high-temperature piezoelectric applications. BLSFs have a general chemical formula of (Bi2O2)2+(A*m*−1B*m*O3*m*+1)2−. The parameter *m* represents the number of BO6 octahedra in the perovskite-like layer (A*m*−1B*m*O3*m*+1)2−. The spontaneous polarization of BLSFs, which takes place mainly in the *a*–*b* plane parallel to the bismuth oxide layer (Bi2O2)2+, can be considered the result of the relative displacement of BO6 octahedra through the *a*-axis. In the family of BLSFs, CaBi4Ti4O15 (CBT, *m* = 4) possesses a high Curie temperature of 790 °C, but its piezoelectric coefficient is very small (*d*33 ≤ 10 pC/N). The poor piezoelectric activity of BLSFs is attributed to the limited switching of spontaneous polarization in the *a*–*b* plane, as well as the difficulty of the poling process when subjected to a defect-pinning-related high coercive field and high conductivity at elevated temperatures [7].

Ion substitution at different crystallographic sites could introduce special lattice defects and then modify the electrical properties of ferroelectric materials. Therefore, many works have reported improvements in the piezoelectric activity of CBTs through the substitution of various cations for A-site Ca2+ and/or B-site Ti4+ in the perovskite-like layer [8−10]. For example, Zhang *et al*. [11] reported manganese-modified CBT piezoceramics with an improved *d*33 value of 14 pC/N, a high *T*C value of ~800 °C and a high electrical resistivity (1×107 Ω·cm at 500 °C). The authors suggested that Mn4+ tended to be reduced to Mn3+ or Mn2+ at high temperatures, which is conducive to the improvement of the polarization switching. In Shen *et al*. [12], Nb/Mn co-doped CBT was found to have a greatly increased piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33 = 25 pC/N) and a stabilized Curie temperature (*T*C = 790 °C). Similarly, Sb/Mn co-doped CBTs were synthesized by Liu *et al*. [13] via the same ceramic process. Both a high *T*C value of 792 °C and a remarkable *d*33 value of 25 pC/N were achieved by the composition with a doping concentration of (Sb2/3Mn1/3) equal to 5 mol%. As reported in these manganese-modified perovskite-type systems, it is assumed that the acceptor Mn2+ will enter the lattice position of Ti4+ because of their similar ionic radii. In addition to Mn doping, other metal ions, especially rare-earth ions, and their composites have also been used to modify the electrical properties of CBTs. Sheng *et al*. [14] reported a type of Nd/Ce co-doped CaBi4Ti4O15 ceramics. The optimized comprehensive performances, including *d*33 = 19 pC/N, loss tangent (tan*δ*) = 0.073%, and *T*C = 794 °C, were measured for the composition of Ca(Nd1/2Ce1/2)0.075Bi3.925Ti4O15. Xin *et al*. [15] studied the effects of B-site dopants Nb, Ta, and W on the microstructures and electrical properties of Ca0.85(Li,Ce)0.075Bi4Ti4O15–0.01MnCO3 ceramics and reported that a *d*33 value close to 20 pC/N was achieved by the Nb-substituted sample, but its *T*C value was only 768 °C. In Ref. [16], a large *d*33 value of up to 24 pC/N together with a high *T*C ≈ 787 °C were identified in the composition of CaBi4Ti3.9(Nb2/3Mg1/3)0.1O15. Recently, Hu *et al*. [17] investigated the codoping effects of Na/Ce on the structures and electrical properties of CBT ceramics, and the optimized composition (*x* = 0.12) with significantly improved electrical properties (*T*C = 765 °C, *P*m = 9.8 µC/cm2, and *d*33 = 20.5 pC/N) was considered a good candidate for high-temperature piezoelectric applications. On the other hand, in addition to the conventional solid-state reaction technique, the spark plasma sintering (SPS) technique was employed to fabricate highly textured CBT ceramics [18], and the *d*33 value significantly increased from 7.2 pC/N for the texture-less sample to 25.3 pC/N for the textured sample (*T*C = 788 °C). Furthermore, a synergistic strategy combining texture engineering and chemical doping was adopted to optimize the electrical properties of the CBT ceramics. Both a significantly improved piezoelectricity with *d*33 ≈ 27.3 pC/N and a high Curie temperature with *T*C ≈ 790 °C were achieved in the samples doped with 0.2 wt% MnO2 [19].

These studies investigated ion-modified CBTs and reported that an improvement in piezoelectric activity was always accompanied by a decrease in the Curie temperature. These two factors seem to be mutually restricted for ferroelectric materials. The substitution of rare-earth elements in the A-site of perovskite is well known in Aurivillius phases to develop their ferroelectric and piezoelectric properties. For example, Subbarao [4] has suggested that the *T*C of BLSFs increases with decreasing size of A-site cations in the perovskite-like layer, so that A-site doping with a smaller-size cation would lead to a higher *T*C. A study on the dielectric properties of Bi3−*x*Gd*x*TiNbO9 ceramics demonstrated that the substitution of Gd3+ for Bi3+ at the A site induced significant lattice distortion, and the corresponding *T*C values linearly increased from 921 to 950 °C with increasing *x* [20,21]. This work provided us with a tack for developing non-texted CBT ceramics with both a large piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33 ≥ 20 pC/N) and a high Curie temperature (*T*C ≥ 800 °C).

In this work, Gd/Mn co-doped CBT ceramics were prepared via the conventional sintering (CS) process. The doping concentration effects of Gd3+ on the structure and properties of CBTs were studied, and the underlying mechanisms of Gd3+/Mn3+ substitution at the A/B-site for synergistically enhancing the piezoelectric activity and Curie temperature of CBTs were also revealed. The proposed approach can also provide guidance for future composition design and performance optimization of BLSFs used for high-temperature piezoelectric devices.

### 2.1 Preparation of samples

According to the chemical formulas of CBT and Ca1−*x*Gd*x*Bi4Ti4O15+0.2 wt%MnO2 (CBT–100*x*GM, *x* = 0, 0.02, 0.06, 0.08, and 0.11), analytical grade CaCO3 (99.0%, Kelong Chemical, China), TiO2 (99.0%, Kelong Chemical, China), Bi2O3 (99.0%, Kelong Chemical, China), Gd2O3 (99.99%, Kelong Chemical, China), and MnO2 (99%, Kelong Chemical, China) were used as raw materials to fabricate polycrystalline ceramics via the solid-state reaction and conventional sintering process. First, anhydrous ethanol was used as the medium, and CaCO3, TiO2, Bi2O3, and Gd2O3 weighted according to their stoichiometric ratios, were ball-milled in a planetary ball mill for 6 h. Then, the dried mixtures were calcined at 850 °C for 4 h. Second, the calcined powders with 0.2 wt% MnO2 added were milled again under the same conditions and granulated with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) as the binder. The pellets were subsequently pressed into discs with a diameter of 10 mm and thickness of 1 mm in a stainless steel die. After burning PVA at 650 °C for 4 h, these discs were sintered in a sealed alumina crucible at 1100 °C for 2 h. The sintered ceramics were ground and polished to a thickness of 0.5 mm, and then their major surfaces were coated with silver paste and then fired at 700 °C for 10 min. Finally, these ceramics were poled by a direct current (DC) field of 8–10 kV/mm for 30 min in silicone oil at 150 °C.

### 2.2 Characterization of samples

The phase structures of the sintered ceramics were analyzed via an X-ray diffractive analyzer (D8 Advance, BRUKER, Germany). The “whole-pattern fitting” refinement mode included in the MDI Jade 6.0 software was used for calculating the lattice parameters. The valence band structures of the ceramic powders were analyzed using an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (Versaprobe III, PHI, Japan). The microstructures of the sintered ceramics were observed by a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; JSM638LV, JEOL, Japan). Moreover, the chemical compositions were analyzed via energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), which is associated with FE-SEM. The dielectric permittivity and loss tangent of the unpoled ceramics as a function of temperature were measured at 100 Hz, 1 kHz, 10 kHz, 100 kHz, and 1 MHz by using a precision LCR meter (TH2838, Tonghui Electronic, China) attached to a programmable furnace. The direct current (DC) resistivity (*ρ*dc) was measured in the temperature range of 300–800 °C by using the two-probe DC method. The *P–E* and *I–E* hysteresis loops of the unpoled ceramics were measured by a ferroelectric analyzer (FETS-2000, YANHE Technology, China) in a silicone oil bath at 150 °C and 1 Hz. The longitudinal piezoelectric charge constant *d*33 of the poled ceramics was measured by a quasi-static *d*33 meter (ZJ-3A, Institute of Acoustics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (IACAS), China). The thermal annealing experiment was conducted by heating the poled ceramics at different temperatures (50–850 °C) for 1 h in air and then measuring their *d*33 values after they cooled to room temperature. The resonant–antiresonant characteristics of the poled ceramics were measured in the frequency range of 20 Hz–2 MHz by the precision LCR meter mentioned above, which was used as an impedance analyzer, according to the electrical impedance method on the basis of Institute of Electrical and Electronic Engineers (IEEE) standards.

### 3.1 Structural properties

Figure 1 displays the crystallographic analysis of the samples. The room temperature XRD patterns of all the samples are shown in Fig. 1(a). All the diffraction peaks detected by the instrument were indexed by the standard powder diffraction file (PDF) card No. 52-1640, and a good match with the referred diffraction data confirmed the formation of single-phase CaBi4Ti4O15 with an orthorhombic structure with space group *A*21*am* in all the samples. No impurity phase was detected from all the samples, indicating that both Gd2O3 and MnO2 were successfully incorporated into the crystalline lattice of CBT. The strongest diffraction peak is observed in the (119) peak for all the samples, which is in agreement with the most intense reflections (1 1 2*m*+1) of Aurivillius-type compounds. As seen from the enlarged diffraction patterns in Fig. 1(b), the diffraction reflection around 2*θ* = 33° presents the doublet peaks (200/020), whose split characteristic is typical for orthorhombic symmetry. At *x* = 0.11, such a doublet peak tends to be merged into a single peak, indicating that orthorhombic symmetry becomes closer to tetragonal symmetry. This result implies that the substitution of Gd3+ leads to decreased structural distortion and possibly induces structural transformation of the orthorhombic unit cell at high concentrations.

*
Fig. 1Crystallographic analysis of samples: (a) room-temperature XRD patterns, (b) enlarged diffraction patterns around 2θ* = 33°, (c) crystal structure and lattice distortion of CaBi4Ti4O15 with space group *A*21*am* (below *T*C), (d) local structure of the perovskite-like layer of CaBi4Ti4O15, and (e) Rietveld refined XRD patterns of CBT–6GM.

It is well known that Aurivillius-type compounds with space group *A*21*am* have structural distortions involving tilt and rotation of BO6 octahedra along the *c*-axis and *ab*-plane of the parent tetragonal structure, respectively [22]. Figure 1(c) depicts the crystal structure and lattice distortion of CaBi4Ti4O15 with space group *A*21*am*. Such a distorted perovskite-like layer linked with TiO6 octahedra usually results in a difference in the lattice parameters *a* and *b*. The local structure of the perovskite-like layer of CaBi4Ti4O15 is described in Fig. 1(d). When considering the similarity of the ionic radius between the substituted ions and the native ions (Gd3+: *r*i = 0.938 Å, Ca2+: *r*i = 1.34 Å, Bi3+: *r*i = 1.35 Å, Ti4+: *r*i = 0.605 Å, Mn4+: *r*i = 0.53 Å, Mn3+: *r*i = 0.58 Å, Mn2+: *r*i = 0.96 Å [23]), it is believed that Gd3+ prefers to enter the A-site (i.e., Ca site) of the perovskite-like layer, while Mn3+ (the valence of the Mn-cation is suggested to be mainly +3 according to Ref. [24]) tends to substitute for Ti4+ located at the B-site. The effects of Gd3+ on the crystalline evolution of CaBi4Ti4O15 can be further determined in terms of the refined XRD patterns. Figure 1(d) shows the refined XRD patterns of CBT–6GM for representative analysis. All diffraction peaks observed from the XRD pattern fit well with the Bragg reflections, and the difference plots demonstrate a good match between the calculated and observed data for the space group *A*21*am*. The lattice parameters can be calculated from the structural refinement results when the residual error of fit is smaller than 10%.

Figure 2 shows the lattice parameters of CBT–100*x*GM as a function of *x*. All the lattice parameters *a*, *b*, *c*, and *V* continuously decrease with increasing *x*. First, the smaller radii of Gd3+ than those of Ca2+ lead to a shorter Ti‒O bond length of the TiO6 octahedron, which then decreases the unit-cell size of the CBT. Moreover, the substitution of Gd3+ for Ca2+ also results in *a* and *b* being close to each other, indicating a reduction in the lattice distortion of orthorhombic unit cells (which is defined as orthorhombicity, 2(*a* − *b*)/(*a* + *b*) [25,26]). It has been reported that the 6*s*2 lone pair electrons of Bi3+ cause a large structural distortion in BLSFs, which affects the bonding of Bi with the A-site atoms of the perovskite-like layer [27]. As the ion size of Gd3+ is smaller than that of Ca2+, the substitution is likely to provide more rattling space for TiO6 octahedra, leading to a decrease in the structural distortion of the perovskite-like layer. Therefore, at a high Gd doping concentration (*x* = 0.11), the unit cell of CBT was transformed from orthorhombic to pseudotetragonal (prototype) symmetry owing to the very small difference between the lattice parameters *a* and *b*. According to the relationship between spontaneous polarization, atomic displacement and the unit-cell volume in the Aurivillius-phase compounds [28], the decrease in the unit-cell volume of CBT with the introduction of Gd3+ is expected to result in greater spontaneous polarization, and the suppression of the structural distortion of the perovskite-like layer would make the motion of non-180o domain walls in the *a*–*b* plane easier. Both greater spontaneous polarization and easier domain switching are conducive to enhancing the piezoelectric activity of BLSFs.

Figure 3 shows SEM images of the samples derived from their original surfaces. These images demonstrate that the sintered CBT-based ceramics present a dense microstructure composed of many plate-like grains with random orientations. This typical morphology is formed due to the highly anisotropic grain growth, which has a much higher growth rate in the direction perpendicular to the *c*-axis of the BLSF crystal, and the (00*l*)-type planes with a lower surface energy tend to grow more preferentially during sintering [26]. After MnO2 was introduced into the CBT, some abnormal grain growth (as shown by the yellow lines in Fig. 3(b)) was observed in CBT–0GM. However, with increasing doping concentration of Gd (*x* = 0.02–0.11), these plate-like grains transform into circular shaped grains with rounded edges and a decreased size. It is well known that oxygen vacancies can help oxygen ions to transport through the crystal lattice, leading to an ionic diffusion process within the grains and/or on the grain boundaries. In this case, the grain growth would be promoted by the mass transfer involved in the diffusion of oxygen ions [29]. For CaBi4Ti4O15, the donor substitution of Gd3+ for Ca2+ at the A-site would decrease the oxygen vacancy concentration in the crystal lattice and then prevent the further grain growth of the ceramics. Therefore, a gradual reduction in the grain size is prospectively observed in the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics with increasing *x*.

*
Fig. 3SEM images of samples derived from their original surfaces (insets are SEM images focused on their thermally etched surfaces).

Furthermore, those SEM images focused on their thermally etched surfaces not only provide clear evidence of the plate-like grain morphology of these CBT-based ceramics but also reveal their random grain orientation behaviors. Some of the lamellar grains grow vertically, and some grow horizontally. This microstructural characteristic agrees well with the fact that the diffraction intensities of the (00l*)-type peaks are much lower than those of the (119) peak in the XRD patterns since all these samples are pressurelessly sintered. On the other hand, as marked by the magenta circles, the thicknesses (*T*) of plate-like grains of CBT–8GM are larger than those of the other samples. The decrease in the aspect ratio *L*/*T* of the plate-like grains may suppress the charge carrier transport due to the decreased contribution from the *a*–*b* plane to the total conductivity of the BLSF crystal. This sample is expected to possess a lower conductivity, which would provide a higher poling electric field for the ceramics. At a higher Gd doping concentration (*x* = 0.11), the significant grain refinement observed in CBT–11GM may imply that excessive Gd3+ begins to substitute for Bi3+ at the A-site of the perovskite-like layer and the increased disorder of A-site cations (Ca2+/Bi3+/Gd3+) remarkably reduces the lattice diffusivity.

Figure 4 shows the compositional analysis of CBT–8GM. All the elements involved in the sample—Ca, Bi, Ti, O, Gd, and Mn—display a uniform distribution without local enrichment, which further confirms the successful diffusion of Gd2O3 and MnO2 into the crystalline lattice of CBT. The layer site of bismuth as well as the oxygen vacancies created at the A-site due to the evaporation of Bi2O3 during high-temperature sintering is suggested to be bound to the introduced Gd3+ as donor-doping ions so that the evaporation of Bi2O3 in the sample is well suppressed.

To confirm the substitution mechanisms of the Gd and Mn ions in CBT, XPS was employed to investigate the valence band structures of the ceramic powders. Figure 5 displays the XPS spectra of the samples. After the C 1s orbital was corrected to 284.80 eV, the fitted peaks of the O 1s spectrum are observed. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the first peak corresponds to the lattice oxygen at the lower binding energy (~530 eV), while the second peak located at ~531 eV, which has a similar binding energy, corresponds to the oxygen vacancy in the lattice. At a higher binding energy (~533 eV), the third peak corresponds to the adsorbed oxygen at the grain boundaries [17]. The substitution mechanisms of Gd ions and Mn ions in CBT can be confirmed on the basis of the changes in their binding energies. When MnO2 was introduced into CBT, Mn ions in the form of Mn3+ entered the crystal lattice to replace Ti4+, resulting in a decrease in lattice oxygen and an increase in oxygen vacancies (Fig. 5(b)). After Gd2O3 was incorporated into the crystal lattice of CBT, the donor substitution of Gd3+ for Ca2+ decreased the concentration of oxygen vacancies in the system and increased the lattice oxygen content (Fig. 5(c)). Additionally, compared with CBT and CBT–0GM, Gd3+ effectively reduced the amount of adsorbed oxygen at the grain boundaries.

### 3.2 Dielectric properties

Figure 6 shows the temperature dependence of the dielectric properties of the samples measured at different frequencies. Above ~400 °C, the relative dielectric permittivity (*ε*r) curves exhibit a low frequency dispersion for all samples, indicating that the lattice defect-induced space charge polarization becomes predominant at high temperatures and at low frequencies in these samples. Typically, the intrinsic dipolar polarization dominates the dielectric behavior for frequencies higher than 104 Hz. Above this frequency, these curves present a typical dielectric anomaly at approximately 800 °C, and their peak position is *T*m, corresponding to the Curie temperature *T*C if *T*m is frequency independent. As marked by the magenta arrow, the maximum dielectric permittivity (*ε*m), associated with the minimum loss tangent (tan*δ*), is significant for the onset of the phase transition process from the ferroelectric state to the paraelectric state. Both the CBT and CBT–100*x*GM ceramics can be identified as normal ferroelectrics on the basis of the uniformity of the characteristic temperatures between the permittivity peaks and loss peaks.

*
Fig. 6Temperature dependence of dielectric properties of samples measured at different frequencies.

Notably, at lower frequencies (100 Hz–10 kHz) and higher temperatures (500–750 °C), the loss curves of CBT–0GM (Fig. 6(b)) show a flabby peak, as marked by the orange ellipse. This relaxed loss peak can be attributed to the viscous domain-wall movement near the T*C, and the minimum loss measured at the *T*C is due to the disappearance of ferroelectric domain walls [30]. This phenomenon disappears after the introduction of Gd3+ (Figs. 6(c)–6(f)). It can be inferred that Gd3+ substitution may decrease the viscous movement of domain walls within the CBT ceramics. On the other hand, the dielectric loss is attributed to the alternating current (AC) conductivity and frequency-independent DC conductivity. The contribution of the AC conductivity to the dielectric loss can be clearly reflected by the strong frequency dispersion of tan*δ* beginning at ~300 °C for CBT and ~400 °C for CBT–100*x*GM. Therefore, Mn3+ substitution may suppress the space-charge conduction behavior of CBT ceramics at high temperatures.

As shown in Fig. 6(a), as marked by the olive arrow, the dielectric permittivity is observed to dramatically decrease with increasing frequency from 100 Hz to 1 MHz, indicating a typically thermally activated Debye relaxation in this frequency region. Around *T*C, the pronounced frequency dispersion of the dielectric permittivity can be attributed to the different relaxation times of the electrical dipoles. Considering that some extrinsic factors, such as point defects, can affect the dielectric behavior of ferroelectrics [31], such a dielectric dispersion phenomenon was significantly suppressed in the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics (Figs. 6(b)–6(f)), implying that the introduction of Gd3+ and Mn3+ modified the defect state within the lattice of CBT.

For a normal ferroelectric, the reciprocal permittivity above its phase transition temperature (*T* > *T*C) obeys the Curie‒Weiss law, which is described by Eq. (1) [32]:

(1) 1ε=T−T0C

where *ε* is the dielectric permittivity at temperature *T*; *C* is the Curie‒Weiss constant; and *T*0 is the Curie‒Weiss temperature, which is generally somewhat lower than *T*C in the case of a first-order phase transition, while for a second-order phase transition, *T*0 = *T*C. In addition, according to the Landau‒Ginzburg theory of ferroelectrics, when the temperature is very close to the *T*C, the Curie‒Weiss law is not valid because of fluctuations in the Gibbs free energy (the onset temperature for the departure of the Curie‒Weiss law on cooling is denoted as *T*CW). Figure 7 shows the temperature dependence of the reciprocal permittivity of the samples measured at 1 MHz. The plots of 1000/*ε*r vs. *T* above *T*CW were fitted by Curie–Weiss’s law for all the samples investigated, and the fitting results are shown in Fig. 7. First, CBT–0GM presents a *T*C value of 793 °C, which is higher than that of CBT (*T*C = 790 °C). A lower amount (0.2 wt%) of MnO2 additive could improve the *T*C of CBT; in the case of a very small size difference between Mn3+ and Ti4+, this result may be attributed mainly to the increased lattice distortion of the perovskite-like layer induced by ionic substitution at the B site. As analyzed via XRD, the unit cell of CBT has an orthorhombicity of 0.5523; after MnO2 was introduced, a significantly increased orthorhombicity (0.9615) can be calculated for the unit cell of CBT–0GM. A similar result was also reported in Ref. [33], where a suitable amount of Mn improving the *T*C of PZT was reported to be related to lattice distortion, as reflected by the tetragonality of the unit cell.

For CBT, the ionic size of Gd3+ is obviously much smaller than that of Ca2+, which could provide TiO6 octahedra with more rattling space and lead to a higher phase transition temperature. It has also been reported that the *T*C of BLSFs tends to increase with decreasing size of A-site-occupied cations [34]. A decreased tolerance factor (*t* = (*r*A + *r*O)/(*r*B + *r*O)) of ABO3 usually corresponds to an increased Curie temperature [35]. As a result, the *T*C values of CBT–100*x*GM increase from 793 to 809 °C with increasing *x* from 0 to 0.08. At a higher Gd doping concentration (*x* = 0.11), the *T*C begins to decrease slightly (806 °C). This result can be attributed to the change in the crystal structure from orthorhombic symmetry to pseudotetragonal symmetry. The tetragonality of CBT increases with the variation in the number of vacancies at the A-site, which is induced by the diffusion of Ca, Bi, and Gd ions, leading to a reduction in the Curie temperature [36]. Furthermore, the fitted values of *T*0 and *C* indicate that *T*0 ˂ *T*C, indicating the first-order phase transition in all the samples investigated. The Curie constant *C* reflects the slope of the reciprocal of the dielectric constant versus the temperature above the *T*C. The displacement-type ferroelectrics obtain *C* values with a magnitude of 105 K, whereas the order-disordered ferroelectrics obtain *C* values with a magnitude of 103 K. For the CBT and CBT–100*x*GM ceramics, the calculated value of *C* is on the order of magnitude of 104, which matches well with that reported for other ferroelectrics, such as KNN–BZN [37] and Na/Ce co-doped CBT [17]. Moreover, this result indicates that the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics are possibly mixed-type ferroelectrics with both displacement and order-disordering characteristics.

### 3.3 Conduction behaviors

Figure 6 shows that at a low frequency of 100 Hz, the tan*δ* values of CBT sharply increase with increasing temperature from 300 °C, which can be considered a sign of the ionic conduction process activated by the high temperature. However, all the CBT–100*x*GM samples maintained a stable tan*δ* value below 400 °C, indicating that the conductivity of CBT was well suppressed through codoping Gd/Mn. As shown in Fig. 1(c), the perovskite-like layer of CaBi4Ti4O15 is composed of four TiO6 octahedra, and ionic conduction is exceptionally viable there because of the existence of oxygen vacancies in the crystal lattice. Oxygen vacancies are normally considered mobile conducting species (i.e., free charge carriers) at high temperatures. In the crystal lattice of BLSFs, intrinsic oxygen vacancies (* VO⋅⋅) are formed due to the inevitable volatilization of Bi2O3 during high-temperature sintering to maintain charge balance with bismuth vacancies ( VBi‴). Because ambient oxygen infiltrates the surfaces of ceramics during cooling, holes are then generated in the lattice through the compensation of oxygen atoms for some oxygen vacancies. In the crystal lattice of CBT–100x*GM, the substitution of two Gd3+ ions for two Ca2+ ions at the A-site eliminates one * VO⋅⋅ associated with two positive charges to maintain charge neutrality. At the B-site, the replacement of two Ti4+ ions by two Mn3+ ions leads to an additional (extrinsic)  VO⋅⋅. The corresponding defect reaction process can be expressed by the following equations with the Kroger–Vink notation (Eqs. (2)–(5)):

(2) 2BiBiX+3OOX→Bi2O3(gas)↑+2VBi‴+3VO⋅⋅

(3) 12O2+VO⋅⋅→OOX+2h⋅

(4) Gd2O3+VO⋅⋅→Ca2+2GdCa⋅+3OOX

(5) MnO2→Ti4+2MnTi′+VO⋅⋅+2OOX

(6) VO⋅⋅+VBi‴→(VO⋅⋅−VBi‴)′

(7) GdCa⋅+MnTi′→(GdCa⋅—MnTi′)X

(8) VO⋅⋅(extrinsic)+MnTi′→(VO⋅⋅−MnTi′)⋅

As shown by Eqs. (6)–(8), since  VO⋅⋅ and  GdCa⋅ are effectively positively charged while  VBi‴ and  MnTi′ are effectively negatively charged, their combination will form the defect dipoles ( VO⋅⋅− VBi‴)ʹ and ( GdCa⋅‒ MnTi′)X, respectively. In addition, it was suggested that the movement of extrinsic oxygen vacancies is limited near the defect center of Ti(Mn) because of the formation of ( VO⋅⋅‒MnTi')· as strongly combined defect dipoles, which do not contribute to the conductive process of the ceramics [13]. As the migration of oxygen vacancies from one site to another can be viewed as the reorientation of defect dipole polarization in the crystal lattice [38], if the long-distance migration of oxygen vacancies responsible for ionic conduction becomes more difficult, a decreased conductivity is expected for the system.

However, the conduction mechanisms of ferroelectric materials are very complicated, as they involve ionic conductivity, which may be extrinsic or intrinsic depending on charge carriers: electronics, holes, and defects (oxygen vacancies, cation vacancies, etc.). The AC conductivity (σ*ac) accounts for the bound and free charge carriers, and its values can be calculated from the dielectric parameters (*ε*r and tan*δ*) via Eq. (9):

(9) σac=ωε0εr×tan⁡δ

where *ω* = 2π*f* is the angle frequency of the applied electric field and *ε*0 is the permittivity of free space.

Figure 8 shows the temperature dependence of the AC conductivity of the samples at different frequencies. The observed variation in conductivity with increasing temperature can be divided into two distinct regions: in the low-temperature region (CBT: 300–500 K; CBT–100*x*GM: 300–673 K), the conductivity shows a weak temperature dependence and strong frequency dispersion. In the high-temperature region (CBT: 500–1073 K, CBT–100*x*GM: 673–1073 K), the conductivity shows a weak frequency dispersion and a strong temperature dependence. The conductivity increases sharply with increasing temperature, indicating typical negative temperature coefficient of resistance (NTCR) behavior. At high temperatures, the intrinsic conduction process dominates over the extrinsic conduction process, and the measured values of *σ*ac are comparable to those of *σ*dc in this case. In addition, as shown by those orange circles marked in Figs. 8(c)–8(f), at a high frequency of 1 MHz, a conductivity anomaly is observed around the *T*C for those CBT–100*x*GM samples with *x* ≥ 0.02. However, with a decrease in the measured frequency (100 Hz–100 kHz), this anomaly gradually disappears. This phenomenon may be explained as follows. As the frequency decreases, oxygen vacancies become active and dominate the growth of the ferroelectric phase, so that no clear anomalies appear in either the temperature dependence of the dielectric permittivity or the electrical conductivity [39].

*
Fig. 8Temperature dependence of AC conductivity of samples at different frequencies.

For normal oxide compounds, the intrinsic conductivity results from the long-distance migration of the component ions or ionic vacancies, whereas the short-distance hopping of the impurity ions existing in the crystal lattice contributes to the extrinsic conductivity. According to the type of conduction process (intrinsic or extrinsic), the total conductivity as a function of temperature can be described by Eq. (10) [40]:

(10) σ=Aexp⁡(−EakBT)+Bexp⁡(−EbkBT)

where K* is the Boltzmann constant and *T* is the temperature; *A* and *B* are pre-exponential factors; and *E*a and *E*b are the activation energies required for the intrinsic and extrinsic conduction processes respectively, which can be estimated from the fitting of these experimental plots in the high-temperature and low-temperature regions, respectively. Figure 8 shows that *E*a and *E*b take significantly different values. This is because the intrinsic defects whose formation requires a high activation energy are the main charge carriers at high temperatures. At low temperatures, the activation energy is related mainly to impurity defects and has a significantly lower value.

For CBT (Fig. 8(a)), the value of *E*a in the high-temperature region is found to be 1.02 eV, which agrees completely with the reported value (~1 eV) of the activation energy of oxygen vacancies for the conduction process in perovskite oxides [39]. For CBT–100*x*GM, the *E*a value of ~1.59 eV estimated for the sample with *x* = 0 (Fig. 8(b)) is very close to the activation energy of the MnCO3-modified CBT, which was reported to be 1.58 eV [12]. With increasing *x* from 0 to 0.08, the *E*a values gradually increase from 1.59 to 1.78 eV (Figs. 8(c)–8(f)). This result agrees with the statement that the oxygen vacancy concentration tends to be suppressed to some extent in the donor-doped materials, so that the ionic conduction is weakened. However, the activation energy of oxygen vacancies for the conduction process involves both their thermal activation and their long-distance migration. The *E*a values do not continue to increase when *x* exceeds 0.08, indicating that the long-distance migration of oxygen vacancies becomes easier at high Gd doping concentrations. This may be attributed to the fact that CBT–11GM has a greatly reduced orthorhombicity (Fig. 2(a)), and the pseudo-tetragonal unit cell possibly makes the long-distance migration of oxygen vacancies in the perovskite-like layer easier. As a result, its *E*a value begins to decrease slightly. This result also agrees with the conclusion reported in Ref. [41].

On the other hand, at low temperatures, the activation energy of the conduction process of the ferroelectric phase may be related to the hopping of carriers between localized sites (such as Ti4+ + é ↔ Ti3+ in CBT). In this work, the *E*b values were found to be 0.08 eV for CBT and 0.09–0.21 eV for CBT–100*x*GM. The calculated value of 0.08 eV for the activation energy may be associated with the small polarons created by the electron and/or hole‒phonon interactions and tends to be reinforced (0.04–0.21 eV) by the lattice mismatch of the distorted perovskite-like layer [42].

When piezoelectric ceramics are used in electronic devices, to maintain the charges induced by stresses for a time long enough to be detected by the back-end detecting circuit, piezoelectric ceramics should possess both a high piezoelectric coefficient and a high DC resistivity. Figure 9 shows the temperature dependence of the DC resistivity (*ρ*dc) of the samples between 300 and 800 °C. As expected, the resistivity values gradually decrease with rise in temperature, indicating the activation process of charge carriers due to thermal energy, as analyzed above. Compared with CBT, CBT–0GM has a higher resistivity. Moreover, the introduction of MnO2 can significantly decrease the descent rate of resistivity between 300 and 500 °C. After Gd3+ was introduced, the resistivity further increased, which was more obvious above 500 °C. In particular, the *ρ*dc value of CBT–8GM at 500 °C reached 3.2×106 Ω·cm, which is equivalent to that of highly textured CaBi4Ti4O15 ceramics fabricated via SPS (*ρ*dc = 2.7×106 Ω·cm at 500 °C [18]). At 650 °C, the *ρ*dc values of CBT–100*x*GM are 2.3×104, 6.2×104, 1.04×105, 1.3×105, and 9.8×104 Ω·cm for *x* = 0, 0.02, 0.06, 0.08, and 0.11, respectively. Owing to the co-doping effects of Gd/Mn, CBT–8GM has a notably increased resistivity, which is approximately 2 orders of magnitude greater than that of CBT (*ρ*dc = 4.7×103 Ω·cm at 650 °C). In addition, at the same temperature, the change in the DC resistivity with increasing Gd concentration is consistent with that of the intrinsic activation energy (*E*a). That is, a larger activation energy corresponds to a higher DC resistivity when the conduction behavior of BLSF ceramics at high temperatures is considered. Moreover, larger intrinsic activation energy also implies that the oxygen vacancy concentration has been largely reduced by introducing Gd/Mn dopants, which is partly responsible for the increase in the DC resistivity and is expected to improve the ferroelectric properties.

### 3.4 Ferroelectric properties

It is well known that both the dielectric and piezoelectric properties of ferroelectric ceramics are largely dependent on their ferroelectric properties. The *P–E* and *I–E* hysteresis loops of the samples were measured at 1 Hz and 150 °C and are shown in Fig. 10. With the exception of the pure CBT, all the samples exhibit a saturated *P–E* hysteresis loop. Two pronounced current switching peaks can be clearly observed from their corresponding *I‒E* hysteresis loops. The ferroelectric properties of CBT are significantly modified by the introduction of MnO2, which is possibly attributed to the improved domain wall mobility. The presence of multi-valence Mn additives allowed better broad switching within the ceramic [36]. In addition, an interesting phenomenon observed from these hysteresis loops is that the positions of the current peaks in the *I–E* loops do not agree with the intercept on the *x*-axis of the *P–E* loops, which may be due to the oxygen vacancies gathering at the grain boundaries exerting a pinning effect on some domain walls. When Gd3+ is substituted for Ca2+, a reduction in the oxygen vacancy concentration can release the pinning effect. As a result, the disparity between these two positions (i.e., the coercive field) decreases with increasing Gd concentration, as marked by those magenta vertical lines. At high Gd doping concentration (*x* = 0.11), the values of the coercive field tend to be consistent. A similar phenomenon has been observed in Nb/Ce-co-doped CaBi4Ti4O15 ceramics [17].

*
Fig. 10P*–*E* and *I*–*E* hysteresis loops of samples.

Some representative ferroelectric parameters, such as spontaneous polarization *P*s, remanent polarization *P*r, and coercive field *E*c (*E*c = (*E*c+ + *E*c−)/2), can be read from these *P–E* and *I–E* hysteresis loops. Figure 11 shows these ferroelectric parameters of the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics as a function of *x*. Clearly, both *P*s and *P*r increase with *x*. The increase in spontaneous polarization is attributed primarily to the reduction in the volume and *a*-axis length of the unit cell. With the help of the donor substitution effect of Gd3+, the decrease in the coercive field facilitates easier movement of non-180° domain walls within the *a–b* plane. As a result, the remanent polarization of the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics also increases with increasing Gd concentration. On the other hand, the increase in remanent polarization may also be related to the decrease in grain size. For those Gd-doped samples, a smaller grain size leads to a higher grain boundary density, which favors the flipping of those fine domains and thus increases *P*r [43]. However, when *x* ˃ 0.08, an increased *E*c value occurs in CBT–11GM. As analyzed previously, at high Gd doping concentrations, excessive Gd3+ tends to interact with * MnTi′ to form defect dipoles ( GdCa⋅‒ MnTi′)X, which intensifies the pinning effect on domain walls. Under an electric field, the local domain evolution around dipolar defects has already demonstrated the physical process of the domain pinning effect, i.e., the difficulty of domain switching due to the presence of defect dipoles [44].

### 3.5 Electroelastic properties

Figure 12 shows the frequency dependence of the impedance and phase angle of the samples measured at room temperature. These resonant spectra correspond to the radial vibration modes of the thin-disk shaped piezoceramic resonators. In the frequency range of 200 Hz–2 MHz, four pairs of resonance‒antiresonance peaks exist in the impedance curves (red) for all the samples investigated. The corresponding characteristic frequencies are associated with reflections of ultrasound within the body of the piezoelectric ceramics. The impedance |*Z*| drops to a minimum at the resonant frequency *f*r and rises to a maximum at the antiresonant frequency *f*a. At the center of the resonant frequency range, the phase angle *θ* roughly increases to a maximum value. Moreover, the instantaneous power increases as the resonance is approached and decreases as the antiresonance is approached [45]. For all the samples investigated, the measured resonant (*f*r ≈ *f*s) and anti-resonant frequency (*f*a ≈ *f*p) pairs correspond to different vibration modes ((*f*sr(0), *f*pr(0)), (*f*sr(1), *f*pr(1)), (*f*sr(2), *f*pr(2)), (*f*sr(3), *f*pr(3))). The first pair peak (*f*sr(0), *f*pr(0)) is the fundamental mode, which is excited by the radial vibration in the largest dimension of the thin piezoceramic disks, and the three others that closely follow the first pair peak are radial overtone modes with progressively increasing frequencies and inharmonic frequency separation. The series resonant frequency (*f*sn) of the piezoceramic thin disk in the *n*-th radial vibration mode can be calculated by Eq. (11):

(11) fsn=αn2πR1s11Eρ(1−σ2)

In the radial vibration mode, the disk subjected to an applied electrical voltage in the thickness direction contracts in the radial direction and expands in the thickness direction due to the effect of the Poisson’s ratio. Clearly, the strength of the harmonics and the spacing between *f*s and *f*p decreases as the order increases for all the samples investigated. (12) σp=b0+b1rs+b2rs2+b3rs3+b4rs4

First, *σ*p was found to be 0.23 and 0.24–0.25 for the CBT and CBT–100*x*GM ceramics, respectively. Co-doping Gd/Mn increases the Poisson’s ratio of CBT. A greater lateral deformation ability may be related to changes in the microstructural features of the ceramics. The increase in the elastic stiffness constant indicates that CBT became “harder” with co-doping Gd/Mn, which may be due to the reduced oxygen vacancy concentration. When *x* ≤ 0.06, the significant decrease in the elastic stiffness constant may be attributed to the relaxation of the structural distortion with the incorporation of less Gd3+ into the perovskite-like layer. When *x* > 0.06, the grain size of the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics begins to decrease meaningfully, and the fine-grain strengthening effect is responsible for the progressive increase in the elastic stiffness. Subjected to the intrinsic inverse relationship between the elastic stiffness and the elastic compliance, both * s11E and – s12E vary with x* in the opposite way relative to * c11p.

Additionally, the variations in k*p and *k*31 with *x* show opposite tendencies with respect to those of *Q*m and *N*p, and the sample with *x* = 0.06 achieves the maximum *k*p and *k*31 values, as well as the minimum *Q*m and *N*p values, among the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics. It is well known that the poling process of ferroelectric ceramics takes place owing to the domain-wall motion. It is well known that the oxygen vacancies as space charges located at domain walls produce the “pinning” effect against the reversion of ferroelectric domains through the interaction between such positively charged point defects and the negatively charged domain walls [48]. This effect results in inadequate poling, leading to a lower *k*p and higher *Q*m of the ferroelectric ceramics. When *x* ≤ 0.06, both the increase in *k*p and the decrease in *Q*m can be attributed to the elimination of intrinsic oxygen vacancies with the introduction of the donor-type Gd3+ into CBT (Eq. (4)). However, when *x* > 0.06, *k*p decreases while *Q*m increases with increasing *x*. This opposite variation can be explained as follows. With increasing doping concentration of Gd3+, an increasing number of defect dipoles (* GdCa⋅‒ MnTi′)X were created in the crystal lattice of CBT (Eq. (7)). The internal bias field established by these defect dipoles prohibits the domain-wall motion in the poling process of ceramics [49]. Notably, the sample with x* = 0.11 has the highest *Q*m value of up to 1407 among the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics, and a high *Q*m value is conducive to increasing the output power and temperature stability of the piezoelectric resonator [50].

Figure 14 shows the resonance‒antiresonance spectra (fundamental radial mode) of CBT–11GM measured at different temperatures. The resonance‒antiresonance pair peaks of the fundamental radial mode gradually shift to the lower frequency side with rise in temperature. The temperature coefficient of the resonance frequency (*TC*f) was calculated to be on the order of −31 ppm/°C in the temperature range of 20–550 °C. Notably, the resonance–antiresonance pair peaks tend to disappear at 700 °C, indicating that the thermal depoling behavior begins at this temperature. Since the resonance–antiresonance spectra of the sample were measured at high temperatures, this temperature can be viewed as the intrinsic thermal depoling temperature of the sample.

*
Fig. 14Resonance‒antiresonance spectra (fundamental radial mode) of CBT–11GM measured at different temperatures (inset shows temperature dependence of planar frequency constant).

Like some other BLSF ceramics [51,52], the amplitude of the electrical impedance (|Z*|) of the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics also decreases gradually with increasing temperature, which results from the increasing conductivity. In this case, the equivalent electrical circuit of the piezoelectric resonator becomes invalid, and the related equations for calculating piezoelectric parameters such as *k*p and *Q*m are also inapplicable [53]. However, the frequency constant (*N*p) of the piezoelectric resonator is related only to its resonance frequency, which is not dependent on its equivalent electrical circuit. The variation in the resonance frequency with temperature in Aurivillius-type structured ceramics depends on the vibration mode.

The inset of Fig. 14 shows the *N*p values as a function of temperature. The *N*p–*T* curve contains two regions with different variational slopes. In the temperature range of 20–550 °C, the linear decrease in the frequency constant with rise in temperature can be attributed to the fact that the elastic stiffness constant (* c11p) of most of piezoelectric ceramics tends to decrease linearly with rise in temperature. When the temperature exceeded 550 °C, the N*p values exhibited a more rapid decreasing trend. This is because the phonon spectrum of piezoelectric ceramics changes significantly around the structural phase transition temperature (*T*C), and anomalies in the elastic compliance (“softening”) lead to anomalous temperature coefficients of the acoustic wave velocity (*v* = ( ρs11E)−1/2) [54].

### 3.6 Piezoelectric properties

For most piezoelectric ceramics, the longitudinal piezoelectric charge constant *d*33 is positive, whereas the transverse piezoelectric charge constant *d*31 is negative, and the value of *d*31 is nearly equal to half that of *d*33, with the opposite sign. However, the measurement of *d*31 requires both a rectangular sample and a special fixture. In this work, the combination of * ε33T,  s11E, σ*p, and *k*p can help us calculate *d*31, further increasing the hydrostatic charge coefficient *d*h, which is an important parameter for assessing piezoelectric materials for use in hydrophone applications. In addition, the piezoelectric voltage constants *g*33 and *g*31 ((V·m)/N) can also be calculated from *d*33 and *d*31 (pC/N), respectively, when the free dielectric permittivity * ε33T (F/m) is known. Equations (20)–(22) are given for calculating these piezoelectric coefficients:

(20) d31=k31ε33Ts11E=kpε33Ts11E(1−σp)/2

(21) dh=d33+2d31

(22) gh=g33+2g31=d33/ε33T+2d31/ε33T

Figure 15 shows the piezoelectric coefficients of the CBT–100x*GM ceramics as a function of *x.* The values of *d*33 and *g*33 increase with increasing *x* until they reach a maximum at *x* = 0.08. Owing to the donor-substituting effect of Gd3+ for Ca2+, the elimination of intrinsic oxygen vacancies relieves its pinning effect on domain-wall motion. In the poling process of ferroelectric ceramics, an easier domain-wall motion could provide them with a larger remanent polarization. As a result, the piezoelectric activity of the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics rapidly increases with increasing Gd doping concentration. However, CBT–11GM shows a slight decrease in the *d*33 values. Although this sample has a continuously increased remanent polarization (AC poling), its notably reduced grain size leads to a larger internal stress and a lower poling electric field strength, which both influence the actual degree of ferroelectric domain switching during DC poling [55]. As expected, *d*31 has the same variation trend with that of *k*p, which can be understood via Eq. (20). The sample with *x* = 0.06, which has a larger *k*p value, has the largest −*d*31 value among the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics. On the other hand, both the *d*h and *g*h values monotonically increase with increasing *x*. The largest hydrostatic piezoelectric coefficient is achieved by CBT–11GM rather than CBT–8GM, which can be attributed to its *d*31 being lower than that of the latter.

*
Fig. 15Piezoelectric properties of CBT–100x*GM ceramics as a function of *x*.

In this work, the CBT ceramics are found to have a *d*33 value of 8 pC/N. After co-doping with Gd/Mn, its piezoelectric activity was enhanced by 2–3 times compared with that of the pure composition. In particular, CBT–8GM obtains an optimized *d*33 value of 23 pC/N. Moreover, it also has a high *g*33 value of up to 21.5×10−3 (V·m)/N, which is close to that of the commercial PZT-5A ceramics (*g*33 = 24×10−3 (V·m)/N, PIEZO SYSTEM INC, USA) [56]. In addition to the donor-substituting effect of Gd3+ for Ca2+ contributing to the increase in piezoelectric activity, this result also benefits from the improved poling characteristics of CBT with the introduction of MnO2. Since the reduced Mn ions can trap weakly bonded electrons to reduce leakage loss, the CBT ceramics can thus be poled with an improved electric field. Similarly, it has been reported that the addition of MnO2 leads to higher *k*t, *k*p, *d*31, and *g*31 values for SrBi4Ti4O15 ceramics at the same poling field [57].

Thermal annealing experiments are usually employed to investigate the extrinsic thermal depoling behavior of piezoelectric ceramics. Figure 16 displays the normalized *d*33 values of the samples as a function of the annealing temperature. First, all the samples investigated keep a stable *d*33 value before the annealing temperature reaches ~300 °C. The good thermal stability observed in this temperature region (RT–300 °C) indicates that non-180° domain walls that are piezoelectrically active and thermally unstable hardly exist in these CBT-based ceramics with an even-layer (*m* = 4) octahedron structure [58]. In succession, the *d*33 values of CBT−100*x*GM begin to decrease gradually, whereas those of CBT continue to remain stable until ~700 °C. Two reasons are speculated for this result. First, those defect dipoles formed by the introduction of Gd3+ and Mn4+ were thermally decoupled and randomized gradually in this temperature range (300–700 °C), and the weakening of the orientated polarization resulting from defect dipoles may induce the gradual degradation of piezoelectric activity. As a result of a lower defect dipole concentration, the *d*33 values of CBT can keep stable. On the other hand, the reduced piezoelectric activity of CBT–100*x*GM in this temperature range may also be due to the local electric field and stress field established by defect dipoles, which can promote the 180° domain-wall motion at elevated temperatures.

*
Fig. 16Normalized d*33 values of samples as function of annealing temperature (inset shows the residual *d*33 values of the samples after being annealed at 700 °C).

Below the *T*C, the thermally activated domain back-switching partly causes a decrease in the remanent polarization of the ferroelectric ceramics. However, when the annealing temperature is close to the *T*C, the *d*33 values of all the samples investigated decrease rapidly and tend to zero after the annealing temperature exceeds the *T*C. In this temperature region (700 °C–*T*C), the randomized process of intrinsic dipoles causes disordering of the ferroelectric domains, leading to rapid degradation of the piezoelectric activity. Notably, weak piezoelectricity can still be measured in all the samples that have been thermally annealed at temperatures exceeding their *T*C. This phenomenon may be attributed to the diffuse ferroelectric‒paraelectric phase transition that is related to the compositional fluctuations in the perovskite-like layer [59].

The entire thermal depoling process is accompanied by domain‒wall motion, which can cause considerable dielectric loss. The extrinsic thermal depoling temperature (*T*d), which is determined as the annealing temperature from which the *d*33 values begin to decrease sharply (i.e., the slope of the line changes again), was estimated to be approximately 700–750 °C for the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics. Since the thermally induced domain breakage is first established with conduction and phase transitions [60], the improved thermal depoling temperature of CBT–100*x*GM benefits from a decreased electrical conductivity and an increased Curie temperature. Moreover, the *T*d value of CBT–11GM is found to be approximately 725 °C, which is consistent with that determined from the temperature-dependent resonance–antiresonance curves.

Furthermore, the inset of Fig. 16 shows the residual *d*33 values of the samples after being annealed at 700 °C. The CBT ceramic shows a residual *d*33 of up to 94% (relative to the initial value before annealing), but its initial piezoelectric activity is very low. Among the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics, the sample with *x* = 0.08 (*T*d ≈ 750 °C) retains a residual *d*33 ≈ 80% after being annealed at 700 °C. This good anti-thermal depoling ability endows this material with great application potential in high-temperature piezoelectric devices, such as ultrasound transducers and accelerometers, with operating temperatures exceeding 500 °C.

Finally, the dielectric, piezoelectric, and electroelastic properties of the samples are summarized in Table 1. Clearly, the piezoelectric activity and Curie temperature of CBT were enhanced synchronously by co-doping Gd/Mn. As analyzed previously, the piezoelectric activity of BLSFs is associated with [BO6] octahedron distortions and oxygen vacancies, while the Curie temperature is related to the tolerance factor of the perovskite-like layer. Through investigations of the structures and properties of the CBT and CBT–100*x*GM ceramics, the synergistic enhancement in the piezoelectric activity and Curie temperature of CBT can be attributed mainly to the donor-substituting effect of Gd3+ at the A-site, as well as the decreased elastic compliance contributed by MnO2 as the B-site dopant. It has been reported that the piezoelectric coefficient is usually greater in those sintered ceramics samples with a lower compliance, which can favor the cracking under poling [61].

It is well known that for most of ferroelectric ceramics, the increase in *d*33 is commonly accompanied by a decrease in the *T*C. Aurivillius-type compounds (BLSFs) are not an exception. The pure CBT ceramic has a relatively high *T*C (790 °C) but a very low *d*33 (8 pC/N). To improve the piezoelectric activity and maintain a high Curie temperature, much attention has focused on ion modification of CBT ceramics. A comparison of the *d*33 and *T*C values of the CBT ceramics with various compositions is shown in Fig. 17. The optimized composition of the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics (CBT–8GM) has a high *d*33 ≈ 23 pC/N associated with a high *T*C ≈ 809 °C, which is more competitive than the others reported in these works.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, the effects of the doping concentration of Gd3+ on the structures and properties of Gd/Mn-co-doped CBT ceramics were investigated systematically. The synchronous improvements in the Curie temperature and piezoelectric activity of CBT are strongly associated with the contributions of Gd and Mn ions in substituting the A- and B-sites of the perovskite-like layer, respectively. The main conclusions are summarized as follows.

1) Single-phase four-layered Aurivillius ceramics with a chemical composition of Ca1−*x*Gd*x*Bi4Ti4O15+0.2 wt% MnO2 (CBT–100*x*GM) were successfully synthesized via a conventional sintering process; at high Gd doping concentrations (*x* = 0.11), the unit cell was transformed from orthorhombic to pseduo-tetragonal (prototype) symmetry.

2) The *T*C values of the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics increased from 793 to 809 °C with increasing *x* from 0 to 0.08. All the samples investigated were found to have a first-order phase transition behavior and were identified as the mixed-type ferroelectrics with both displacement and order−disordering characteristics.

3) The introduction of MnO2 greatly promoted the ferroelectric domain switching of CBT; in addition to the donor substitution effect of Gd3+, an increased remanent polarization associated with a reduced coercive field was achieved in the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics when *x* ≤ 0.08.

4) Above 400 °C, the electrical conduction of the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics can be attributed to the intrinsic behavior that is predominated by the long-distance migration of oxygen vacancies. The corresponding activation energy (*E*a) was found to increase from 1.59 to 1.78 eV with increase in *x* from 0 to 0.08.

5) Three overtone modes were observed to follow the fundamental radial mode in the CBT–100*x*GM ceramics. The resonance‒antiresonance pair peaks of the sample with *x* = 0.11 tend to disappear at 700 °C, indicating that its thermal depoling behavior begins at this temperature.

6) The optimized composition with *x* = 0.08 had a high *T*C ≈ 809 °C and a high *d*33 ≈ 23 pC/N, as well as a *g*33 value up to 21.5×10−3 (V·m)/N, indicating that the piezoelectric activity and Curie temperature of CBT can be enhanced synchronously by the co-doping of Gd/Mn.
