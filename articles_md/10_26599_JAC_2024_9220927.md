## 1 Introduction

With the mature development of 5G network communication technology, the demand for microwave dielectric ceramics is continually growing, necessitating higher standards. Microwave dielectric ceramics play a crucial role in enabling the efficient operation of passive devices in telecommunications infrastructure, such as filters, antennas, and resonators [1,2]. The escalating standards for microwave dielectric ceramics are driven by the imperative to achieve optimal dielectric constants (*ɛ*r), high-quality factors (*Q*×*f*), and near-zero resonant frequency temperature coefficients (*τ*f). Extensive research has been dedicated to enhancing the application of microwave dielectric ceramics in 5G/6G communication systems to develop high-performance materials [3−5].

Among various ceramic materials, Ti-based ceramics have garnered significant attention in both scientific and industrial domains due to their cost-effectiveness and exceptional properties [6−12]. Recent studies have extensively explored the ZnO–TiO2 system ceramics [13−16] for various applications, including desulfurization catalysts, white pigments, and dielectric materials. Within the ZnO–TiO2 system [17−20], three key compounds have been identified: Zn2TiO4, ZnTiO3, and Zn2Ti3O8. Notably, Zn2TiO4, with its exclusive inverse spinel structure, demonstrates remarkable phase stability at elevated temperatures. In contrast, ZnTiO3 in the ilmenite phase exhibits thermal decomposition into Zn2TiO4 and rutile TiO2 at temperatures exceeding 945 °C, indicating temperature sensitivity and limited sintering capability [21,22]. Initial reports by Kim *et al*. [22] on the microwave dielectric properties of ZnTiO3 revealed values of *ɛ*r ≈ 19, *Q*×*f* ≈ 30,000 GHz, and *τ*f ≈ −55 ppm/°C. Recent research has primarily focused on exploring the synthesis process, phase stability, and performance regulation of ZnTiO3 ceramics [13,23−25]. However, the enhancement of microwave dielectric properties has predominantly relied on the addition of sintering aids, such as ZnTiO3+1 wt% B2O3 ceramics (*Q*×*f* = 49,000 GHz, *τ*f = −32.35 ppm/°C) [26] and (Zn0.9Mg0.1)TiO3+0.5 wt% Zn–B–Si (*Q*×*f* = 61,630 GHz, *τ*f = −76 ppm/°C) [27], with limited improvements. The introduction and substitution of Li+ ions hold promise for improving sintering characteristics and enhancing the microwave dielectric properties of ceramics [28–33]. Particularly noteworthy is the observation by Huang *et al*. [34] that substituting Mg2+ sites in CaMgSi2O6 ceramics with Li+ resulted in outstanding microwave dielectric properties, a finding further validated in Li2*x*Mg2−*x*SiO4 ceramics [35]. It was demonstrated that the introduction of Li+ ions can lead to the development of phase-stable solid solutions in Zn/Ti-based microwave dielectric ceramics, exhibiting favorable compatibility and excellent microwave dielectric properties [36–46]. Examples include ordered spinel Li2MTi3O8 (M = Zn, Mg, Co, Ni) with space group *P*4332, LiZnNbO4 with space group *P*4122, and disordered spinel Li4Ti5O12 with space group *Fd** 3¯m*, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Despite these advancements, systematic studies on how Li+ ions affect the crystal structure and microwave dielectric properties of ZnTiO3-based ceramics remain limited, presenting a significant opportunity for further research.

The unique lattice types and tunable chemical compositions of ZnTiO3-based microwave dielectric ceramics give them different phase structures and thus different dielectric properties. Based on this phase engineering strategy, this work provides an insight into the role of Li+-induced phase structure modulation on the properties of ZnTiO3-based microwave dielectric ceramics. We synthesized Zn1–x*Li2*x*TiO3 ceramics using the solid-state method, leveraging the similar effective ionic radii of Li+ and Zn2+ (* RLi+ = 0.76 Å,  RZn2+ = 0.74 Å). Our investigation systematically explored the phase transition process to establish the constitutive correlation between changes in composition, the evolution of crystal structure, and microwave dielectric properties. Ultimately, we identified ceramic components with outstanding microwave dielectric performance. Furthermore, we applied some methodologies such as the Phillips–van Vechten–Levine (P–V–L) chemical bond theory, Raman vibrational mode analysis, assessment of octahedral distortion (Δ*), evaluation of ionic polarizability, bond valence, and bond energy theory to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the evolution of crystal structure and intrinsic microwave dielectric properties. These investigations are poised to provide theoretical insights for optimizing microwave dielectric properties in ZnTiO3-based ceramics.

## 2 Experimental procedure

The high purity raw materials, ZnO (> 99.9%, Sinopharm), Li2CO3 (> 99.9%, Sinopharm), and TiO2 (> 99%, Sinopharm) of Zn1–*x*Li2*x*TiO3 (*x* = 0, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1) ceramics were weighed in stoichiometric ratios and ball-milled with anhydrous ethanol for 10 h. The resulting powders were dried at 100 °C for 2 h and calcined in a muffle furnace at 800 °C for 6 h. The calcined powders underwent a second round of ball milling for 10 h using the same conditions as before, resulting in finer and more homogeneous powder. After drying, the powders were mixed with 5 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) to form granules. These granules were then pressed into cylindrical columns measuring 10 mm in diameter and 6 mm in height under 20 MPa. The samples were placed in a well-sealed crucible and burned with feedstock powder as sacrificial powder to mitigate the volatilization of Li+. Subsequently, the samples underwent a sintering process in the muffle furnace, with temperatures ranging from 940 to 1230 °C. Prior to the sintering process, a calcination step was carried out at 550 °C to eliminate the organic matter PVA.

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD, X'pert Pro MPD, Philips) with a detection range of 10°–120° was used to identify the physical phase. The details of the structural changes induced by component variations were further explored by Rietveld structure refinement using Fullprof software based on XRD data. The transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-2100F, JEOL) was utilized to acquire both the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images. The SAED patterns and HRTEM images were captured to examine the structural characteristics of the samples at a high resolution. Zn1–*x*Li2*x*TiO3 samples were polished to a mirror finish using a polishing machine (UNIPOL-802, Kejing) and then subjected to thermal etching at a temperature 50 °C below the sintering temperature for 30 min. The microscopic morphology of the samples was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Quanta 250, FEI). The determination of grain sizes was carried out by measuring the linear intercept method [47]. Raman spectra were performed using a Raman scattering spectrometer (inViaTM, Renishaw). The determination of *ε*r and *Q*×*f* values was carried out using the Hakki–Coleman method (TE011 mode) with the assistance of a network analyzer (E5071C, Agilent Technologies). A temperature chamber at 25–85 °C was used to determine the *τ*f value of the ceramics. *τ*f was calculated by Eq. (1) [32]:

(1) τf=f85−f25(85−25)f25

The resonant frequencies at 85 and 25 °C were denoted by *f*85 and *f*25, respectively.

## 3 Results and discussion

The XRD patterns of Zn1−*x*Li2*x*TiO3 (*x* = 0, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1) ceramics at the corresponding optimum sintering temperatures (940, 1120, 1120, 1120, 1080, 1160, 1200, and 1230 °C) are shown in Fig. 2. With the introduction of Li+ into the lattice of Zn1−*x*Li2*x*TiO3 ceramic, a series of phase transitions occur. When 0.1 ≤ *x* 3 (Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS#00-026-1500) with a space group *R** 3¯ to the rutile phase TiO2 (JCPDS#00-021-1276) and the cubic ordered spinel phase Li2ZnTi3O8 with a space group P*4332. When *x* = 0.2, the XRD diffraction peaks can match exactly with the standard card (JCPDS#01-086-1512) of cubic spinel Li2ZnTi3O8, which means that the derived by-product rutile TiO2 successfully enters the crystal lattice of spinel to form a solid solution. As *x* increases to 0.25, the intensities of the diffraction peaks (022), (210), and (110) gradually weaken, suggesting a decrease in the ordering of the spinel phase. When *x* = 0.5, a disordered spinel phase structure (space group *Fd** 3¯m*) is formed, which matches well with the standard card of Zn2TiO4 ceramics (JCPDS#96-901-2445), and no additional diffraction peak is observed. When *x* increases to 0.75, a phenomenon of coexistence of two phases is observed, namely the Zn2TiO4 and Li2TiO3 phases. When *x* reaches 1, a pure phase of Li2TiO3 is formed, with a space group of *C*2/*c*. Li+ successfully induces the generation of a multiple phase transition.

More details of the component–structure relationships of Zn1−*x*Li2*x*TiO3 ceramics were investigated by Rietveld refinement. The refinement profiles of the Zn1−*x*Li2*x*TiO3 ceramics are shown in Fig. 3, along with the corresponding low refinement reliability factors (*R*wp, *R*exp, and *R*p), and the theoretical model and the experimental data can be fitted well. ZnTiO3 (*R** 3¯), Li2ZnTi3O8 (P*4332), Zn2TiO4 (*Fd** 3¯m*), and Li2TiO3 (*C*2/*c*) are chosen as structural models for single phase ceramics at *x* = 0, *x* = 0.2 and 0.25, *x* = 0.5, and *x* = 1, respectively. Details of the crystal structure parameters obtained by refinement are listed in Table 1, including crystal system, cell bulk *V*, bond lengths, and valence (* VLi+) for the Li–O bond. The phase transition process and the distribution of cations in the ceramic Zn1–x*Li2*x*TiO3 (*x* = 0, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1) are presented in Fig. 4. Apparently, the component changes induced a series of phase transitions. Li+ keeps replacing the Zn2+ position (6*c*) in the ZnTiO3 lattice. The first phase transition occurs when *x* is 0.1. This phase transition leads to the formation of the cubic spinel phase Li2ZnTi3O8 and the rutile phase TiO2. After refinement, the mass ratio of the two is determined to be 95.42 : 4.58. As the Li+ substitution is increased to 0.15, some of the rutile phase TiO2 dissolves into the spinel phase lattice, despite no changes in the phase compositi on. Consequently, the mass fraction of rutile decreased to 4.16%. This interstitial solid solution formation also leads to expansion of the main phase lattice, resulting in an increase in lattice volume, as shown in Table 1. When *x* = 0.2, TiO2 completely enters the ordered phase spinel lattice. A single spinel pure phase with the space group *P*4332 is formed. At *x* = 0.2 and 0.25, Li+ exclusively occupies the 4*b* site to form octahedra, while Zn2+ prefers to occupy the tetrahedral position (8*c*). In addition, the main crystalline phase cell volume is smaller than the cell volume of *x* = 0.1, 0.15 ceramics, which is ascribed to the different occupancy changes of cations inside the crystals. As *x* increases to 0.5, the second phase transition occurs, and the phase structure successfully transforms from an ordered spinel to a disordered spinel structure with a space group *Fd** 3¯m*. Concurrently, Zn2+ ions preferentially occupy the tetrahedral positions (8*a*) due to the octahedral site preference energy of 0 kcal/mol [48,49]. The remaining vacancies are filled by Li1+ ions, and the rest of the ions are randomly distributed at the 16*d* site, resulting in a completely disordered state. At *x* = 0.75, the third phase transition occurs, and the phase composition of Li2TiO3 and Zn2TiO4 has a mass ratio of 78.05 : 21.95. Ultimately, it transforms into a single pure phase of the monoclinic crystal system Li2TiO3 with a space group *C*2/*c* at *x* = 1.

SAED patterns and HRTEM images were used to further investigate the crystal structure evolution of Zn1−*x*Li2*x*TiO3 (*x* = 0, 0.2, 0.25, 0.5, and 1) ceramics. As shown in Fig. 5, the presence of bright diffraction spots confirms that the ceramic is well crystalline. The SAED patterns for *x* = 0.2 and 0.25 (Figs. 5(c) and 5(f)) exhibit characteristics consistent with primitive cubic spinel phases. Additionally, diffraction crystal planes (110), (210), and (211) can be observed along the zone axis [* 1¯11] and [21 1¯], respectively. These observations indicate that the ceramics can be classified as ordered spinel with a space group P*4332 in the range of *x* from 0.2 to 0.25. In addition, the simulation results (Figs. 5(d) and 5(e)) are consistent with the test results. The distribution and morphology of the diffraction spots in the SAED at *x* = 0.5 are significantly different from those of the ordered phase. A face-centered cubic disordered phase structure is obtained, which is consistent with the XRD results. Notably, the (110) interplanar distances of the ordered phase decrease with increasing substitution (*x* from 0.2 to 0.25), which is consistent with the lattice parameters obtained from the refinement.

The second phase, Zn2TiO4, produced by the third phase transition, is also an essential factor influencing the grain size and morphology of ceramics.

And then, with the introduction of Li atoms, the decrease in relative mass starts to play a dominant role, and the bulk density starts to decrease. For the Li2*x*Zn1–*x*TiO3 ceramics, a further increase in Li+ content induces a rearrangement of the octahedra. After a series of phase transitions, ilmenite ZnTiO3 transforms into a rock salt structure, Li2TiO3, which has a significant impact on the microwave dielectric properties of the ceramics. Figures 7(b)–7(d) depict the variation patterns of microwave dielectric properties of Zn1–*x*Li2*x*TiO3 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 1) ceramics as a function of content *x*. Generally, the dielectric constant is influenced not only by internal ion polarization but also by some external factors (e.g., porosity and second phases). As shown in Fig. 7(b), the denseness (relative density is only 86.8%) of the ceramic at *x* = 0 sintered at 940 °C has an important effect on *ε*r. Within the first two-phase region of Zn1–*x*Li2*x*TiO3, the *ε*r values of the ceramics are 21.9 and 20.9 for *x* = 0.1 and 0.15 sintered at 1120 °C, respectively, which are significantly higher than that (18.9) of ZnTiO3. This difference can be attributed to two factors. On one hand, increasing the relative density reduces the number of pores in the ceramic and increases the value of *ε*r. On the other hand, the presence of the second phase TiO2 with a high *ε*r (100) [50] is also a dominant factor in the increase of *ε*r of the composite ceramics. Notably, according to Eqs. (2)–(4), the increase of Li+ (*x* from 0.1 to 0.2) contributes to the continuous solid solution of TiO2 with the Li2ZnTi3O8 phase, which induces a continuous decrease in *ε*r of the ceramics. Furthermore, the trends of*ε*r in the ranges of *x* from 0.2 to 0.5 and from 0.75 to 1 are consistent with those of the polarizability of a unit cell (*α*/*V*m). Also, *ε*r (19.21) of Li1.5Zn0.25TiO3 sintered at 1200 °C is mainly attributed to the combined effect of the phase composition and the decrease in the relative density.

The effect of porosity on *ε*r is estimated through based on Eq. (3) [51,52]:

(3) εr-mea=εcorr[1−3P(εcorr−1)2εcorr+1]

where *P*, *ε*corr, and *ԑ*r–mea refer to the porosity fraction, the corrected *ε*r, and the measured *ε*r of the sintered ceramics, respectively. As shown in Fig. 7(b), the gap between *ԑ*r–mea and *ε*corr gradually decreases with an increase in Li+ content, especially when 0.2 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.75, where the two are almost close to each other. This suggests that the influence of pores on *ε*r can be neglected within this range. To further investigate the effect of ionic polarizability on *ε*r, the theoretical dielectric constants (*ε*th) of the single–phase system ceramics Zn1–*x*Li2*x*TiO3 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 1) ceramics are calculated by the Clausius–Mosotti equation (Eq. (4)) [53]:

(4) εth=3Vm+8παth3Vm−4παth

where *V*m is the molecular volume, and *α*th refers to the molecular polarizability.

According to the Shannon’s rule and crystal structure of Zn1–*x*Li2*x*TiO3 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 1) ceramics, *α*th can be obtained from Eq. (5):

(5) αth(Zn1−xLi2xTiO3)=(1−x)α(Zn2+)+2xα(Li+)+(Ti4+)+3α(O2−)

where *α*(Zn2+) = 2.04 Å3, *α*(Li+) = 1.2 Å3, *α*(Ti4+) = 2.93 Å3, *α*(O2−) = 2.01 Å3. Also, for *ε*th of multiphase ceramics, it is defined according to Lichtenecker empirical rule [54] as shown in Eq. (6):

(6) lnεth=V1lnεr1+V2lnεr2

where *V*1 and *V*2 are the volume fractions of the two phases (*V*1 + *V*2 = 1); *ε*r1 and *ε*r2 are the dielectric constants of the two phases. Li+ enters the ZnTiO3 lattice and induces structural changes that have significant effects on *Q*×*f* and *τ*f of the ceramics. Figure 7(c) demonstrates the trend of *Q*×*f* with increasing Li+ content for Zn1−*x*Li2*x*TiO3 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 1) ceramics. As *x* increases, *Q*×*f* continues to rapidly increase and reaches a maximum value of 105,013 GHz at *x* = 0.25 sintered at 1080 °C. However, *Q*×*f* then starts to rapidly decrease until *x* = 1. For high dense microwave dielectric ceramics, the phase composition and crystal structure are the crucial factors affecting the *Q*×*f* values of the ceramics. When *x* = 0.1, the ceramic undergoes the first phase transition, and *Q*×*f* rapidly increases from 23,968 to 41,677 GHz. This is primarily due to the dominant presence of the main phase Li2ZnTi3O8, which has a high *Q*×*f* value (72,000 GHz) [11] within the two-phase region. The increase of Li+ promotes the continuous solid solution of TiO2 with Li2ZnTi3O8, which reduces the dielectric loss in the ceramic. For 0.2 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.25, the increase in *Q*×*f* of ceramics with the same crystal structure (cubic order spinel with *P*4332 space group) strongly depends on the lattice vibrations inside the crystal. The packing fraction (PF) is commonly used to evaluate the intrinsic loss of ceramics, and it can be defined as the ratio of the total volume of packed ions inside the crystal to the unit cell volume [55]. For ceramics with the same crystal structure, an increase in the PF indicates a suppression of non-harmonic vibrations in the crystal, leading to a reduction in dielectric loss and optimization of the *Q*×*f* value. Obviously as the results shown in Table 2, the PF of ceramics at 0.2 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.25 remains positively correlated with *Q*×*f*. Similar results are found for ATiO3 (A = Ni, Mg, Co, and Mn) [56] and Zn0.5Ti1–*x*Zr*x*NbO4 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2) [57] material systems. When *x* increases to 0.5, a second phase transition occurs, resulting in a significant decrease in the *Q*×*f* value of the ceramic to 36,203 GHz sintered at 1160 °C. The presence of specific cations constructs the formation of Li+ diffusion channels, thereby increasing conductivity and transmission losses, ultimately leading to a decrease in the *Q*×*f* value. More specifically, Li+ ions can migrate from position 8*a* to 16*d* for the ceramic at *x* = 0.5. Additionally, the accumulation of cations in the B-position within the (Zn2/3Li1/3)tel(LiTi4/3)octO4 (*x* = 0.5) spinel structure intensifies lattice vibrations, which results in larger dielectric losses.

The *τ*f value primarily depends on factors such as phase composition and structural characteristics, including octahedral distortion, structural ordering, bond energy, and bond valence of cations [58]. Figure 7(d) illustrates the trend of *τ*f with increasing *x* for Zn1–*x*Li2*x*TiO3 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 1) ceramics. The first ceramics with a near-zero *τ*f (+1.4 ppm/°C) are obtained at *x* = 0.1. This can be largely attributed to the presence of the second phase, TiO2, which has a high *τ*f value (+400 ppm/°C) [50] and acts as an effective regulator of *τ*f of the composite ceramic. Similarly, second *τ*f near-zero ceramics (+8.2 ppm/°C, 1200 °C) are obtained at *x* = 0.75 because of the presence of the second phase Li2TiO3.

Raman spectroscopy is a valuable technique for investigating the local structure of crystals, including short-range ordering and cation occupancy. In addition, it also provides valuable insights into vibrational properties to microwave dielectric properties. Figure 9(a) represents the Raman spectrum of Zn1–*x*Li2*x*TiO3 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 1), which are fitted by the Gaussian Lorentz function. Based on the Raman polarization selection rules, the Raman vibrational modes of ZnTiO3 are 5Ag+5Eg. The vibrations detected at 175, 266, 341, 483, and 709 cm−1 can be assigned as Ag symmetrical phonon modes [63]. Within the spinel region (0.1 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.5), the Raman peak at 398 cm−1 is usually referred to as the A1g vibrational mode of the ZnO4 tetrahedra, and the vibrational mode at 440 cm−1 is the tensile vibration of the LiO4 tetrahedra [28]. The intensity of the vibrational mode at 440 cm−1 weakens with the increase of the Li+ content, and the vibrational modes at 398–440 cm−1 shift towards the low-frequency direction, which suggests that Li+ preferentially occupies the octahedral position, and the tetrahedral position is continuously occupied by the heavier ion Zn2+. The appearance of an additional peak at 606 cm−1 for *x* = 0.1 and 0.15 indicates the formation of the rutile phase TiO2, which is consistent with XRD data. The Raman peak observed at 709 cm−1 can be assigned to the stretching vibration of the TiO6 octahedron at *x* = 0. After the phase transition at *x* ≥ 0.1, the Raman peak at 756 cm−1 can be assigned to the symmetric stretching A1g vibrational mode of ceramic TiO6. The additional peak at 821 cm−1 near the A1g mode is related to the tilting of the TiO6 octahedron. The co-edge connection of TiO6 with LiO6 and the sharp shortening of certain bond lengths lead to the tilting of the TiO6 octahedron to higher frequencies [30]. The intensity of the Raman peak at 821 cm−1 consistently weakens as the Li+ content increases in the ceramics. This weakening can be attributed to the diminishing ordering of the internal structure of the ceramics and the elongation of the Ti–O bond length. This observation aligns with the bond valence data for the Ti–O bond presented in Table 3. For *x* =1, the peaks at 286, 336, and 394 cm−1 should be attributed to Li–O stretching vibrations in LiO6 octahedra, and the peaks at 563 and 653 cm–1 can be attributed to Ti–O stretching vibrations in TiO6 octahedra [64].

As *x* increases, within the same range of the crystal structure variation, the A1g Raman shifts exhibit an opposite trend to that of *ε*r. This phenomenon can be attributed to the lower vibrational frequencies associated with lower vibrational energies, resulting in a decrease in chemical bond energy and enhanced polarization susceptibility [65]. In fact, a low FWHM with a low damping behavior indicates low energy loss between internal phonons, resulting in a high *Q*×*f* value [66].

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, Zn1–*x*Li2*x*TiO3 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 1) microwave dielectric ceramics were fabricated through solid-state synthesis. Varying Li+ content induces complex phase transitions within the components, significantly affecting the microwave dielectric properties of Zn1–*x*Li2*x*TiO3 ceramics. Within the range of 0.1 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.25, the ceramics undergo a transition from the rhombohedral ilmenite phase (space group *R** 3¯) to the cubic ordered spinel phase (space group P*4332). For 0.25 3¯m*) occurs, while for *x* ≥ 0.75, a monoclinic phase (space group *C*2/*c*) emerges. These structural phase transitions result in the observation of two near-zero *τ*f values at *x* = 0.1 and 0.75, respectively. Moreover, the increased Li+ content significantly enhances the *Q*×*f* value of the ceramics, elevating it from 23,968 to 105,013 GHz. *ε*r is mainly influenced by the phase composition within the two-phase region. However, for single-phase ceramics, ionic polarizability and cell volume combine to affect *ε*r. Specifically, the Ti–O bonds in the octahedron have an important effect on the *ε*r, as evidenced by detailed analysis based on the P–V–L theory. Additionally, our investigation revealed that the ionic polarizability of Ti4+ was underestimated by 11.3%–13.3% in comparison to Shannon’s reported value of 2.93 Å3. The recalculated *α*(Ti4+) stabilizes around 3.29 Å3. The increase in *U* plays a pivotal role in enhancing *Q*×*f*. The progressive increasing *E* of the octahedron and the valence of the Ti–O bond reduce the recovery of octahedral distortions and therefore effectively modulate *τ*f.
