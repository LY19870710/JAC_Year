## 1 Introduction

Carbon/carbon (C/C) composites have emerged as attractive candidates for lightweight and thin-walled thermal protection structures in aerospace fields owing to their low density (−3), low coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE: ~1.0×10−6 K−1), and high-temperature resistance and specific strength [1−3]. However, C/C composites are subject to serious ablation during application, including severe scouring and ultrahigh-temperature oxidation, compromising their structural integrity [4,5].

To address these challenges, preparing coating on C/C composites has proven to be an effective approach for isolating them from harsh atmospheres [6]. Among the ultra-high-temperature ceramics (UHTCs), HfC is an excellent candidate for thermal protection coatings due to its ultrahigh melting point (~3963 ℃) and good thermal stability. Additionally, its oxide also has a high melting point (HfO2: ~2890 ℃) and low vapor pressure [7]. However, its susceptibility to cracking or peeling during abrupt temperature changes and mechanical erosion decreases its ablation performance [8,9].

In response to these issues, a one-dimensional (1D) nanostructure toughening strategy has been developed. These 1D nanomaterials with large specific surface areas and high aspect ratios are more suitable for toughening phases at the nanometer scale [10]. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) have been used as reinforcements for ceramic coatings due to their unique mechanical, thermal, physical, and chemical properties [11], and CNT-toughened buffer layers were constructed between the SiC coatings and the C/C–ZrC–SiC substrates, improving the interface adhesion strength and thermal shock resistance [12,13]. In addition, SiC nanowires [14,15] were introduced into SiC-based coatings, which reduced cracking and peeling issues and significantly increased the fracture toughness. Li *et al.* [16] prepared SiC nanowire-toughened HfC coatings on the C/C composites, resulting in reduced microcracks and minimized peeling. However, when the ablation temperature exceeds 2000 ℃, the SiC nanowires undergo phase transformation and crystal growth and even break into short rods or particles, compromising the unique advantages of 1D nanomaterials. Moreover, the oxidation products (SiO and SiO2) of SiC inevitably evaporate, which results in severe consumption of nanowires and diminished toughening effects [17].

Then, 1D UHTC nanomaterials were developed, such as ZrC nanowires and HfC nanowires, which possess both excellent properties of bulk UHTC materials and superior mechanical properties of nanowires [18,19]. The ZrC nanowires-reinforced ZrC coating was prepared and displayed good ablation resistance with mass and linear ablation rates of 0.59±0.06 mg·s−1 and −0.92±0.09 μm·s−1, respectively [20]. Ren *et al.* [21] synthesized a HfC nanowire-reinforced HfC coating, leading to reduced microcrack sizes. In addition, ZrC nanowires and HfC nanowires were also introduced into the C/C composites to reinforce their mechanical properties [22,23], confirming their enhancement as a toughening phase.

Hf*x*Ta1−*x*C, as a solid solution of HfC and TaC, exhibits a high melting point (~4000 ℃) and outstanding thermal properties, and the Hf and Ta atoms in Hf*x*Ta1−*x*C solid solutions can be replaced with each other throughout the composition above 927 ℃ [24]. Additionally, the Hf0.5Ta0.5C solid solution possesses higher microhardness, elastic modulus, and thermal conductivity compared to HfC [25], as well as superior high-temperature stability above 1900 ℃ and ablation resistance above 2100 ℃ [26]; thus, incorporating Hf0.5Ta0.5C solid solution nanowires into HfC coatings is expected to improve their ablative performance.

In this work, novel Hf0.5Ta0.5C solid solution nanowires were developed and grown *in situ* on the C/C composites by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) with the aim of simultaneously enhancing mechanical and ablation properties of HfC coatings. The phase composition, morphology, and microstructure of the synthesized Hf0.5Ta0.5C solid solution nanowires were reported. The effects of Hf0.5Ta0.5C solid solution nanowires on the mechanical properties, thermophysical properties, and ablation resistance of HfC coatings were investigated. In addition, the mechanisms of the preparation and improved mechanical properties of the toughened HfC coating were discussed in detail.

### 2.1 Preparation of samples

The substrates were C/C composites with a density of ~1.75 g·cm−3, which were prepared by thermal gradient chemical vapor infiltration with 2.5D needle-punched carbon felts. Before deposition, the C/C composites were machined to *ϕ*30 × 10 mm3, ultrasonically washed with deionized water and then dried in an oven (101A-2, Beijing Kewei Yongxing Instrument Co., Ltd., China) at 80±5 ℃. The Hf*x*Ta1−*x*C solid solution nanowires were prepared with a HfCl4–TaCl5–CH4–H2–Ar system. HfCl4 and TaCl5 powders (99.9% purity; Hua Jing Powdery Material Science & Technological Co., Ltd., China) were used as Hf and Ta sources, and CH4, H2, and Ar (99.99% purity; Wei Guang Technology Co., Ltd., China) were used as carbon source, reducing gas, and diluting gas, respectively. A Hf*x*Ta1−*x*C solid solution nanowire-toughened HfC coating was prepared by a two-step CVD method. A schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 1(a). First, the as-prepared C/C composites were immersed in Ni(NO3)2·6H2O/ethanol solutions with a molarity of 0.02–1.0 mol·L−1 for 12 h and dried naturally in the shade. Then, the C/C composites covered with catalyst were hung in the deposition chamber of the CVD furnace, and the HfCl4 and TaCl5 powders with a molar ratio of 1 : 1 were loaded in the evaporation crucibles in the sublimation chamber. As indicated by arrow “ ① ”, the furnace was heated in a mixed H2 (300–400 mL·min−1) and Ar (200–300 mL·min−1) atmosphere, and CH4 (60–70 mL·min−1) was introduced for deposition when reaching the designed temperature (1050–1150 ℃) and kept for 1–2 h. Second, the HfC coating was deposited on the Hf0.5Ta0.5C solid solution nanowires with the HfCl4–CH4–H2–Ar system, as indicated by arrow “ ② ”. The deposition temperature ranged from 1250 to 1350 ℃, and the flow velocities of H2, Ar, and CH4 ranged within 400–500, 100–200, and 70–80 mL·min−1, respectively; the deposition time ranged from 8 to 10 h. The HfC coating without nanowires was prepared as a contrast specimen. The densities and porosities of the HfC coatings with and without Hf0.5Ta0.5C nanowires are displayed in Table 1. After the incorporation of Hf0.5Ta0.5C nanowires, the density of the HfC coating increased from 2.17 to 2.85 g·cm−3, and the porosity decreased from 7.96% to 5.99%.

### 2.2 Performance tests of samples

The hardness (*H*) and elastic modulus (*E*) of the coatings were tested via the indentation method. Before the test, the cross section of the coating was polished close to the mirror surface. The pressurload was constant (10 mN), and the pressing rate was 2 mN·s−1. The load‒displacement curves were recorded during the test, and the *H* and *E* values were calculated according to the Olive–Pharr method [27]. Each sample was tested for 10 points with each point spacing greater than 20 μm, and the final *H* and *E* were the mean values.

The fracture toughness (*K*IC) of the coating was measured via a microhardness tester (FV 800, Shanghai Shangcai Testing Machine Co., Ltd., China). A constant load of 0.2 kg was applied to the polished coatings to generate cracks around the indentation. Five points were chosen to apply the load, and the position spacing of each indentation was greater than 50 μm. Then, *K*IC was calculated with Eq. (1) [28], where *δ* is a geometric factor; *C* = (*C*1 + *C*2)/2 is the average length of two radial cracks from the indentation center to the crack edge; *P* is the applied load.

(1) KIC=δ×(E/H)1/2×(P/C3/2)

The interfacial bonding strength between the HfC coating and the C/C composites was tested by an electronic universal testing machine (CMT5304-30 KN, Shenzhen Sansi Testing Technology Co., Ltd., China). A specimen with a size of *ϕ*12 × 8 mm3 was glued to the mold, dried in an oven at 90±5 ℃ at 12 h, and then fixed on the electronic universal machine for tensile testing. The tensile loading speed was 0.5 mm·min−1. The interfacial bonding strength (*σ*) was calculated according to Eq. (2), where *F* is the maximum tension of coating shedding, and *A* is the adhesion area of the coated specimen [29].

(2) σ=F/A

CTE of the HfC-coated C/C composites was tested via a dilatometer (DIL 402E, NETZSCH, Germany), and the size of each specimen was *ϕ*6 × 15 mm3. A thermal conductivity meter (LFA 427, NETZSCH, Germany) with a laser flash method was used to test the specific heat capacity (*c**p*) and thermal diffusivity (*α*) of specimens with a size of *ϕ*12.6 × 2 mm3, and the tests were performed at a heating rate of 5 ℃·min−1 in an Ar atmosphere. ### 2.3 Characterizations

The phase compositions of the nanowires and coatings were examined by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X’Pert Pro MPD, PANalytical Almelo, the Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation. The morphologies were observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; TESCAN MIRA, TESCAN, Czech Republic) with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) and with an electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA; SHIMADZU, Japan) equipped with a wave-dispersive spectrometer (WDS). The microstructures were characterized by a transmission electron microscope (TEM; FEI TECNAI G20, FEI Company, USA) with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDX). The roughness of the coatings was measured by a confocal microscope (C130, OPTELICS, Japan).

### 3.2 Morphology and deposition mechanism of toughened HfC coating

Figure 3 shows the SEM images and phase composition of the HfC coatings. As shown in Fig. 3(a1), the HfC coating without nanowires exhibits a rough surface with many bumps and pits, and obvious cracks can be found at the intersection of the bumps. According to the XRD pattern inset in Fig. 3(a1), five sharp and symmetrical peaks imply the good crystallization of the HfC coating prepared by CVD. Figure 3(a2) shows the corresponding cross-sectional SEM image. It can be observed that the coating completely covers the C/C composites with a thickness of ~100 μm, but obvious transverse cracks remain in the coating, as shown in the high-magnification SEM image (Fig. 3(a3)). Figures 3(b1)–3(d3) illustrate the morphology evolution of the HfC coating toughened by Hf0.5Ta0.5C solid solution nanowires. At the initial stage (Figs. 3(b1) and 3(b2)), the nanowires transform into core–shell structured rod-like products with a diameter of ~6 μm. This indicates that the nanowires provide nucleation sites for the coating during deposition, and the HfC deposits on the nanowire surface and grows along the radial direction. Figure 3(b3) shows a cross-sectional SEM image of coatings, it can be found that the HfC deposits around the nanowire surface; moreover, HfC is deposited at the bottom of the nanowire layer and forms a HfC coating with a thickness of ~30 μm. With increasing deposition time, as shown in Figs. 3(c1)–3(c3), the diameter of the core–shell structured products increases, and the interspaces are filled continuously; the thickness of the coating increases to ~60 μm (Fig. 3(c4)). Continuously extending the deposition time (Figs. 3(d1)–3(d3)), a compact HfC coating toughened by Hf0.5Ta0.5C solid solution nanowires is obtained, and the thickness of the coating is more uniform with a value of ~120 μm; there are no obvious cracks within the coating, and the sharp and narrow symmetrical XRD peaks confirm the good crystallinity of the toughened HfC coating.

### 3.3 Mechanical properties of toughened HfC coating

During the ablation process, the coatings need to sustain severe mechanical denudation from the oxyacetylene flame, making the interfacial bonding strength between the coating and the substrate crucial. A weak interfacial bonding strength can lead to coating failure by cracking or peeling off during ablation. The tensile test results show that introducing Hf0.5Ta0.5C nanowires to the HfC coating improves the interfacial bonding strength between the coating and the C/C composites from 12.6 to 19.3 MPa, which increases by 53.17%, owing to that the toughened HfC coating has higher density and smaller porosity, and the pull-out or peel off of the nanowires consumes several types of energy, clearly enhancing the interfacial bonding strength between the coating and the substrate.

Figure 5(a) shows the load‒displacement curves of the HfC coatings during the nanoindentation test. Under the same pressure load, a smaller maximal displacement implies a greater hardness, and a larger unloading slope indicates a greater elastic modulus. During the test, the HfC coating toughened with Hf0.5Ta0.5C solid solution nanowires exhibited smaller displacement and larger unloading slope, indicating higher *H* and *E*. The calculated *H*, *E*, and *K*IC values are shown in Fig. 5(b). *H* and *E* of the HfC coating without nanowires are 15.96±0.82 and 140.28±5.99 GPa, respectively. After incorporating the nanowires, *H* and *E* increase to 21.47±0.97 and 215.04±9.29 GPa, respectively, which is attributed to the unique 1D geometric structure and outstanding mechanical properties of the nanowires. Moreover, *K*IC of the HfC coating increases from 3.62±0.25 to 5.78±0.37 MPa·m1/2 under the toughness of the Hf0.5Ta0.5C solid solution nanowires. The higher fracture toughness can inhibit the cracking and stripping of the coating during the ablation process, thereby improving the ablation resistance. A representative SEM image of the residual impression morphology is shown in Fig. 5(c), in which there are several radial cracks along the indent diagonal direction. The toughening mechanism is displayed in Fig. 5(d). The evenly distributed and interconnected nanowires provide more interfaces for the coating. When the load is applied to the surface of the coating and causes cracks, the load is transmitted to the nanowires, and the nanowires are pulled out, destroyed, and even peeled off, which consumes large amounts of fracture energy and prevents the cracks from propagating, thereby improving the fracture toughness of the coating. In addition, the nanowire bridges further pin or deflect cracks and inhibit crack growth.

## 4 Conclusions

The novel Hf0.5Ta0.5C solid solution nanowires with a single-crystal structure were synthesized on C/C composites via a chemical vapor deposition method to enhance mechanical performance and ablation resistance of the HfC coating. On one hand, the uniformly distributed nanowires induced the simultaneous deposition of the HfC coating in both the horizontal and vertical directions, facilitating the formation of a compact coating. On the other hand, the isotropic toughening of randomly oriented nanowires reduced the number of microcracks in the coating. The fracture toughness of the Hf0.5Ta0.5C solid solution nanowire-toughened HfC coating increased from 3.62±0.25 to 5.78±0.37 MPa·m1/2 due to the evenly distributed and interconnected nanowires that pinned, deflected or prevented the propagation of cracks. The mass and linear ablation rates of the toughened HfC coating were −0.35 and −0.46 μm·s−1, respectively, which were lower than those of other ceramic-coated C/C composites reported in recent years, confirming the superior ablation resistance of Hf0.5Ta0.5C solid solution nanowire-toughened C/C composites.
