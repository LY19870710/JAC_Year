## 1 Introduction

The rapid development of wireless communication technology and electronic equipment has enriched people’s material life but has also inevitably led to severe electromagnetic (EM) pollution [1−3]. This interferes with the normal operation of electronic equipment on the one hand and threatens human health on the other hand. Therefore, the development of new EM functional materials that can effectively attenuate EM waves and reduce the harm of EM waves to a minimum is urgently needed.

Researchers have reported on various EM functional materials through strategies such as process control, structural modification, and mechanism innovation, promoting explosive development in this field. However, the complexity of the application environment places greater demands on EM functional materials in terms of multifunctionality. EM functional materials with hydrophobic, high-temperature stability, corrosion resistance, thermal insulation, photothermal, flame retardant, antibacterial, shape memory, and other functions have been reported [4−6]. The development of EM functional materials with multiple functions is an inevitable trend. Recently, Wu *et al.* [7] prepared carbon nanotube films via the floating catalyst chemical vapor deposition (CVD) method and subsequent defect engineering strategies, which resulted in excellent electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding, switchable infrared camouflage, and energy conversion.

Owing to their large specific surface area, unique electronic properties, and excellent optical performance, transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) with two-dimensional (2D) layered structures have attracted attention in multiple fields [8,9]. MoS2 is a typical member of the TMD family and has been widely reported in the field of EM functional materials. Compared with MoS2, although MoSe2 is also a hexagonal layered structure, its electronic properties are different. MoSe2 has a smaller band gap, which is beneficial for improving conductivity [10]. In addition, it also has a larger interlayer spacing and a more stable 1T phase, making it easier to regulate its EM response through intercalation, defect engineering, multiphase hybridization, and other means [11]. The 1T phase and the 2H phase are the two most studied phases of MoSe2. Compared with 1T with metallic properties, the 2H phase has greater thermodynamic stability and semiconductor properties, which are conducive to the regulation of the band gap through doping, modification or pattering, and tailoring of the EM response [12]. Wang’s group [13] was the first to report MoSe2-absorbing materials; MoSe2/paraffin with 80 wt% loading has a minimum reflection loss (RL) of −60.23 dB and an effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) of 5.68 GHz. However, as a semiconductor material, its poor conductivity limits its application in EM functional materials. To improve the EM properties of MoSe2, a common method is to combine it with carbon materials. On the one hand, it can increase the overall conduction loss of the material, and on the other hand, it introduces more interfaces, increasing the mechanism of EM attenuation. For example, Su *et al.* [14] reported Co/C@MoSe2 composites with hollow core‒shell structures obtained via hydrothermal reactions and pyrolysis processes. The introduction of MoSe2 and the designed hollow core–shell structure were beneficial for optimizing the EM characteristics of the composites. The sample shows excellent microwave absorption (MA) performance, with a minimum RL of −42.0 dB (2.5 mm) and a minimum EAB of 5.1 GHz. Xu *et al*. [15] prepared a bacterial cellulose (BC)-derived carbon nanofiber/MoSe2 nanocomposite, in which MoSe2 microspheres were dropped on a BC network similar to a dew-covered spider web. The optimal RL can reach −53.3 dB with only 20 wt% filler. However, research on the use of MoSe2 and its hybrid materials in multifunctional applications still needs to be conducted.

In this work, molybdenum diselenide@reduced graphene oxides (MoSe2@rGOs) were successfully prepared through a hydrothermal reaction in which MoSe2 nanosheets grow vertically on rGO sheets. By tailoring conduction genes and polarization genes, the transformation and regulation of EM functions can be effectively achieved. The MoSe2@rGO hybrid with a mass ratio of 60% has an optimal RL value of −68.7 dB at a thickness of 2.32 mm and a broad EAB of 5.04 GHz. Moreover, the average shielding efficiency can reach 98.7% at a 90% mass fraction. A radar cross section (RCS) simulation and an EM energy conversion device demonstrate the practical application potential of the material. This work effectively proves the advantages of structural assembly and gene regulation strategies in realizing EM materials with multiple functions.

### 2.1 Synthesis of MoSe<sub>2</sub>@rGO hybrids

The preparation of MoSe2@rGO hybrids is a hydrothermal process. First, 0.04 g of GO (Nanjing XFNANO Materials Tech Co., Ltd., China) was ultrasonically treated for 1.5 h in a mixture of absolute ethanol (Shanghai Wokai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) and deionized water (homemade in the laboratory) with a volume ratio of 1:1. Then, 0.605 g of Na2MoO4·2H2O (Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China), 0.395 g of Se powder (Shanghai Aladdin Industrial Co., Ltd., China), and 0.095 g of NaBH4 (Tianjin Beilian Fine Chemical Development Co., Ltd., China) were added to the GO solution and stirred for 0.5 h. The mixed solution was transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave (50 mL, Shanghai Baikal Technology Group Co., Ltd., China) and heated at 220 °C for 24 h. After the autoclave was naturally cooled to room temperature, the obtained precipitates were collected by centrifugation, washed several times alternately with water and ethanol, and then dried at 60 °C for 10 h. In addition, MoSe2 was prepared via the same method as above for comparison.

### 2.2 Characterization

The morphology of the samples was detected by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Regulus8230, Hitachi, Japan), a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai F20, FEI, USA), and a high-resolution TEM (Tecnai F20, FEI, USA). The composition, phases, and structure of the samples were measured via Fourier transform infrared spectroscope (FT-IR; NICOLET iS50, Thermofisher Scientific, USA), an X-ray radiation diffractometer (XRD; D/max-2500/PC, Rigaku, Japan), a Raman spectroscope (DXR2xi, Thermofisher Scientific, USA; 488 nm laser), and an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; EACALAB Xi+, Thermo Scientific, USA; mono Al Ka radiation, *hʋ* = 1486.6 eV).

The EM parameters and scattering parameters (*S*11 and *S*21) were measured by a vector network analyzer (VNA; Anritsu 37269D, Anritsu, Japan). The samples and paraffin were uniformly mixed at different mass ratios (5:5, 6:4, 7:3, 8:2, and 9:1) and pressed into a toroidal shape (*ϕ*out = 7.00 mm; *ϕ*in = 3.04 mm).

### 3.1 Morphology and structural characterization

The formation and growth process of MoSe2 on a graphene sheet is shown in Figs. 1(a) and 1(b). Multiple functional groups (carboxyl, ketone, epoxy, and hydroxyl) are present on the surface of GO, some of which are retained during the hydrothermal reduction of GO to rGO. They can react as nucleation sites for MoSe2. The FT-IR spectrum confirms the existence of multiple functional groups in the MoSe2@rGO hybrids (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) [16]. During the reaction process, Se and Mo are reduced to HSe− and Mo4+ under the action of NaBH4, after which HSe− can react with OH− to form Se2− [17]. Mo4+ was adsorbed to the surface of the rGO due to electrostatic forces, and nanograins with Se− were formed. During the sustained hydrothermal process, the nanograin grew continuously and formed a larger MoSe2 nanosheet structure anchored on the rGO. The specific reaction equations are shown in Eqs. S(23)–S(26) in the ESM. The MoSe2 nanosheets were almost uniformly coated on the rGO sheet. Interestingly, the MoSe2 nanosheets present a 3D structure rather than being completely tiled on rGO.

The obtained pure MoSe2 presents a flower-like microsphere structure (Figs. S2(a)–S2(c) in the ESM). The formation process of MoSe2 microspheres is considered to involve three stages: the initial nucleation process, the directional growth of nanograins into nanosheets, and the self-assembly of nanosheets into 3D structures [24]. The microspheres are formed by the assembly of many nanosheets due to the long reaction time (Figs. S2(c) and S2(k) in the ESM). The morphologies of the MoSe2@rGO hybrids are shown in Figs. S2(d)–S2(f) and S2(l) in the ESM. Owing to the abundant functional groups on the surface of rGO, it is evenly covered with nanosheets, which form many folded structures (Figs. S2(d) and S2(e) in the ESM). Figure S2(f) in the ESM shows that the nanosheets are grown vertically. One side of each nanosheet is in contact with the surface of rGO, and the other side is perpendicular to the surface (the red dotted line in Fig. S2(f) in the ESM), resulting in a folded structure. This structure increases the specific surface area of the material, promotes contact between graphene sheets, and facilitates the migration of electrons. As a result, the construction of a conductive network can be promoted. The EDS mappings and spectra of the MoSe2@rGO hybrids further demonstrate the presence of C, Mo, and Se (Figs. S2(g)–S2(j) in the ESM).

The TEM image of the MoSe2 microspheres revealed that they were not well dispersed and that the microspheres were in contact with each other (Fig. 2(a)). The microspheres are flower clusters composed of nanosheets (Figs. 2(b) and 2(c)). The introduction of GO promotes the uniform distribution of MoSe2, as the large number of active sites on its surface can anchor MoSe2 nanosheets (Fig. 2(d)). The hybridization of the two components results in a folded structure (Fig. 2(e)). Several nanosheets gathered together and stood upright on the rGO sheet, affording a 3D structure to the 2D rGO. In the enlarged image, in addition to the flat MoSe2 nanosheets, vertical MoSe2 nanosheets are present, which promote binding with rGO (Figs. 2(f) and 2(g)). The lattice spacing of 0.684 nm accords with the (002) plane of MoSe2 (Fig. 2(h)). In addition, the diffraction rings corresponding to the (100) and (110) planes of MoSe2 in the selected area also confirmed the successful synthesis of the sample (Fig. 2(i)) [25].

### 3.2 EM response of MoSe<sub>2</sub>@rGO hybrids

The real (* ε′) and imaginary ( ε″) permittivity values of the MoSe2@rGO hybrids with different mass fractions are shown in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b). The permittivity curves also increase with increasing mass fraction of the hybrids because the conductive network formed by the hybrids is more perfect. The permittivity can be adjusted simply by changing the mass fraction. For samples with mass fractions of 70%–90%, the  ε″ values decrease significantly in a lower frequency range, indicating that conductive loss plays an important role in this frequency band. The highest  ε″ value of the 90% sample exceeds 135, which means that it may have good EMI shielding performance. To analyze the effects of charge transport ( εc″) and polarization relaxation ( εp″) on dielectric loss more clearly, we calculated their values. On the basis of Debye theory,  ε″,  εc″, and  εp″ have the following relationships (Eqs. (1)–(3)) [31,32]:

where f*, *τ*, and *σ* are the frequency, relaxation time, and direct current (DC) conductivity, respectively; *ε*s, *ε*∞, and *ε*0 are the static relative, “infinite” high, and vacuum permittivity, respectively.

On the basis of Eq. (2) and the fitted conductivity in Fig. S3 in the ESM, the * εc″ values of all the samples in the range of 2–18 GHz are obtained (Fig. 4(c)). The  εc″ values increase with increasing mass fraction, indicating that greater conduction loss occurs. The electron migrating on the rGO sheet and the electron hopping between adjacent rGO sheets will form a microcurrent, resulting in conduction loss [33]. In addition, the  εc″ curves decrease when the frequency increases, indicating that the contribution of conduction loss decreases at high frequencies.

In addition to conduction loss, polarization relaxation also plays an important role in the attenuation of EM waves. The defects in the MoSe2 nanosheets and rGO, the functional groups in rGO, and the interfaces between rGO and MoSe2 can react as polarization genes to generate dielectric loss [34,35]. Several peaks of the  εp″ curves confirm the existence of these polarization relaxation mechanisms (Fig. 4(d)). The dielectric loss angle (tanδ*E) curves of the MoSe2@rGO hybrids are shown in Fig. 4(e). The state of the curves is almost the same as that of * ε″. Additionally, the tanδ*E values increase with increasing mass fraction of the sample, indicating that more dielectric loss is generated.

Figure 4(f) shows that polarization relaxation contributes more than charge transport when the mass fraction is lower than 90%. However, charge transport dominates the dielectric loss when the mass fraction is 90%, which means that a better conductive network improves the conductivity of the samples. Figure 4(g) shows the proportions of  εc″ and  εp″ in detail. The proportion of  εc″ is only 5.1% for 50% MoSe2@rGO hybrids, but it increases to 59.5% for 90% MoSe2@rGO hybrids. The cole–cole plots of the samples are depicted in Figs. 4(h) and 4(i) and Fig. S4 in the ESM. These cole‒cole semicircles indicate that multiple relaxation mechanisms exist in the hybrids [36].

The 3D RL plots of all the samples are depicted in Figs. 5(a)–5(e). As the mass fraction increases, the MA performance first improves and then decreases. The minimum RL values are −48.7, −68.7, −15.1, −10.4, and −3.5 dB for the 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, and 90% MoSe2@rGO hybrids, respectively. Figure 5(f) shows the RL values vs. different mass fractions and thicknesses. RL values below −10 dB cover a large area at a lower mass fraction. Furthermore, as shown by the dotted arrow in Fig. 5(f), the MA performance becomes better at a lower mass fraction and thinner. The 2D RL plots of the samples are shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM. The 60% MoSe2@rGO hybrids possess the best MA performance, with a minimum RL value of −68.7 dB at a 2.32 mm thickness, and the EAB can reach 5.04 GHz. Moreover, the RL values of the 50% and 60% samples are ≤ −10 dB within a large thickness range. The absorption peaks shift to lower frequencies with increasing thickness, which conforms to the quarter-wavelength model. These results show that the samples can effectively absorb EM waves over a wide range of thicknesses and that the absorption band can be moved by changing the thickness. However, the samples with 70%–90% mass fractions have poor performance due to their higher conductivity. High conductivity leads to high permittivity and poor impedance matching between air and the material. Although an increase in conductivity enhances the current caused by charge carriers, thereby enhancing the ability for conduction loss, when the conductivity is too high, the high-frequency oscillation skin current on the material surface radiates EM waves outward, causing many EM waves to be reflected and unable to enter the material for absorption.

Many EMI shielding materials cause secondary reflection of EM waves, resulting in environmental EM pollution. Therefore, it is necessary to reduce the reflection of EM waves as much as possible and increase their absorption. The green shielding (*g*s) index can measure the ability of EMI shielding materials to reduce secondary EM pollution [34]. The color map of the *g*s values is depicted in Fig. 6(g). In high-frequency areas, the *g*s values are greater than 1.0, indicating that absorption is the main EMI shielding mechanism and that green EMI shielding is realized. The MoSe2@rGO hybrids have the potential to become a green shielding material. The change in mass fraction leads to a change in conductivity, so the EMI shielding performance is strongly affected by the variation in conductivity. Figure 6(h) shows the effect of conductivity on the SER and *R*. According to the fitting curves, increasing conductivity can increase the SER and *R* values. The MA and EMI shielding performance can be driven by conductivity. As shown in Fig. 6(i), in area I, the increased conductivity improves the MA performance. In area II, the MA performance decreases as the EMI shielding performance increases, which is the result of the competition between *R* and * εc″ promoted by conductivity. In area III, high conductivity induces reflection dominance, and EMI SE is further enhanced. In addition, 80% of the MoSe2@rGO hybrids can simultaneously realize EM wave absorption and shielding.

On the basis of the excellent EM attenuation ability of MoSe2@rGO hybrids, EM energy conversion devices have been designed. As shown in Fig. 8(a), when EM waves are incident on the surface of MoSe2@rGO hybrids, a small portion of the EM waves are reflected, whereas the majority of the EM waves enter the interior of the material and are absorbed, thereby converting into thermal energy. Owing to the Seebeck effect, the temperature difference between the upper and lower ends of the P‒N junction generates current, thereby converting thermal energy into electrical energy [33]. The equivalent circuit is shown in Fig. 8(b), which consists of resistance (*R*e), capacitance (*C*), magnetoresistance (*R*m), and inductance (*L*). The attenuation constant (*α*) can be used to evaluate the attenuation ability of materials to EM waves [62]. As shown in Fig. 8(c), the *α* curves indicate that MoSe2@rGO hybrids with higher mass fractions have higher *α* values, indicating greater loss of EM waves.

The EM energy absorbed into the material is converted and stored separately, and their ratio (*W*r) is shown in Fig. 8(d) [31]. *W*r values increase with increasing mass fraction, indicating that more EM energy can be converted. Figures 8(e) and 8(f) show that the storage efficiency (*W*s) and conversion efficiency (*W*d) both show opposite trends with changes in mass fraction, indicating that high concentrations of samples increase the attenuation ability. *W*d is composed of contributions from conduction loss (*W*c) and polarization relaxation (*W*p) (Figs. 8(g) and 8(h)). *W*c dominated at low filling concentrations, whereas *W*p dominated at high filling concentrations, suggesting competition between conduction and polarization genes.

The EM response mechanism of the material is shown in Fig. 9. Figure 9(a) shows a schematic diagram of the absorbing materials. The sample is coated on a metal back plate; most of the incident EM waves are absorbed inside the nanostructures, and very few EM waves are reflected by the sample surface. Figure 9(b) shows a schematic diagram of the shielding materials. When the EM wave reaches the sample surface, some EM waves are reflected because of the mismatched impedance and some of them are absorbed and transformed into heat energy; only a small number of EM waves are transmitted. The mechanisms of attenuating EM waves are shown in Figs. 9(c)–9(e). Figure 9(c) shows the dielectric loss caused by polarization relaxation. The defects and functional groups in graphene and MoSe2, as well as lattice distortion, can induce the directional arrangement of dipoles caused by defects under alternating EM fields (Figs. 9(f)–9(i)). As the frequency of the EM field subsequently increases, the turning speed of the dipole cannot match the speed of the EM field changes, resulting in relaxation and the conversion of EM energy into thermal energy [63−65]. The interface between rGO and MoSe2 forms a capacitor-like structure in which the charges are asymmetrically distributed [33,66]. Conduction loss can be produced by the migration of electrons on the surface of the MoSe2@rGO hybrids and the hopping of electrons between adjacent MoSe2@rGO hybrids (Fig. 9(d)) [67,68]. The samples with higher mass fractions will build denser conductive networks and create more conduction loss. Electrons can move directionally in graphene and be scattered by lattice or free electrons (Fig. 9(j)). In addition, electrons in multilayer graphene can move through multiple directional transport channels (Fig. 9(k)) [69]. In addition, multiple scattering events can also consume EM waves, which occur between MoSe2@rGO sheets distributed in all directions and vertical MoSe2 nanosheets (Fig. 9(e)) [70].

Among them, polarization genes and conduction genes play crucial roles in determining EM properties. “Genetic engineering” implemented through microstructural tailoring involves electron migration and dipole motion, which alter the EM response of the material. The synergy and competition between dielectric genes are achieved through changes in the filling concentration. At low filling concentrations, polarized genes dominate, and the EM attenuation ability of the MoSe2@rGO hybrids is weak, but the impedance matching is good. As the filling concentration gradually increases, the attenuation ability of the material increases, and impedance matching also changes on the basis of changes in EM parameters, achieving excellent MA performance by balancing the two. At high filler concentrations, the conductive network gradually improves, and conduction genes become dominant. Although the material has a strong attenuation ability, impedance matching deteriorates, and most EM waves are reflected, resulting in a decrease in MA performance and an increase in EMI shielding performance [71]. Therefore, the transition from excellent MA performance to green EMI shielding performance has been achieved through the competition and synergy between conduction genes and relaxation genes, as well as the balance between attenuation ability and impedance matching characteristics.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, MoSe2@rGO hybrids were successfully prepared through a hydrothermal reaction in which the MoSe2 nanosheets grew vertically on the rGO sheets. By tailoring conduction genes and polarization genes, the transformation and regulation of EM functions can be effectively achieved. The MoSe2@rGO hybrids with a mass ratio of 60% have an optimal RL value of −68.7 dB at a thickness of 2.32 mm and a broad EAB of 5.04 GHz. Moreover, the average shielding efficiency can reach 98.7% at a 90% mass fraction. An energy conversion device is designed to collect discarded electromagnetic energy. This work effectively proves the advantages of structural assembly and gene regulation strategies in realizing multifunctional EM materials.
