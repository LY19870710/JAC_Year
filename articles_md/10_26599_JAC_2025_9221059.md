## 1 Introduction

As we wholeheartedly embrace the 5G era, electronic devices such as smartphones and laptops have seamlessly integrated into our daily lives, providing unprecedented convenience and connectivity [1−3]. However, the widespread adoption of these technological innovations comes with the challenge of managing electromagnetic radiation. To mitigate this issue, microwave absorbing materials, particularly novel multi-component variants, have garnered significant attention [4,5].

ABO3 perovskite oxides have attracted extensive attention as dielectric absorbers not only because of their good absorption properties but also because of their unique crystal structure, which provides rich entropic tunability [6]. Consequently, the entropy-driven enhancement of microwave absorption in hexagonal perovskites represents a novel approach for developing single-phase materials with exceptional wave-absorbing performance. In these structures, the nearest surrounding O ions form an octahedral structure (BO6) with the B-site, which is typically a transition metal cation with a short ionic radius [5,7,8]. The A-site, on the other hand, accommodates cations with a large ionic radius positioned within the gaps of the octahedral framework [9−11]. The distortion of the octahedron and alterations in the connectivity mode influence the degree of overlap between the O-2p orbitals and the d orbitals of the transition metals located at the B-sites [12,13]. By leveraging such structural and electronic tunability through entropy engineering, ABO3 perovskites hold great promise for the design of advanced materials with superior microwave absorption capabilities [14−16].

The concept of entropy allows for the integration of multiple components, often different types of atoms or elements, into a single-crystal structure, making it energetically favorable for the various components to coexist in a uniform and ordered arrangement. As a result, it mitigates issues commonly observed in composite materials, such as performance inconsistencies caused by mismatched properties (e.g., thermal expansion), thereby enhancing material reliability and applicability in practical scenarios [17]. Zhu *et al*. [18] successfully synthesized high-entropy perovskite ceramics, specifically Ba(Zn0.2Yb0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2V0.2)O3. The ceramics, with a thickness of 2.12 mm, exhibited outstanding performance, attaining a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −54.09 dB and an effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) of 2.81 GHz within the X-band frequency range. Zhao *et al*. [19] utilized off-axis holography to reveal considerable distortion within a high-entropy perovskite structure, which plays a crucial role in enhancing dipole polarization. Nevertheless, within this framework, researchers often neglect the potential contributions of mesentropic materials, which may also demonstrate advantageous performance [20,21]. Liu *et al*. [22] investigated mesoporous perovskite ceramics (Sr0.25Ba0.25Ca0.25La0.25)TiO3 characterized by significant defects, which exhibit a highly complex dielectric constant and polarization loss at gigahertz frequencies. Importantly, this material maintains excellent shielding performance within a specific X-band range, even after prolonged high-temperature annealing in an atmospheric environment.

In general, iron-based perovskite oxides exhibit a stable crystal structure and possess the capacity to be doped with a variety of metal elements [23]. For example, the introduction of Ca2+ induces minor alterations in the octahedral configuration of FeO6, resulting in lattice distortion and thus enhancing the interface and dipole polarization [24]. This approach focuses on employing entropy as a mechanism to incorporate alkaline earth metal cations of varying radii into a medium-entropy perovskite, (Ba1/3Sr1/3Ca1/3)FeO3. The hexagonal medium-entropy (Ba1/3Sr1/3Ca1/3)FeO3 perovskite characterized by a significant number of oxygen vacancy defects was successfully synthesized. The distortion of the crystal structure and the presence of defects strongly influence the dielectric loss associated with electromagnetic wave absorption. Additionally, the electron transitions between iron and oxygen within the crystal field, in the context of the abundant oxygen vacancies, are examined. This study offers a novel approach to the design of perovskite composition and structure through entropy engineering, aiming to achieve a synergistic effect between dielectric loss and magnetic loss in electromagnetic wave applications.

### 2.1 Preparation of medium-entropy (Ba<sub>1/3</sub>Sr<sub>1/3</sub>Ca<sub>1/3</sub>)FeO<sub>3</sub>

The compound (Ba1/3Sr1/3Ca1/3)FeO3 was synthesized using a coprecipitation method. The metal ion salts utilized in this process included barium chloride monohydrate (BaCl2·2H2O, analytical reagent grade, AR, 99.5%; Tianjin Fengboat Chemical Reagent Technology Co., Ltd., China), strontium chloride hexahydrate (SrCl2·6H2O, analytical reagent grade, AR, 99.5%; Beijing Yinokai Technology Co., Ltd., China), calcium chloride (CaCl2, analytical reagent grade, AR, 96%; Tianjin Kemi Ou Chemical Reagent Technology Co., Ltd., China), and ferric nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, analytical reagent grade, AR, 99%; Tianjin Kemi Ou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China). These salts were dissolved in deionized water in accordance with the specified molar ratios to create a metal cation solution. Subsequently, an excess of sodium hydroxide (NaOH, analytical reagent grade, AR, 96%; Tianjin Huadong Reagent Factory, China) and anhydrous sodium carbonate (Na2CO3, analytical reagent grade, AR, 99.8%; Tianjin Fengshan Chemical Reagent Technology Co., Ltd., China) were accurately weighed and dissolved in distilled water to produce a precipitant solution. The metal cation solution was then combined with the precipitant solution, followed by static settling, filtration, drying, and ultimately calcination in a muffle furnace at a heating rate of 5 °C/min, and heat preservation for 4 h. This duration was established to ensure adequate reaction and crystallization of the material once the sample reached a steady state. All the chemicals employed in this study were commercially sourced.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The crystalline structures of the calcined samples were analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Panaco Empyrean; PANalytical, the Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation, and the diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2*θ* range of 20°–70°. GASAII software was used to determine the cell parameters and crystal structure [25]. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-IT800, JEOL, Japan) in conjunction with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) was employed to characterize the morphologies and elemental distributions of the synthesized samples. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) samples were prepared through a micro-nano processing technique (lift-out) of the powder utilizing a focused ion beam (FIB; Helios NanoLab600i, FEI, USA). Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) was conducted using a transmission electron microscope (Talos F200X, FEI, USA), and the elemental distribution was further analyzed with an energy dispersive X-ray detector (5030, Bruker, Germany). An X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; Thermo Fisher Scientific-ESCA, USA) was utilized to assess the composition and chemical states of the elements present on the surface. The magnetic properties of the synthesized samples were measured using a physical property measurement system (PPMS; Quantum Design-MPMS3 7T, USA). The presence of oxygen vacancies in the material was evaluated through an electron paramagnetic resonance spectrometer (EPR; Bruker-A300, Germany), with a central magnetic field of 3500 G, a sweep width of 200.00 G, and sweep time of 30 s. The medium-entropy powder was combined with paraffin wax at a mass ratio of 9 : 1, subsequently melted and mixed, and then pressed into a ring with an outer diameter of 7.00 mm and an inner diameter of 3.04 mm. This sample was analyzed using a vector network analyzer (VNA; E5080B, Keysight, USA) to measure its dielectric and magnetic parameters across a frequency range of 2–18 GHz. CST Studio Suite 2020 software was used to simulate the wave absorption performance of materials for applications under ideal conditions [26]. The measured values of the *X*-axis and *Y*-axis of the PEC substrate and the absorption layer are from 100 to −100 mm, based on the thickness and frequency at the minimum reflection loss of the sample.

### 3.1 XRD and morphologies

The XRD patterns of iron-based oxides are presented in Fig. 1(a). The prominent peaks indicate that a well-crystallized structure is formed in the sample, which corresponds to the position and intensity of the perovskite Sr0.7La0.3FeO3 (PDF#04-007-6520), without the presence of a secondary or mesophase at a temperature of 1100 °C. As the temperature increases, a secondary phase with a spinel structure, BaFe2O4 (PDF#00-044-0897), gradually emerges. During the atmospheric sintering process, the non-stoichiometric oxygen content in the sample significantly influences the crystal structure and the valence state of iron (Fe) ions [27−29]. With the continued increase in temperature, the loss of oxygen ions is accelerated, making it challenging to maintain the octahedral configuration within the structure. Consequently, the stoichiometric ratio of oxygen ions to cations is progressively diminished, leading to the transformation of the hexagonal structure into a spinel configuration. Figure 1(b) shows the Rietveld results for the sample prepared at 1100 °C, which has a low *R*-factor value (*R*wp: 5.06% and *R*p: 3.98%) and high reliability. The cell parameters are *a* = *b* = 5.523519 Å and *c* = 13.550784 Å. EPR spectroscopy was employed to identify unpaired electrons within the materials. At the same time, the sample fired at 1200 °C was also refined, and the results are presented in Fig. 1(c). The *R*p and *R*wp values are 4.50% and 5.99%, respectively, indicating high reliability. The second phase was confirmed to be the spinel phase, with a phase content of 25.92%. As illustrated in Fig. 1(d), the EPR curve exhibits an approximately symmetric Lorentzian profile with a *g* value of 2.003, thereby providing further evidence for the existence of oxygen vacancies. The concentration of oxygen vacancies detected is 2.177×1015 spins/g. The hysteresis loop of the sample is presented in Fig. 1(e), indicating that the 1100 °C-derived sample is a typical paramagnetic material. The microscopic morphologies of the samples, as observed through SEM, provide a more direct representation of this phenomenon. The morphologies of the samples, as depicted in Figs. 1(f) and 1(g), exhibit hexagonal sheets with a thickness of approximately 1 μm. Figure 1(h) illustrates the spatial analysis of elemental distribution on the surface of the sample using SEM-EDS. The results indicate that the distribution of elements is uniform, with no significant segregation observed.

### 3.2 XPS analysis

The chemical state and relative abundance of anions significantly influence the crystal structure and properties of materials. Consequently, this study investigated the chemical states of ions and the underlying reasons for the structural and property variations among different samples, utilizing XPS. In the context of the 1100 °C-derived sample, as depicted in Fig. 2 and Table 1, the wide-scan XPS spectra at the correct binding energies confirm the presence of various elements. In particular, Figs. 2(a)–2(c) illustrate that all the elements of the A-sites exhibit both low and high binding energy peaks. Specifically, the Ca 2p spectrum comprises the 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 states, with a binding energy difference of 3.4 eV. The Sr 3d spectrum, as depicted in Fig. 2(b), reveals two distinct doublets. These doublets correspond to Sr in the perovskite lattice, denoted as Sr2+ (indicated by the green areas labeled as Sr-L), and to the oxygen vacancies and other cations in A-sites surrounding the Sr ions (represented by the orange areas labeled as Sr-V) [30]. The presence of surrounding oxygen vacancies reduces the electron binding energy of the Sr2+ inner shell. The lower binding energy peaks at 132.76 and 134.51 eV correspond to Sr-V 3d5/2 and Sr-V 3d3/2, and the peaks at 133.18 and 134.94 eV correspond to Sr-L 3d5/2 and Sr-L 3d3/2, respectively. The ratio of the peak area *A*Sr-L/*A*Sr-V is approximately 1 : 1.25. The 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 peaks of Ba2+ correspond to 779.63 and 794.94 eV, and loss features are observed in the Ba 3d region at 790.22 and 804.07 eV [31−33]. Moreover, the O 1s and Fe 2p spectra are shown in Figs. 2(d) and 2(e). The three O 1s peaks are attributed to lattice oxygen (OL, at 528.9 eV), vacant oxygen (OV, at 530.91 eV), and absorbed oxygen (OS, at 531.8 eV), with high energy peaks in the sample [32,34]. The presence of oxygen defects significantly enhances the dipole relaxation of materials, while both these defects and the surface adsorption of oxygen offer numerous polarization sites [19,35]. Additionally, the asymmetric charge distribution induced by oxygen vacancies promotes the formation of abundant dipoles, thereby improving the material’s absorption capability. As indexed in Fig. 2(e), the 2p3/2 peaks of Fe were fitted by the method of multiple splitting peaks, and the splitting peaks are 709.15, 710.15, 711.05, 712.15, 713.25, and 718.65 eV. The results indicate that Fe ions in the sample primarily exist in the +3 oxidation state [36,37]. These findings are consistent with the significant presence of oxygen vacancies (Ov) in the O 1s spectrum. Based on the XPS results, the paramagnetism observed in the material may be attributed to superexchange interactions involving Fe3+ ions [38,39]. The outer electron shell of Fe3+ is half-filled, which contributes to a relatively stable electronic structure; consequently, the electron exchange between cations is challenging. Furthermore, the net spin of the d orbitals of Fe3+ ions located on either side of the oxygen ion in the Fe3+–O2−–Fe3+ configuration is antiparallel [40,41].

### 3.4 Electromagnetic parameters

Electromagnetic waves enter a material and expand inside it, which can meet the requirements of absorbing materials. That is, the wave attenuation coefficient (*α*) and impedance matching (*Z* * =Zin/Z0) can be calculated as Eqs. (1) and (2) [43,44]:

(1) α=2πfc×(μ″ε″−μ′ε′)+(μ″ε″−μ′ε′)2+(μ′ε″+μ″ε′)2

(2) Z=|ZinZ0|=|μr/εr|tanh(j(2πfdc)μrεr)

where c* is the speed of light in vacuum; *f* is the frequency; *Z*0 represents the air impedance; *Z*in stands for the normalized input characteristic impedance; *d* symbolizes the sample thickness. In an electromagnetic wave environment, phase differences occur due to relaxation polarization in the medium, and the complex permittivity (*ε*r) and complex permeability (*μ*r) become complex numbers. *ε'*, *ε"*, and *μ"*, *μʹ* are the real and imaginary parts of *ε*r and *μ*r, respectively, as illustrated in Eqs. (3) and (4) [45]:

(3) εr=ε′−jε′′

(4) μr=μ′−jμ′′

The real part represents the absorption and reflection ability of the medium to the electromagnetic wave, while the imaginary part represents the influence of the medium on the propagation speed and phase difference of the electromagnetic wave. Accordingly, the ability to waste electromagnetic waves is closely related to the permittivity (*ε*r) and complex permeability (*μ*r) of the materials.

The variation in the complex permittivity and permeability parameters of the sample as a function of frequency was assessed using the coaxial method. Figures 4(a)–4(c) illustrate the values of *εʹ* and *ε"* with the frequency within the range of 2–18 GHz. The parameters *εʹ* and *ε"* demonstrate multiple dielectric resonances across the frequency spectrum, facilitating the conversion of electromagnetic energy into thermal energy [46]. Notably, *εʹ* tends to decrease with increasing frequency, which can be attributed to the frequency dispersion characteristics inherent to the materials. As the frequency increases, the response of the dipoles within the sample progressively lags behind the variations in the electromagnetic wave. Generally, the imaginary permittivity (*ε"*) of the 1100 °C-derived sample decreases with increasing frequency, while the samples prepared at 1000 and 1200 °C display multiple resonances within the frequency range, accompanied by a slight increase [47]. The observed phenomenon is primarily attributed to the electron hopping frequency between metal ions, which matches the resonance frequency of the incident microwaves. The parameter *ε"* signifies the dielectric loss capacity of the materials, which primarily encompasses the polarization loss (*ε*p) and conductivity loss (*ε*c) relationships governing these losses as Eqs. (5)–(7) [48]:

(5) ε′′(ω)=εp′′+εc′′=(εs−ε∞)ωτ1+ω2τ2+σεoω

(6) εp′′=(εs−ε∞)ωτ1+ω2τ2

where *ε*∞ denotes the high-frequency limit of the dielectric constant, while *ε*s represents the static value of the dielectric constant. Additionally, *ε*0 refers to the dielectric constant in free space; *τ* signifies the relaxation time; *σ* indicates the conductivity; *ω* represents the angular frequency. The loss associated with conductivity is inversely related to the frequency and constitutes the primary loss mechanism at low frequencies. Conversely, a notably high conductivity may negatively impact the impedance matching of materials [49]. Furthermore, relaxation polarization is a critical factor at high frequencies. At high frequencies, the alternating electric field produced by the movement of charge under microwave radiation facilitates relaxation polarization and enhances the dielectric loss characteristics of materials. Concurrently, lattice distortions, defects, and oxygen vacancies within ceramics further contribute to relaxation polarization [19,50]. When the material interacts with electromagnetic waves, resonance occurs at specific frequencies, which is reflected in the dielectric constant’s format. The migration and transition of electrons within a material, along with lattice distortions and the binding of defect vacancies, influence the frequency resonance [51]. Furthermore, the material facilitates the conversion of electromagnetic wave losses into thermal energy, thereby enhancing internal lattice vibrations, which subsequently interact with electromagnetic waves. Compared with the permittivity dielectric constant, the permeability parameter has a lower value. As illustrated in Figs. 4(d)–4(f), the imaginary component of the permeability and the tangent of the magnetic loss demonstrate a similar variation trend. Additionally, the negative value of *μ"* at high frequencies indicates that, under the influence of electromagnetic waves, the movement of electrons generates a reverse magnetic field due to electromagnetic induction. This phenomenon results in the stimulation of excess magnetic energy through reflection, leading to a negative permeability value [52]. Moreover, a comparison of Figs. 4(c) and 4(f) reveals that the variations in the dielectric properties and permeability are mutually compensatory.

To understand the polarization relaxation phenomenon of materials more clearly, according to Debye’s theory, the relationship between the real and imaginary parts of the dielectric constant is as Eq. (8) [53]:

(8) (ε′−εs+ε∞2)2+(ε′′)2=(εs−ε∞2)2

Cole–Cole circles are formed between the dielectric parameters, indicating that polarization loss plays an important role in dielectric loss. Multiple numbers and different sizes of Cole–Cole circles correspond to multiple polarization relaxation processes and relaxation time, respectively [54]. As illustrated in Figs. 5(a)–5(c), the samples derived at 1000 and 1200 °C exhibit a reduced relaxation polarization at low frequencies, with conductance loss identified as the primary loss mechanism. Polarization relaxation occurs across multiple frequency ranges, wherein dipoles experience directional polarization in response to alternating electromagnetic fields. Carriers have enough time to pass through the material at lower frequencies and cause resistance loss. The dipoles lag behind the electromagnetic field, leading to polarization losses that are more prominent at higher frequencies. The sample has a relatively obvious transition to relaxation characteristics at 4–6 GHz, and the relaxation characteristics gradually increase with increasing frequency. During the preparation process, the formation of vacancies, cation substitutions, dislocations, and other defects within the crystal structure can also lead to the generation of dipole pairs. The delayed rotation of these dipole pairs in an alternating electromagnetic field facilitates the conversion of electromagnetic energy into Joule heat [51,55].

Additionally, eddy current loss is another important parameter influencing electromagnetic wave losses. The relationship between the eddy current loss coefficient (*C*0) and frequency is as Eq. (9) [56]:

(9) C0=μ′′(μ′)−2f−1

The eddy current loss coefficient *C*0 is an important basis for determining the type of magnetic loss, and it changes with the frequency. Figure 5(d) shows the relationship between the eddy current loss coefficient and frequency of each sample, and its change trend is consistent with the tangent value of magnetic loss. At low frequencies, natural resonance serves as the primary loss mechanism for the sample, whereas at high frequencies, eddy current loss may become the predominant magnetic loss mechanism. A lower value of *C*0 suggests that the materials exhibit a relatively diminished capacity for magnetic loss, which implies that dielectric loss becomes significant at high frequencies within these materials [46,57].

### 3.5 Microwave absorption

According to the dielectric and magnetic parameters of the material, the absorption energy is measured using the reflection loss (RL) (Eqs. (10) and (11)) [58]:

(10) RL=20lg|Zin−Z0Zin+Z0|

(11) Absorptance(%)=100−100×10−RL/10

In general, when the reflection loss value is less than −10 dB, the absorption capacity of the material to electromagnetic waves reaches 90%, which is regarded as effective absorption. Figure 6 shows the wave-absorbing properties of the medium-entropy ceramics. The sample prepared at a lower temperature of 1000 °C only has a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −24.3 dB (4.5 mm) but has better EAB (4.7 GHz). The samples fired at 1100 °C exhibit better RLmin and EAB of −40.58 dB (1.2 mm) and 4.16 GHz, respectively. The sample sintered at 1200 °C has the best RLmin of −48.03 dB (4.2 mm) but smaller EAB of 3.7 GHz. The impedance matching of the sample fired at a lower temperature is poor, which affects the transmission of electromagnetic waves into the material. However, it still demonstrates a strong ability to absorb electromagnetic waves, particularly in the high-frequency band, and exhibits a wide EAB. These results indicate that (Ba1/3Sr1/3Ca1/3)FeO3 possesses a significant dielectric loss capacity. In contrast, the sample fired at a higher temperature exhibits improved impedance matching due to the formation of a second phase of spinel. The presence of this phase interface enhances the ability of the material to attenuate electromagnetic waves, resulting in a better RL value.

Figures 6(a), 6(d), and 6(g) also show the relationships between sample thickness and frequency change and the corresponding impedance matching. With increasing thickness (t*m), the RLmin peak begins to move to a low frequency, which can be explained by the quarter-wavelength theory (Eq. (12)) [59]:

(12) tm=nλ4=nc4f|μr||εr|,n=1,3,5…

where *λ* represents the wavelength. The equation shows that the thickness (*t*m) is inversely proportional to the frequency (*f*) when the other parameters are unchanged, so the frequency (*f*) gradually decreases as the thickness (*t*m) continues to increase. The theoretical derivation is consistent with the experimental results. Furthermore, impedance matching is another critical factor affecting electromagnetic absorption, represented by the normalized impedance (*Z*). Generally, the closer the *Z* is to 1, the more electromagnetic waves can enter the medium material and be consumed. The impedance matching range of the samples derived at 1000 and 1100 °C is 0.8–1, which follows the same trend as the variation of the complex dielectric constant. Compared with the samples derived at 1000 and 1100 °C, the sample prepared at 1200 °C has a greater impedance (1.5–2). As shown by the XRD results, the sample sintered at 1200 °C exhibits a two-phase structure, and the accumulation of free charge at each interface, as well as at the boundaries between different phases, results in interface polarization and increased impedance (*Z*). The incorporation of entropy design introduces additional structural defects and complex chemical environments, thereby enhancing the dielectric properties of the material and optimizing impedance matching. Concurrently, the concentration of oxygen vacancies plays a crucial role in regulating both the medium and magnetic loss capacity [35]. Additionally, the presence of oxygen vacancies generates a significant number of defect sites and hole charge carriers within the medium-entropy perovskites, which in turn leads to an increase in dipole polarization [60,61].

According to the magnetization versus magnetic field (*M*–*H*) characteristics of the material, the sample reveals a nearly paramagnetic behavior. The loss of the samples to electromagnetic waves primarily arises from dielectric loss and minor magnetic loss. Dielectric loss is predominantly attributed to conductivity loss and polarization loss. The hexagonal lamellar structure of the material facilitates electron conduction at low frequencies. At high frequencies, the introduction of various cations into the A-sites, along with the low symmetry regions resulting from microstructural distortions, the chemical short-range order within the nanoregion, the presence of oxygen vacancies, point defects, and layer faults, collectively enhance the polarization of dipoles, thereby achieving effective absorption of electromagnetic waves.

To further evaluate the performance of the sample and its potential application in practical scenarios, CST software was used to simulate and compare the radar cross section (RCS) of ideal electrical conductors (PECs) with that of samples on PECs [62]. As shown in Fig. 7, the RCSs of the 1100 °C- and 1200 °C-derived samples decrease by 27.79 and 45.14 dB·m2, respectively. These findings indicate that the sample prepared at 1100 °C has significant potential value for practical applications. Figure 7(b) illustrates a comparison of the properties of (Ba1/3Sr1/3Ca1/3)FeO3 with those of reported perovskite oxide absorbing materials [63−71]. These results indicate that entropy engineering can be employed to optimize both RLmin and EAB of perovskite oxides. This approach offers a novel perspective and direction for the design and optimization of perovskite absorbing materials. Figure 7(c) illustrates a schematic representation of the wave-absorbing mechanism of the material. The presence of oxygen defects and dipole polarization, and the accumulation of positive and negative charges at the grain boundaries within the materials enhance the dielectric loss of electromagnetic waves. Additionally, the movement of electrons within the lattice, facilitated by the hexagonal structure, contributes to the conduction loss of the material, ultimately resulting in exceptional wave-absorbing properties.

## 4 Conclusions

A medium-entropy iron-based perovskite (Ba1/3Sr1/3Ca1/3)FeO3 material was investigated, with a focus on its electromagnetic properties and underlying mechanisms. These results indicate that the crystal structure of the material can be modulated by the characteristics of various principal elements through entropy engineering, thereby enhancing its absorption performance. The complex chemical environment, characterized by varying ionic radii and valence states, contributes to the distortion of the FeO6 octahedron and the presence of oxygen vacancies and dislocations. The existence of abundant oxygen vacancies, defects, and lattice distortions within the structure promotes dipole polarization and relaxation loss, significantly enhancing the ability of the material to dissipate electromagnetic waves. Additionally, the optimization of impedance matching enables the realization of effective microwave absorption performance in the material. These factors collectively promote the dielectric alterations in entropy perovskite and facilitate the dielectric loss of electromagnetic waves. Notably, the minimum reflection loss observed for the sample at a thickness of 1.2 mm is −40.58 dB, with a maximum effective absorption bandwidth of 4.16 GHz. The CST simulation results demonstrate that the material exhibits a favorable far-field response, with a RCS reduction value of 27.79 dB·m², thereby establishing a robust theoretical and practical foundation for future applications.
