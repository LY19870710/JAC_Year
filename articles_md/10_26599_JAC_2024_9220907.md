## 1 Introduction

Barium aluminosilicate (BaAl2Si2O8, BAS) is an excellent candidate for application in the fields of radome technology and electronic packaging due to its low thermal expansion coefficient and good dielectric properties [1−5]. The incorporation of Sr into BAS to form Ba*x*Sr1−*x*Al2Si2O8 (BSAS) ceramics overcomes the issue of the low-temperature phase transition of BAS and endows BSAS ceramics with a unique resistance to water vapor corrosion [6−9]. However, the low tolerance to damage and defects of BSAS ceramics limits their development for industrial applications [10].

The introduction of reinforcements is an effective approach for improving the strength and toughness of ceramics [11−14]. Typical reinforcements include zero-dimensional (0D) ceramic particles (e.g., SiC particles and Si3N4 particles) [15,16], one-dimensional (1D) nanotubes (e.g., carbon nanotubes and boron nitride (BN) nanotubes) [17,18], nanowires (e.g., carbon nanowires and SiC nanowires (SiCnws)) [19−21], and two-dimensional (2D) materials (e.g., graphene and BN nanosheets [22−24]). The enhancing effects of carbon nanotubes, SiC nanosheets, and BN nanosheets on the mechanical properties of BAS ceramics have been confirmed, with optimized flexural strength and fracture toughness ( KIC) reaching approximately 190 MPa and 3.0 MPa·m1/2, respectively [10,17,25]. However, research on the mechanical properties of BSAS ceramics has rarely been conducted.

SiCnws have the characteristics of high strength and hardness, which are inherited from SiC ceramics, as well as good toughness and elasticity, which are typical of nanomaterials [26,27]. Recently, SiCnws have been utilized in ceramic matrices such as SiC and Al2O3, as well as in ceramic matrix composites such as carbon fiber/lithium aluminosilicate, demonstrating outstanding strengthening and toughening effects [28−30]. Thus, SiCnws can potentially serve as highly efficacious reinforcements for BSAS ceramics. The strengthening and toughening effect of reinforcements is mainly related to their dispersion, especially for reinforcements on the nanometer scale. However, nanomaterials can easily agglomerate, making it difficult to take full advantage of the strengthening and toughening effect of SiCnws. Surface modification or the use of dispersants are common methods for improving the dispersibility of nanoreinforcements [31]. However, both of these methods introduce organic compounds, which may lead to unexpected reactions and product formation during ceramic sintering, affecting the purity and further influencing the performance of the ceramics. At the same time, the toughening effect of the reinforcements can also be affected by interfacial bonding. Usually, a strong interfacial bond is formed between the SiCnws and the ceramic matrix, and cracks cannot be effectively deflected [12]. Optimizing both the SiCnws dispersion and the deflection of cracks is crucial to fully exploit the strengthening and toughening effect of SiCnws [17].

A powder with good fluidity is key to improving the dispersion of SiCnws during the densification process, and the construction of a low-modulus interface is crucial for the deflection of cracks according to the criterion of He and Hutchinson [32,33]. The introduction of SiO2 with a low softening temperature and a low modulus can satisfy these two requirements. The softening temperature of SiO2 is only 900 °C [34], and SiO2 can thus cover ceramic particles under pressure, reducing the contact stress in the sintering process and improving the fluidity of ceramic powders [35]. At the same time, molten SiO2 in the sintering process is embedded as an interface between SiCnws and BSAS grains, which can weaken the interfacial bond between them.

Based on the above considerations, SiO2 was introduced into SiCnws/BSAS multiphase ceramics to form an embedded interface and thus optimize the strengthening and toughening effect of the SiCnws. The interface structure was optimized by adjusting the SiO2 content, and the strengthening and toughening mechanisms were studied.

### 2.2 Performance tests and characterization

The density and apparent porosity of the ceramics were measured using the Archimedes method according to Eqs. (1) and (2) [36,37]:

(1) ρ=m1ρ0m3−m2

(2) V=m3−m1m3−m2×100%

where * ρ (g/cm3) represents the bulk density of the ceramics; V denotes the apparent porosity;  ρ0 represents the density of water;  m1 is the mass of the dried sample;  m2 represents the mass of the sample immersed in water;  m3 represents the mass of the sample that has been soaked in water after removing surface moisture through wiping. To ensure the accuracy of the data, at least five samples were tested for each assembly.

To assess the flexural strength of the ceramics (with dimensions of 4 mm × 3 mm × 36 mm), a three-point bending method was employed with a span of 30 mm and a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min (according to the standard ASTM C1161-18). At least five samples were tested for each assembly. The flexural stress ( σf) and flexural strain ( ϵf) were calculated using the equations provided in Ref. [38], which are as Eqs. (3) and (4):

where  P is the maximum load (N);  L is the span (mm); D* is the displacement (mm); * b and  h are the width and the height of the sample (mm), respectively.

To determine  KIC, the single-edge notch beam method was employed (according to the standard ASTM C1421-18). Specimens with dimensions of 4 mm × 3 mm × 26 mm were used for testing with a span of 20 mm and a crosshead speed of 0.05 mm/min. The Vickers hardness was obtained using a microhardness tester (LM248AT, LECO Co., USA) with an applied load of 1000 g and holding time of 15 s (according to the standard ASTM C1327-15). Each test was repeated at least five times to ensure the reliability of the results.

Microstructural analysis was conducted using focused-ion beam (FIB) microscope (Helios G4 CX, FEI, USA) and double Cs-corrected transmission electron microscope (TEM; Themis Z, FEI, USA). Prior to obtaining backscattered electron (BSE) images, the sample surfaces were polished successively using diamond paste with particle sizes of 3, 1, and 0.25 μm. For TEM observations, thin slices with a thickness of approximately 100 nm were prepared from samples SiC10 and SO15 using FIB. Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) and scanning transmission electron microscopy-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (STEM-EDS) were performed via TEM. The phase composition was determined via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Avance, Bruker, Germany) using Cu Kα radiation. The grain size of the samples can be calculated using the Scherrer equation (Eq. (7)):

(7) t=Kλβcosθ

where  t (nm) represents the size of the grains along specific crystallographic directions;  β denotes the full width at half maximum of the diffraction peak;  θ (°) is the Bragg diffraction angle;  λ represents the incident wavelength (0.154 nm);  K is a constant (0.89).

### 3.1 Microstructures and phase compositions

The samples BS0 and SiC10 exhibit a high bulk density (~3.30 g/cm3) and a low relative density (~98.9%) (Table 2). As the SiO2 content increases from 5 to 35 vol%, the bulk density decreases from 3.25 to 2.91 g/cm3. The density of SiO2 is approximately 2.20 g/cm3, which is lower than that of SiC (3.20 g/cm3) and BSAS (3.30 g/cm3). Therefore, with increasing SiO2 content, the theoretical density and bulk density decrease gradually. In contrast to the bulk density, the relative density increases with the introduction of SiO2, reaching approximately 99.35%. Such an increase in relative density means an increase in the densification degree of the as-fabricated samples. As shown in Table 2, sample BS0 has the highest porosity (1.17 vol%) and the highest closed porosity (0.92 vol%). After the introduction of the SiCnws, the porosity of the SiC10 sample decreases slightly (0.78 vol%), but the total porosity still exceeds 1 vol%. This shows that the introduction of SiCnws does not improve the densification degree of the BSAS ceramics, and their agglomeration still affects the densification degree. With the introduction of SiO2, the porosity of the as-fabricated samples decreases to 0.6 vol%, indicating that molten SiO2 can effectively reduce the number of closed pores and improve the densification degree of the ceramics.

The distributions of BSAS, SiCnws, and SiO2 in the samples are shown in Fig. 2. As seen from the BSE image of sample BS0 (Fig. 2(a)), the ceramic has only a single phase with a large number of closed pores. This corresponds to the densification degree of the BSAS ceramic discussed above. As shown in Fig. 2(b), for sample SiC10, the white BSAS matrix contains dispersed black, rod-shaped SiCnws that appear to be mostly concentrated within certain regions of the matrix, which is referred to as the SiCnws-enriched region. At the same time, many closed pores can still be observed. The SiCnws are affected by severe agglomeration, and the presence of interlocking SiCnws in sample SiC10 results in larger closed pores. With the introduction of SiO2, gray areas appear in the BSE images and continue to expand (Figs. 2(c)–2(f)). During the sintering process, BSAS grows along a preferred orientation, and molten SiO2 covers the SiCnws more easily and is embedded between the BSAS and the SiCnws [35]. Since white BSAS is predominant, SiO2 tends to combine with the SiCnws to form a SiCnws/SiO2 region, as shown in Figs. 2(c)–2(e). The distribution of the SiCnws/SiO2 regions appears more uniform than that of the SiCnws-enriched regions in sample SiC10, indicating that the introduction of SiO2 improves the dispersibility of the SiCnws to some extent. As the SiO2 content reaches 35 vol%, gray SiO2 is predominant, and a large SiO2-enriched region appears (Fig. 2(f)). The grain size of BSAS is clearly smaller than that of the other samples because the excess molten SiO2 limits the growth of the BSAS grains during the sintering process. In the sample S35, the grain size of BSAS is significantly smaller than that of the other samples. As shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM, the grain size of BSAS along two optimal growth directions was calculated based on the XRD results ( 1¯12) with  2θ=25.8∘ and ( 2¯20) with  2θ=27.0∘. With increasing SiO2 content, the grain size of the BSAS gradually decreased. It is evident that molten SiO2 restricts the growth of BSAS grains during the sintering process.

### 3.2 Mechanical properties

The mechanical properties of the SiCnws/BSAS ceramics were investigated, and the corresponding results are shown in Fig. 5. Figure 5(a) illustrates the variation in  ϵf and  KIC of the samples. The flexural strength of the pure BSAS ceramic is 126±14 MPa (sample BS0), and the flexural strength increases to 141±12 MPa after the introduction of 10 vol% SiCnws (sample SiC10), which clearly demonstrates the strengthening effect of the SiCnws. After the introduction of SiO2, the flexural strength of the ceramics is greatly improved. With the addition of 15 vol% SiO2, the flexural strength reaches 193±16 MPa (sample SO15), which is 52.6% greater than that of sample BS0. According to Fig. 5(a), when the SiO2 content exceeds 15 vol%, the brittle phase adversely affects the mechanical properties of the ceramic. At SiO2 contents of 25 and 35 vol%, the flexural strength is slightly lower.  KIC shows the same variation trend. It reaches a maximum (3.1±0.5 MPa·m1/2) for the sample SO15, which is 94.0% higher than that of the sample BS0 (1.6±0.3 MPa·m1/2). Then,  KIC decreases substantially as the SiO2 content exceeds 15 vol% and finally reaches 2.2 MPa·m1/2 (sample SO35).

The flexural strength of the as-fabricated samples is consistent with the above analysis, and sample SO15 exhibits the maximum flexural strength. The flexural modulus was also calculated and is shown in Fig. 5(b). The pure BSAS ceramic has the highest flexural modulus (144.7±2.3 GPa). After the introduction of SiCnws, the flexural modulus decreases to 83.5±3.8 GPa. SiCnws have a higher Young’s modulus than BSAS, and the modulus of multiphase ceramics should increase after the introduction of SiCnws [40]. However, due to the agglomeration of the SiCnws, the density decreases, and the generation of closed pores causes the flexural modulus of the ceramic to become significantly lower than the ideal value [41]. Although the porosities and densities of the samples BS0 and SiC10 are similar, the presence of interlocking SiCnws in the sample SiC10 results in larger closed pores. This would cause a destructive effect on the overall flexural modulus and cause a decrease rather than an increase in the flexural modulus after the introduction of SiCnws. With the introduction of SiO2, the flexural modulus of the as-fabricated samples decreases further, reaching approximately 70 GPa. The introduction of the low-modulus SiO2 phase reduces the overall flexural modulus of the ceramics.

In addition to  ϵf and  KIC, Fig. S7 in the ESM also compares the Vickers hardness of the different samples. The hardness reaches a maximum for the sample SiC10 (7.31±0.07 GPa) and decreases gradually with the introduction of SiO2. SiC is a type of ceramic with an extremely high hardness. Due to the load support function of the SiCnws, the hardness of the multiphase ceramics is 13% greater than that of the pure BSAS ceramic (6.48±0.07 GPa). SiO2 is a brittle material with a low hardness, and its introduction into ceramics decreases its overall hardness.

Figure 6 displays the fracture surfaces of the samples. The pure BSAS ceramic exhibits a typical layered structure (Fig. 6(a)). Usually, intergranular and transgranular fractures occur in the BSAS grains, leading to brittle fracture of the ceramic and smooth fracture morphology. SiCnw pullout can be clearly observed in the SiC10 sample, especially near the closed pores (Fig. 6(b)). Most of the closed pores are located in the SiCnws-enriched region. As cracks propagate to the closed pores, the internally agglomerated SiCnws become exposed, which results in SiCnw pullout; this phenomenon cannot result in effective consumption of the fracture energy  (Γ). The layered fracture area in the sample SiC10 is smaller than that in the sample BS0, indicating that the introduction of SiCnws can refine the BSAS grains. As a result, the flexural strength and fracture toughness of the sample SiC10 increased by 11.9% and 18.8%, respectively, compared with those of the sample BS0 (Fig. 5(a)).

### 3.3 Strengthening and toughening mechanism

The criterion of He and Hutchinson serves as a systematic framework for evaluating the propagation behavior of cracks between different elastic materials. The propagation of cracks at the phase interface can be evaluated according to the criterion of He and Hutchinson [43−45]. As shown in Fig. 4, SiO2 acts as an embedded interface between the BSAS and the SiCnws. According to the criterion of He and Hutchinson, Dundur’s elastic mismatch parameter (* α) can be calculated as Eqs. (8)-(10) [46]:

For cracks perpendicular to the interface, the critical value that leads to crack deflection and penetration can be determined, as shown by the gray curve in Fig. 7, which is named the crosshatched curve. The calculated  Γ1/Γ2 values of the two types of interfaces are on different sides of the critical curve, which explains the different crack propagation behaviors at the two interfaces. For the BSAS/SiCnw interface, cracks expand continually and penetrate the SiCnws. At the SiO2/SiCnw interface, cracks are deflected and propagated along the SiCnws. At the same time, under the influence of a shear force, branch cracks and multiple deflections can be observed when the cracks propagate in the SiO2-enriched region (Fig. 8(d) and Fig. S10 in the ESM). Branch cracks evolve around the main cracks, thus relieving the stress concentration at the crack tip. Branch cracks and multiple deflections can also extend the crack propagation path, thereby contributing to achieving an enhanced fracture toughness of the ceramics.

According to the above analysis, the strengthening and toughening effect of the SiCnws is mainly enhanced as a consequence of the optimization of the nanowire dispersion, which is in turn due to the introduction of SiO2. Benefiting from the enhanced fluidity of the ceramic powders due to the molten SiO2 during the sintering process, the dispersion of SiCnws becomes more uniform (Fig. 2), the density of the as-fabricated samples increases, and the closed pores disappear (Fig. 3), which leads to an increase in the flexural strength and fracture toughness of the ceramics. Furthermore, with the formation of the embedded SiO2 interface, cracks are deflected according to the criterion of He and Hutchinson, and the crack propagation path can be effectively extended. The toughening effect of the SiCnws is further explored. Therefore, the flexural strength of sample SO15 is 52.6% greater than that of sample BS0, while the fracture toughness increases by 94.0%.

## 4 Conclusions

SiCnws/Ba0.75Sr0.25Al2Si2O8 multiphase ceramics with an embedded SiO2 interface were prepared via hot pressing at 1450 °C. Molten SiO2 improves the fluidity of the ceramic powder during the sintering process and prevents the agglomeration of the SiCnws, which allows the strengthening and toughening effects induced by the SiCnws on the ceramics to be better exploited. The SiO2 interface is evenly distributed between SiCnws and BSAS phases. At the same time, the low-modulus SiO2 interface deflects the cracks, which toughens the ceramics. As a result, for a SiO2 content of 15 vol%, the flexural strength and fracture toughness increase by 52.6% and 94.0%, respectively, compared with those of the pure BSAS ceramic. The strategy proposed in this work, which consists of designing an embedded interface with a low softening temperature and a low modulus, provides a new route for strengthening and toughening ceramics.
