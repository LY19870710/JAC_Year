## 1 Introduction of high-entropy strategy

The ever-increasing global energy demand is accelerating the transition from an era dominated by fossil fuels to a new era of renewable energy. Even though renewable energy sources are highly favored for their cleanliness and regenerative capacity, deficiencies in the stability and efficiency of energy supply still constrain their extensive application. To overcome these bottlenecks, developing high-performance energy storage devices has become particularly critical. Rechargeable metal-ion batteries, such as lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) and sodium-ion batteries (SIBs), have emerged as research focus in the field of energy storage, yet they still encounter numerous challenges in terms of capacity, stability, and sustainability [1−8]. As depicted in Table 1, employing a high-entropy strategy is expected to realize the precise regulation of battery performance and overcome the inherent limitations of materials [9−13].

In recent years, high-entropy materials (HEMs), as an emerging concept in the field of materials science, have gained prominence in multiple frontier fields, such as catalysis, thermoelectric conversion, and electrochemical energy storage [14−17]. Unlike traditional materials, HEMs founded on the design concept of “multi-element solid solution”, enabling simultaneous regulation of the capacity fading of electrode materials, the interfacial compatibility of electrolytes, and the cycling durability of full cells via the entropy-increasing dominant distribution of more than five elements at lattice sites. In recent years, the successful application of high-entropy materials in frontier fields such as lithium–sulfur batteries and solid electrolytes (SEs) has verified their distinctive value in realizing the synergistic optimization of “high energy density–high safety–low cost”, offering a brand-new material design dimension for next-generation energy storage technology (Fig. 1) [18−20].

In 2004, Yeh et al.* [17] initially discovered high-entropy alloys (HEAs), which subsequently prompted research on the mechanical properties of HEMs. In 2016, Bérardan *et al.* [21] pioneered the application of the high-entropy approach to battery material design, successfully synthesizing a novel SE (Mg,Co,Ni,Cu,Zn)1−*x*Li(Na)*x*O. This material formed oxygen vacancies via an intrinsic charge compensation mechanism, enabling Li+ or Na+ to be substituted into rock-salt structures. As *x* increased from 0.02 to 0.33, the ion conductivity increased proportionally with the quantity of Li+/Na+ ions. In 2022, a novel high-entropy Na superionic conductor (NASICON) SE Na3.5Mg0.1Sc0.15In0.15Ti0.3Hf0.3ZrSi2PO12 was successfully synthesizedby Zeng *et al.* [23], with a total conductivity reaching 1.1 mS·cm−1 and a bulk conductivity of 3.3 mS·cm−1 at room temperature, which was equivalent to that of the fastest sodium-ion conductors. In 2023, Li *et al.* [20] extended the high-entropy concept to the NASICON structure and fabricated high-purity Na3V(Al0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2In0.2Ga0.2)(PO4)3 (NVMP) cathode materials. Through consecutive redox reactions of V3+/V4+/V5+, the NVMP has attained a high reversible specific capacity of 102 mAh·g−1 (2.5–4.4 V vs. Na+/Na) and manifested excellent long-term cycling stability, retaining 86.8% of its initial capacity after 5000 cycles at 20 C. Even under extremely low-temperature conditions (−20 °C), the NVMP cathode still proffered outstanding cycling performance, with a capacity retention rate as high as 94.2% after 1000 cycles at 5 C.

### 1.1 Concept of high-entropy materials

To date, the definition of HEMs remains controversial and is mainly classified into two major categories: composition-based and entropy-based. This concept and its theoretical basis were initially introduced by Yeh *et al.* [17] in 2004. They successfully designed a multi-principal element alloy with single-phase solid-state structures by incorporating various elements at equimolar concentrations. This innovation not only signally aggrandized the configurational entropy but also motivated the formation of single-phase solid solutions. Since then, the term HEAs has been broadly adopted to describe solid solution alloys containing five or more elements, with the atomic percentage of each element ranging from 5% to 35%. Compared with traditional alloys, HEAs demonstrate higher melting points, decent corrosion resistance, and enhanced mechanical properties resulting from multi-element blending [24−27]. Subsequently, high-entropy ceramics (HECs) have undergone a rapid upsurge and manifested a series of remarkable characteristics, including notable enhancements in hardness and melting point, as well as exceptional durability, and abrasive and corrosion resistance under extreme conditions [28−33]. The uniqueness thereof lies in the existence of five or more cations or anions in the solid solution sublattice, which generates extremely high configurational entropy when the content of principal elements is increased. In a broad sense, HEMs refer to materials composed of five or more principal elements in equimolar or near-equimolar proportions. More specifically, the concentration of each element ranges from 5% to 35%, with the requirement that these elements be uniformly and randomly distributed within a single phase.

On the other hand, to determine whether a single phase is formed within a multi-component system, the assessment can be initially conducted by calculating its Gibbs free energy (Δ*G*) as Eq. (1) [34]:

(1) ΔG=ΔH−TΔS

The variation in Δ*G* is jointly determined by the enthalpy change (Δ*H*) and the entropy change (Δ*S*), with the thermodynamic temperature being *T*. Specifically, when Δ*H* is negative, the contribution of enthalpy will predominate the formation of the single-phase solid solution; nevertheless, when Δ*H* is positive, the spontaneity of the reaction will hinge on the product of temperature and entropy change (*T*Δ*S*). If *T*Δ*S* surpasses Δ*H*, resulting in a negative total Δ*G*, an entropy-driven reaction will ensue. Rost *et al.* [16] demonstrated the pivotal role of entropy in stabilizing structures: HEAs undergo a distinctive transformation from a single phase to a multi-phase state during low-temperature heat treatment and then reversibly revert to a single phase after high-temperature heat treatment (Fig. 2(a)). Consequently, increasing the system entropy can prominently expedite the development of single-phase materials.

Within the thermodynamic framework, entropy fundamentally quantifies the disorder or degeneracy of microscopic configurations corresponding to a macroscopic equilibrium state. For solid solutions, the total hybrid entropy arises from multiple synergistic contributions: configurational entropy (ΔS*config), vibrational entropy, magnetic dipole entropy, electronic random entropy, excess entropy (*S*ex), mixing entropy (*S*mix), energy entropy, reaction entropy, etc. [35]. Δ*S*config stems from the combinatorial distribution of multi-component atoms in lattice sites [36]. Vibrational entropy is associated with phonon mode variations induced by atomic size mismatch and bonding heterogeneity [37]. Magnetic dipole entropy originates from disordered spin arrangements in magnetic dipoles [38]. Electronic random entropy derives from degenerate electron spin/orbital states near the Fermi level [39]. *S*ex signifies nonlinear deviations from ideal mixing behavior due to atomic interactions [40,41]. *S*mix is employed to delineate the complicacy of electrolytes and influences the transfer and diffusion of ions within the electrolyte [42]. Energy entropy pertains to the disordered energy distribution resulting from the concentration disparity of liquid electrolytes [43]. Reactive entropy denotes the dynamic redistribution of defects/vacancies during non-equilibrium synthesis [44]. Notably, in HEMs, Δ*S*config dominates and acts as a thermodynamic driver for phase stabilization [45]. This multi-scale entropy coupling enables fine-tuning of free energy landscapes, providing critical guidance for designing metastable functional materials with tailored defect engineering [35,44,46]. Generally, the ΔSconfig of the system is positively correlated with both the categories of elements and the degree of element disorder within the lattice. Consequently, an efficacious approach to achieving entropy-driven reactions is to incorporate more elements into the material structures. More notably, as the element species in the material increase, Δ*H* drastically decreases, while Δ*S* consistently rises [29]. When a system transitions from one macroscopic state (*Ω*1) to another macroscopic state (*Ω*2), Δ*S*config is intimately correlated with the variation in the microscopic state quantity. Let *Ω* denote the number of the system’s microscopic states in a given state. Then, in the course of the transition from *Ω*1 to *Ω*2, Δ*S*config can be accurately calculated in accordance with the Boltzmann equation (Eq. (2)) [34]:

(2) ΔSconfig=kln(Ω2/Ω1)

where *k* represents the Boltzmann constant. For convenience in calculation, Δ*S*config in the solid solution can be approximately expressed as Eq. (3) [34,47,48]:

(3) ΔSconfig=−R∑i=1NCilnCi

where *R* denotes the gas constant; *N* represents the number of components; *C**i* signifies the mole fraction of the *i*th component. Therefore, it can be inferred that when the mole ratios of all components are identical, the Δ*S*config of the system will reach its maximum value [49].

Under ideal conditions, metal atoms share one or more Wyckoff positions, and the distribution of elements demonstrates randomness. Nevertheless, owing to the disparities in electronegativity and atomic radius among various elements, a selective preference emerges between adjacent elements, resulting in the formation of short-range ordered structures (SROs), which accordingly reduces the system entropy [16]. In contrast to HEAs, adjacent cations in high-entropy oxidations (HEOs) are invariably separated by intermediate anions. This distinctive structure impedes the formation of SRO, consequently increasing Δ*S*config. Additionally, the diversity of sublattice compositions in HEO systems further complicates Δ*S*config. Hence, Sarkar *et al.* [49] presented a modified equation for Δ*S*config of A*x*B*y*O*z*-type perovskite structures, which can be employed for calculation via Eq. (4):

(4) ΔSconfig=−R[x(∑a=1Mxalnxa)A-site+y(∑b=1Nyblnyb)B-site+z(∑o=1Pzolnzo)O-site]

where *xa*, *yb*, and *zo* represent the mole fractions of the cation and anion position components, respectively, and *M*, *N*, and *P* denote the numbers of each type of cation, respectively. Based on the definition of entropy, we compute Δ*S*config by the aforementioned equation and classify systems according to their numerical ranges [50]: When Δ*S*config ≥ 1.5*R*, it is a high-entropy system; when 1*R* config ≤ 1.5*R*, it is a medium-entropy system; when Δ*S*config 51] utilized sodium zirconium diphosphate NaZr2(PO4)3 (NZP) as the research starting point and successfully constructed high-entropy compositions encompassing up to eight elements on the central tetrahedral lattice of NASICON structures, with the chemical formula fixed as NaZr2(EO4)3, where E represents elements such as B, Al, Si, P, As, Sb, S, Se, and Te. With increasing species and decreasing step sizes of involved elements, the quantity of synthesized samples exhibited exponential growth. The experimental results indicated that Δ*S*config could reach a maximum of ~2.0405*R*, with two typical compounds being Na3Zr2(B0.5Al0.6Si0.4P0.3As0.3Sb0.3S0.3Se0.3)O12 and Na3Zr2 (B0.6Al0.5Si0.4P0.3As0.3Sb0.3S0.3Se0.3)O12.

The introduction of a high-entropy strategy in the design of NASICON materials represents an innovative means for enhancing battery performance and durability. Through the synergistic effect of multiple elements, HEMs demonstrate better and distinctive merits than traditional single-component composites. Owing to their diversified elemental combinations, high-entropy material-based batteries are capable of precisely tailoring the requisite electrochemical performance, holding the promise of achieving higher energy density [52], longer service life [53], and faster charging speed [10], resulting in revolutionary breakthroughs in battery technology [54].

### 1.2 Core effects and fundamental benefits of HEMs

Yeh *et al.* [17] reported that the high-entropy effect elicited by complex multi-component elements prominently modified the material properties of HEMs. This alteration is attributed primarily to four core effects: the entropy stabilization effect, the lattice distortion effect, the sluggish diffusion effect, and the cocktail effect [28,55−57] (Fig. 2(b)).

(1) Entropy stabilization effect [18,54]: As entropy predominates in thermodynamic systems, the high-entropy stabilization effect takes effect, thereby regulating the structure and phase transitions. In accordance with the Gibbs free energy principle, the increase in entropy markedly reduces the free energy of solid solutions, subsequently facilitating the formation and stability of single-phase solid solutions under high-temperature conditions and alleviating the propensity of solute crystallization. This results in more complex solute–solvent interactions and greater ion movement freedom, enhancing the salt solubility in solvents. Hence, in a high-entropy milieu, the mutual solubility among elements is clearly enhanced, and the phase structure is stabilized through increased entropy.

(2) Lattice disorder effect [58,59]: Atoms of varying sizes randomly occupy lattice positions, inevitably causing lattice distortion and forming a strong lattice strain field, which hinders dislocation slip, consequently altering the crystal structure and ultimately enhancing the mechanical properties of materials. Additionally, lattice defects such as vacancies, impurity atoms, and dislocations disrupt the periodicity of the lattice, leading to discontinuity and irregularity in the phonon propagation path, increasing the probability of phonon scattering, and subsequently weakening the X-ray diffraction intensity, thermal conductivity, and electrical conductivity.

(3) Sluggish diffusion effect [60,61]: Diverse species of atoms are randomly distributed at lattice positions. The asymmetric bondings between adjacent atoms and the local electronic structures vary with the randomly distributed lattice. Owing to the marked influence of local lattice distortion on the energy distribution between lattice positions, coupled with the disparate potential energies and diffusion rates of various elements, the effective movements of atoms are substantially impeded; accordingly, the diffusion rate clearly decelerates. This sluggish diffusion effect not only boosts the recrystallization temperature, retards the growth rate of grains, and refines the grains and precipitates nano-phase, which facilitates the maintenance of nano-crystal during manufacturing, thus enhancing processability; but also, moderate lattice distortion has been testified to modify the energy landscape of ion diffusion pathways, forming a percolation network that is beneficial for migration. Nevertheless, severe lattice distortion may lead to the collapse of the lattice structures, consequently affecting the overall performance of the materials. Notably, the degree of lattice distortion is jointly determined by multiple properties, such as the size, valence state, electronegativity, and mutual Coulombic forces of the atoms or ions coexisting in the same (sub)lattice.

(4) Cocktail effect [62−64]: The cocktail effect refers to the synergistic interactions of components and structures at diverse micro- and macro-levels. Leveraging elemental diversity, the multi-element synergy within HEMs results in more distinctive and superior properties than single-element doping. This performance enhancement is not attributable to a single component but arises from the configurational synergy within the multi-element system. Even minor alterations in elemental composition or stoichiometric ratios can trigger substantial functional transitions in the system. Consequently, in practical applications, the cocktail effect can be exploited adequately to tailor the properties of materials. For example, the Mg0.2Co0.2Ni0.2Cu0.2Zn0.2O anode system not only offered a conductive metal phase but also established an oxide framework with distinct expansion coefficients to accommodate volume variations. Elements with greater electronegativity (Cu2+, Ni2+, and partial Co2+) form a three-dimensional (3D) metal nanonetwork, ensuring sustained electronic conductivity in the charged state; moreover, the electrochemically inert Mg2+ cations stabilize an oxide nanophase, which accommodates Li+ ions while maintaining a semi-coherent interface with Co2+ and Zn2+ in the charged state. Through multi-element synergy, HEMs can induce disordered nanoscale entanglement of cations with dissimilar properties in metal oxides, accordingly increasing the stability and electrochemical performance of the anode [62]. Hence, in the design of battery materials, a thorough evaluation of optimal elemental combinations and their synergetic effects on material properties should be implemented to maximize their performance.

Zhou *et al.* [55] further expanded upon the four primary effects and elaborated on six prominent advantages, specifically: (1) Zero or low volumetric strain: HEMs effectively inhibit the lattice structure alterations and volume expansions of electrode materials during ion insertion or extraction by augmenting configurational entropy, consequently enhancing the cycling stability and service life of battery materials. (2) Suppression of adverse phase transformations: The high-entropy strategy mitigates the adverse phase transitions in battery materials during charge and discharge processes. These phase transitions frequently result in structural degradation and capacity attenuation, consequently adversely influencing battery performance. (3) Facilitation of the conversion process: HEMs facilitate reversible conversion reactions, which are of paramount importance for enhancing the cycling stability of electrode materials and the overall performance of batteries. This facilitation ensures the efficacy and durability of electrochemical reactions. (4) Construction of low-energy transport channels via lattice distortions: Lattice distortions induced by multiple-element incorporation create low-energy ion diffusion pathways, observably augmenting ionic conductivity and furnishing favorable conditions for efficient ion transport. (5) Disorder-facilitated ion diffusion: Augmented local disorders within HEMs offer additional paths for ion migration, which significantly increases the diffusion kinetics of electrode materials and renders ion transport smoother and more efficient. (6) Cocktail-induced synergistic effects: interactions among different components in HEMs promote synergistic performance. This is not merely an additive outcome but a nonlinear performance improvement achieved through the meticulous selection of elements and adjustment of stoichiometric ratios. Notably, these fundamental advantages are interdependent and coexist, collectively constituting the distinctive performance system of HEMs [13].

### 1.3 Advanced characterization techniques for HEMs

HEMs, which are expected to exhibit homogeneous single-phase structures, require advanced techniques to validate their compositional and structural complexity. Inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) can be used to quantitatively verify the near-equimolar stoichiometry of transition metals (TMs) [65], while energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) coupled with scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) can be used for nanoscale elemental mapping to detect heterogeneity or impurities [66]. Synchrotron X-ray diffraction (SXRD) resolves phase purity with sub-0.001 Å lattice resolution, confirming entropy-stabilized structures [67], while X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) probes element-specific local coordination environments for distinguishing random solid solutions from SRO clusters [68]. By combining X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analyses, XAS provides complementary insights: XANES reveals electronic properties such as oxidation states and local symmetry through absorption-edge features [69], while EXAFS quantifies structural parameters, including bond lengths and coordination numbers, for individual elements [70]. This dual capability is critical for identifying atomic-scale mixing homogeneity in multi-component systems; for example, XANES distinguishes chemical bonding variations, and EXAFS resolves subtle structural distortions. Hard X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (HAXPES) reveals bulk electronic structures (e.g., d-band centers) and correlates multi-element synergy with intermediate adsorption energies [71]. *In-situ* XRD complements these static analyses by tracking real-time structural evolution under operational conditions—for instance, resolving entropy-driven phase transitions during high-temperature annealing or electrochemical cycling [72]. *In-situ* TEM captures dynamic synthesis processes, such as rapid thermal quenching (~104 K·s−1), which stabilizes metastable phases, and degradation mechanisms during operation [72]. Four-dimensional scanning TEM (4D-STEM) maps local strain and detects 1–2 nm ordered clusters via sub-nanometer diffraction, demonstrating the potential lattice disorder and strain [73]. Atomic probe tomography (APT) provides 3D chemical mapping at near-atomic resolution, enabling direct visualization of elemental segregation and chemical correlations within nanoscale volumes, which is indispensable for resolving phase boundaries and compositional fluctuations in high-entropy systems [74].

Emerging tools, e.g., machine learning (ML), can decode complex spectral data (e.g., XANES, EXAFS) [75], while the “entropy–functionality–stability” model guides material design across battery components, linking characterization insights to performance optimization.

## 2 Crystalline structures and ion diffusion mechanism of NASICON battery materials

As demonstrated in Fig. 3(a), there are two monomer structures of NASICON materials, including a high-conductivity monoclinic structure (*C*2/*c*) and a low-conductivity hexagonal structure (*R** 3¯c*) [76]. Some *C*2/*c* structures can transform into *R** 3¯c* structures upon heating at high temperatures [77]. Na*x*MM′(XO4)3 is usually represented by the general formula of NASICON-structure materials, where *x* = 0–4, M or M' = Mn, Fe, Ti, Nb, etc. In the *R** 3¯c* structure, two MO6 or M'O6 octahedra and three XO4 tetrahedrons are interconnected via shared vertices, constructing a stable skeleton structure and 3D Na ion transfer channels, where Na ions migrate under the driving force, hence manifesting as the microscopic Na ion transport. Na ions occupy two skeleton gap positions: Na1 (6b) and Na2 (18e). With increasing Na ion concentration, the low-energy Na1 sites are preferentially occupied, while excess Na ions are randomly distributed at the low-energy Na1 sites or the high-energy Na2 sites. Currently, the *R** 3¯c* structure can be converted into the *C*2*/c* structure only by slight distortion [76].

Nevertheless, the ion diffusion/migration mechanism and the pertinent alkali ion pathway remain controversial. Anantharamulu *et al.* [1] reported that two distinct Na ion sites exist in *R** 3¯c* structures: Na1 (6b) and Na2 (18e), with Na ions transmitting along the Na1–Na2 channels. Park *et al.* [78] discovered that in *C*2/*c* structures, a third nascent Na ion site, Na3 (36f), is formed due to the splitting of Na2 sites, leading to the formation of conductive channels Na1–Na2 and Na1–Na3. Laboratory findings displayed in Fig. 3(b) show that these two different transmission channels (Na1–Na2 and Na1–Na3) can be observed in the *C*2/*c* structure of Na1−*x*Zr2P3−*x*Si*x*O12. Furthermore, Monchak *et al.* [79] used high-resolution synchrotron radiation X-ray and powder neutron diffraction to characterize and preliminarily evaluated the latent Li diffusion pathway by comparing the bond valence mismatch with the nuclear negative density map reconstructed by the maximum entropy method. The migration of Li ions in NASICON-structured Li1+*x*Al*x*Ti2−*x*(PO4)3 (LATP) may occur along the Li1 (6b)–Li3 (36f) sites. Zhang *et al.* [77] proposed a plausible 5-Na-site model to expound the sub-lattice in *C*2/*c* structures. Na atoms manifested lower atomic displacement correction parameters, which rendered the amendatory data of neutron powder diffraction more convincing. In *R** 3¯c* NASICONs, Na ions are predominantly distributed at the Na1 (6b) and Na2 (18e) sites, with a fraction occupying the Na3 (36f) sites. Upon transformation from the *R** 3¯c* to the *C*2/*c* phase, the Na2 (18e) sites differentiate into Na2 (4e) and Na3 (8f), and synchronously, the Na3 (8f) sites differentiate into Na4 (8f) and Na5 (8f). The Na5 site and other homologous sites occupied by supernumerary alkali ions in the *C*2/*c* structure serve as fast interchange sites during ion transport, which is conducive to lowering the activation energy and increasing the ionic conductivity (Figs. 3(c) and 3(d)).

On one hand, Zou *et al.* [80] elaborated that ion conduction channels in NASICON structures were highly sensitive to temperature and time and proposed strategies for enhancing the conductivity of monoclinic NASICON SEs (Fig. 3(c)). On the other hand, Hayamizu and Haishi [81] thoroughly studied the effects of Li1+*x*Al*x*Ge2−*x*(PO4)3 (LAGP) with various thicknesses (0.5–4 mm) on the diffusion behaviors of Li ions by manipulating pulsed field gradient nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) (PFG-NMR) technology. The results shown in Fig. 3(e) revealed that for long sampling time, the diffusion coefficients of Li ions in LAGPs with different thicknesses were approximately equal. However, for short sampling time, Li ion diffusion in thinner samples was more inclined to perform horizontal displacement to circumvent the influence of surface boundaries.

As an instant parameter used to assess ion diffusion kinetics, the ion migration energy barrier has been high-profile. An increase in the alkali metal concentration is beneficial for decreasing the migration distance of diffusion and reducing the migration energy barrier, consequently increasing the ion diffusion kinetics. Using NMR technology, Kuo and Du [82] verified that amplifying the substitution concentration of Al3+/Ge4+ would cause certain octahedrons of LAGP to dilate due to the elongation of Al–O bonds, shrinkage of Li–O bonds and loss of coordination number. Comparative analysis of the pair distribution function revealed that the occupancy rates of three Li sites increased, which indicated that the Li+ concentration in interstitial areas increased, thereby reducing the migration energy barrier and heightening the ion diffusion kinetics. Kang’s simulation ultimateness [83] based on density functional theory (DFT) specified that substituting Ge4+ for Al3+ in LATP could reduce the ion migration energy barrier, although the reduction degree of the energy barrier varied among various paths. Notably, the reduction in the migration barrier is not only due to the increase in the transmission channel size but also to the strong electrostatic repulsion of Li ions (18e). Ihlefeld *et al.* [84] investigated the effect of grain size on the grain boundary (GB) conductivity of Na1+*x*Zr2Si*x*P3−*x*O12 (0.25 ≤ *x* ≤ 1.0, NZSP) films. The conclusions were drawn that decreasing the Si content and increasing the sintering temperature could accelerate the average grain growth and densification of NZSP, thereby increasing the GB ion conductivity. Moreover, a few studies have elucidated the advantageous impact of secondary phases (MF, M2B4O7, M3BO3, etc., M = Na/Li) on ameliorating the carrier transport behavior over GBs [85−88]. According to the findings reported by Zhao *et al.* [87], the sintering temperature of densified Na2B4O7-doped NZSP reached 1,000 °C, with total ionic conductivity of 1.72×10−3 S·cm−1.

In general, the approaches used to improve the ion conductivity in NASICON can be summarized as follows: (1) appropriately increase the alkali ion and vacancy concentration in the matrix lattice; (2) broaden the transport channel size via introducing appropriate substitutions (this larger channel size can reduce the activation energy and energy barrier for ion transport); (3) lower the resistance at GBs by controlling grain size distribution, optimizing GB phase composition and boosting the degree of phase purity and density [6].

## 3 Preparation methods for high-entropy NASICON-type materials

The selection of synthesis methods and response parameters (temperature, time, precursor, solvent, concentration, etc.) is crucial for effectively controlling the size, morphology, pore diameter, and porosity of particles. The physical and electrochemical properties of these electroactive materials are considered pivotal determinants of cell performance. Although some synthesis methods are intricate and demand for costly raw materials, they can supply more suitable particle properties than other methods.

In this section, we briefly elaborate on the technological characteristics, advantages, and limitations of each preparation method (solid-phase method, sol–gel method, melt–quenching method, co-precipitation method, etc.). We aim to summarize and furnish more simplistic, economical, efficient, and secure composite routes from the perspective of material synthesis to achieve large-scale manufacturing of high quality SE materials with high ionic conductivity, pure phase, and uniform particle size, and electrode materials with large specific surface areas, uniformity, and high ion/electron conductivity.

### 3.1 Solid-phase synthesis method

Solid-phase synthesis method, just as its name implies, in which the reactive raw materials that participate in the reaction are typically solid substances, is one of the most broadly applied technologies for the synthesis routes of NASICON materials. This method is superior in terms of high selectivity, high yield, relatively low cost, and uncomplicated processing. Nonetheless, several shortcomings exist, such as high energy consumption, nonuniform grain size, and agglomeration propensity. These factors can affect the electrical conductivity of the products, necessitating rigorous grinding to achieve optimal performance.

#### 3.1.1 Melt-quenching method

Samples prepared with the melt quenching method involve adequately mixing raw materials in due proportion first, followed by heating in a crucible to release the volatile constituents of materials, subsequently overheating again to the melting temperature (~1400 °C) and holding for sufficient time, then quenching over the preheated brass plates (~300 °C) and waiting for slow cooling to room temperature. Owing to the high-temperature requirements of this method, both lithium (boiling point (b.p.) 1342 °C) and sodium (b.p. 883 °C) begin to volatilize above 800 °C, and the loss of Na/Li raw materials has become a nonnegligible problem. Moreover, owing to its high-temperature molten characteristics, the melt-quenching method leads to high energy consumption, large grain size, and high density. Nevertheless, the ionic conductivity of the samples synthesized by this method is relatively low, severely restricting its practical application.

#### 3.1.2 High-temperature solid reaction method/mechanical activation method

The high-temperature solid-phase reaction approach, often referred to as the mechanical activation method, involves the following steps: Raw materials are placed in a ball mill in accordance with a pre-determined ratio for comminution and homogeneous blending. A prolonged annealing process is subsequently conducted at 800–1200 °C. Finally, sophisticated grinding is implemented again in a high-energy ball mill. Owing to the repetitive sophisticated grinding of the powder, this method not only reduces the sintering temperature required for the samples (compared with the melt-quenching method) but also effectively averts the volatilization of Na/Li sources in the raw materials. Consequently, during the pellet-pressing process, powder samples with smaller particle sizes and more uniform distributions can be acquired.

The electrochemical performance of NASICON powders fabricated by the solid-phase reaction method is predominantly influenced by essentials like precursor materials, ball milling techniques, calcination modes, temperatures, and durations. For example, Na*x*V2(PO4)3 (NVP) powder prepared with Na2CO3 as the precursor exhibited a smaller size, narrower size distribution, and fewer agglomerations than those prepared with NaOH, thereby demonstrating superior rate capability [7].

The high-temperature solid-phase reaction approach is considered promising for commercial applications because of its relatively low energy consumption, simplified process flow, and smaller particle size. Nevertheless, elevated calcination temperatures and extended reaction times can result in the formation of poorly conductive secondary phases and particle agglomeration, leading to non-uniform chemical properties and reduced battery capacity at high charge/discharge rates. Nonetheless, given the increasing demand for cost-effective battery solutions in future markets, it remains a viable option with significant potential for further development.

### 3.2 Liquid-phase synthesis method

The liquid-phase synthesis approach is currently one of the frequently employed methods for synthesizing nanoscale ultrafine powders in both laboratories and enterprises. It typically involves dissolving raw materials, conducting a mixing reaction, followed by dehydration and polymerization, and ultimately acquiring the product through low-temperature calcination. Since this method does not necessitate high-temperature calcination, it results in low energy consumption; in addition, the solution can be homogeneously doped through stirring, and controllable nano-structured particles with smaller powder particle sizes are readily achievable. Consequently, the sintered products usually exhibit higher main phase purity and ionic conductivity. Nevertheless, the drawbacks of this method include the excessively high cost of raw materials (such as organic precious metal salts) and the existence of environmental issues with intermediate process products, which are unfavorable for large-scale production. Additionally, compared with the solid-phase method, the liquid-phase synthesis method is more complex.

#### 3.2.1 Sol–gel method

The sol–gel method is a frequently utilized approach in liquid-phase synthesis. The two principal chemical reactions involved in the synthesis process are the chelation reaction between complex cations and citric acid, as well as the polyester reaction of excessive citric acid and ethylene glycol. The chelation reaction ensures homogeneous ion dispersion, while the polyester reaction offers local stoichiometric stability to avoid unnecessary miscellaneous precipitation. Notably, factors such as pH, organic compound concentration, and sintering atmosphere critically influence the properties of the precursors in these two reactions. Xu *et al.* [89] reported that an increase in pH promoted the ionization of citric acid, allowing more carboxyl groups to participate in the chelation reaction, thus resulting in uniform metal ion dispersion and reduced impurity phase formation. When the concentration of organic polymers in the solution increased, the concentration of the impurity phase AlPO4 decreased (Fig. 4(a)). Nevertheless, further increases led to the emergence of numerous unknown impurity phases. Additionally, Liu *et al.* [90] reported that particle agglomeration was reduced when the dried gel was sintered in an Ar atmosphere, subsequently yielding a more refined powder sample than traditional air sintering. Materials can achieve uniform reactions at the molecular level via the sol–gel reaction process with a lower sintering temperature and a relatively smaller particle size. However, the experimental process flow of the sol–gel method is generally more intricate than that of solid-phase synthesis. Furthermore, since the sol–gel method employs toxic and flammable organic solvents, certain difficulties are associated with large-scale mass production.

#### 3.2.2 Co-precipitation method

Co-precipitation is an exquisite process wherein a precipitant interacts with two or more metal salt solutions to jointly generate solid products. In contrast to solid-phase synthesis approaches, co-precipitation does not entail high-temperature calcination conditions and is capable of fabricating precursors with high purity and small particle sizes. Compared with other liquid-phase synthesis methods, co-precipitation employs raw materials that are not only low in cost but also safe and non-toxic, showcasing significant cost advantages and environmental friendliness.

Ji *et al.* [91] reported that the reaction temperature (controlled by a water bath) and pH collectively affected the residual amount of germanium raw materials in samples. The research by Kotobuki and Koishi [92] further revealed the profound impact of alkali salts on the morphology and crystallinity of the precursor powder. When Li2C2O4 was substituted with Li3PO4, the particle sizes of the prepared precursor powders decreased, and the LATP sample exhibited lower crystallinity.

The co-precipitation method distinguishes itself from liquid-phase synthesis methods and has several remarkable advantages: a relatively straightforward preparation process, cost efficiency, the readily controllable production process conditions, and a short synthesis cycle. Although, in certain circumstances, the addition of the precipitant may bring about excessive local concentrations, ultimately triggering agglomeration or compositional inhomogeneity, and these blemishes do not overshadow its vast potential for large-scale mass production.

#### 3.2.3 Hydrothermal/solvothermal synthesis methods

The hydrothermal/solvothermal synthesis method is a widely employed wet synthesis technique that involves placing a uniformly stirred precursor solution under high-temperature, enclosed or high-pressure conditions (such as an autoclave lined with polytetrafluoroethylene) to conduct hydrothermal/solvothermal reactions. With the assistance of microwave radiation, the processing time can be dramatically reduced from several hours to just a few minutes. Factors such as dopant concentration, precursor and solvent selection, hydrothermal/solvothermal reaction time and temperature, as well as subsequent sintering process, significantly influence the morphology, crystal phase purity, crystallinity, density, and ionic conductivity of the final products.

Kim *et al.* [93] reported that there was an optimal dopant concentration for achieving the purest product; either excessive or insufficient doping would result in impurity generation. As the hydrothermal reaction time increased, the particle size of LATP gradually decreased, while the density of the material significantly increased; however, overly prolonging the reaction time might lead to particle agglomeration, thereby reducing the ionic conductivity. The size distribution of porous NaTi2(PO4)3 (NTP) nanocrystals can be regulated by adjusting the organic solvent of the reaction medium, found by Yang *et al.* [94]. In previous investigations, multiple morphologies of NTP-based negative electrode nanomaterials, such as nanocubes [95], honeycomb-like microplates [96], microflowers [97], and nanospheres [98], have been synthesized through combinations of diverse organic solvents and precursors. Yen *et al.* [99] reported a two-stage sintering method for preparing samples by using a hydrothermally synthesized precursor, and the final sintered samples could achieve a density of 99.07%. Peng *et al.* [100] explored the effects of different Al sources and dosages on LATP powder properties and reported that using Al2(SO4)3 could effectively decrease the particle size of the synthesized powder.

During the hydrothermal/solvothermal synthesis process, the reaction system lies in a non-equilibrium state, and the solvent maintains a critical or supercritical state, with the reactants exhibiting extremely high reactivity. This approach is frequently utilized for synthesizing nanoscale materials that are difficult to obtain under normal temperature and pressure conditions. This method offers advantages such as a narrow size distribution, controllable morphology, rapid reaction kinetics, high phase purity, and crystallinity. Owing to their relatively small grain size, the powders synthesized via the hydrothermal method manifested superior discharge capacity and rate performance compared with those prepared via the solid-state and sol–gel techniques. Nevertheless, the stringent reaction environment required and certain safety risks during production limit its application in large-scale commercial production.

### 3.3 Novel synthesis method

In recent years, researchers have endeavored to explore and attempt multiple advanced synthesis approaches for the fabrication of NASICON materials. These methods include spray drying [101], templating [102], direct ink writing [103], large-area pulsed laser deposition [104], aerosol freeze-drying [105], etc. Each method possesses distinct advantages and characteristics, furnishing diverse options for synthesis of NASICON materials.

For example, spray drying is a commonly employed powder preparation technique that involves atomizing a solution or suspension into minute droplets and rapidly drying them in hot air to form uniform granules. This methodology can effectively control particle size and morphology, accordingly endowing NASICON materials with higher specific surface areas and better ionic conductivity. For example, Shin *et al.* [106] fabricated Li2O–2B2O3 (LBO) core–shell-coated LATP microspheres via one-pot spray pyrolysis (Fig. 4(b)). The addition of the sintering aid LBO inhibited the formation of insulating second phases. Under the synergistic effect of the spherical morphology and core–shell structure, the total ionic conductivity reached 1.366×10−4 S·cm−1 at 900 °C, which was an order of magnitude greater than that of the uncoated LATP under the same temperature conditions. Although it is capable of producing high-quality powders, substantial equipment investment and high energy consumption impose constraints on its feasibility for industrial-scale production. The templating method exploits designated template structures to guide grain growth, resulting in highly ordered microstructures that enhance the electrochemical performance. Materials fabricated via this method typically exhibit superior mechanical strength and stability, whereas the employment of expensive template materials and the intricate removal process augment manufacturing costs. The direct ink writing method leverages 3D printing technology to precisely deposit materials at designated locations, enabling the construction of intricate structures. Despite its high precision, its slow printing speed makes it challenging to meet the requirements of large-scale production. Large-area pulsed laser deposition uses high-energy laser beams to bombard target materials, depositing high-quality thin films on substrates. This approach is applicable to the preparation of high-quality SEs and devices with complex geometries, further broadening the adhibition range of NASICON materials. However, the high cost of facilities and intricate operation render it inadequate for large-scale production. The aerosol freeze-drying method combines aerosol technology and freeze-drying technology, enabling the rapid solidification of materials at low temperatures and circumventing adverse effects from high-temperature treatment. Nonetheless, high equipment maintenance costs and low production efficiency currently limit its widespread adoption. Despite the individual merits of these aforementioned advanced synthetic methods, at present, they are confined primarily to compensating for the technical limitations of traditional methods.

In addition, sintering processes play a vital role in the crystallization and formation of single-phase substances. Research conducted by Sun *et al.* [107] indicated that as the sintering time increased, the diffraction peaks of the samples became progressively sharper and more concentrated, signifying the continuous grain growth. Nevertheless, overly long sintering durations can also facilitate the formation of secondary impurity phases, sequentially affecting the purity and performance of samples. Waetzig *et al.* [108] pointed out that under high-temperature conditions, the evaporation loss of Li/Na not only triggered phase transformation but also led to anisotropic grain growth. When the grain size exceeded a certain extent, crevices emerged within the GBs, subsequently leading to microcracks. These microcracks severely impacted the mechanical strength and ionic conductivity of materials, restricting their performances in practical applications [109,110].

To address the issue of Na/Li evaporation during sintering, approaches can generally be initiated from two aspects: expediting the heating rate and decreasing the holding time. On one hand, a series of advanced rapid sintering techniques have been developed, such as spark plasma sintering (SPS) [111,112], field-assisted sintering technology (FAST) [113,114], ultrafast high-temperature sintering (UHS) [115−119], microwave-assisted sintering (MAS) [120,121], and carbon thermal shock (CTS) [122,123]. These techniques achieve rapid heating and cooling through disparate mechanisms, effectively reducing the evaporation loss of alkali metals and enhancing the quality and performance of materials.

SPS exploits the high temperature and pressure generated by the pulsed current to accomplish the sintering process within an extremely short duration. This technique is capable of heating materials to the requisite temperature within several seconds. Owing to the rapid heating rate, the residence time of materials at high temperatures is extremely short, effectively minimizing the evaporation loss of Li/Na.

FAST achieves rapid sintering by accelerating the diffusion and combination of particles through an external electric field. This technology not only enhances the sintering efficiency but also enables sintering at relatively lower temperatures, further lowering the risk of Li/Na volatilization. Its advantages consist of the absence of costly equipment and the avoidance of potentially contaminating graphite molds. Research conducted by Rosenberger *et al.* [114] indicated that the application of a direct current voltage during the sintering process could result in elevated density, alterations in grain morphology, and abnormal grain growth, with these effects intensifying as the voltage increased. This technology simplifies operational procedures while increasing the sintering efficiency.

UHS employs a specialized heating device to heat materials to an extremely high temperature within an extremely short period and then rapidly cools them. This technique can complete the sintering of samples within seconds, suppressing the deterioration of GB impedance caused by excessive grain growth, which is frequently applied in the sintering of SEs. Research findings of Lin *et al.* [117] have suggested that as the heating rate increases and the current expands, the densification of high-temperature-sintered samples with lower activation energy also increases; nevertheless, excessive current or prolonged heating time might lead to the decomposition of carbon felts and contamination of samples. Additionally, Wu *et al.* [118] pointed out that introducing a pre-sintering procedure in the sintering process could significantly reduce the risk of ceramic plate cracking due to a rapid temperature increase during UHS. This finding implied that by optimizing process parameters, UHS technology could maintain high efficiency while increasing the sintering quality.

MAS can heat the interior and surface of materials simultaneously, achieving homogeneous and rapid heating. This method has the advantages of selective heating, directly heating polar molecules within substances, reduced heat transfer time and energy loss, and effectively lessening the evaporation loss of alkali metals. Compared with sintered via a traditional muffle furnace, MAS features low energy consumption, high relative density, and energy utilization. Unlike SPS, it does not require sintering molds, making the process simpler.

CTS realizes rapid synthesis and microstructural reconfiguration by synchronously exerting instantaneous high temperatures on the inner and outer layers of materials, lowering the risk of phase separation. This approach possesses high-temperature-driven efficient reduction and crystallization abilities, which can directly increase the reactivity of carbon sources and notably reduce the generation of intermediate products, thereby enhancing the product purity. In contrast to traditional sintering processes, CTS can reduce energy consumption by more than 80%, shorten the synthesis time from several hours to seconds, and acquire a more uniform grain size distribution. Compared with the sol–gel method, CTS does not require complex precursor preparation steps and has a simpler and more efficient process flow. Nevertheless, in comparison with conventional sintering techniques, CTS relies more on high-precision temperature control equipment and is prone to local particle agglomeration. Additionally, unlike hydrothermal methods, the applicability of CTS to thermally sensitive materials is somewhat limited [122].

On the other hand, lowering the sintering temperature below the volatilization temperature of alkali metals can be achieved by employing the cold sintering technique [124−126]. Specifically, cold sintering technology combines heat, pressure, and water, typically operating within a lower temperature range (e.g., 200–400 °C), which is significantly lower than the high temperature required for traditional sintering (commonly exceeding 1000 °C). This low-temperature environment allows low-boiling-point elements such as Li or Na to remain stable during the sintering process, preventing their volatilization or decomposition at high temperatures, consequently effectively inhibiting element loss and excessive crystal growth. The densification in cold sintering primarily occurs through a dissolution–precipitation process in a transient aqueous environment, maintaining microstructural stability and ensuring the performance and quality of the final product. Dai *et al.* [126] enabled the co-sintering of electrode materials and SE through cold sintering technology without unexpected side reactions, achieving a relative density of 99.6% and an ionic conductivity of 1.02×10−3 S·cm−1 at room temperature (Fig. 4(c)).

Although researchers have constantly endeavored to develop and apply various advanced sintering technologies, these technologies are still at a relatively immature stage and require further exploration of more appropriate sintering process parameters to meet the performance requirements of electrolytes and electrodes for solid-state batteries (SSBs). Future research should focus on optimizing process conditions, enhancing sintering efficiency and product quality while lowering costs and minimizing environmental impacts.

### 4.1 Overview of NASICON-type cathodes

In contemporary times, SIBs are increasingly recognized as promising alternatives to LIBs because of their more abundant element resources, more uniform geological distribution, lower cost, and safer traits [127]. NASICON-structured cathode materials have distinct advantages because of their robust covalent framework, high ionic conductivity, and adjustable molecular formula [128]. Nevertheless, NASICON-type cathode materials also face numerous challenges, such as low specific capacities, poor electronic conductivity, and limited energy density, which are attributed primarily to the larger ionic radius of Na ions, the predominance of 2-electron reactions in many NASICON cathodes, and the inferior reversibility of high-voltage redox couples [129]. Furthermore, these materials encounter issues such as sluggish ion diffusion kinetics and unstable crystal structures during cycling. Specifically, during Li+/Na+ ion insertion/de-intercalation, cathodes typically undergo irreversible phase transitions and concurrent migrations of TMs within structures, leading to severe structural deterioration and rapid capacity attenuation after cycling, which impedes their practical applications [130].

In recent years, multiple modification strategies have been implemented to enhance the electrochemical activities of NASICON cathodes [131,132]: (1) particle dimensional regulation: adjusting the morphology and magnifying the specific surface area to expose sufficient active sites and ameliorate the ion transmission tracks, accordingly facilitating ion diffusion [133,134]; (2) element doping: incorporating heteroatoms to construct lattice defects and alter crystalline structures to efficaciously expedite redox activity and electroconductivity [101,135,136]; (3) interface engineering: restraining electrode decomposition and electrolyte alteration to inhibit adverse interfacial reactions and extend the service life [4,137,138]; (4) material coating/composite: combining with electroconductive materials (graphene, carbon nanomaterials, etc.) to increase the superficial electroconductivity and hinder the aggregation of active materials (AMs) [139−141].

Notably, the electrochemical performance of current rechargeable LIB/SIB systems heavily rely on the properties of their cathodes. To date, the high-entropy strategy has been successfully applied to different types of cathode materials to improve their performances.

### 4.2 Applications of high-entropy strategy for NASICON-type cathodes

Adopting a high-entropy strategy involving the incorporation of multiple elements, such as Mn/Fe, which are abundant, low-toxicity, and low-cost, to replace toxic and expensive elements such as V has proven to save costs. In addition, the promotion of highly reversible redox reactions and maintenance of stable structural evolution during consecutive multi-electron transfer processes to ensure the long-term stability of electrochemical performance are two primary concerns for enhancing the energy density of NASICON cathodes [3,142].

#### 4.2.1 Promotion of energy density

Through the interposition of multi-element and the design of “ladder model reaction” electric pairs, multielectron redox reactions can be effectively triggered [2,143−146]. High entropy can activate high-potential redox centers, facilitate the conversion process, and increase the output voltage, thereby improving the energy density. TM elements such as certain electrochemically active elements (Mn [143,147], Cr [144,148], Fe [2,149], etc.) partially substituting V or something else can activate V4+/V5+ analogous redox couples for the facilitation of conversion processes, enable the realization of intermediate phases in the equilibrium state and suppress the irreversible phase transitions aroused by the redox reaction of V4+/V5+ [20] or Cr3+/Cr4+ [150] or Mn3+/Mn4+ [151] redox couples in NASICON phosphate cathode system, which concurrently promotes the working voltage induced by high entropy cocktail effect [20]. In addition, the polyanion groups, owing to their potent electronic induction effect and robust covalent bonds, display higher working voltages, accordingly prominently enhancing energy density and safety. Through the introduction of strongly electronegative F− [152], N3− [153] or P2O74− [130,154] into the polyanion groups of NASICON cathodes, leveraging their strong electronegativity induction effects can not only reduce the polarization but also increase the operating voltage and overall performance of the materials.

In 2022, Wu *et al.* [22] synthesized single-phase high-entropy NASICON phosphates Na3(Ti0.2V0.2Mn0.2Cr0.2Zr0.2)2(PO4)3 (HE-N3M2P3) and Na(Ti0.2V0.2Mn0.2Cr0.2Zr0.2)2PO4O*x* (HE-NMP). Both materials manifested phase structures similar to *R** 3¯c* of NVP. Although their redox reaction activity and electrochemical performance were not yet ideal at that time, this pioneering study established a solid theoretical and practical foundation for the subsequent synthesis of stable single-phase high-entropy NASICON-structured cathodes with excellent sodium storage electrochemical performance. Subsequently, in 2024, Zhu *et al.* [155] introduced an innovative NASICON-structured Na4Cr0.7Fe0.4Mn0.3V0.3Al0.2(PO4)3 (HE-NASICON) cathode (Fig. 5(a)). Cr, Fe, Mn, and V were employed primarily for charge compensation. Among them, Cr3+/Cr4+ and Mn3+/Mn2+ exhibited relatively high redox potentials. Furthermore, both Cr and Al can activate the multi-electron reactions of V (V2+/V3+, V3+/V4+, V4+/V5+), which is conducive to continuous and efficient redox reactions and the realization of high-voltage multi-electron reactions (Fig. 5(b)). In addition, Al contributed to stabilizing the crystal structures and suppressing the Jahn–Teller effect of Mn. Specifically, after 2000 cycles at 10 C (1.2 A·g−1), HE-NASICON exhibited a reversible capacity of ~165.0 mAh·g−1 at 0.1 C and maintained 70.5% of its initial capacity, with a volume change as low as 1.45%, indicating excellent structural stability and a long cycling lifespan (Fig. 5(c)). In 2024, Hao *et al.* [156] successfully fabricated a high-entropy-substituted sodium vanadium phosphate cathode material Na3.32V1.6Cr0.08Fe0.08Mn0.08Mg0.08Ca0.08(PO4)3 (HE-V1.6) (Fig. 5(d)). In contrast to pure NVP (p-NVP), the high-entropy substitution not only effectively activated the V4+/V5+ redox couple (Fig. 5(e)) but also exhibited remarkable sodium storage capabilities across a broad temperature range (from −20 to 60 °C) (Fig. 5(f)).

The high-entropy strategy is not only likely to reduce the bandgap, accordingly increasing the electronic conductivity but also capable of promoting multi-electron redox reactions by introducing various electrochemically active elements. For example, in 2024, Chen et al.* [52] developed a high-entropy Na3V(FeTiMnZrCuAl)0.5(PO4)3 (HE-NVP) cathode material (Fig. 6(a)). Shorter Na1–O bonds and longer Na2–O bonds formed in HE-NVP after high-entropy doping (Fig. 6(b)). The introduced electrochemically active elements such as Ti, Fe, and Mn not only facilitated multi-electron redox reactions but also enabled the reversible participation of multi-electrons within the working voltage range of 1.4–4.1 V, successfully activating six pairs of redox couples (V2+/3+ (1.6 V), Ti3+/4+ (2.2 V), Fe2+/3+ (2.5 V), V3+/4+/Mn2+/3+ (3.6 V), V4+/5+ (4.0 V)) (Fig. 6(c)), and the bandgap of NVP decreased from 3.4 to 3.0 eV, thereby attaining outstanding electrochemical performance. Specifically, HE-NVP demonstrated high discharge capacities of ~158.8 and 116.1 mAh·g−1 at 0.5 C and 5 C, respectively, and retained 95% of its capacity after 170 cycles at 2 C.

Through the meticulous fusion of high-entropy design and multi-electron transfer reactions, along with the optimization of the electron induction effect, not only can the cycling stability and rate performance of the material be prominently elevated, but it is also bestowed with exceptional electrochemical performance and durable application potential. In 2024, Ding et al.* [157] successfully accomplished a high-entropy modification strategy by introducing synergistic doping of five elements (Ga3+/Cr3+/Al3+/Fe3+/In3+) into NVP (viz. Na3V2−*x*(GaCrAlFeIn)*x*(PO4)3; *x* = 0, 0.04, 0.06, 0.08) (Fig. 6(d)), thereby activating the reversible multi-electron redox reaction of V5+/V2+. The optimal formulation Na3V1.7(GaCrAlFeIn)0.06(PO4)3 (HE-N3VM3P3) exhibited outstanding cycling stability and rate capability (90.97%@5000 cycles@30 C, 2.0–4.3 V). Moreover, in half-cell tests (1.4–4.3 V), this material provided a capacity of ~185 mAh·g−1, presenting an astonishing energy density of ~522 Wh·kg−1. Even under the full-cell configuration (1.6–4.1 V), it maintained highly competitive capacity (121 mAh·g−1) and energy density (402 Wh·kg−1), fully demonstrating its considerable application potential in SIBs. Furthermore, DFT calculations revealed that the high-entropy electrode possessed more freely mobile electrons near the Fermi level, further boosting its electrochemical performance (Fig. 6(e)).

The application of the high-entropy strategy powerfully facilitates favorable phase transitions, enabling the realization of intermediate phases in equilibrium states and rendering previously unattainable potential regulation possible. The Na3V1.8(CrMnFeZnAl)0.2(PO4)3 (HE-NVP-0.2) cathode, as displayed in Fig. 7(a), synthesized by Zhou *et al.* [158] triggered a rearrangement of different Na sites via crystal structure reconstruction, sequentially reducing the lattice mismatch between Na3(VM)2(PO4)3 and Na1(VM)2(PO4)3 phases. This accelerated the rapid phase transition of the intermediate phase, which could be distinctly verified with *in-situ* XRD and *in-situ* digital image correlation (DIC) techniques (Fig. 7(b)). This material achieved a maximum specific capacity of 119.8 mAh·g−1 through sequential V3+/V4+/V5+ redox reactions and maintained an 80% capacity retention rate after 3000 cycles at 10 C. Similarly, in 2022, the high-entropy Na3V1.9(Ca,Mg,Al,Cr,Mn)0.1(PO4)2F3 (HE-NVPF) cathode (Fig. 7(c)) synthesized by Gu *et al.* [159] exhibited outstanding electrochemical performance, with an average voltage (~3.81 V) and energy density reaching 445.5 Wh·kg−1. More importantly, the introduction of high entropy facilitated the disordered rearrangement of Na+ at the Na2 active sites, subsequently avoiding unfavorable discharge behavior in the low-voltage region and further increasing the average working voltage (Fig. 7(d)), enabling the storage of all Na ions on the high-voltage platform (Fig. 7(e)). This implies that in NASICON-type crystals with invariant active centers, previously unachievable potential regulation has become feasible, providing a novel approach to enhancing the average working voltage of SIB cathodes.

Owing to the stronger F–V bond interaction than the O–V bond interaction, fluorine-containing cathodes such as Na3V2(PO4)2F3 (NVPF) universally display a working potential exceeding 1.60 V and an energy density significantly higher than that of NVP. In 2024, Fu *et al.* [162] prepared a NASICON-type high-entropy carbon-coated Na3V1.9(Mg,Cr,Al,Mo,Nb)0.1(PO4)2F3 (NVPF-HE@C) material by the sol–gel method. The introduction of Mg, Cr, Al, Mo, and Nb enhanced the stability of the Na2 sites without altering the crystal structure of NVPF and suppressed intermediate reactions at low potentials (3.4 V) (Fig. 8(c)). This improvement further elevated the specific capacity, capacity retention rate, and Na+ diffusion coefficient of the NVPF-HE@C cathode. The fluctuation of the Na+ diffusion coefficient in the low-potential region changed from 10−14–10−10 to 10−12–10−10 cm2·s−1 (Fig. 8(d)). The initial specific capacities of NVPF-HE@C cathode at 1 C and 5 C were 128 and 82 mAh·g−1, respectively. After 800 cycles at 5 C, it still exhibited a high discharge specific capacity of 68 mAh·g−1, with a corresponding capacity retention rate of 83%. The application of high entropy provides an effective approach to improve the electrochemical performance of NVPF cathodes and holds potential applicability in energy storage applications of other electrode materials.

#### 4.2.2 Promotion of structural stability

The disordered state conspicuously enhances the redox activity of materials in electrochemical reactions, markedly elevates ionic and electronic conductivity, and accordingly, significantly ameliorates the kinetic performance of local charge transfer and diffusion during the cycling process. The HE-NVMP developed by Long *et al.* [163] effectively enhanced the occupancy of electrochemically active Na+ ions at the Na2 site (Fig. 9(a)) and extended the distance between Na2 and V atoms (Fig. 9(b)). Both of these factors are beneficial for rapid Na+ ion migration (Fig. 9(c)). Furthermore, high-entropy can reduce lattice/volumetric strain to increase structural stability of electrode under the cocktail effect of multiple elements, especially those electrochemically inactive elements. Lin *et al.* [164] verified that high-entropy doping would induce chemical disorders in cathode materials, including cation mixing and vacancy formation (the anchoring effect of cationic vacancies), which is conducive to strengthening the crystal structure stability via the entropy stabilization mechanism. In 2024, Zhang *et al.* [165] disclosed the high-entropy effect engendered by multi-element synergy within Na3.12MnTi0.9(VFeMgCrZr)0.02(PO4)3 (HE-NMTP) (Fig. 9(d)) through DFT computations and machine learning-assisted molecular dynamics simulations, which not only significantly elevated the electronic conductivity and Na-ion diffusion kinetics (Fig. 9(e)) but also effectively mitigated the voltage hysteresis resulting from Mn/Na2 intrinsic anti-site defects (IASDs) and energy dissipation (Fig. 9(f)), substantially enhancing the reversibility and stability of crystal structures.

Owing to the multi-electron reactions of electrochemically active components, considerable volume changes typically occur in batteries. Nevertheless, the high-entropy strategy enables the formation of a conductive framework with a portion of electrochemically inert elements during charge/discharge processes via the cocktail effect, effectively sustaining structural stability and minimizing volume variations. For example, in 2023, Li et al.* [20] devised a high-purity high-entropy system by the sol–gel method, specifically, Na3VAl0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2In0.2Ga0.2(PO4)3 (NVMP) cathode material (Fig. 9(g)), which exhibited a volume change of merely 1.1%, significantly lowering than that of other NASICON-structured cathode materials, such as Na3VCr(PO4)3 (5.3%), Na3VMn(PO4)3 (7.9%), Na3Cr2(PO4)3 (4%), Na3V2(PO4)3 (8%), and Na3V1.5Cr0.5(PO4)3 (6.8%) (Fig. 9(h)) [20]. This is primarily because, in this system, except for V, the remaining electrochemically active elements (such as Ga, In, Al, Fe, and Cr) remain invariant during the electrochemical process, thereby reducing volume changes. Furthermore, the designed NVMP accomplished the reversible redox reaction of the V4+/V5+ pair through consecutive V3+/V4+/V5+ redox reactions, attaining a high reversible specific capacity of 102 mAh·g−1 within the voltage range of 2.5–4.4 V vs. Na+/Na. Moreover, this material effectively inhibited the structural degradation caused by electrolyte cycling above 4.0 V, maintaining 86.8% of its capacity after 5000 cycles at 20 C and demonstrating exceptional long-term service life (Fig. 9(i)). The high-entropy effect significantly enhanced the electrode conductivity, accelerated the charge transfer and diffusion kinetics during cycling, and improved the overall performance.

In 2023, Ahsan *et al.* [10] synthesized a high-entropy Na4VFe0.6Mn0.2Cr0.1(CoMgAl)0.1(PO4)3 (NVFP-HE) NASICON material. Through *in-situ* partial substitution of V with six metals (Fe, Mn, Cr, Co, Mg, and Al) at the octahedral lattice positions, the irreversible transformation of V4+/V5+ in the high-voltage regions, which led to capacity attenuation, was observably inhibited, consequently substantially ameliorating structural reversibility, rate capability, and cycling stability. EXAFS results further revealed that interpositions of metals not only altered the lengths of the V–P and V–Na bonds—resulting in shorter V–P bonds and longer V–Na bonds in NVFP-HE (Fig. 10(a))—but also induced lattice distortion, broadened the Na–V space, accelerated the diffusion rate of Na ions within the lattice, and ultimately markedly elevated the electrochemical performance (Fig. 10(b)). Specifically, at a cutoff voltage of 4 V, the synthesized NVFP-HE cathode not only exhibited a high discharge capacity of 141.98 mAh·g−1 at 1 C but also retained a capacity of 85.77 mAh·g−1 at a current rate as high as 50 C. Moreover, after 10,000 charge–discharge cycles at 50 C, it still retained 66.7% of its initial capacity.

The high-entropy doping and intrinsic Fe vacancies allowed the HEV-NFPP cathode to form self-consistent lattice defect states, which were beneficial for regulating local variations during the charging/discharging processes (Fig. 10(f)), alleviating volumetric strain, and furnishing additional active centers, thereby boosting the rate capability (61.1 mAh·h−1 at 10 A·g−1) and cycling stability (over 15,000 cycles at 5 A·g−1) of the HEV-NFPP cathode.

#### 4.2.3 Pinning effect

TM ions tend to migrate to Na/Li sites under high voltage, which impedes Na+/Li+ transport and compromises structural stability. The characteristic “pinning” effect of metal ions with larger ionic radii on Na+/Li+ vacancies can restrain TM sliding and adverse phase transitions to structural collapse of electrode materials in high-voltage regions by increasing the configurational entropy. Currently, relevant application of high-specific-energy NASICON electrode materials have emerged [152,168]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that substituting oxygen with highly electronegative fluorine can significantly lower the covalent binding energy between TMs and oxygen. Leveraging the “pinning” effect of fluorine, this substitution notably mitigated lattice distortions and boosted material stability, thereby markedly facilitating the kinetic performances of the sodium storage reaction [169,170].

The conventional NASICON-structured cathode material NVP has an exceptionally long cycle life exceeding 2000 cycles, yet its energy density is relatively mediocre (approximately 370 Wh·kg−1). In recent years, manganese-based phosphate Na4MnCr(PO4)3 (NMCP), which features high redox potential and low cost, has emerged as one of the most promising NASICON-type cathode materials with high energy density due to its distinctive three-electron reaction mechanism. Nevertheless, the exploitation of manganese-based NASICON cathode materials with both high energy density and long-term cycling stability still confronts formidable challenges due to severe structural distortion triggered by the Jahn–Teller effect of Mn3+ during repetitive charge/discharge processes and the inherent kinetic limitations of manganese-based phosphates [8,150,151,171,172].

In 2023, Zhao *et al.* [173] proposed a dual-pinning strategy involving the coordination of anions (F−) and cations (Al3+) to address the deficiencies of NVMP materials (Fig. 11(a)), such as capacity attenuation and sluggish Na+ diffusion kinetics during cycling induced by the Jahn–Teller effect (Fig. 11(b)). This strategy clearly promoted the structural stability of the material without compromising the intrinsic advantages of Mn-based NASICON materials and simultaneously increased the migration rate of Na+, consequently increasing the specific capacity and remarkable long-term cycling stability of the material (Fig. 11(c)). In 2024, Xu *et al.* [174] synthesized a high-entropy carbon-coated NASICON-type Na3V2(PO4)2F3 (HE-NVPF@C) cathode by introducing five metal elements (Al, Zn, Mn, Cr, and Nb) into the V sites of VO4F2 octahedral structures. Research demonstrated that the dual-pinning effect of the high-entropy doping strategy effectively mitigated Jahn–Teller distortion, minimized lattice distortions during Zn2+ extraction and insertion, significantly enhanced the electronic conductivity (Fig. 11(d)), and substantially lowered the migration energy barrier (Fig. 11(e)), thereby markedly improving cycling stability (Fig. 11(f)). Specifically, the HE-NVPF@C cathode exhibited a reversible capacity of ~77.5 mAh·g−1 at 2 C and exceptional cycling stability (with a capacity loss of merely 0.0031% per cycle at 20 C for 6000 cycles). The areal capacity reached 2.17 mAh·cm−2, with capacity densities of 78.2 mAh·g−1 at 50 °C and 61.6 mAh·g−1 at −20 °C. This feasible high-entropy approach has opened novel prospects for the development of practical wide-temperature-range zinc-ion batteries (ZIBs) with long cycle lifetimes and high areal capacities.

### 5.1 Overview of NASICON-type electrolytes

As feasible substitutes for traditional organic liquid electrolytes, SEs are non-flammable, non-leaking, and non-volatile. These characteristics fundamentally address the challenges related to safety and energy density in traditional LIBs and offer novel prospects for the advancement of all-solid-state batteries (ASSBs). Through the introduction of SEs, high-voltage cathodes and lithium metal anodes become compatible, thereby substantially elevating the energy density of ASSBs. Consequently, ASSBs are perceived as vigorous contenders in the realm of next-generation energy storage devices and have elicited extensive attention from both the academic and industrial communities. In recent years, many studies have explored diverse types of SEs, including inorganic conductors, organic polymer electrolytes, and organic–inorganic composite electrolytes, with the objective of attaining more efficient and safer energy storage solutions [4,129].

NASICON SEs exhibit numerous pronounced advantages, such as favorable environmental stability (against water and oxygen), a broad electrochemical window, moderate ionic conductivity (10−4–10−3 S cm−1) at room temperature, and excellent mechanical strength. In addition, the facile sintering process, low cost, relatively low reduction potential, and comparatively high theoretical specific capacities of corresponding anodes (Na and Li) impulse themselves to be promising SE candidates (Fig. 12(a)) [76]. However, further improvements in conductivity are essential, and challenges remain regarding the high interface impedance between SE and electrode materials, as well as the formation of impurity phases at GBs during synthesis (Fig. 12(b)) [19,76,132,139,180]. Element doping and sintering aids enhance conductivity by facilitating densification through the formation of solid solutions, liquid phases or second phases during sintering [180−183]. In addition, various strategies have been used to address the imperfect solid–solid interface contact and prevent corrosions of electrodes and AMs during cycling. These include constructing intermediate buffer layers, co-sintering electrodes and electrolytes, surface coating treatments, and introducing certain amounts of liquid electrolytes to fortify wettability [19,184−188].

In the nascent stage of NASICON SEs, the ionic conductivity of the Li/Na analog system was relatively low, which was attributed primarily to the excessively small ion channels, which constrained the free migration of ions within 3D network structures. Through persistent structural refinements, effective tactics for increasing the overall ionic conductivity of NASICON-structured SEs encompass approaches such as enhancing densification, GB engineering, and single crystallization [80]. Optimizing the percolation radius by regulating the lattice network size through ion substitution can ameliorate the migration of alkali metal ions; in addition, achieving shorter transport pathways, a connected 3D network, and higher Li/Na occupancy by increasing the concentration of alkali metal ions in materials can further increase their performance [189].

The following sections will succinctly introduce how researchers improve local crystal structures through ion substitutions at different element positions and increase the concentration of Na/Li ions by introducing low-valent ions to optimize the transport path and lower the migration energy barrier, ultimately attaining a significant enhancement in ionic conductivity.

#### 5.1.1 M-site doping

Owing to the lattice limitations, the feasibility of ion substitution hinges on the compatibility of ion radii. The introduction of low-valent ions with well-matched ionic radii can increase the quantity of alkali metal ions, thereby significantly increasing the ionic conductivity [190]. Nevertheless, if ions with mismatched ion radii are incorporated, not only does this fail to increase the ionic conductivity, but it also gives rise to segregation and precipitation of the secondary hetero-phase, inducing alkali metal ions to diffuse in the bulk phase and accumulate at GBs to form space charge layers, ultimately leading to a reduction in the ionic conductivity. This phenomenon has been confirmed by the research of Kothari and Kanchan [191,192]. To date, doping modifications for NASICON have focused primarily on M sites. By leveraging high-entropy metal cation mixtures, which capitalize on the high-entropy effect and the inherent “cocktail effect” of the material, the ionic conductivity can be significantly enhanced [23,193].

#### 5.1.2 Alkali-site doping

Studies have demonstrated that an appropriate doping concentration of alkali metal ions is capable of moderately elevating the ionic conductivity of a sample, primarily by enhancing the GB conductivity [194]. When heteroatoms are introduced via ion implantation, the atoms within the material collide and are displaced by implanted ions, giving rise to a series of lattice defects, such as vacancies, interstitial atoms, and dislocations [195]. These defects not only disrupt the original crystal structure and influence the atomic arrangement and symmetry but also potentially alter the local stress field. Additionally, the implanted ions may form new compounds or solid solutions with the original atoms, modifying the phase composition and distribution, which further affects the microstructure of the material. The extremely intense mechanical stress induced by ion implantation can not only effectively deflect the growth path of dendrites but also attract residual stress and significantly increase the mechanical strength of the material [195,196]. Notably, the modification of a material’s microstructure by ion implantation is a complex process that is comprehensively influenced by multiple factors, such as energy, dosage, types of implanted ions, and intrinsic properties of the material.

#### 5.1.3 Anionic site doping

Researchers have endeavored to substitute polyanions or oxygen within polyanions with other elements to optimize material properties [162,174]. Kizilaslan *et al.* [197] and Ahmed *et al.* [198] found that when S, which possesses relatively weak electronegativity, replaced O, the electrostatic interaction between Li and S was weaker than that between Li and O, leading to an increase in the bond length. The incorporation of S attenuated the restraint exerted by the PO4 tetrahedron on Li ion migration, thereby diminishing the activation energy barrier for migration. Furthermore, the introduction of S augmented the volume of the LiO6 octahedron, broadening the ion transport pathways and contributing to an overall escalation of conductivity. Li *et al.* [199] and Kang *et al.* [200] reported that when Cl was substituted for O, the coordination environment of Li+ underwent alteration, facilitating its transportation within the lattices. The bond length increased after Cl doping, increasing the grain size and rendering the particles more compact; concurrently, the distances between Al/Ti and O1/C1 and between Al/Ti and O2/Cl2 decreased, while the distance between Li1 and O2/Cl2 increased, promoting Li+ transport. Miao *et al.* [201] revealed that when F−, which features a smaller ionic radius and stronger electronegativity, was substituted for O, the NASICON-structured lattice expanded, widening the ion transport paths. To maintain electrical neutrality, F− substitution for O2− increased the concentration of interstitial Li+ and enhanced the ionic conductivity of the doped samples. Simultaneously, F doping could effectively reduce the formation energy of Li vacancies, facilitating their rapid formation and further lowering the diffusion barrier of Li+ [202].

#### 5.2.1 Lattice distortion

Botro and Janek [193] indicated that the lattice distortions induced by high entropy not only hinged upon the radius of the selected ions but were also intimately associated with the Coulombic interactions between them and adjacent ions, as well as with alkali metal ions. The local distortions resulting from the introduction of HEMs gave rise to the overlapping of the site-energy distribution of alkali metal ions, forming a percolation network with minimal energy disparity, therefore conspicuously reducing the activation energy for transport. Proverbially, ionic conductivity is not predicated on the average migration rate between all site pairs but is rather determined by the swiftest percolation pathway of ions within the system. Therefore, ions percolate rapidly along energetically favorable paths in high-entropy lattices, accordingly escalating the ionic conductivity.

Nevertheless, doubts have been raised that concurrently with the augmentation of configurational entropy after doping, local Coulombic interactions and induction effects also intensify [203]. The increase in the bond intensity leads to an increase in the lattice strength, signifying an escalation of the activation energy barrier, which may impede the enhancement of ionic conductivity in NASICON SEs [204,205]. To address this concern, research conducted on NASICON SE Li1−3*x*Ga*x*Zr2(PO4)3 by Duan *et al.* [206] in 2021 revealed that as the Ga3+ content increased, the configurational entropy also rose. However, owing to the inductive effect, the reinforcement of P–O bonds in the compound proceeded in tandem with the intensification of Li–O bond interactions, eventually resulting in an increment in the activation energy barrier and a higher energy cost demanded for transitions. Although this seemingly contradicts the boosted ion conductivity—where stronger bonds imply higher lattice intensity and higher energy barriers during transitions—both two competing factors comply with the Meyer–Neldel rule [207]. At this juncture, configurational entropy assumed a dominant position, and the augmented entropy compensated for the elevated activation energy barrier, ultimately attaining higher ionic conductivity.

In 2022, Zeng *et al.* [23] further investigated this issue and discovered that the introduction of diverse elements triggered short-term structural distortions in the crystals, thereby modifying the activation energies for ion diffusion. On one hand, under the influence of high-entropy metal cations, in materials featuring 2/3D structures, the probability of energy variance between adjacent sites can be approximated, facilitating ion transitions. On the other hand, these disorders might augment the site-energy difference between adjacent sites in some channels of one-dimensional conductors, generating certain high-energy barriers. These intricate interactions reveal the delicate equilibrium of the ionic conduction mechanism in HEMs.

#### 5.2.2 Promotion of interfacial stabilization

The high interfacial resistance and the formation of irregular dendrites severely impede the practical application of NASICON SEs. The main causes include (1) inadequate contact at the rigid solid–solid interface; (2) the generation of insulating side reaction products, such as carbonates; (3) the emergence of microscopic pores within the material due to element volatilization at high temperatures, which reduces material density and makes these microscopic defects prone to inducing uneven dendrite growth under the local high current density, ultimately resulting in short circuits; (4) after interfacing with electrodes, owing to the space charge layer effect and the intricate electrochemical–mechanical environment, probabilistic oxidation and decomposition reactions of AMs in electrodes and SEs [6,208].

On the cathode side, a common methodology for enhancing interface contact is the introduction of liquid-based wetting agents, among which ionic liquids (ILs) have garnered significant attention owing to their remarkable non-flammability, non-volatility, and thermal and electrochemical stability [209]. Another efficacious approach is the composition of cathode materials with SEs, followed by ensuring favorable interface contact between them through a co-sintering process [210,211]. On the anode side, interface contact is frequently augmented by introducing an intermediate buffer layer (such as the PVDF-HFP/LiTFSI buffer layer employed in Das’ experiment [212]) or via *in-situ* construction of an ion/electron hybrid conducting layer and other means, such as building up composite negative electrodes/alloy negative electrodes [186,213−215]. Ideally, high-entropy doping can lead to the generation of advantageous secondary phases at GBs, which are capable of withstanding the stress–strain variations during Na/Li deposition and detachment processes on account of their outstanding Young’s modulus, prominently enhancing the interface contact while effectively resisting dendrite growth.

The surface carbonate by-products can be effectively processed and decomposed using some facile annealing processes. Gao *et al.* [216] presented a straightforward annealing approach, which could not only successfully decompose the carbonate by-products on the surface of NZSP but also form a more Na-affinitive Na+-deficient surface. After this treatment, the Na/NZSP interface resistance clearly decreased (from 13,100 to 636 Ω·cm2).

The introduction of multiple components into the grain structure can clearly inhibit the phase expansion/contraction and transition metal migration, thereby retarding the crack propagation induced by high-intensity stress. Previous studies have shown that the highly anisotropic thermal expansion of lattice parameters *a* and *c* leads to intergranular cracking during sintering and cooling [212]. On one hand, experiments conducted by Das *et al.* [212] revealed that high entropy, especially the addition of neighboring valence elements, could mitigate anisotropic thermal expansion, further facilitating the generation of crack-free sintered SEs and effectively impeding dendrite growth. On the other hand, the research by Fu and Ferguson [217] indicated that high-entropy SEs could achieve densification at relatively low sintering temperatures, which was conducive to enhancing the density of SEs and thereby increasing the ionic conductivity. NASICON SEs usually necessitate sintering at high temperatures (> 1200 °C), and the ionic conductivity of oxide electrolytes (10−4–10−3 S·cm−1) is typically one order of magnitude lower than that of sulfide electrolytes (10−3–10−2 S·cm−1) [4,218,219]. In 2023, Sun *et al.* [220] discovered that the high-entropy strategy could reduce the porosity of GB particles at lower sintering temperatures, increase the GB density, and lower their resistance (Fig. 13(a)). They successfully fabricated a high-entropy hexagonal prismatic structure Na4.9Sm0.3Y0.2Gd0.2La0.1Al0.1Zr0.1Si4O12 (HE-SYGLAZ) at a relatively low sintering temperature (950 °C) through a two-step solid-state reaction method. Research findings revealed that sintering at 950 °C for 25 h was sufficient for densifying GBs, and further increasing the sintering temperature (1000 °C) or prolonging the sintering time (35 h) did not notably enhance the density and ionic conductivity (Fig. 13(b)). Under the synergistic effect of multiple elements, the GB resistance of HE-SYGLAZ was significantly lower than that of undoped Na5YSi4O12 at the same synthesis temperature, and the total ionic conductivity was also enhanced. Moreover, the narrow electronic band gap at the GBs and other defects leads to electron accumulation in these regions, accelerating dendrite growth. Some metal ions with larger ionic radii and lower electronegativity (Pr3+, Eu3+, Lu3+, etc.) not only broaden the migration paths of alkali metal ions but also generate more available vacancies to achieve charge balance, resulting in a broader band gap and lower electronic conductivity (Fig. 13(c)), effectively impeding electron transfer at GBs and suppressing the growth of dendrites (Fig. 13(d)) [221]. In addition, the disorders in the crystal structures resulting from the high-entropy strategy can avert unfavorable chemical reduction reactions that may occur upon physical contacts with Li/Na metal. Experiments by Das *et al*. [212] and Lu *et al*. [222] revealed no obvious asymmetry or splitting via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) before and after contact with Li, indicating the excellent chemical stability of the synthesized high-entropy SE upon contact with Li.

#### 5.2.3 Boosting ion transport

The ionic conductivity is jointly determined by the ionic concentration and mobility. Notably, both the ionic concentration and mobility in crystalline materials are markedly influenced by the vacancy concentration and the size of the ionic diffusion bottleneck [223,224]. The enhancement mechanism of ionic conductivity due to the high-entropy effect can be analyzed in conjunction with the following points:

(1) Diversification of ionic diffusion paths: The high-entropy effect reinforces the diversification of ionic diffusion paths and generates more available vacancies for achieving charge balance. In the case of multi-metal ion insertion, a disordered arrangement emerges in the lattice, enabling ions to migrate in multiple dimensions and at various sites via different channels and pathways, thereby reducing the mutual hindrance among ions, effectively impeding electron transfer, and signally enhancing the ionic migration rate [55,175].

(2) Reduction in the ion binding energy (activation energy): The high-entropy effect leads to a decrease in the ion binding energy (activation energy). In crystals with long-range ordered structures, ions tend to form stable arrangements, resulting in increased ion binding energy. Conversely, in the short-range disordered state of high entropy, local distortions cause the potential energy distributions of alkali metal ions to overlap, forming a percolation network with smaller energy differences. Consequently, the ion binding energy reduces, accelerating ion dissociation and migration. Ions in high-entropy lattices can move rapidly along the swiftest percolation pathways, thereby significantly augmenting ionic conductivity [23,193].

(3) Enhancement of configurational entropy and microstate diversity: Under the high-entropy effect, owing to the abundance of atomic or ionic substitution sites, the system exhibits higher configurational entropy and more complex microstates, promoting disordered movements of ions and facilitating ion transfer, thereby manifesting higher rate capacities [44,225−229].

In brief, the high-entropy strategy not only increases the ionic conductivity of SEs by more than an order of magnitude but also significantly improves the cycling stability of batteries.

Zeng *et al.* [23] devised three non-filled high-entropy materials: Li(Ti,Zr,Sn,Hf)2(PO4)3 (LTZSHPO), Na(Ti,Zr,Sn,Hf)2(PO4)3 (NTZSHPO), and Li3(La,Pr,Nd)3(Te,W)2O12 (LLPNTWO), representing Li-NASICON, Na-NASICON, and Li-garnet frameworks, respectively. By calculating the site-energy differences upon doping with different metals, it was discovered that local disorders resulting from the doping of these high-entropy metal cations effectively facilitated site percolation via overlapping site-energy distributions and conspicuously enhanced the ionic conductivity. Guided by this theory, a novel high-entropy oxide inorganic NASICON SE Na3.5Mg0.1Sc0.15In0.15Ti0.3Hf0.3ZrSi2PO12 was successfully synthesized, with a total conductivity reaching 1.1 mS·cm−1 and a bulk conductivity of 3.3 mS·cm−1 at room temperature.

Das *et al.* [212] developed a high-entropy Li-filled NASICON SE [Li1.3Sn1.7/3Zr1.7/3Ti1.7/3Al0.1Sc0.1Y0.1(PO4)3] (HE-LZST), which manifested a total ionic conductivity of 1.42×10−4 S·cm−1 (the highest value reported in NASICONs containing Zr–Sn–Ti) and a low activation energy of 0.33 eV. The relative density of sintered particles reached 94%. Symmetric cells with a PVDF-HFP/LiTFSI buffer layer exhibited stable performance for 500 cycles at 0.2 mA·cm−2; full cells using LiFePO4 retained 99% capacity after 100 cycles at 1 C; while full cells using NMC 811 delivered a capacity of 140 mAh·g−1 at C/3. Owing to the introduction of hetero-valent atoms, the 36f sites were allocated to the additional lithium ions, while the Li occupancies at the 6b and 36f Wyckoff positions were variable. *Ab initio* molecular dynamics (AIMD) studies have demonstrated that the “push-out” mechanism accounts for the high ionic conductivity of the lithium-rich NASICON SE [212,230,231]. When additional Li+ ions are introduced, this mechanism results in strong electrostatic repulsion among positively charged Li+ ions, compelling new Li+ ions to move away from neighboring ions and surmounting the binding energy, which is typically maintained near Li+ ions in place. This displacement initiates a domino effect, whereby displaced ions are likewise repelled by their adjacent ions, thereby effectively ejecting them from their initial positions [212,230,231].

In 2025, Lu *et al.* [222] successfully synthesized the high-entropy NASICON-type material Li1.3Al0.4Ti0.5Zr0.5Sn0.5Ta0.1(PO4)3 (LATZSTP) (Fig. 13(e)) to address the issue of Ti reduction with an ionic conductivity of 1.25×10−4 S·cm−1. The LiFePO4/LATZSTP/Li system demonstrated an initial capacity of 143 mAh·g−1 and a retention rate of 90.4% after 100 cycles, which was attributed to the formation of non-electron-conducting Al–O6, Zr–O6, and Ta–O6 octahedra within the framework (Fig. 13(f)), effectively blocking electron conduction and preventing Ti reduction (Fig. 13(g)).

## 6 High-entropy NASICON-type anodes

Current research on SIB anode materials primarily focuses on four categories: carbon-based, titanium-based, organic, and alloy systems. Among carbon-based materials, graphite has significant limitations in sodium storage applications because of the substantial ionic radius disparity between Na+ (0.102 nm) and Li+ (0.069 nm). This dimensional mismatch induces interlayer expansion during Na+ intercalation/deintercalation, leading to thermodynamic instability of graphite intercalation compounds. Furthermore, alloy anodes face challenges of structural pulverization during cycling, while metallic Na/Li anodes raise safety concerns owing to their high reactivity and low melting points. Notably, the overwhelming majority of sodium-free anode materials undergo irreversible capacitance loss during the pre-forming process, as a considerable quantity of Na+ is consumed to form a stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer during full battery activation, whereas NASICON anodes are not constrained by this predicament. NASICON-type frameworks exhibit structural resilience (+ channels with +), which have emerged as promising anode candidates [132,232].

NTP, NZP, and NVP are regarded as the most promising anode materials within the current NASICON system [132]. An ideal NASICON anode material should possess the following attributes: a low working voltage, high specific capacity, stable structure (with minor volume deformation), high initial coulombic efficiency, and competitive energy density. Nevertheless, because of the insufficient conductivity of NVP and NTP particles, certain conductive agents (such as graphene, ordered mesoporous carbon, and carbon shells) are typically added for coating modification to enhance their performance [132].

While high-entropy design strategies remain unexplored in NASICON anodes, recent advances in HEOs suggest potential synergistic benefits [62]. For example, in comparison with oxide anodes without multiple elements, the cycling stability, overall energy density, and efficiency of HEOs have increased, which benefits from the moderate introduction of electrochemically inactive elements (e.g., Mg, Al, and Ti) in structures to sustain conductive frame stability, metal elements with low redox potential to promote operating voltage and energy density, polyvalent state elements, mostly with high valences (e.g., Mn, V, and Mo), to stimulate more oxygen vacancies for electron transmission, and selective elements with feeble bonds to mitigate the high-voltage hysteresis of reversible electrodes.

## 7 Challenges and prospects

Current research indicates that the high-entropy strategy application in battery design remains in its nascent stages, with no established guidelines beyond empirical knowledge. Critical analysis has confirmed that the configuration entropy induced by multi-element incorporation directly governs the electrochemical performance through synergistic mechanisms because the valence state, atomic radius, and element electronegativity exert varying degrees of impact [13,56,233]. To advance this field, systematic investigations must resolve how chemically tunable parameters (element selection, elemental stoichiometry, doping sites, etc.) regulate thermodynamic metastability windows and Na+/Li+ migration barriers in model systems such as high-entropy NASICON-type materials. Such efforts will establish predictive composition–property relationships, which are essential for optimizing cocktail effects and lattice distortion engineering.

While the high-entropy effect demonstrates significant advantages, it also has inherent limitations that require systematic consideration. During prolonged heat treatment, owing to the existence of multiple components in HEMs, their thermal/chemical/electrochemical stability is influenced by the intricate interactions among components, and thermodynamic metastability might result in phase separation or undesired ordered transitions [44]. For example, Huang *et al.* [234] demonstrated that local ordering in a RuPtPdFeCo HEA could be controlled by modulating the Ru concentration, enabling switchable configurations between Ru cluster segregation and ordered Ru network formation in multicomponent systems. Processing challenges include inevitable microstructural defects (e.g., dislocations, stacking faults, and GBs) formed during synthesis or operation [235]. These defects often act as kinetic bottlenecks, particularly at GBs, where the lattice mismatch between adjacent grains creates high-energy states. To reduce GB energy, solute/impurity atoms may segregate at GB regions, forming undesired secondary phases that degrade the performance [236]. Advanced synthesis strategies such as UHS [237] or CTS [122,123], coupled with nano-structuring approaches [236,238], can suppress atomic diffusion and delay phase separation/GB segregation.

Furthermore, high-entropy designs may compromise functional properties (e.g., capacity, mechanical strength, and magnetic performance) [239]. To address this trade-off, element-specific doping (e.g., Al or Si) can be strategically introduced to compensate for functional losses while maintaining high configurational entropy. This balanced approach enables synergistic optimization of entropy-driven stability and target functionalities [44].

The design principles of HEMs in NASICON-type cathodes, anodes, and electrolytes exhibit intrinsic commonalities, all of which stem from the fundamental logic of entropy-driven stabilization and multi-component synergy. This is because the application of high-entropy strategies in cathodes, anodes, and electrolytes is an extension based on four core effects and six fundamental advantages. Specifically, (1) the performance is enhanced through the “cocktail effect” of multi-element systems. Cathodes and anodes achieve high capacity and structural stability by adopting high-entropy strategies, while electrolytes optimize ionic conductivity and interface compatibility through multi-anion synergy. The component screening framework developed in cathode entropy engineering (such as ML-guided element selection) can accelerate the development of entropy-stabilized SEs. (2) Promotion of thermodynamic stability via configurational entropy. Cathodes stabilize the framework during cycling by suppressing cation disorders, anodes mitigate volume expansion through entropy stabilization, and electrolytes inhibit dendrite growth by attaining entropy-maximized GB configurations. The kinetic stability mechanisms verified in electrolytes (such as the slow diffusion effect at high-entropy interfaces) can inspire new stabilization strategies for conversion-type cathodes. (3) The inherent defect tolerance of high-entropy systems enables tailored functionality. Cathodes and anodes prolong the cycle life through stress buffering induced by lattice distortions. High-entropy SEs suppress vacancy aggregation to improve ion transport. The defect engineering approaches for high-entropy anodes (such as entropy-guided vacancy ordering) can guide the design of entropy-driven defect-tolerant cathodes. In the future, the “entropy–functionality–stability” triangle model established for cathodes can be extended to electrolyte/anode systems.

#### 7.1.1 Element selection

The electronegativity matching between adjacent crystallographic sites is critical for maintaining charge neutrality, as excessive electronegativity results in electron transfer, leading to the acquisition or loss of electrons and subsequent variations in the valence state. Nonetheless, in certain circumstances, this electron transfer can be triggered purposefully to tailor the electron density at lattice sites, accordingly modulating the functional traits of the target material. For example, Liang *et al.* [240] substituted Co with various weakly electronegative TM cations, and the resulting charge transfer increased the O electron density in the TM–O octahedral configuration. The results proved that highly disordered cations propelled the lattice oxygen anchoring and the low electronegativity of TM species promoted charge transfer. This enhancement led to a specific capacity improvement to 203.6 mAh·g−1. Therefore, when designing material components, the electronegativity of dopants or substitution sites needs to be precisely tailored based on the functional characteristics of the target material.

Elements can be screened based on their different electrochemical properties. Electrochemically positive ions such as Fe3+, Co3+, Ni2+ Cu2+, and Mn2+ can react as redox activation sites to boost electrode capacity, while electrochemically negative ions such as Mg2+, Sn4+, Ti4+, and Mn4+ can react as structural stabilizers to boost cycling stability [55,241,242]. Notably, the electrochemical performance of identical elements varies in distinct electrode systems (such as layer oxides and Prussian blue analogs) and distinct battery systems (such as sodium and zinc ions) [243−245].

In addition to their electrochemical reactivity, the properties of their crystal field compatibility, such as bonding and conductivity properties, should also be considered when determining their material molecular dynamics [246,247]. The key parameters include ion radius, Fermi level, valence electron density, and other closely related factors [14,248−250]. For example, doped/substituted ions at identical lattice positions usually have homogeneous ionic radii because an appropriately similar ionic radius range is conducive to the formation of lattice distortions, facilitating alkali metal ion/vacancy disarrangement and kinetics. Since distinct differences in ionic radii may trigger superfluous lattice deformation, severely degrading performance can even lead to final structural collapse [155,250].

In the end, commercial viability mandates simultaneous evaluation of elemental abundance, the cost, recyclability, and toxicity thresholds of selected elements.

#### 7.1.2 Stoichiometric proportions of components

Non-equimolar stoichiometric deviations of dopant/substitutional elements significantly modulate configuration entropy while maintaining constant elemental diversity, thereby inducing measurable property differences through d-orbital hybridization gradients and cationic disorder transmission.

#### 7.1.3 Doping sites

TM site dopant/substitution can efficaciously alleviate adverse phase transitions during Na+/Li+ intercalation/de-intercalation through the synergistic effect of diverse elements, broaden the Na+/Li+ ion transport channel, and facilitate the convertible storage of Na+/Li+, thereby ameliorating the overall structural resilience, moisture stability, and mechanical properties of cathode materials [248].

Alkali metal sites are predominantly substituted by Mg2+, Ca2+, and K+ [55,251−254] and demonstrate dual functionality. Compared with TM sites, on one hand, these substitutions exhibit a characteristic “pillar” effect through strong M–O bonds by suppressing TM sliding and adverse phase transitions to maintain interlayer spacing during cycling. On the other hand, dopants/substitutions at alkali metal sites induce Na+/Li+ site disorders, which create percolation networks that reduce migration barriers and, in turn, increase the migration rate.

Polyanion sites modify material properties by altering the binding energy between elements, thereby suppressing the oxygen vacancy formation and mitigating Jahn–Teller distortions. Superseding O by F has been proven to decrease the TM–O covalent binding energy, accordingly mitigating lattice structural transformations and increasing stability [248].

Dual-site doping synergistically enhances ion diffusivity while restricting adverse phase transitions through entropy-mediated stabilization [173].

### 7.2 Selection of preparation method

Compared with conventional synthetic routes, innovative techniques yield enhanced performance metrics. Three emerging approaches (non-equilibrium plasma-enhanced carbothermal shock synthesis [255], flash Joule thermal processing (FJTP) [256,257], and UHS [115,116]) have been successfully implemented for fabricating HEMs with tailored microstructural features. These methods circumvent the thermodynamic limitations of equilibrium synthesis by enabling rapid phase formation, atomic-scale mixing homogeneity, and metastable state preservation through extreme thermal gradients. Crucially, they facilitate precise control over GB engineering and defect density modulation—key determinants of ionic/electronic transport in energy storage materials. The scalability and energy efficiency of these synthesis routes position them as transformative strategies for the industrial-scale production of high-entropy battery materials (HEBMs).

### 7.3 Theoretical calculation approaches

Current computational frameworks for HEMs predominantly rely on DFT [258−260] and AIMD [261−263], which necessitate extensive data processing to screen and optimize material compositions. While these methods effectively assess structural stability and phase homogeneity in conventional systems, their application to HEMs remains constrained by the inherent challenges of modeling highly disordered configurations and multi-element interactions. This limitation underscores the urgent need for advanced computational paradigms tailored to the structural complexity of HEMs.

Recent advances have introduced innovative methodologies, including cluster-expansion Monte Carlo (CEMC) simulations [264], ML-driven property prediction [265], high-throughput computational screening (HTC) [266−268], and special quasi-random structure (SQS) modeling [269]. Although techniques such as HTC, ML, and SQS—which inherently depend on robust foundational datasets—have been validated primarily in HEAs, their extension to HEMs has promising potential for the rational design of HEBMs [270−281]. Notably, ML-based frameworks enable accelerated identification of entropy-stabilized phases by correlating configurational entropy with electrochemical descriptors [282], while SQS models provide atomistically representative supercells for simulating disorder-induced property enhancements [283].

Three critical pathways have emerged for advancing computational capabilities: (1) refinement of first-principle theories to account for entropy-dominated phase equilibria in multi-component systems; (2) algorithmic optimization of existing methods (e.g., hybrid DFT-ML workflows) to mitigate computational costs while preserving predictive accuracy; (3) development of multiscale modeling architectures that synergize quantum mechanical precision with mesoscale kinetic simulations. Addressing these challenges will establish a robust computational foundation for the discovery of entropy-engineered materials, ultimately bridging the gap between theoretical prediction and experimental realization in HEBM development.

## 8 Conclusions

The high-entropy strategy has led to novel trials for the development of high-performance NASICON battery materials. The use of high-abundance, low-toxicity, and low-cost alternative elements (such as toxic and costly metals such as V) can reduce material costs and enhance environmental friendliness. The high-entropy strategy, by introducing multiple elements with distinct properties, significantly augments configurational entropy, thereby achieving customized enhancements in battery material performance and manifesting unprecedented application potential.

In this review, we delve into the effects of high-entropy or compositional distortions on the electrochemical properties of materials, highlighting their advantages, with the aim of helping readers fully understand the high-entropy strategies. For the first time, recent advances in high-entropy strategies for NASICON-type battery materials were collated and analyzed in detail, involving cathodes, anodes, and electrolytes, revealing their tremendous potential in enhancing battery performance.

Cathodes: NASICON-structured polyanion cathode materials demonstrate remarkable advantages because of their sturdy covalent framework, high ionic conductivity, and tunable molecular formula. Nevertheless, owing to the large ionic radius of sodium ions, the predominance of two-electron reactions in numerous NASICON cathodes, poor reversibility of high-voltage redox pairs, sluggish ion diffusion kinetics during cycling, and unstable crystal structures, NASICON cathodes encounter certain issues, such as low specific capacity, low conductivity, and low energy density, thereby impeding their practical applications.

(1) High-entropy strategy is capable of activating high-potential and multi-electron redox couples, facilitating favorable conversion processes (such as the equilibrium states of some hard-to-attain intermediate phases), and suppressing unfavorable phase transitions; it can also narrow the bandgap and enhance the electronic conductivity, thereby increasing the output voltage and significantly increasing the energy density. By introducing strongly electronegative F− or N3− into polyanion-based NASICON cathodes, with the aid of the strong electronegativity induction effect, not only can the working voltage be further elevated, but also the overall performances of the material can be enhanced.

(2) High-entropy strategy reinforces the crystal structure stability of cathode materials through multiple effects, such as vacancies, pinning effects, the entropy stabilization mechanism, and the cocktail effect, etc., and can minimize the lattice/volume strain resulting from multi-electron reactions, thereby attaining excellent cycling stability. The high-entropy effect generated by the synergistic effect of multiple elements not only broadens the sizes of ion transport channels, shortens the distance of ion transport paths, and significantly improves the electronic conductivity and ion diffusion kinetics, but also effectively alleviates voltage hysteresis and energy dissipation.

Electrolytes: NASICON SEs exhibit numerous pronounced advantages, such as favorable environmental stability (against water and oxygen), a broad electrochemical window, moderate ionic conductivity (10−4–10−3 S·cm−1) at room temperature, and excellent mechanical strength. In addition, the facile sintering process, low cost, relatively low reduction potential, and comparatively high theoretical specific capacities of the corresponding anodes (Na and Li) make them promising SE candidates. Nevertheless, issues such as high interfacial impedance and irregular dendrite growth still constrain the enhancement of the ion conductivity of NASICON SEs, and further amelioration is urgently required.

(1) The high-entropy effect is capable of markedly suppressing phase expansion/contraction and the migration of TMs within grain structures, as well as retarding crack propagation induced by high-intensity stress, further facilitating the generation of crack-free sintered SEs and effectively impeding dendrite growth.

(2) The high-entropy effect can decrease the porosity of GB particles at lower sintering temperatures, increase the GB density, and reduce the GB resistance.

(3) The disorders within crystal structures resulting from the high-entropy effect can avert adverse chemical reduction reactions that may occur upon physical contact with reactive metals such as Li/Na.

(4) The high-entropy effect reinforces the diversification of ionic diffusion paths and generates more available vacancies for achieving charge balance. In the case of multi-metal ion insertion, disorderly arrangement emerges in the lattice, enabling ions to migrate in multiple dimensions and at various sites via different channels and pathways, thereby reducing the mutual hindrance among ions, effectively impeding electron transfer, and signally enhancing the ionic migration rate.

(5) The high-entropy effect leads to a decrease in the ion binding energy (activation energy). In crystals with long-range ordered structures, ions tend to form stable arrangements, resulting in increased ion binding energy. Conversely, in the short-range disordered states of high-entropy, local distortions cause the potential energy distributions of alkali metal ions to overlap, forming a percolation network with smaller energy differences. Consequently, the ion binding energy decreases, accelerating ion dissociation and migration. Ions in high-entropy lattices can move rapidly along the swiftest percolation pathways, thereby significantly increasing the ionic conductivity.

(6) Under the high-entropy effect, owing to the abundance of atomic or ionic substitution sites, the system exhibits higher configurational entropy and more complex microstates, promoting disordered movements of ions and facilitating ion transfer, thereby manifesting higher rate capacities.

In brief, the high-entropy strategy not only increases the ionic conductivity of SEs by more than an order of magnitude but also significantly improves the interface stability of batteries during cycling.

Anode: The overwhelming majority of sodium-free anode materials undergo irreversible capacitance loss during the pre-forming process, as a considerable quantity of Na+ is consumed to form a stable SEI layer during full battery activation, whereas NASICON anodes are not constrained by this predicament. However, NASICON anode materials exhibit poor electronic conductivity and often demand further modification. While high-entropy design strategies remain unexplored in NASICON anodes, recent advances in HEOs suggest potential synergistic benefits. Leveraging the principal effects and fundamental advantages of high-entropy is anticipated for the development of high-performance NASICON anodes.

This review thoroughly expounds the notable influence of the high-entropy strategy on the internal lattice structures, interfaces, transport kinetics, and ionic conductivity of materials. It identifies challenges currently encountered in the domain of NASICON-type battery design based on the high-entropy strategy. While envisioning future technological breakthroughs, it presents objective and impartial evaluations as well as pragmatic and feasible evolutionary proposals.
