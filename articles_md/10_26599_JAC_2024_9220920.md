## 1 Introduction

Dielectric capacitors are core components in electronic devices and electrical power systems that take advantage of their ultrahigh specific powers, high breakdown strengths and excellent reliability [1−3]. Recently, due to their large polarization, ferroelectric oxide films, especially those with perovskite structures, have exhibited very high energy densities and hence have received much research interest [2−4]. To evaluate the energy storage performance of a ferroelectric material, the charged energy density (*W*c), the discharged or recyclable energy density (*W*rec) and the energy efficiency (*η*) can be obtained from the following equations: * Wc=∫0PmaxEdP,  Wrec=∫PrPmaxEdP, and  η=Wrec/Wc, where P*max, *P*r, and *E* are the maximum polarization, remnant polarization, and applied electric field in the polarization (*P*)*–*electric field (*E*) hysteresis loop (*P*–*E* loop), respectively **(**Fig. 1(a)). Based on these equations, a slim and extended *P–E* loop, which features a high *P*max, a low *P*r, and a large maximum applicable electric field (≈ dielectric strength (*E*b)), is thus highly desired for dielectric energy storage applications. However, with few disruptions in its long-range polar order, single-phase normal ferroelectrics, including both bulk ceramics and films, display a plump *P‒E* loop with early saturation of their electric polarization (Fig. 1(b)).

To obtain a slim *P‒E* loop, most researchers have used chemical methods, including doping [5,6], substitution [7,8], and solid solutioning [9,10], to design and prepare relaxor ferroelectric (RFE) films. Among these efforts, solid solutions with coexisting ferroelectric and paraelectric phases have emerged as ideal chemical systems for dielectric energy storage. The coexisting paraelectric phase breaks the long-range ferroelectric order and induces nanoscale polar domains in the film, leading to its relaxor ferroelectric-like behavior and consequently a slim *P‒E* hysteresis loop. For example, films of the BiFeO3*‒*BaTiO3*‒*SrTiO3 solid solution, which combines two different ferroelectric materials with a paraelectric component, have displayed a polymorphic phase boundary (PPB) consisting of nanoscale heterophase domains, resulting in an improved energy storage density and efficiency [10]. However, sizable remnant polarization, due to random mixing of heterogeneous nanodomains (driven by the mixing entropy in multicomponent RFE), limits further improvement of the energy storage efficiency. The other chemical methods also have their drawbacks, either counteracting effects from the introduction of doping/substituting defects or manufacturing complexity when introducing multiple, mutually balanced cation elements into the oxide lattice [4].

To supplement chemical methods, several physical methods for film structure manipulation, such as grain engineering [4,11−15], strain [16] and domain engineering [17,18], as well as the construction of heterogeneous interfaces (“interface engineering”) [19−23], have been utilized to prepare perovskite ferroelectric films with desirable slim *P*‒*E* loops for enhanced energy storage performance. Han *et al*. [14] prepared thick nanograined (grain size ~15±5 nm) Pb(Zr0.52Ti0.48)O3 (PZT) films (~4 μm) via high kinetic energy aerosol deposition. These normal ferroelectric PZT films exhibited relaxor ferroelectric behaviors, highlighted by a slim and tilted *P‒E* loop with large maximum polarization and a high dielectric strength *E*b. Such behaviors are attributed to the mechanical interactions among particles during the high kinetic deposition process [14,15], which induces a superfine nanograin microstructure in the PZT films.

Another grain engineering approach has focused on pseudo one-dimensional nanograin growth in ferroelectric films, achieved through high-density nucleation at a reduced deposition temperature and use of an oriented conductive buffer layer to promote a (001) grain texture [11]. In such an engineered grain structure, the ferroelectric grains self-assemble as densely packed arrays of columnar nanopillars (“superfine columnar nanograins”), extending across the film thickness. These columnar nanograins are separated by thin grain boundary layers, which are poorly crystallized and hence are nonferroelectric or at most weakly ferroelectric. This periodic nanostructure consisting of columnar grain*‒*grain boundary arrays has an in-plane polarization gradient, which reduces the average electric polarization and delays its saturation under an applied electric field. Together with an enhanced *E*b due to the size effect, this approach has significantly improved the energy storage performance of BaTiO3 films [11]. The latest progress on submicron-thick films integrated with Si included a high recyclable energy density of ~161.1 J/cm3 and a large energy responsivity of ~373.8 J/(kV·m2). Unfortunately, due to a stable tetragonal phase with a polar vector along (001), these grain-engineered BaTiO3 films still maintain sizable remnant polarization, thereby increasing the energy efficiency (~65%‒80%) far from the desired level (close to 90% or higher). A schematic of the half *P*‒*E* loop of such films is shown in Fig. 1(b) (marked as “columnar nanograins”).

On the other hand, a strain-induced heterophase nanodomain structure in epitaxial Ba(Zr0.2Ti0.8)O3 thick films (~0.35‒1.8 μm) has shown excellent energy storage efficiency (up to ~96%) [18], which is achieved by an ultra-adaptable nanodomain structure consisting of a metastable T phase and a stable rhombohedral (R) phase. The T phase is induced by the film strain and is dominant near the film bottom interface, while the R phase is abundant near the top part of the film and exists in the form of a hierarchical polytwin. Meanwhile, the energy densities achieved in these thick films are limited by their ultra-adaptability to the applied electric field, i.e., early polarization saturation in comparison with that of single-phase, columnar nanograined BaTiO3 films. Consequently, these films broke down at much smaller electric fields, displaying less impressive energy densities (~100 J/cm3 in submicron films and ~50‒60 J/cm3 in micrometer-thick films) [18,24] than that of the aforementioned BaTiO3 film. A schematic of the half *P*‒*E* loop of such films is shown in Fig. 1(b) (marked as “Coexistence of T+R”).

In this work, we combined two approaches to prepare columnar nanograined Ba(Zr0.2Ti0.8)O3 (BZT) ferroelectric films on Si with gradient phases along the growth direction. In this case, both in-plane (*x*, *y*-axes) and out-of-plane (*z*-axis) polarization gradients are present, leading to the formation of three-dimensional polarization inhomogeneities in the BZT film and hence a slim and extended *P*‒*E* loop that endures a high electric field (schematically shown in Fig. 1(b) and marked as “columnar nanograins with coexistence of T+R”). Consequently, a high energy density and a high efficiency are simultaneously achieved. To create columnar nanograin arrays with reduced grain sizes, we lowered the deposition temperature to 500 °C. Such a temperature also suppresses the formation of misfit dislocations, thereby leaving the film on Si with a residual compressive strain [4,11]. As illustrated in our previous work [18,24], the bottom part of the BZT film possesses a metastable T phase under a high misfit strain, which is now separated by amorphous grain boundaries. As the strain field decays with an increasing film thickness, the metastable T phase inside each nanograin gradually transforms into the stable R phase, creating a gradient phase distribution along the film/grain growth direction. This gradient T/R polymorphic phase structure, together with the columnar nanograin morphology, endows the BZT film with a very large recyclable energy density *W*rec (~208 J/cm3) and a high energy efficiency (~88%), both of which are obtained under a large external electric field of 5.7 MV/cm. These BZT films also displayed a high Curie temperature and a low dielectric loss. Taken together, these desirable dielectric properties indicate that, based on the combination of the strain-induced phase boundary and columnar nanograin strategies, novel ferroelectric film capacitors can be fabricated for future energy storage and high-field dielectric applications.

## 2 Materials and methods

**Raw materials:** A BZT ceramic target was prepared by a conventional solid-state reaction method. High-purity BaCO3, ZrO2, and TiO2 reagents (99% purity, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) in stoichiometric molar ratios (5 : 1 : 4) were ball-milled for 12 h and calcined at 1250 °C for 4 h. Then, the calcined powders were ball-milled again for 12 h and pressed in a stainless steel mold at 50 MPa for 10 min. The pressed disk was sintered at 1350–1450 °C for 2 h and machined into a sputtering target with the desired shape and size (circular plate, diameter (*Φ*) = 50 mm, thickness (*t*) = 4 mm). A R phase structure with a lattice parameter of ~4.06 Å was confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the BZT target, as reported in our previous work [18]. The LaNiO3 ceramic target (*Φ* = 50 mm, *t* = 4 mm, 99.9%) and the SiO2/(100) Si substrates (10 mm×10 mm×0.5 mm) were purchased from the Anhui Institute of Optics and Fine Mechanics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China. Commercially available Pt (*Φ* = 50 mm, *t* = 3 mm, 99.99%) and Ti (*Φ* = 50 mm, *t* = 4 mm, 99.99%) circular plate targets were obtained from Beijing Goodwill Metal Technology Co., Ltd., China.

**Film growth:** The (00*l*)-textured polycrystalline BZT films were prepared via radio frequency (RF) magnetron sputtering. A bilayer bottom electrode of Pt/Ti and a conductive buffer layer of LaNiO3 were deposited on Si in sequential order from their corresponding targets. During the sputtering process, the substrate temperature was maintained at 350 °C for LaNiO3/Pt/Ti (with a deposition pressure of 0.3 Pa) and 500 °C for BZT (with a deposition pressure of 1 Pa and a targeted thickness of ~650 nm). Pure Ar and mixed Ar/O2 (with a flow ratio of 4:1) were utilized for the deposition of Pt/Ti and LaNiO3/BZT, respectively. After deposition of the BZT layer, the film was annealed in a pure oxygen atmosphere (~2 Pa) for 10 min at 500 °C, and then the heater was turned off. The metal−insulator−metal (MIM) capacitor structures were formed by depositing circular Au top electrodes (0.2 mm in diameter) on the film surface at room temperature through the use of a shadow mask.

**Film characterization:** The phase structure and crystallographic orientation of the BZT films were analyzed by using an X-ray diffractometer (SmartLab 9 kW, Rigaku, Japan) under 2*θ*-scans with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5418 Å). A transmission electron microscope (JEM-2010, JEOL, Japan) was utilized to investigate the nanostructures of the BZT films. The surface morphologies of the BZT films were analyzed via atomic force microscopy (AFM), while the ferroelectric domain structure near the film surface was analyzed via piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM). Both microscopic analyses were carried out with a scanning probe microscope (SPM-9700HT, Shimadzu, Japan). The polar symmetry of the film material near the surface was also investigated via second harmonic generation studies, which were carried out at room temperature in reflection mode using a femtosecond laser (Chameleon, 800 nm, 140 fs, 80 MHz, Coherent, USA) with beam spots of ~10 and ~100 μm in diameter for the 90o- and 45o-incident geometries, respectively. Finally, for the electrical characterizations, the *P–E* hysteresis loops and maximum applicable fields/dielectric strengths *E*b of the BZT films, as well as the leakage currents (*I–V* curves), were measured by using a ferroelectric tester (Multiferroic II, Radiant Technology, USA). The frequency- and electric field-dependent dielectric properties were measured using a high-precision LCR meter (TH2838H, Tonghui Electronics, ±200 V with an amplifier). A temperature-controllable probe station (HFS600E-PB2, Linkam, UK) was used to measure the dielectric and ferroelectric properties of the BZT films as a function of temperature, from which the temperature stability of the energy storage performance was evaluated.

## 3 Results and discussion

From early results on bulk ceramics, partial substitution of B-site Ti4+ ions with larger Zr4+ ions will result in a larger unit cell and destabilize the ground-state T phase at room temperature [25−27]. With an increasing amount of Zr4+, the stable bulk phase at room temperature changes into orthorhombic (O-phase, 2% ≤ Zr% ≤ 8%) and then rhombohedral phases (R-phase, Zr% ≥ 7%) [27]. If plotted in a phase diagram, the T–O and O–R phase boundaries move up along the temperature axis with reduced interphase energy barriers [25]. At the same time, the Curie temperature, above which the BZT material transforms into a paraelectric cubic (C) phase, decreases with increasing Zr4+ amount, reaching triple points at approximately 14% and 70 °C for T–O–C and 15% and 65 °C for O–R–C [26]. A further increase in the substitutional amount of Zr4+ (Zr% > 15%) stabilizes the cubic phase near or below room temperature, which is now directly bordering the low-temperature ferroelectric rhombohedral phase. At Zr% = 20%, the Curie temperature is approximately 31 °C, which is very close to room temperature [26]. In our previous works on high-energy density epitaxial BZT films [18,24], Ba(Zr0.2Ti0.8)O3 was chosen due to its small energy differences and shallow energy wells between the polar phases, thereby leading to the formation of a T+R heterophase polydomain nanostructure under the effect of residual misfit strain (Fig. 1(c)) [28]. Specifically, a metastable T phase in the BZT film was induced under the effect of a compressive biaxial strain, which was imposed by a (100)-oriented cubic or pseudocubic substrate [18]. The metastable T phase was abundant near the bottom interface of the film, where the residual strain was at its maximum. Along the film growth/thickness direction, the T*-*rich phase mixture was gradually transformed into an R-rich mixture (the R phase is the bulk stable phase) due to the relaxation of the residual strain from a decaying long-range stress field. Consequently, a gradient T+R phase mixture was formed in the BZT film, with the T-rich region near the bottom interface and the R*-*rich region near the surface [18].

In this work, with the insertion of a template layer of the conductive perovskite LaNiO3 (LNO), which can be easily grown on Pt/Ti/Si with a (100) orientation [29], the BZT films grew with a columnar nanograin morphology and a (001) texture. Nevertheless, the local coherency between (001) BZT and (100) LNO ensures a nondisrupted stress field inside each columnar grain [11]. Such a stress field also decays along the film growth direction, leading to the formation of an inner-grain gradient phase mixture. This microstructure will have a close-to-zero remnant polarization under the effects of an in-plane polarization gradient (grain vs. grain boundary) and an out-of-plane polarization gradient (T phase vs. R phase, Fig. 1(d)). Furthermore, under an applied electric field, the synergistic effects of in-plane polarization confinement (by periodic grain boundaries) [11] and out-of-plane ultra-adaptive polarization switching (between the T/R polymorphs) [18] will create an elevated and extended polarization saturation path (Fig. 1(b)), which is ideal for dielectric energy storage.

Figure 2(a) displays a representative XRD 2*θ*-scan spectrum of the BZT films. The pure perovskite structure with only (00l) diffraction peaks indicates the *c*-axis-oriented growth of the BZT film on LNO(100). The Pt layer shows a (111) orientation on Si(100), which is typical for sputtered platinum. The bulk R phase of BZT has a lattice constant of 4.06 Å according to our previous work [18], while the measured out-of-plane lattice parameter from Fig. 2(a) is ~4.147 Å, verifying a large in-plane compressive strain in the BZT film. The 5 μm×5 μm AFM image (Fig. 2(b)) reveals the dense and smooth surface morphology of the BZT film, which has a root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of ~4.0 nm and an in-plane equiaxed nanograin structure with an average in-plane diameter of ~60 nm. Further investigations of the microstructure and domain structure of the BZT films were carried out via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 3) and PFM (Fig. 4)/second harmonic generation (SHG, Fig. 5), respectively.

TEM revealed (001)-oriented columnar nanograins with a gradient T/R* phase mixture in the BZT film. Figure 3(a) shows a representative low-magnification TEM image of the BZT/LNO/Pt/Ti thin film heterostructure deposited on SiO2/(100)Si. The BZT film has a thickness of ~650 nm and a microstructure consisting of densely packed columnar nanograins throughout the film thickness, which has an average in-plane diameter of ~60 nm and is consistent with the AFM surface scan result (Fig. 2(b)).

The cross-sectional energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental scan image of the film heterostructure is displayed in Fig. 3(b). Clean-cut heterointerfaces and uniform distributions of the constituent elements in the designed layers were revealed, indicating suppressed interdiffusion of elements (due to the low deposition temperature) and hence good film quality. High-resolution TEM analyses were utilized to examine the lattice parameters and orientation of the local film structures, which were sequentially imaged from the bottom interface to the top surface of the BZT film (Figs. 3(c)–3(h), areas were selected from the grains in the white dashed box in Fig. 2(a)). While the out-of-plane orientation was (001) for all selected areas, which also showed similar lattice constants, the angles between the (100) and (001) plane orientations, presented in Fig. 3(k) as a function of the local film thickness, were different and showed a clear trend of transformation from ~90° near the bottom of the film (Fig. 3(d)) to ~85° near the surface of the film (Fig. 3(g)). This trend in the (100)/(001) angle corresponds to a tetragonal to rhombohedral transition and verifies the gradient of the T/R phase structure.

Figure 4 shows PFM images of the BZT film (scan area: 2 μm×2 μm). Figures 4(a) and 4(d) show the height images from the in-plane (IP) and out-of-plane (OOP) scans, respectively. These two images are virtually identical and can be regarded as small-area AFM scan images, which show a film morphology similar to that of the large-area AFM scan in Fig. 1(b). Figures 4(b) and 4(e) show the IP and OOP amplitude images, with average amplitudes of ~90 pm (IP) and ~30 pm (OOP), respectively. Moreover, Figs. 4(c) and 4(f) show IP and OOP phase images with average phase angles of ~50° (IP) and ~130° (OOP), respectively. The high IP PFM amplitude and large canted phase angles (i.e., angles of the polarization vector with respect to the IP and OOP axes) indicate a high concentration of the R phase near the film surface.

To verify the macroscopic symmetry of the BZT film near its surface, SHG studies were performed on the BZT film in reflection mode [18,30]. Notably, the transmission mode is not applicable for films grown on optically opaque SiO2/Si. The characteristic SHG spectra for both the 45°- and 90°-incident geometries are shown in Fig. 5. While the reflected SHG signals from the 45°-incident geometry indicate the possibility of the existence of both in-plane and out-of-plane polarization vectors, the strong nonzero SHG signals from the 90° incident geometry reveal the existence of in-plane polarization. Given that the tetragonal phase in the film is *c*-axis oriented (from the XRD and TEM results), such SHG patterns obtained from the film’s near-surface region can only be explained by a rhombohedral-rich phase structure. In fact, the fitting data points obtained using a rhombohedral phase model agree well with the measured data. Together with the TEM and PFM data, the SHG results support our proposed microstructure model of a “gradient T+R phase mixture”.

The room temperature energy storage performance of the BZT film capacitors was evaluated via *P‒E* loop measurements at 1 kHz (Fig. 6(a)). Under an increasing applied electric field, the BZT film maintained a slim *P‒E* hysteresis loop until electric breakdown. At a breakdown field (*E*b) of ~5.7 MV/cm (370 V), the BZT film showed a large maximum polarization (*P*m) of 92.1 μC/cm2 and a close-to-zero remnant polarization (*P*r) of 5.2 μC/cm2. In the inset of Fig. 6(a), the dielectric strength/breakdown field (*E*b) was statistically fitted via the two-parameter Weibull distribution method [5,6], yielding an *E*b value of 5.7 MV/cm and a Weibull modulus (*β*) of 15.8. The outcome of the Weibull model suggested a reliable high dielectric strength for the BZT film. As elaborated in our proposed microstructural model, the high electric field endurance can be attributed to the columnar nanograin structure. Such a nanostructure not only results in a size effect that enhances the dielectric strength (*E*b) [11] but also creates an in-plane polarization gradient, significantly delaying the saturation of the electric polarization. Meanwhile, the high polarizability and small hysteresis of the BZT film can be attributed to the gradient T/R phase structure, in which the two polymorphic phases with a small energy difference can be easily transformed into each other under the application (R→T) and withdrawal (T→R) of an external electric field (*E*). The R→T transformation yields a high maximum polarization under an increasing *E*, while the reversed transformation leaves a close-to-zero *P*r due to the special domain pattern of R phase that can eliminate net polarization [18]. Consequently, the elevated slim *P–E* loop under a high maximum applicable electric field (*E*max = 5.7 MV/cm, ~*E*b) yielded an ultrahigh *W*rec (208 J/cm3) with a desirable energy efficiency (*η*, ~88%), as shown in Fig. 6(b). In addition, the energy density of the BZT film increases with the applied electric field in an approximately quadratic fashion, which fits fairly well with the capacitive energy density equation of a linear dielectric [1]. The energy efficiency (*η*) is well above 90% up to an applied electric field (*E*) of ~4.62 MV/cm (~80% of *E*b). Only after *E* reached 95% *E*b (~5.4 MV/cm) did *η* decrease to ~90%.

The leakage current density*–*electric field (*J–E*) curve is displayed in Fig. 6(c). With an increasing electric field (in the form of a direct current (DC) bias), the leakage current density (*J*) increased at a decreasing rate, reaching ~2×10−6 A/cm2 at *E* = 600 kV/cm. Such a low leakage current indicated a good insulating BZT film. The *J–E* curve was well fitted to the modified Schottky contact model (inset of Fig. 6(c)), which explains the low ramping rate of the leakage current and can be attributed to an increased interface energy barrier near the LNO/BZT heterojunction for charge carriers [31]. Meanwhile, Fig. 6(d) displays the frequency dispersion behavior of the dielectric properties of the film. A high dielectric constant (*ε*r ≈ 259@1 kHz) and a low loss tangent (tan*δ* ≈ 2.0%@1 kHz), together with a weak frequency dependence of the dielectric properties (*ε**r* ≈ 245, tan*δ* ≈ 4.5%@1 MHz), were revealed for the BZT film. Both the high *ε*r value and the weak frequency dispersion are consequences of the formation of ultraadaptive nanophase mixtures in the BZT film [18]. In the inset of Fig. 6(d), the room-temperature dielectric properties are presented as functions of the DC bias field (*E*DC). A low dielectric loss (tan*δ* DC (0–1.2 MV/cm) and a low dielectric tunability (% change in dielectric constant under an applied electric field) of ~24.3% at 1.2 MV/cm were revealed, which contributed to the superior energy storage performance of the film [32]. Overall, the BZT films displayed low conductivity and excellent and robust dielectric performance at room temperature, which are consistent with their slim and extended *P*–*E* loops shown in Fig. 6(a).

The temperature dependence of the dielectric constant was investigated, and the results (*ε*r*–T* curves) are presented in Fig. 7(a). The BZT film showed a high Curie temperature (*T*c) of ~460 °C, which is ideal for capacitor applications and can be attributed to a compressive residual strain [33,34]. Furthermore, although the dielectric peak decreased with increasing measuring frequency, no obvious shift in *T*c was observed. These behaviors are nonrelaxor but characteristic of a diffuse phase transition, which agrees well with those observed in bulk ceramics with similar compositions [25,26]. Data fitting using the modified Curie‒Weiss law [35] yielded a critical exponent *β* between 1.6 and 2.0 (inset of Fig. 7(a)), confirming a high degree of diffuseness for the ferroelectric–paraelectric phase transition in our BZT films. In fact, since these films consist of both columnar nanograins (with an in-plane diameter of ~60 nm) and inner-grain gradient phase mixtures (with even smaller feature sizes [18]), such a high *β* value is expected from the size effect of the nanopolar structures [36]. Finally, to evaluate the temperature stability of the energy storage performance, *P‒E* loops of the BZT film were measured in the temperature range between 25 and 200 °C (at *E*max = 3.1 MV/cm, > 0.5*E*b), and the results are presented in Fig. 7(b). Both the recyclable energy density (*W*rec) and the energy efficiency (*η*) exhibited small variations in this broad temperature range (±9.6% for *W*rec and ±2.1% for *η*). This stable energy storage performance can be attributed to an elevated Curie temperature and a small interphase energy barrier between the T and R polymorphic phases.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, an ultrahigh energy density of ~208 J/cm3 and a high energy efficiency of ~88% were simultaneously achieved in submicron-thick Ba(Zr0.2Ti0.8)O3 ferroelectric films sputter-deposited on Si at 500 °C. These films feature a microstructure consisting of columnar nanograins and inner-grain gradient phase mixtures. The latter is composed of a strain-stabilized T phase, which is dominant near the film bottom, and a stable bulk R phase, which is abundant near the film surface. A synergistic effect between the nanograin and the gradient phase mixture led to the aforementioned energy storage performance, together with good frequency and temperature stabilities. The design and fabrication of such self-assembled nanostructures (columnar nanograin + gradient heterophases) in ferroelectric films have paved the way for the creation of next-generation film capacitors with both a high energy density and high efficiency. This work has also demonstrated an effective method for fine-tuning the microstructure and electrical properties of a ferroelectric film.
