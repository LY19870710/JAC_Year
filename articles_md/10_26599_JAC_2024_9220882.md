## 1 Introduction

Capacitors, dielectric antennas, resonators, filters, and substrates are fundamental components of a radio frequency (RF) circuit and play a crucial role in the rapid advancement of wireless communication systems. As electromagnetic waves expand to higher millimeter waves, the delay and attenuation of the signal become more pronounced. To address these challenges, a substantial number of base stations must be constructed to ensure signal coverage, and dielectric materials suitable for high-frequency communication must be developed to reduce delay. In addition, the development of the Internet of Things has caused a sharp increase in data connection terminals and data transmission capacity. Massive MIMO technology, as a crucial solution for 5G and 6G networks, has received widespread attention and has greatly increased the demand for RF devices. Microwave dielectric ceramics, as the key materials of RF devices, have been proposed with new requirements, particularly for low permittivity and high quality factor (*Q*·*f*), to reduce signal delay and enhance frequency selectivity [1,2]. Moreover, a low sintering temperature is recommended, which is beneficial for energy conservation and reduces greenhouse gas production [3]. Another requirement is low density to meet light weight requirements. However, few ceramic materials have a permittivity below 6 (abbreviated as low-*ε*r6), low loss, or low sintering temperature [4,5]. Consequently, it is necessary to explore ceramic systems simultaneously with low permittivity (*ε*r), low loss, and low sintering temperatures.

Organic compounds typically possess lower *ε*r than inorganic matrices, which can be ascribed to chemical bonds with strong covalency in organic molecular chains, such as C–C and C–H bonds. Thus, finding low-*ε*r6 materials in inorganic compounds with strong covalent bonds is an effective method. In general, phosphates exhibit low *ε*r because of the strong covalent bonding of the [PO4] tetrahedra [6−11]. Bian *et al*. [12−16] reported that pyrophosphates A2P2O7 (A = Ca, Sr, Ba, Mg, Zn, Mn) and AMP2O7 (A = Ca, Sr; M = Zn, Cu) could densify below 1150 ℃ and possess low *ε*r (4 (Re = La, Ce, Nd, Sm, Tb, Dy, Y, and Yb) were systematically investigated by Cho *et al*. [17], who reported high *Q*·*f* values (approximately 60,000 GHz), low *ε*r values ( 1300 ℃). However, energy conservation and environmental protection have become key issues advocated by the electronic components industry. A3(PO4)2 (A = Sr, Ba) has special positive *τ*f and low *ε*r, which is different from other phosphates [18]. In addition, the mixed divalent cation phosphates AM2(PO4)2 (A = Ca, Sr, Ba; M = Mg, Zn) have been widely studied [19,20], and SrZn2(PO4)2 exhibited exceptional properties of *ε*r = 6.29 and *Q*·*f* = 21,600 GHz and excellent co-firing ability [21]. Recently, LiMgPO4 has received considerable attention because of its low bulk density, low sintering temperature, and excellent chemical compatibility with Ag [10,22−24]. By adding an appropriate amount of TiO2, the temperature coefficient of resonant frequency (*τ*f) can be adjusted to +1.2 ppm/℃, showing the potential application of LTCC materials [23]. Hence, Li-rich orthophosphates have received widespread attention [25−28]. Nevertheless, the dielectric properties of other stable long-chain structures of metaphosphates have not yet been explored. Sadin *et al*. [11] first reported this year the potential application of LiB(PO3)3 (B = Ca, Sr) as microwave dielectric ceramics, which have low *ε*r6, low sintering temperatures, and low bulk density and have attracted considerable research interest. Moreover, a wide range of ceramic materials containing rare-earth elements, including Re3B5O12 (Re = Y, Sm, Eu; B = Al, Ge) [29,30], ReVO4 (Re = Yb, Ho) [31], and K2NiF4-structure ceramics [32,33], display excellent *Q*·*f* values. Consequently, LiLn(PO3)4 (Ln = La, Sm, Eu) ceramics based on the LiO2–Ln2O3–P2O5 ternary phase diagram were investigated as promising low-*ε*r6, low-loss, and low-density candidates.

In this paper, LiLn(PO3)4 ceramic samples were prepared through a solid reaction process, and their microstructure, Raman spectrum, and dielectric properties were comprehensively explored. Moreover, Phillips–van Vechten–Levine (P–V–L) theory was used to quantify bond characteristics, and the structure–performance relationship was discussed to provide a guidance for performance improvement. Notably, outstanding microwave dielectric properties (*ε*r = 5.04–5.26, *Q*·*f* = 41,607–75,968 GHz, *τ*f = −19.64 to −47.49 ppm/℃) were obtained for the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics. Given its potential as candidate dielectric substrate materials for microwave applications, LiEu(PO3)4 ceramics were designed as microstrip patch antennas. The simulation and optimization were conducted using the high frequency structure simulator (HFSS) software. Finally, a prototype of the microstrip patch antenna was fabricated, and its performance was measured to evaluate its practicality.

## 2 Experimental methods

The LiLn(PO3)4 (Ln = La, Sm, Eu) system ceramics were prepared using the solid-state reaction method, employing high-purity raw materials with Li2CO3 (99.5%), La2O3 (99.99%), Sm2O3 (99.99%), Eu2O3 (99.99%), and NH4H2PO4 (99%). Prior to synthesis, La2O3, Sm2O3, and Eu2O3 were pretreated at 900 ℃, and the NH4H2PO4 particles were thoroughly ground in an agate mortar before weighing. Then, raw materials were weighed in accordance with the molar ratio of LiLn(PO3)4 and ball-milled for 12 h to mix and grind. Zirconium dioxide balls and ethanol were used as the ball grinding medium and dispersion medium, respectively. After drying, the mixed powders were heated at 800 ℃ to initially synthesize the crystal structure. Subsequently, the powders were remilled and dried again using the same method before granulation. Afterward, the powders were unidirectionally compacted into cylinders at 200 MPa. Finally, the green bodies were heated to 550 ℃ for 4 h and then sintered at 850–950 ℃ for 4 h in air.

The first-principles calculations were performed within density functional theory using the projector augmented plane-wave (PAW) method, implemented in the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) [34]. For the exchange-correlation potential, the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) proposed by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof was employed [35]. The plane wave cutoff energy was set to 550 eV [36]. An effective Hubbard-*U* term was employed to treat strongly correlated f-electrons, and the *U* parameters were 6.5, 7.1, and 7.4 eV for La, Sm, and Eu, respectively [37]. Brillouin zone integration was performed using a 4 × 4 × 3 *k*-mesh. The convergence conditions for electronic and ionic optimizations were 1×10−5 eV and 0.03 eV/Å, respectively.

The structure of the sintered LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics was confirmed by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; SmartLab, Rigaku) using Cu Kα radiation with a step width of 0.02°, and the structural parameters were obtained by least squares fitting with the Fullprof module. The bulk density was measured by Archimedes method, and the cross-sectional morphology and elemental composition of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics were detected by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-7610F, JEOL Ltd.) equipped with an energy-dispersive spectroscope (EDS). A transmission electron microscope (JEM-F200, JEOL Ltd.) was utilized for selected area electron diffraction (SAED) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM). Low-frequency dielectric properties were measured by an LCR meter (E4980AL, Agilent). The microwave dielectric properties and return loss (*S*11) of the prototype antenna were evaluated using a vector network analyzer (N5234A, Agilent) employing Hakki–Coleman method and transverse TE01*δ* mode (TE01*δ*) cavity method [38−40]. The *τ*f value was measured within a temperature range of 25–85 ℃ using a temperature chamber (BPH-120A, Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd.). In addition, the infrared reflection spectra were recorded between 50 and 1300 cm−1 using a Bruker IFS 66v FTIR spectrometer, and the Raman spectrum was acquired in the range of 50–1300 cm−1 under excitation from an argon laser radiation source with a wavelength of 532 nm (LabRAM HR Evolution).

### 3.1 Phase synthesis

Figures 1(a)–1(c) show the differential scanning calorimetry/thermogravimetry/derivative thermogravimetry (DSC/TG/DTG) analysis of the LiLn(PO3)4 raw materials from room temperature to 970 ℃ at a linear heating rate of 10 K/min. The raw materials used in the analysis were obtained from mixed powders after ball milling. All the mixed powders exhibit similar reaction processes. Taking Fig. 1(c) as an example, a small endothermic peak appears at 201.04 ℃ in the DSC curve. Simultaneously, the TG curve shows an obvious mass loss of approximately 10.10%, with a clear peak at 197.06 ℃ in the DTG curve, corresponding to the decomposition of NH4H2PO4 and the removal of NH3. Subsequently, H3PO4 undergoes a series of dehydration reactions to ultimately generate tetrametaphosphate ((HPO3)4), which results in a continuous weight loss of approximately 10.70% according to the TG curve. The specific reaction equation (the reaction II) is depicted in Fig. 1(d). The corresponding reaction peak is observed in the DTG curve at 293.25 ℃. Although there is no obvious endothermic peak in DSC, its endothermic state demonstrates the sustained progress of the reaction. Afterwards, the synthesis of LiEu(PO3)4 was initiated at 360.76 ℃, and an obvious peak was observed in the DTG curve. As the temperature further increased, the TG curve continuously decreased, indicating the continuation of the reaction. All chemical reactions concerning weight are completed below 800 ℃. The total weight loss reached 8.70%, which aligns with the calculated values of the chemical reaction III displayed in Fig. 1(d). It should be pointed out that due to differences in heating rate and holding time, the thermodynamic conditions during calcination are more rigorous than those during comprehensive thermal analysis. To achieve better crystallinity, the synthesis temperature of the LiLn(PO3)4 (Ln = La, Sm, Eu) powders was determined to be 800 ℃.

### 3.2 Crystal structure characterizations

The XRD patterns of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics sintered at various temperatures are presented in Fig. 2. All diffraction peaks with similar shapes can be identified as LiLa(PO3)4 (PDF#97-041-6877), LiSm(PO3)4 (PDF#97-024-8138), and LiEu(PO3)4 (PDF#97-041-6878), which crystallize in a monoclinic system with a *C*2/*c* space group. As depicted in Figs. 2(b) and 2(c), the diffraction peaks corresponding to SmP3O9 and EuPO4 are detected at 950 ℃, which may be attributed to the volatilization of Li at high temperatures. In addition, Fig. 2(d) shows the XRD patterns of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics sintered at the optimized temperatures. A significant peak shift can be observed in the enlarged XRD patterns. The (111), * (4¯02), and  (3¯12) characteristic peaks shift to higher 2θ* angles with varying Ln-site ionic radii, implying a reduction in the interplanar spacing. This shift can be explained by the decrease in Ln-site ionic radius (*r*Eu = 1.07 Å [CN = 8] Sm = 1.08 Å [CN = 8] La = 1.16 Å [CN = 8]). Moreover, the structural parameters of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics may exhibit a decreasing trend. To validate this hypothesis, LiLa(PO3)4 and LiEu(PO3)4 structures were used as the initial models [36], and Rietveld refinements were performed. The refinement patterns are shown in Fig. 3(a), and the specific values of the structural parameters and reliability factors are listed in Table 1. Good agreement is found between the observed and calculated values, and the *R*-factors are less than 15%, which indicates the reliability of the structural parameters. As shown in Fig. 3(b), the cell volume (*V*) and *abc* gradually decrease with decreasing ionic radius, corresponding to the peak shift of the XRD patterns.

In addition, the Raman spectra of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics are plotted in Fig. 6. Given the overlap and degeneracy of the peaks, the number of Raman peaks is less than the theoretical value (26Ag + 28Bg) [42,43]. The first and second intense peaks of the typical Raman modes are located at 1169.8–1173.3 and 694.76–694.78 cm−1, respectively, which is a general characteristic of phosphoric anions (PO3)44−, indicating the structure of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics. The typical Raman modes are attributed to the antisymmetric stretching vibrations υ*as(O–P–O) and symmetric stretching vibrations *υ*s(P–O–P). From Fig. 6, it is evident that the Raman peak near 1170 cm−1 undergoes a shift toward a high frequency with varying Ln-site ions. The blueshift can be attributed to the decrease in the Ln-site ionic radius, for which the reduced ionic radius leads to a decrease in the cell volume, resulting in higher bond strength values, which corresponds to enhanced stretching vibrations of O–P–O. Moreover, the peaks at 1212–1296 and 1000–1100 cm−1 are ascribed to symmetric *υ*s(O–P–O) and asymmetric *υ*as(P–O–P), respectively [44]. Raman peaks below 650 cm−1 are assigned to the antisymmetric bending vibrations *δ*as(O–P–O) and symmetric bending vibrations *δ*s(P–O–P), which are difficult to precisely assign because such peaks overlap with Ln–O stretching vibrations and some external modes at lower frequencies [45]. Similar observations have been made for other metaphosphates [46, 47].

Figure 7 shows a schematic diagram of the LiLn(PO3)4 structure, and the base units are [LiO4], [LnO8], and [PO4] polyhedra. The projection of the LiLn(PO3)4 structure along the b*-axis is exhibited in Fig. 7(a), and two [PO4] tetrahedra ([P1O4] and [P2O4]) are connected by corner sharing along the *b*-axis, forming (PO4)3− zig-zag chains. The zig-zag chains are isolated from each other by [LiO4] and [LnO8] polyhedra serving as connecting bridges [36]. All the P–O bonds show different distances, ranging from 1.3427 to 1.6702 Å (Table 4), which indicates that the [PO4] tetrahedron is distorted. In particular, the P–O–P bridge possesses a longer P–O bond distance, whereas the shorter distance corresponds to the nonbridging bond. In addition, the [LiO4] and [LnO8] polyhedra are arranged alternately along the *b*-axis by edge sharing to form infinite chains (Fig. 7(b)). The distorted [LiO4] tetrahedron is formed by a Li atom connecting two different O atoms, and each [LiO4] tetrahedron shares all four O atoms with two [LnO8] polyhedra and four different [PO4] tetrahedra. The Ln (Ln = La, Sm, Eu) atoms are coordinated to four different O atoms within a distorted polyhedra, and the Ln–O bond has a maximum bond length ranging from 2.3234 to 2.6467 Å, indicating that the [LnO8] polyhedra occupy a significant space proportion in the three-dimensional framework.

In addition, the structural evolution is closely related to the bond length. Thus, the average bond lengths were calculated by simplifying the types of chemical bonds, and the results are presented in Fig. 8(a). With the change in Ln-site atoms, the Li–O bond length decreases from 1.9507 to 1.9003 Å, and the P–O bond length decreases from 1.5174 to 1.5042 Å. Consequently, the reduction in the cell volume originates from the decrease in the bond length. The normalized bond length represents the chemical bond state, which is defined as the ratio of the actual bond length to the theoretical bond length. A normalized value below 1 indicates compression; otherwise, it indicates elongation [48]. As depicted in Fig. 8(b), the Li–O and P–O bonds remain compressed, and the degree of compression of the Li–O bond increases with decreasing ionic radius. Although the Ln–O bond becomes increasingly elongated with decreasing ionic radius, the decrease in the number of compressed bonds is nearly 3.8 times greater than the increase in elongation. Each bond length directly affects the volume of the polyhedra, and the compressed bonds lead to the contraction of polyhedra, resulting in reduced structural parameters.

### 3.3 First-principles calculations

Using the structure refined by XRD data as the initial model, the crystal structure of the LiLn(PO3)4 (Ln = La, Sm, Eu) ceramics was optimized to achieve the lowest energy state. During the relaxation process, the atomic coordinates were adjusted, and the corresponding cell volume of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics changed. To evaluate the degree of structural change, the structural parameters after structure optimization and the standard deviation between the refined and relaxed structures of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics are listed in Table 5. The relaxed structural parameters are in good agreement with the refined values, confirming the validity of the calculations. Based on the optimized crystal structure, the energy band structure and density of states (DOS) of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics were calculated by first-principles calculations, as shown in Fig. 9. The calculated band structures of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics exhibit similar features. It is observed that the top of the valence band is nearly flat, while the bottom of the conduction band exhibits noticeable dispersion. The conduction band bottom and valence band top of LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics are localized at the G-point, indicating that the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics present an insulator character with direct bandgaps of approximately 6.02, 6.08, and 6.06 eV, respectively. From the partial density of states and total density of states (TDOS) diagram (Figs. 9(d)–9(f)), it can be concluded that the valence band is mainly contributed by the p-orbitals of O atoms, while the contribution in the conduction band bottom originates from the d-orbitals of Ln atoms. Obviously, resonance peaks in the energy interval between the P atom and the O atom are observed, suggesting strong covalent bonding between the two atoms. In addition, the density of states in the d-orbitals of the La atoms are slightly weaker than that of the other two states, which might be influenced by the 4f-orbitals.

Additionally, the electron localization function (ELF) can be utilized to describe the bonding properties within a crystal, indicating the degree of electron localization in the spatial distribution [49]. ### 3.4 Sintering characteristics

Figure 10(a) shows the bulk density and relative density of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics sintered at different temperatures. All the bulk density curves display a consistent trend, increasing with increasing sintering temperature and reaching saturation (3.06–3.29 g/cm3) at the highest sintering temperature, which is ascribed to the decrease in the gradual elimination of pores. An increase in the sintering temperature promoted mass transfer and exclusion of pores. In particular, the LiLa(PO3)4 and LiEu(PO3)4 ceramics obtained a higher relative density of approximately 93%, whereas the LiSm(PO3)4 ceramics exhibited a slightly lower relative density of 90%. Nevertheless, the samples melted with a further increase in the sintering temperature. The microstructure and porosity of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics can be visually observed from the SEM image, which is the microscopic manifestation of the macroscopic density. Thus, the cross-sectional microstructure of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics was characterized, and the results are shown in Figs. 10(b)–10(d). A relatively dense microstructure and clear globular grains can be observed in all the samples sintered at the optimized temperatures, and some tiny pores can be observed in the LiSm(PO3)4 ceramics. Moreover, the grain size was measured using the Nano Measurer software, revealing a mean grain size ranging from 3.11 to 3.79 μm for the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics. The mean grain size decreases with the replacement of La with Sm and Eu, which is consistent with the trend of the cell volume. To confirm whether the elemental content of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics was in accordance with the original set proportion, the EDS analysis was performed, and the results are plotted in Fig. 10. Li cannot be detected because of its low ionization energy and rapid diffusion. The atomic ratios of Ln : P : O (5.1%−5.3% : 22.5%−24.1% : 70.0%−72.4%) are detected in the selected area of the ceramic samples, which is consistent with the theoretical atomic ratio (5.9% : 23.5% : 70.6%) of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics. Consistent with the XRD results, the single spherical particles and acceptable atomic ratio further confirmed the successful synthesis of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics.

### 3.5 Dielectric properties and structure–property relationships

The low-frequency dielectric spectra of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics are presented in Fig. 11. With increasing frequency, *ε’* gradually decreases and stabilizes at frequencies above 1×105 Hz. Simultaneously, the significant dielectric relaxation can be observed, which is mainly attributed to space charge polarization. Generally, the polarization mechanisms of a dielectric stem from space charge polarization, dipole orientation polarization, ion displacement polarization, and electron displacement polarization. In the low-frequency band, all these polarization mechanisms contribute to the dielectric response, where the main sources of the dielectric constant are space charge polarization and dipole orientation polarization [50]. Therein, space charge polarization requires longer establishment time and exhibits significant relaxation phenomena at lower frequencies, while the polarization range of dipole orientation polarization can extend below 109 Hz, and no obvious relaxation peak is observed in Fig. 11. Furthermore, it can be observed that the relaxation peak of the LiEu(PO3)4 ceramics extends to 6×105 Hz, and the dielectric loss value (tan*δ*) is significantly greater than that of the LiLn(PO3)4 (Ln = La, Sm) ceramics, which may be attributed to the smaller grain size in the LiEu(PO3)4 ceramics. In crystallography, grain boundaries are mismatched regions where charges tend to accumulate. The smaller grains in the LiEu(PO3)4 ceramics indicate a high density of grain boundaries, resulting in increased dielectric loss.

### 3.6 Microstrip patch antennas

Finally, microstrip patch antennas are designed and fabricated on the basis of LiEu(PO3)4 ceramics to evaluate their application in the field of wireless communication. As shown in Fig. 18(a), a ceramic substrate with dimensions of 25 mm × 25 mm × 2 mm was manufactured at 925 ℃ for 4 h. The length and width of the patches were set to 10.2 and 15.0 mm, respectively, and the patches were coated with silver paste. In addition, the feed is completed by a coaxial probe. Figure 18(b) shows the antenna measurement setup of the microwave anechoic chamber, and Fig. 18(c) displays the simulated and measured return loss (*S*11). The measured values showed a center frequency of 5.08 GHz and a −10 dB bandwidth (BW) of 360 MHz, which is consistent with the simulated results of 5.10 GHz and BW of 270 MHz. The slight deviation can be attributed to insufficient fabrication and measurement accuracy. The distribution of the electric field of the microstrip patch antennas simulated by HFSS is shown in Fig. 18(d). The electric field remains unchanged in the width direction and only changes along the length direction of the patch. The electric fields at both ends of the patch are amplified above the patch, generating directional radiation. Figure 18(f) presents the simulated 3D pattern of the microstrip patch antenna at the center frequency of 5.10 GHz. The H-plane and E-plane are two slices from the 3D radiation pattern with different azimuthal angle (Phi) angles. To demonstrate the radiation performance of the microstrip patch antennas, the normalized far-field radiation patterns were simulated and measured at frequencies of 5.10 GHz in the plane Phi = 0° and Phi = 90°, as shown in Fig. 18(e). It can be seen from Fig. 18(e) that the broadside radiation pattern is observed at resonance frequencies, and the measurement results for the radiation pattern agree well with the simulation results. The fluctuation of the measurement pattern is mainly caused by the diffraction of the limited grounding plane and antenna bracket. Figure 18(e) shows the simulated and measured results of the gain in the microstrip patch antennas at Phi = 0° and pitch angle (*θ*) = 0°. The measured gain varies between 4.08 and 5.27 dB, and the simulated result shows a gain variation between 5.78 and 6.30 dB, which is slightly larger than the measurement result. This discrepancy can be attributed to the insufficient precision in antenna manufacturing and the insertion loss between the antenna and the feed network. Despite this disparity, the excellent radiation characteristics observed confirm the potential of LiEu(PO3)4 ceramics as promising candidates for high-frequency communication applications.

## 4 Conclusions

Low-permittivity and low-loss dielectric ceramics play a vital role in minimizing signal attenuation, maintaining signal integrity, and maximizing the efficiency of microwave devices. In this study, the low *ε*r LiLn(PO3)4 ceramic systems were successfully prepared via the traditional solid-state method, and the crystal structure, sintering characteristics, and dielectric properties were comprehensively investigated. The XRD results confirmed that the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics are indexed as a single-phase monoclinic structure sintered at 850–940 ℃, the shift in characteristic peaks was observed in the XRD patterns as Ln varied, which is attributed to the decreasing ionic radius of Ln-site. Structure refinement confirmed reductions in structure parameters, which stemmed from the reduction of chemical bond lengths and the compression of the [LiO4] and [PO4] tetrahedra. In addition, the blueshift in Raman spectroscopy of the LiLn(PO3)4 structure indicates a reduction in the cell volume, which is consistent with the refined results. Notably, the LiLn(PO3)4 (Ln = La, Sm, Eu) ceramics exhibited excellent microwave dielectric properties of *Q*·*f* = 41,607–75,968 GHz, *τ*f = −19.64 to −47.49 ppm/℃, *ε*r = 5.04–5.26 and small density (3.04–3.26 g/cm3) at low sintering temperatures of 910–925 ℃. The P–V–L theory was employed to investigate the bond characteristics influencing the dielectric performance. The increased *Q*·*f* value of the LiLn(PO3)4 ceramics was attributed to the enhancement of PF, bond covalency, and lattice energy, while the stability of *τ*f was determined by the strengthened bond energy. In addition, infrared reflectance spectra indicated that the *Q*·*f* value of the LiLn(PO3)4 system can be further improved by optimizing the preparation process. Finally, a microstrip patch antenna prototype was successfully fabricated using the LiEu(PO3)4 ceramics as a dielectric substrate. The result demonstrated an excellent antenna performance, with BW of 360 MHz and peak gain of 5.11 dB at a central frequency of 5.08 GHz. Consequently, the low-*ε*r LiLn(PO3)4 systems are promising candidates for microwave communication.
