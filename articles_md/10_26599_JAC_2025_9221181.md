## 1 Introduction

With the rapid advancements in new energy systems, electronic information technologies, aerospace, and related sectors, extreme service environments have increasingly stringent demands on the coupled thermal, mechanical, and electrical properties of materials. As a new-generation ultralight multifunctional material, silicon carbide (SiC) fiber aerogels exhibit considerable promise for applications in wide-range thermal protection, electromagnetic wave (EMW) absorption, and environmental remediation [1,2]. These aerogels ingeniously integrate the high porosity characteristic of ceramic aerogels with the intrinsic high-temperature stability of SiC, offering unique chemical inertness, exceptional thermal resilience, and superior dielectric loss performance. Since Su *et al*. [3] first developed SiC nanowire aerogels via chemical vapor deposition-assisted self-assembly, a wide array of aerogel systems have been introduced, including pure SiC nanowires [4−7], SiC@SiO2 composite fibers [8,9], and core‒shell-structured SiC/SiO*x* architectures [10]. Through the adoption of innovative fabrication techniques such as electrospinning-ice templating integration [11,12] and three-dimensional (3D) printing combined with carbothermal reduction [13,14], these materials have been successfully extended to diverse applications, including EMW absorption, high-efficiency thermal insulation, oil‒water separation, and advanced energy storage.

However, current research faces two major challenges. First, conventional fabrication methods use fiber assemblies at a single nanoscale. This leads to uniform pore structures, which hinder cross-scale synergistic interactions and limit performance improvements. Second, most existing systems are designed primarily for flexible applications [15]. There has been little progress in the development of rigid, high-strength aerogels that can perform load-bearing functions under high-temperature conditions. Consequently, the design of SiC fiber aerogels featuring hierarchical pore architectures and enhanced mechanical strength is critically important. Hierarchical pore engineering has been demonstrated as an effective strategy for optimizing multifunctional performance. For example, SiC fiber/nanowire aerogels prepared by electrospinning-freeze casting [12] and SiC aerogels with hollow skeletons prepared via cellulose/carbon fiber/SiO2 multiphase composite strategies have been reported [16]. These multilevel structures enable broadband EMW absorption and efficient thermal insulation while maintaining high strength. Nevertheless, conventional fabrication methods are often hindered by high raw material costs and intricate processing steps, significantly limiting their scalability for large-scale applications.

Biomass-derived materials present promising alternatives owing to their renewability, cost-effectiveness, and structural diversity [17]. In particular, one-dimensional biomass fibers, such as kapok fibers, offer distinct advantages for hierarchical pore construction because of their hollow morphologies, high aspect ratios, and inherent weavability. On the basis of these characteristics, various kapok fiber-based materials have been developed, demonstrating great potential in applications such as oil‒water separation and EMW absorption [18−21]. Therefore, the use of the natural micron-scale hollow tubular structure of kapok fibers as a template to prepare SiC microtubes offers a novel and feasible strategy for constructing hierarchical porous SiC aerogels.

In this study, we constructed a hierarchical porous SiC microtube/nanowire composite aerogel through a synergistic process comprising high-temperature carbonization, freeze-forming, and carbothermal reduction, utilizing kapok fibers as the biomass template. The transformation mechanism from kapok fibers to SiC microtubes was systematically investigated, revealing the structure‒performance relationships between hierarchical pores (microscale lumens and nanowire networks) and material properties. The resulting aerogel exhibits outstanding comprehensive performance while maintaining cost efficiency: superior EMW absorption (minimum reflection loss (RL) of –56.39 dB with an effective bandwidth of 6.04 GHz at a thickness of 2.0 mm), excellent thermal insulation (0.021 W·m–1·K–1), high compressive strength (2.85 MPa), and robust multipollutant adsorption capacity (45–67 times its own weight). This work offers novel design strategies and technical approaches for the development of high-performance, economically viable SiC aerogels.

### 2.1 Materials

Kapok fibers were supplied by Zichun Textile Co., Ltd., China. Agarose gel (analytical grade, 3000–9000 g·mol−1), trimethoxymethylsilane (C4H12O3Si, 98%), and SiO2 sol (30%) were obtained from Shanghai Mobei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China. Silica and silicon powders were obtained from Anrui Nano Materials Co., Ltd., China.

### 2.2 Preparation of microtube aerogels

The preparation process of the microtube aerogel is illustrated in Fig. 1. Initially, kapok fibers were carbonized at 850 °C in a tube furnace to produce carbon microtubes. Subsequently, 0.2 g of carbonized kapok fibers (in carbon microtubes), 0.05 g of agarose gel, and 1.5 mL of SiO2 sol were dispersed in 50 mL of deionized water via vigorous mechanical stirring followed by ultrasonication. The resulting mixture was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and subjected to vacuum freeze-drying for 48 h to obtain the aerogel precursor. Finally, the samples were pyrolyzed by heating to 850 °C at a rate of 5 °C·min–1 and held for 2 h under an argon atmosphere to obtain a C microtube aerogel (C-MTA). The obtained C-MTA was placed above the mixed powder of silica and silicon (mass ratio of 7 : 1), and a C/SiC microtube aerogel (C/SiC-MTA) was obtained by heating to 1300 °C at a rate of 5 °C·min–1 and holding for 2 h under an argon atmosphere. A SiC microtube/nanowire aerogel (SiC-MTNA) was subsequently obtained by further heating to 1450 °C at a rate of 5 °C·min–1 and maintaining it for 2 h under the same atmosphere. Hydrophobic modification of SiC-MTNA was performed via chemical vapor deposition (CVD). During the CVD process, 1 g of aerogel and 5 mL of C4H12O3Si were placed separately in a sealed container and heated at 120 °C for 2 h.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The phase composition and microstructure of the synthesized samples were comprehensively characterized via various analytical techniques, including an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Shimadzu 7000, Shimadzu, Japan), a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Gatan-682, Japan), and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-2100F, Japan). Thermogravimetric (TG) analysis of the aerogel green body was conducted via a thermal analyzer (Netzsch STA449F3, Germany) under continuous argon flow to assess the thermal stability. The bulk density of the aerogel was calculated by measuring the geometric volume and mass of the sample. The true density was determined via a gas pycnometer (Micromeritics AccuPyc 1330, USA). The porosity of the aerogel was then calculated on the basis of its bulk density and true density. The nitrogen adsorption‒desorption isotherms and pore size distribution curves of the aerogels were analyzed via a nitrogen adsorption apparatus (NOVA 2200e, USA). The compressive strength of each sample was determined via a universal testing machine (JHYC, China). The room temperature and high-temperature thermal conductivity of the aerogel samples were measured via the transient plane source method with a thermal conductivity analyzer (Hot Disk-TPS 2500 S, Sweden), and the high-temperature thermal insulation performance was quantitatively evaluated via an infrared thermograph (Fluke TiX640 Expert HD, USA). The electromagnetic parameters were measured over the 2–18 GHz frequency range via the coaxial transmission line method with a vector network analyzer (Anritsu MS4644A, Japan).

## 3 Results and discussion

The microstructural morphology of the samples was characterized via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Figures 2(a) and 2(b) present SEM and optical images of C-MTAs. The inset in Fig. 2(a) demonstrates that C-MTAs can be placed atop dandelion structures, highlighting their ultralightweight nature. Additionally, SEM analysis (Figs. 2(a) and 2(b)) revealed a rich porous architecture within the aerogel. This hierarchical porosity is attributed to the freeze-forming process, during which the progressive growth of ice crystals compacts the microtubes into pore walls, subsequently leading to void formation upon sublimation [11,12].

The enlarged image in the upper right corner of Fig. 2(b) displays individual carbon microtubes, indicating that the hollow structure of the original kapok fiber (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) remained intact after carbonization. To gain deeper insight into the high-temperature behavior and structural stability of kapok fibers, a TG curve was recorded under an argon atmosphere from 25 to 1400 °C (Fig. S2 in the ESM), which revealed three distinct stages. Stage I (25–175 °C) results in a mass loss of approximately 7.32%, which is attributed primarily to the evaporation of adsorbed water. Stage II (175–800 °C) shows the most significant mass loss (42.79%), corresponding to the decomposition of organic components. In stage III (800–1400 °C), the mass loss markedly decreases to only 2.13%. This indicates substantial material stability within this high-temperature range, and the small loss may be due to the further decomposition of residual substances. The TG results clearly demonstrated that the kapok fiber matrix stabilized after the main mass loss stage, providing the thermodynamic basis for preserving its hollow structure during subsequent high-temperature processing.

Following pyrolysis at 1300 °C (Figs. 2(c) and 2(d)), the aerogel surface exhibited a light gray hue, which may suggest the formation of SiC through reactions between the carbon microtubes and the mixed precursor powders. A small number of SiC nanowires are discernible in Fig. 2(d). Upon increasing the pyrolysis temperature to 1450 °C, a uniform light gray coloration was observed, with SEM analysis confirming the extensive formation of SiC nanowires throughout the aerogel matrix (Figs. 2(e) and 2(f)). The energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) results (Figs. 2(g) and 2(h)) revealed the abundant presence of Si in the microtubes (Si: 24.5 at%, C: 75.5 at%), indicating substantial conversion of carbon microtubes to SiC-dominated microtubes while retaining a carbon-rich phase. High-magnification imaging (Fig. 2(i)) revealed that the SiC microtubes consisted of densely packed particulate aggregates with compact pore walls. Elemental mapping analysis (Figs. 2(j)–2(l)) confirmed that these particulates were SiC, corroborating the phase transformation process.

A more detailed microstructural analysis of SiC-MTNA was performed via TEM. As shown in Fig. 2(n), the aerogel consists of SiC tube walls and SiC nanowires. High-magnification TEM images of these two components are presented in Figs. 2(m) and 2(o), respectively, revealing that both structures are composed of SiC nanocrystals. Numerous “dark spots” are observed within the SiC microtube walls, and a magnified view reveals that these dark spots are SiC nanocrystals. Additionally, Fig. 2(p) shows that a portion of the SiC nanowires display intertwined growth. The interfaces formed between adjacent nanowires, as well as those between nanowires and microtubes, are considered critical for effective EMW attenuation. These structural features collectively contribute to enhanced electromagnetic energy dissipation through interfacial polarization and multiple scattering mechanisms. In conclusion, we confirmed the successful preparation of multistage porous SiC fiber aerogels via microscopic morphology analysis.

Figure 3(a) illustrates the proposed formation mechanism of SiC microtubes and nanowires. When the temperature exceeds 1200 °C, the mixed Si and SiO2 powders generate SiO gas via Reaction (1), resulting in a high concentration of SiO vapor within the furnace tube during pyrolysis at both 1300 and 1450 °C. Upon encountering the carbon microtubes, the SiO gas reacts with carbon atoms through Reaction (2), forming SiC nuclei and CO gas along the pore walls [17]. These SiC nuclei progressively grow, transforming the original carbon pore walls into SiC structures. Simultaneously, the continuous production of SiO and CO gases facilitates the formation of additional SiC nuclei and CO2 gas via Reaction (3). Owing to the inherently low surface energy of the SiC (111) crystal plane, saturated SiC preferentially precipitates along this orientation, leading to the formation of nanowires. At 1300 °C, the limited diffusion of SiO gas within the carbon matrix, combined with the barrier effect of the nascent SiC layer, results in incomplete conversion of the carbon pore walls, leaving residual unreacted carbon. The insufficient CO output from the incomplete Reaction (2) impedes the efficiency of the gas-phase Reaction (3), thereby yielding only sparse SiC nanowires within the aerogel. In contrast, at 1450 °C, Reaction (2) proceeds to completion, producing a sufficient quantity of CO gas that robustly drives Reaction (3). This facilitates the retention of the aerogel’s three-dimensional porous microtube architecture while enabling the in situ* growth of abundant SiC nanowires through gas-phase reactions.

To further examine the influence of temperature on the structural evolution of microtube aerogels, Fig. 3(b) presents XRD patterns of samples pyrolyzed at 850, 1300, and 1450 °C. The sample treated at 850 °C exhibited a broad diffraction peak at 22.5°, characteristic of disordered carbon. Upon increasing the temperature to 1300 °C, two prominent β-SiC diffraction peaks emerged at 35.8° and 60.0°, attributable to the (111) and (220) crystal planes of 3C-SiC (JCPDS No. 29-1129) [9,22], respectively. Additionally, a faint diffraction peak corresponding to the (311) crystal plane was discernible at 71.8°, indicating that the carbon microtubes had initiated a reaction with silicon sources to form a C/SiC composite structure, albeit not fully converted. At 1450 °C, the characteristic peaks of the three β-SiC groups intensified significantly and exhibited sharper peak shapes. Concurrently, a weak peak appeared at approximately 33.6°, associated with a polycrystalline phase formed by stacking faults (SFs) within the SiC lattice [17]. These results indicate the complete transformation of carbon microtubes into SiC, which is consistent with the SEM observations.

The Raman spectra in Fig. 3(c) further characterize the structural evolution of microtubes at different pyrolysis temperatures. All three samples exhibit characteristic peaks at 1330 cm−1 (D band) and 1590 cm−1 (G band), corresponding to the degree of disorder due to structural defects (vibrations of sp3 hybridized carbon) in carbon and the graphitization structure reflecting the ordered lattice (in-plane stretching vibrations) of sp2 hybridized carbon, respectively [23]. The intensity ratio of D band to G band (I*D/*I*G) represents the degree of disorder in the graphite structure. As the treatment temperature increases from 850 to 1300 and then to 1450 °C, the *I*D/*I*G ratio increases from 0.99 to 1.02 and significantly increases to 1.19. This indicates reduced graphitic ordering and a continuous increase in the defect density. These changes are attributed primarily to silicon atoms penetrating the carbon framework during the carbothermal reduction process at elevated temperatures, resulting in Si‒C bond formation as well as induced lattice distortion. Notably, the characteristic peak observed at 782 cm−1 for the 1450 °C sample is assigned to the transverse optical (TO) phonon mode of β-SiC (3C-SiC) [24]. This directly confirms the formation of Si‒C bonds and the generation of the SiC crystalline phase. The significant increase in the *I*D/*I*G ratio for this sample further verifies that the SiC formation process is accompanied by substantial accumulation of carbon structural defects. These structural defects form many heterogeneous interfaces, which significantly improve the interface thermal resistance and the interface dipole polarization effect.

Figure S3 in the ESM shows the pore structure characteristics of SiC-MTNA through N2 adsorption‒desorption analysis. The type IV isotherm with H3-type hysteresis confirms a meso-macroporous hierarchical structure [25]. Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis revealed a specific surface area of 36.13 m2·g–1, with a pore size distribution (inset) demonstrating predominant mesopores (6–32 nm). True porosity measurements revealed that the obtained SiC-MTNA possesses a high porosity of 94.89%. Combined with SEM observations, the aerogel integrates micrometer-scale pores formed by bundled microtubes with a mesoporous network consisting of entangled nanowires, collectively establishing a rich hierarchical pore system.

The practical application of SiC-MTNAs necessitates superior mechanical robustness. The compression test results (Fig. 4(a)) indicate that a sample weighing only 0.04 g (with a density of 51.3 mg·cm–3) can withstand a load of 500 g, which is 12,500 times its own weight, without structural damage. Under extreme thermal conditions, the aerogel maintained structural integrity when exposed to a 1100 °C butane flame and exhibited no visible cracking after immersion in liquid nitrogen for 2 min (Fig. 4(b)), highlighting its wide operating temperature range. Thermogravimetric analysis (Fig. 4(c)) revealed that the sample mass remained nearly unchanged when heated to 1300 °C, indicating that no chemical reactions occurred. The observed mass increase above 900 °C in air is attributed to the oxidation of silicon-containing components into silica.

Comparative mechanical testing was performed on aerogels pyrolyzed at different temperatures (Fig. 4(d)). The results show that the sample prepared at 1450 °C reached a compressive strength of 2.85 MPa at 10% strain. This represents a 15.8-fold improvement over the aerogel derived at 850 °C. The enhanced mechanical performance (Fig. 4(e)) can be attributed to two key reinforcement mechanisms. First, stable pore-wall structures form through the interweaving of SiC nanowires and microtubes. This architecture helps reduce structural damage at junction points under compression. Second, the exceptional intrinsic strength of single-crystalline SiC nanowires [6,8] provides a rigid reinforcing framework. Furthermore, the nanowire network bridges adjacent microtubes, enabling efficient stress transfer and dissipation. This occurs through mechanisms such as nanowire pull-out and interfacial friction [16]. Together, these effects synergistically enhance the overall mechanical integrity.

The surface-modified SiC-MTNA material demonstrated remarkable oil-adsorption potential. Hydrophobicity characterization (Fig. 5(a) and Fig. S4 in the ESM) revealed that the as-prepared SiC-MTNA exhibited hydrophilic properties and could be fully immersed in water, whereas the modified sample displayed superhydrophobic characteristics with stable buoyancy on water surfaces. When forcibly submerged, the modified material exhibits specular reflection at the gas‒liquid interface, confirming effective air entrapment within its structure. Contact angle measurements revealed a postmodification angle of 135.1°, further validating its hydrophobic performance. Cyclic stability evaluation through adsorption‒combustion testing (Figs. 5(b) and 5(c)) demonstrated that the modified SiC-MTNA achieved complete adsorption of dyed ethanol within 5 s, maintaining nearly undiminished adsorption capacity through multiple cycles.

In the oil‒water separation experiments (Fig. 5(d)), the material exhibited exceptional adsorption performance for surface-floating dyed silicone oil, significantly reducing the residual oil content in treated water. Adsorption capacity assessments (Fig. 5(e)) revealed that the modified material achieves organic adsorption capacities ranging from 45 to 67 times its own weight for six substances, including n-hexane and soybean oil, with performance variations attributable to differences in the surface tension and density parameters of the target compounds. This outstanding adsorption capacity stems from the unique multilevel pore structure of the material (Fig. 5(f)), which is specifically manifested in the three-dimensional network structure formed by the interweaving of microtubes and nanowires, providing ideal physical adsorption sites for oil‒water separation. Moreover, the mesoporous size distribution of 6–32 nm (Fig. 3(c)) dominated the powerful capillary force to capture organic molecules effectively. In addition, the superhydrophobic surface (contact angle of 135.1°) ensures the selective adsorption of organic substances and the repulsion of water by reducing the surface energy, thereby demonstrating excellent adsorption capacity for organic solvents.

The SiC-MTNA material demonstrated exceptional thermal insulation properties. Figure 6(a) shows the variation in the thermal conductivity of the material from 25 to 1000 °C. As shown, it has an ultralow thermal conductivity of 0.021 W·m–1·K–1 at room temperature. While the thermal conductivity gradually increases with temperature, it remains at a low value of 0.052 W·m–1·K–1 even at 1000 °C, which is superior to most reported SiC-based nanofiber aerogel insulation materials [26−29] (Table S1 in the ESM).

To investigate the thermal insulation effect of SiC-MTNAs more directly, initial heating tests were conducted using an alcohol lamp to simulate conventional heat sources on fresh flowers (Fig. 6(b)). The experimental results revealed that unprotected flowers exhibited significant dehydration and withering after 10 s of continuous heating, whereas aerogel-protected flowers maintained their original morphology without notable deformation even after 90 s of exposure to the same heat source. For quantitative evaluation of the high-temperature protective performance of the material, the temperature change on the back side of the aerogel after heating was recorded by an infrared camera through the simulation of a high-temperature environment with a butane burner. As shown in Fig. 6(c), when a 1.3 cm thick SiC-MTNA was exposed to butane flame heating, the heated surface reached 1137.2 °C, whereas the back side maintained an exceptionally low temperature (Fig. 6(d), originates primarily from the aerogel’s abundant porous architecture. This encompasses the inherent hollow architecture of the microtubes, the hierarchical micro/nanoporous structures formed by the interconnection of microtubes and SiC nanowires, and the interstitial voids between these components. These multiscale porous networks effectively hinder heat transfer by suppressing both solid-phase conduction and gas-phase convection. In addition, both SiC microtubes and nanowires exhibit excellent infrared shielding capabilities, significantly attenuating infrared thermal radiation at elevated temperatures and thereby reducing the thermal conductivity under high-temperature conditions [30]. As a result, SiC-MTNA demonstrates exceptional thermal insulation performance across a wide temperature range, from ambient to high-temperature environments.

The EMW absorption performance of the three synthesized aerogel samples was also systematically investigated. Figure 7 displays the electromagnetic parameters of the respective aerogels. As shown in Figs. 7(a) and 7(b), the real part (ε*′, storing energy) and imaginary part (*ε*″, dissipating energy) of the complex permittivity [31,32] both exhibit an overall decreasing trend with increasing frequency. This behavior aligns with the characteristic pattern observed in dielectric-dominated wave-absorbing materials. The * ε′ and  ε″ ranges of the C-MTA pyrolyzed at 850 °C are 6.51–3.03 and 9.89–2.06, respectively. The C/SiC-MTA obtained through pyrolysis at 1300 °C exhibited expanded permittivity ranges of 9.53–4.19 for  ε′ and 11.29–3.83 for  ε″. This enhancement was attributed to the optimization of the carbon phase structure and the emergence of enhanced interfacial polarization effects at the newly formed C/SiC heterointerfaces within the microtubes. In contrast, SiC-MTNA synthesized at 1450 °C has the lowest  ε′ values (3.39–2.92), likely due to the intrinsically low electrical conductivity of SiC. However, its  ε″ values (6.82–3.93) are significantly higher than those of the other aerogels, indicating a superior capacity for EMW attenuation. The  ε″ spectrum of SiC-MTNA displays several pronounced fluctuation peaks, which are potentially attributable to interfacial polarization between the SiC microtubes and nanowires, as well as dipole polarization arising from structural defects within both components. The Cole‒Cole semicircles presented in Fig. 7(c) and Fig. S5 in the ESM further corroborate the presence of polarization relaxation processes within the aerogel. According to Debye relaxation theory, each semicircular arc corresponds to a discrete Debye relaxation mechanism [33−35]. SiC-MTNA results in the greatest number of semicircles, suggesting the involvement of multiple polarization relaxation mechanisms enabled by its rich heterogeneous interfaces and defect structures.

The SiC-MTNA displays the highest tan*δ* values, particularly exhibiting pronounced peaks in the 11.5–14.2 GHz frequency range, underscoring its superior high-frequency EMW attenuation performance. The attenuation constant (*α*), a critical indicator for assessing EMW absorption efficiency, is illustrated in Fig. 7(e). The SiC-MTNA consistently exhibits the highest *α* values from about 4 to 18 GHz, reaffirming its enhanced EMW attenuation capability within this broad frequency range. where *Z*0 and *Z*in represent the vacuum impedance and input impedance of the absorber, respectively, while *d*, *f*, and *c* denote the thickness of the absorbing material, frequency, and speed of light in vacuum, respectively. *ε*r stands for the complex permittivity, *μ*r represents the complex permeability. As illustrated in Figs. 7(f)–7(h) and Fig. S6 in the ESM, which depict the relationships among RL, frequency, and thickness for the three sample groups, C-MTA exhibited relatively weak EMW absorption performance. Its RL values remained above –10 dB across the entire 2–18 GHz frequency range and within the 1.5–5.0 mm thickness range (Figs. S6(a) and S6(b) in the ESM). This suboptimal performance is likely due to the intrinsically high electrical conductivity of the carbon materials, which tends to induce significant reflection of incident EMWs. Upon increasing the pyrolysis temperature to 1300 °C, the aerogel demonstrated markedly improved absorption performance, achieving an RLmin of –24.28 dB at 10.6 GHz with a sample thickness of 2.5 mm. Further increasing the pyrolysis temperature to 1450 °C resulted in the formation of SiC-MTNAs with the best EMW absorption performance. At a thickness of only 2.0 mm, SiC-MTNA achieves an RLmin of –56.39 dB at 14.7 GHz, along with an EAB of 6.04 GHz. Notably, RL values below –10 dB were consistently maintained across the full 1.5–5.0 mm thickness range, enabling near-complete absorption coverage across the C, X, and Ku bands (Fig. 7(i)).

The superior EMW absorption performance of SiC-MTNAs is directly related to their improved impedance matching characteristics, as reflected by the normalized input impedance ratio (*Z*in/*Z*0) [38,39]. Figures 7(j) and 7(k) display the frequency-dependent RL curves and corresponding *Z*in/*Z*0 ratios for the three aerogel groups at a thickness of 2.0 mm. Among these, SiC-MTNA exhibited *Z*in/*Z*0 values closest to 1, indicating optimal impedance matching and correlating with its superior RL performance at this thickness. A comparative evaluation of the minimum RL values and EABs reported for various SiC-based absorbers [11,16,17,27,40−43] (Fig. 7(l)) further underscores the outstanding performance of the current SiC-MTNA. Specifically, it achieves both the lowest RLmin and the broadest EAB among the compared materials, establishing its superiority in EMW absorption applications.

The radar cross-sectional (RCS) characteristics of square perfect electric conductor (PEC) plates and PEC plates coated with different MTAs under identical EMW irradiation conditions were simulated via computer simulation technology (CST), with a particular emphasis on analyzing the minimum RCS values [44,45]. The simulation parameters included a frequency of 14.7 GHz and a 2.0 mm coating thickness. Figures 8(a)–8(d) present 3D representations of RCS energy density distributions for four samples, characterized by spindle-shaped lobes of varying dimensions and orientations. Larger lobe areas correspond to stronger EMW reflectivity and weaker absorption performance. Notably, the lobe volumes were significantly reduced when the PEC surfaces were coated with MTAs, indicating effective EMW suppression and absorption capabilities. Among the samples, C-MTA resulted in the largest lobe volume, followed by C/SiC-MTA, whereas SiC-MTNA resulted in the smallest and nearly imperceptible lobe volume. This trend is consistent with the RL values recorded at a 2.0 mm thickness and 14.7 GHz frequency for the three aerogel groups (Fig. 7(j)). Figure 8(e) shows the RCS values of the uncoated PEC and the three aerogel-coated samples across an angular range from –90° to 90°. All aerogel-coated surfaces exhibited consistently lower RCS values than did the bare PEC, further validating the substantial EMW attenuation and stealth functionality imparted by the MTA coatings. Additionally, Fig. 8(f) presents a histogram comparing the RCS reduction values of the three aerogel samples relative to those of the uncoated PEC at fixed angles. SiC-MTNA exhibited the most significant RCS reduction, achieving 36.80 dB2 at a 0° incidence angle, thereby demonstrating exceptional potential for practical applications in EMW absorption technologies.

The exceptional EMW absorption performance of SiC-MTNAs is due to the synergistic interplay between their distinctive microstructure and multimechanism electromagnetic dissipation processes (Fig. 8(g)). The micron-scale SiC microtube skeleton and nanowire network optimize impedance matching (Fig. 7(k), Z*in/*Z*0 ≈ 1), minimizing reflection and promoting EMW penetration. Concurrently, abundant heterogeneous interfaces (microtube–nanowire junctions and nanowire interweaving sites) induce strong interfacial polarization [46,47], as validated by multiple Cole–Cole semicircles (Fig. 7(c)) and sustained high attenuation constants across 2–18 GHz. Furthermore, high-density defects (Fig. 3(c), *I*D/*I*G = 1.19) from carbothermal reduction act as dipole polarization centers for orientation polarization [48−50], dominating loss at higher frequencies (peak tan*δ* at 11.5–14.2 GHz). Conductive SiC microtube walls and nanowire networks further contribute to conduction loss. Ultimately, EMWs undergo multiple reflections and scattering in the three-dimensional hierarchical pores formed by microtubule cavities and nanowire networks, causing three core mechanisms—interface polarization, defect polarization, and conductivity loss—to produce a synergistic enhancement effect, efficiently converting electromagnetic energy into thermal energy.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, this study successfully fabricated SiC-MTNA with a highly porous architecture by employing natural kapok fibers as structural templates through a sequence of high-temperature carbonization, freeze assembly, freeze-drying, and carbothermal reduction processes. The resulting hierarchical porous structure, composed of interconnected SiC microtubes and nanowires, facilitates multiscale synergistic enhancement effects. This distinctive morphology endows the aerogel with excellent EMW absorption performance, achieving an RLmin of –56.39 dB at 14.7 GHz and an EAB of 6.04 GHz. In addition, the material demonstrates robust mechanical strength with a compressive strength of 2.85 MPa, along with remarkable thermal insulation, exhibiting a thermal conductivity of 0.021 W·m–1·K–1 at 25 °C and retaining a low value of 0.052 W·m–1·K–1 at 1000 °C. Following surface modification, the aerogel also has a high organic solvent adsorption capacity, ranging from 45 to 67 times its own weight. This work offers valuable insights into the design of multifunctional, cost-effective SiC-based nanofiber aerogels. Furthermore, the repeatability and reliability of the preparation process provide new ideas for the development of multifunctional and high-performance SiC-based fiber aerogels in the future.
