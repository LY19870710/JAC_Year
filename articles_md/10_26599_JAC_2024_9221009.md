## 1 Introduction

Tantalum and its alloys exhibit high melting points [1], exceptional high-temperature mechanical properties, and superior creep strengths [2]. They are regarded as highly promising structural materials for the aerospace and nuclear industries [3,4]. However, tantalum alloys exhibit poor resistance to oxidation in high-temperature and oxygen-rich environments. Typically, tantalum alloys undergo rapid oxidation above 500 °C, and the primary oxide formed is Ta2O5 [5]. The oxide is neither dense nor robust and shows a significant difference in the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) compared with that of the Ta substrate, which makes it unable to block the penetration of oxygen [6]. Consequently, it tends to fail rapidly at temperatures well below its operating temperature. Therefore, reliable thermal protection coatings are urgently needed to enhance the oxidation resistance of tantalum alloys, which is crucial for expanding their application temperature range and application fields [7,8].

Ultra-high-temperature ceramics (UHTCs) are chemically and structurally stable at high temperatures, low oxygen, and extreme air erosion conditions and typically include several high-melting-point transition metal compounds, including borides, carbides, and oxides [9]. Among them, carbide ceramics [10] have a high melting point of more than 3000 °C, better thermal shock resistance, and higher high-temperature strength, making them a reliable choice for improving the oxidation resistance of tantalum alloys. Ceramic/metal carbide coatings have excellent physicochemical properties, such as high melting point, high hardness, and good thermal conductivity [11,12]. The oxide it forms has a low oxygen diffusion rate. This effectively hinders the inward diffusion of oxygen [13]. Temperature tolerance and bonding strength with the substrate are the two most critical indicators determining the comprehensive ablation resistance capability of the thermal protection coating on the Ta alloy. The former dictates the coating’s ablation resistance temperature and duration. The latter ensures the integrity of the coating to prevent peeling off during its service life. Currently, four thermal protection coating solutions are either applied or expected to be applied to Ta–W alloys.

Slurry sintering [14] and pack cementation (PC) [15] are common techniques for preparing refractory metal silicide (RMSi2) coatings. A representative study by Li *et al*. [16] demonstrated that the prepared MoSi2–WSi2–HfSi2–TiSi2 coating effectively protected the Ta substrate from oxidation at 1800 °C for 10.2 h. The reported oxidation resistance of the RMSi2 coatings was all attributed to the formation of a silicate film (SiO2, ZrSiO4, HfSiO4, etc.) with low oxygen permeability and moderate viscosity, as well as excellent self-healing ability. Nevertheless, the temperature tolerance of refractory metal silicide coating materials is limited to 1800 °C, as the oxidation products of the coating are predominantly SiO2 phase, along with minor quantities of high melting point oxides such as ZrO2 or HfO2. The preparation of coatings with a higher content of Hf or Zr and a lower content of Si on the surface of refractory alloys poses a significant challenge for the preparation of thermally protective coatings on tantalum alloys.

Plasma spray and chemical vapor deposition (CVD) are two candidate surface coating techniques that can be used to prepare UHTC thermal protection coatings on Ta–W alloys, although little research has been reported. Plasma-sprayed UHTC coatings present drawbacks such as high porosity, weak adhesion to the substrate, and lamellar stacking characteristics [17,18]. Furthermore, CVD-UHTC coatings have a limited thickness (usually no more than 50 μm for each layer). The low deposition efficiency accompanied by the high cost of CVD further limits its industry application [19,20].

In summary, the current surface coating solutions continue to struggle to meet both requirements simultaneously. This challenge hinders the ability to provide Ta–W alloys with enhanced oxidation and ablation resistance at temperatures exceeding 2000 °C.

In recent years, our research group has been actively developing laser surface technology for fabricating UHTC coatings, as the most significant technical advantage of this method lies in the metallurgical bond formed between the prepared ceramic coating and the metal substrate [21,22]. Moreover, the rapid cooling and heating of the laser cladding process results in a finer microstructure and a denser coating. Nevertheless, current research confirms that it is difficult to produce intact UHTC coatings with laser surface technology [23] because of the extremely high energy density of the laser and the inherent brittleness of UHTC materials. A representative work from the research group of the US Air Force Research Laboratory (AFRL) [24] has attempted to prepare a ZrB2–ZrC–B4C composite ceramic coating on a tungsten substrate by selective laser melting (SLM) technology for the first time. However, the thermal expansion mismatch between the ceramic coating and the substrate has been proven to be detrimental to coating integrity.

The high-speed laser cladding (HSLC) has alleviated these problems [25]. HSLC technology uses a positive defocusing method to achieve the best coupling between the laser and powder by adjusting the relative position of the laser focal plane and the powder focal plane (Fig. 2(a)). With a specific energy input, most laser energy is directed toward the powder, with a powder utilization rate of up to 85%. Only a small fraction of laser energy penetrates the powder beam and impacts the rapidly moving workpiece surface, forming a micro-melt pool there. Upon rapid solidification, a coating with a metallurgical bond to the surface of the workpiece is obtained, with a dilution rate generally below 5% [26,27]. Our team recently employed HSLC technology to prepare a UHTC composite coating with metallurgical bonding characteristics on the surface of a tantalum substrate [21]. The coating primarily comprises (Zr,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phase with a volume content of more than 90% and Ta(W) metal solid solution phase. The innovative design facilitates continuous transitions in microstructure and properties from the substrate to the top layer of the coating. However, the main coating still contains a small content of refractory metal phase, which is detrimental to the high-temperature ablation resistance of the coating.

In this study, the HSLC process was first used to prepare an UHTC–refractory metal composite coating with a metallurgical bond on a Ta–10W substrate. The UHTC–refractory metal composite coating then undergoes silicification through the pack cementation process. This process aims to transform the refractory metal phase in the coating into the corresponding silicide phase, resulting in a UHTC–RMSi2 composite coating. Subsequently, the microstructure and ablation mechanism of the UHTC–RMSi2 composite coating were systematically investigated, and the results revealed excellent anti-ablation resistance at temperatures exceeding 2300 °C for the Ta–10W substrate.

### 2.1 Preparation of powder

High-purity hafnium carbide powder (purity: 99.9%, particle size: 100–500 nm, Jilin Changyu Advanced Materials Co., Ltd., China) was added to hot water at a solid content ratio of 50%. Subsequently, 1% polyethylene glycol (PEG, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) was added as a dispersant, followed by the addition of an 8% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) solution to obtain a mixed slurry. The mixed slurry was agitated using a mechanical mixer at a speed of 500 r/min for 2 h. The near-spherical HfC micron-level composite powder was prepared through a spray granulation device. The specific preparation conditions were as follows: an inlet air temperature of 190 °C, an outlet air temperature of 130 °C, an atomizer frequency of 50 Hz, and a peristaltic pump speed of 10 r/min. Finally, the composite powder obtained was sieved to obtain the required HfC ceramic powder. The flowability of the prepared powder was 56 s/50 g. Figure 1(a) exhibits the micro-morphologies of the spray granulated powder, revealing that the majority of the powder possesses good sphericity. Figure 1(b) illustrates the uniform distribution of C and Hf elements on the surface of the spherical powder. The size distribution statistics of the powders show that the *D*ave (average diameter) of the powders is approximately 16.08 μm. The powders have good homogeneity and flowability, which is favorable for the subsequent HSLC process.

### 2.2 Preparation of the coating

Ta–10W refractory alloy (Beijing Zhongke Yannuo New Materials Technology Co., Ltd., China) was selected as the substrate. A sample with a size of 30 mm × 20 mm × 5 mm was obtained by wire electrical discharge machining and sequentially polished with 400#, 600#, and 800# waterproof sandpaper. Finally, the prepared samples were sandblasted and ultrasonically cleaned to remove surface grease. The HSLC experiment for coating preparation was performed after drying the HfC ceramic powder at 120 °C for 30 min. The coating was deposited through an RC-LCD-4000-IPG HSLC system (IPG (Beijing) Fiber Laser Technology Co., Ltd., China) with a synchronous powder feeder. Before the HSLC system was started, the laser focus and powder focus were adjusted to an overlapping position and 1 mm away from the substrate surface. The schematic diagram of the HSLC process is displayed in Fig. 2(a). The specific process parameters are presented in Table 1.

Si powder was used as the source powder; Al2O3 and Y2O3 powders were used as fillers to prevent the source powder from agglomerating during deposition; NaF powder was used as an activator to activate the generation and adsorption of infiltrating atoms on the sample surface during pack cementation (Si, Al2O3, NaF, and Y2O3 powders are all derived from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China). After ball milling, the siliconized powder, weighing 12 g, was placed into a crucible along with the sample for compaction. An appropriate amount of neutral alumina and silica sol was mixed to prepare a sealing slurry to seal the gap between the crucible lid and the crucible. The sealed crucible was put into the center of the heating tube of the tube furnace, which was then evacuated. Argon was introduced as the shielding gas during pack cementation. The flow rate of argon was 150 mL/min. The heating rate of the tube furnace was set to 10 °C/min. The temperature was increased to 1200 °C and held for 24 h. After that, the furnace was cooled to room temperature at a rate of 10 °C/min. The schematic diagram of the PC process is displayed in Fig. 2(b). A detailed process exploration and comparison of the ablation resistance of coatings prepared with different process parameters can be found in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

### 2.3 Ablation test

A plasma ablation torch was used to perform the ablation test on the sample, with real-time flow rates of 60.1 and 17.9 L/min for argon and nitrogen to form the plasma, a real-time current of 700 A, and an operating voltage of 72.8 V. During the plasma ablation process, argon and nitrogen can be excited to form plasma, generating high temperatures and energetic particles that are used to ablate the sample. Furthermore, the distance between the ablation torch and samples was 60 mm, with a heat flux density of 8.0 MW/m2. The surface central temperature of the sample exceeded 2300 °C, which was monitored by an infrared thermometer. The schematic diagram of the plasma torch ablation unit is shown in Fig. 3. The dimensions of the ablated sample were 30 mm × 20 mm × 5 mm. ### 2.4 Characterizations

The phase composition of the coating was characterized and analyzed using a D/MAX-2600 X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Rigaku, Japan). The data were collected at scattering angles (2*θ*) in the range of 10°–90° with a scanning speed of 5 (°)/min and a step length of 0.02°. The microstructure and morphology of the coating after ablation were characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Gemini SEM 300, ZEISS, Germany). A coating with a length of 5 μm was cut on the samples using a focused ion beam (FIB; Helios 5 CX, Thermo Scientific, USA) and characterized by a high-spatial-resolution transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200S, Thermo Scientific, USA).

### 3.1 Morphological analysis of the HSLC coating

Figure 4 shows the XRD pattern of the HSLC coating. The coating mainly comprises a (Hf,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phase and a Ta(W) metal solid solution phase. This coating is labeled as (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) coating. By applying the Bragg equation: 2*d*sin*θ* = *nλ*, the lattice constant of the (Hf,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phase is calculated to be 4.568 Å, which is smaller than the lattice constant (4.641 Å) of the HfC standard PDF card (PDF#73-0475). Owing to the smaller tantalum atom (with an atomic radius of 0.209 nm) partially replacing the larger hafnium atom (with an atomic radius of 0.216 nm) in the HfC lattice, the (Hf,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phase forms. The resulting stress causes the lattice to contract, meaning that the lattice constant decreases [28], leading to a greater angle shift in the diffraction peak (2*θ*) of the (Hf,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phase in the coating than in the standard diffraction peak of HfC [29,30] (Figs. 4(b) and 4(c)). During the preparation process of HSLC technology, the HfC powder and Ta(W) material on the surface of the substrate are fully melted under the effect of a high-density laser. Moreover, the substrate elements diffuse into the molten pool of the coating through the Marangoni effect [31,32]. Additionally, the Ta(W) metal phase enters the coating and reacts in the liquid molten pool to form a (Hf,Ta)C solid solution.

Figure 5(a) displays the cross-section SEM morphology of the (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) coating. The image reveals that the coating with a thickness of ~370 μm is nearly dense with few irregular pores. The coating–substrate interface shows good metallurgical bonding. There is a small amount of un-melted HfC feedstock powder near the upper part of the interface, which corresponds well with the HfC peak in the XRD pattern (Fig. 4). The distributions of Hf, Ta, C, and W elements within the two phases with different grayscale in the top area A of the coating were meticulously analyzed using EDS mapping (Figs. 5(b) and 5(c)). The gray-black phase mainly contains Hf, Ta, and C elements. Combined with the XRD analysis, it is deduced that the gray-black phase corresponds to the (Hf,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phase (spot 2). Moreover, the gray-white phase mainly contains Ta element and a small amount of W, which is analyzed as the Ta(W) metal solid solution phase (spot 1).

The variations in Hf, Ta, and W elements from the top to the bottom of the coating were tested by EDS line scanning, as shown in Fig. 6(b). One can find that Hf, Ta, and W elements obviously change around the coating–substrate interface, while there is no distinct evolution of the above elements in the main coating. During the preparation of HSLC technology, owing to the existence of a temperature gradient inside the melt pool, the area near the heat source is hotter and has a higher surface tension. There is a strong Marangoni effect in the molten pool, and the substrate elements diffuse into the coating, leading to a gradual change in the content of ceramic and metal phases at the interface. However, the liquid melt pool solidifies in a very short time [33], preventing the substrate elements from completely diffusing to the coating surface. Instead, the element content decreases gradually with increasing distance from the coating–substrate interface.

The distributions of (Hf,Ta)C and Ta(W) phases were further analyzed by high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) scanning technology (Fig. 6(e)). Elements Hf and C and a small amount of Ta are concentrated in the gray-black phase, which is analyzed as the (Hf,Ta)C phase. Moreover, Ta and W elements are mainly in the bright-white phase, which is inferred to be the Ta(W) metal phase. The above results are consistent with the diffraction peaks of the two phases observed via XRD (Fig. 4).

To further investigate the crystal structure characteristics of the (Hf,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phase and Ta(W) metal solid solution phase, a thin TEM sample from the area A in Fig. 7(c) was prepared by the FIB technique. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern with a [ 1¯  1¯  1¯] zone axis taken from the bright white phase area in the inset of Fig. 7(a) is indexed as the Ta(W) solid solution phase that has a body-centered cubic (BCC) crystal structure. Figure 7(b) shows the region with the [ 2¯  1¯  1¯] zone axis selected in the gray-black area, which is analyzed as the (Hf,Ta)C solid solution phase that has a face-centered cubic (FCC) crystal structure. The above results are consistent with the XRD analysis (Fig. 4). For the outer silicon diffusion layer, two different phases are identified according to the SEM and EDS mapping results of the area A (Fig. 9(b)) as well as the XRD results (Fig. 8): the gray-black TaSi2 phase and gray-white (Hf,Ta)C phase. The PC process at 1200 °C for 24 h leads to the formation of longitudinal microcracks inside the outer silicon diffusion layer. The formation of microcracks is also related to the diffusion of Si atoms in the coating. It has been reported that as the temperature rises, the concentration of lattice vacancies increases. The movement direction of these vacancies is opposite to the direction of Si atoms that infiltrate the coating during the pack cementation process. When more vacancies are present, microcracks are formed [34]. In addition, the formation of microcracks is also related to the difference in the coefficient of thermal expansion between (Hf,Ta)C–TaSi2 coating and (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) coating. There is a large difference in the thermal expansion coefficients between the (Hf,Ta)C–TaSi2 coating and (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) coating. During the PC process, this difference leads to thermal stresses between the (Hf,Ta)C–TaSi2 coating and (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) coating, which ultimately leads to cracking of the (Hf,Ta)C–TaSi2 coating.

Interestingly, an obvious interdiffusion diffusion layer is observed at the interface between the outer diffusion layer and the inner (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) layer (Fig. 9(c)). According to the EDS results (spot 3 in area B), the atomic ratio of Ta to Si is approximately 5 : 3, indicating that the main phase of this layer is Ta5Si3 [35]. The formation of the interdiffusion diffusion layer can mitigate the difference in composition to a certain extent, effectively preventing the propagation of longitudinal cracks from the outer diffusion layer into the inner (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) layer. The inner layer that does not react with Si during the PC process still exhibits the microstructure characteristics of the (Hf,Ta)C and Ta(W) solid solution phases (area C).

### 3.3 Ablation behavior of (Hf,Ta)C–TaSi<sub>2</sub> composite coating

To determine the specific effect of the coating on the ablative properties of TaW alloys, we tested the bonding energy of both the (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) coating and (Hf,Ta)C–TaSi2 coating. The detailed data are provided in Section S1-2 in the ESM. Moreover, we carried out ablation experiments on the (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) coating and (Hf,Ta)C–TaSi2 coating. The (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) coating was destructively ablated, and the substrate was exposed after continuous ablation at 2000 °C for 12 s. However, the (Hf,Ta)C–TaSi2 coating remained stable after 300 s of constant ablation at 2300 °C. Details of the ablation process can be found in Section S1-3 in the ESM.

Figure 11(a) depicts the surface central temperature of (Hf,Ta)C–TaSi2 coating as a function of ablation time during the plasma ablation process under a heat flux of 8.0 MW/m2. Details of the comparative ablation results for different heat flux densities can be found in Section S1-3 in the ESM. The surface temperature remains stable above 2300 °C, with no abrupt changes observed. This finding indicates that the surface structural integrity and stability of the oxide layer of the coating can be preserved throughout the entire ablation process. Figure 11(b) shows the temperature distribution profile on the ablated surface. During the steady ablation process, the surface temperature gradient from the central region to the edges is approximately 700 °C. This temperature difference results in significant alterations in the coating structure after ablation. The macroscopic morphology of the coating surface throughout the ablation process (Fig. 11(c)) shows that the molten phase on the surface is gradually blown away. After ablation, the sample surface is entirely covered by a layer of white oxide with no obvious ablation pits. The ablation pattern of the coating is caused by the synergistic effect of mechanical scouring during plasma ablation and the oxidative behavior of the coating. The linear ablation rate of the coating is −0.67 µm/s, and *R*l of the sample is negative, which is due to the volume expansion of the coating. Oxidation of the coating during rapid cooling and phase transition of the oxide causes the volume expansion.

(1) During the ablation process, numerous gaseous products (Ta2O5, SiO, CO, etc.) [42] are violently released and fail to escape promptly, resulting in the formation of extensive pores and microcracks within the coating.

(2) When HfC is oxidized to HfO2, a volume expansion of approximately 30% occurs. The volume expansion caused by the phase change can also lead to crack formation [43].

(3) The thermal stress caused by rapid cooling after ablation between the oxide layer and the coating can also cause microcracks [41]. Microcracks can effectively relieve the thermal stress caused by CTE mismatch, which is beneficial to maintaining the integrity of the coating structure and preventing the coating from failing prematurely during the ablation process.

The loose top layer with a thickness of approximately 50 μm (Fig. 15(c)) exhibits the typical ablation characteristics of accumulation and combination of oxide particles. The top of the coating experiences severe external oxidation, and the higher temperature also leads to the growth of external oxide grains [44]. The EDS analysis reveals that the main component of the oxide particles is HfO2 (spot 1). The top layer features many pores, which are caused by the evaporation of the substantial SiO2 glass phase formed during the ablation process. Therefore, a minor amount of residual Si is detected inside the pores (spot 2).

The middle layer of the coating is relatively dense (Fig. 15(d)), but some transverse cracks have formed due to the volume expansion caused by the phase change and rapid cooling after ablation. The main component of the middle layer is also HfO2 (spot 3).

According to the results of EDS mapping (Fig. 15(g)), the bottom layer can be categorized into Hf–Ta–O composite glass film (Hf6Ta2O17 phase with little Ta2O5 phase (spot 4)) and (Hf,Ta)Cx*O*y* oxide film. The formation of (Hf,Ta)C*x*O*y* can be attributed to the diffusion of oxygen atoms to the surface of the carbide, where oxygen can occupy carbon vacancies. During the ablation process, (Hf,Ta)C is initially oxidized to form (Hf,Ta)C*x*O*y* oxycarbide and carbon phases [45]; thus, the carbon formed remains in the oxide layer as carbon precipitates. At temperatures above ~600 °C, carbon precipitates and oxidizes to CO2/CO, departing the coating [46,47]. The Hf–Ta–O composite glass film can fill the pores and microcracks in the oxide layer and prevent oxygen from penetrating, which further protects the interior of the coating from oxidation. The molten phases formed on the surface of the oxidized coating are easily washed away by the plasma flame, while these phases can remain in the inner middle layer due to partial pinning by the HfO2 solid particles.

The EDS line scan analysis exhibits the evolution of the element from the ablated top layer to the unoxidized coating (Fig. 15(b)). With increasing oxidation depth, the O content decreases while the Ta content clearly increases. After long-term ablation, even in the most severely ablated environment in the center of the ablation zone, the ablation behavior of the coating is restricted to the (Hf,Ta)C–TaSi2 coating and the top area of the (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) coating. In contrast, most of the (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) coatings remain largely unaffected by oxidation (Fig. 15(f)), retaining structural integrity and exhibiting superior resistance to ablation.

Notably, Refs. [14−16] mainly report the static oxidation behavior of thermal protective coatings prepared on Ta alloys. Furthermore, their resistance temperature does not exceed 1800 °C. Therefore, we conclude that the UHTC-RMSi2 composite coating prepared in this study exhibits the highest ablation resistance to firmly protect the Ta alloys.

Based on the above, Reactions (5)–(17) occur mainly during the ablation process:

(5) 2(Hf,Ta)C(s)+5O2(g)=2HfO2(s)+Ta2O5(s)+2CO(g)

(6) 2TaSi2(s)+6.5O2(g)=Ta2O5(s)+4SiO2(l)

(7) HfC(s)+1.5O2(g)=HfO2(s)+CO(g)

(8) HfC(s)+2O2(g)=HfO2(s)+CO2(g)

(9) 2TaC(s)+3.5O2(g)=Ta2O5(s)+2CO(g)

(10) TaC(s)+O2(g)=TaO(g)+CO(g)

(11) 6HfO2(s)+Ta2O5(s)=Hf6Ta2O17(s)

(12) C(s)+O2(g)=CO2(g)

(13) C(s)+O2(g)=CO(g)

(14) Hf6Ta2O17(s)=Hf6Ta2O17(l)

(15) Ta2O5(s)=Ta2O5(l)

(16) SiO2(l)=SiO2(g)

(17) Ta2O5(l)=Ta2O5(g)

The standard Gibbs free energy (Δ*G*) values for Reactions (6)–(10) at different temperatures calculated using HSC 6.0 software are presented in Fig. 18(a). The oxidation reaction of TaSi2 phase results in the lowest Gibbs free energy, suggesting that this phase initially undergoes oxidation to form Ta2O5 and SiO2 at the onset of ablation (Reaction (6)). Then, (Hf,Ta)C phase undergoes oxidation (Reactions (7)–(9)) to form HfO2, Ta2O5, CO(g), and CO2(g). As the ablation temperature continues to rise, Ta2O5 transitions to a liquid phase (Reaction (15)). Subsequently, SiO2 and Ta2O5 further form their respective gaseous phases (Reactions (16) and (17)).

A solid solution reaction between HfO2 and Ta2O5 facilitates the formation of Hf6Ta2O17 (Reaction (11)) [39]. The protective mechanism of the anti-ablation coating was further revealed based on the binary phase diagram of HfO2–Ta2O5 system oxides [48]. As shown in Fig. 18(b), there is an intermediate compound Hf6Ta2O17 in the HfO2–Ta2O5 system, which can be stable only within a certain range of HfO2 fractions (0.80–0.90). In this work, the Hf/Ta ratio in the (Hf,Ta)C–TaSi2 coating is not high. However, the Ta2O5 with a low melting point (1872 °C [49]) formed in the ablation center during the ablation process was quickly blown away, resulting in a higher Hf/Ta ratio in the top layer (spots 1 and 2 in Fig. 15(b), line 2 in Fig. 18(b)) and middle layer (spot 3 in Fig. 15(d), line 3 in Fig. 18(b)) than in the bottom layer (spot 4 in Fig. 15(e), line 1 in Fig. 18(b)).

*
Fig. 19Schematic diagram of ablation mechanism of coating.

(1) In the initial stage of ablation, (Hf,Ta)C, TaSi2, and HfC in the composite coating undergo initial oxidation to form (Hf,Ta)Cx*O*y*, HfO2, Ta2O5, and SiO2. Consequently, a relatively dense solid‒liquid oxide film formed on the surface of the coating in a short period. HfO2 with a high melting point does not easily melt during the ablation process, which increases the adhesion between the oxide layer and substrate, thereby enhancing the resistance of the coating to mechanical scouring. This oxide film restricts the penetration of oxygen into the interior. Simultaneously, some gaseous products (SiO, CO, etc.) escape.

As the surface temperature increases and the ablation time is extended, a large amount of SiO2 glass phase evaporates violently, producing SiO and CO high-vapor-pressure oxides. These gas products are more easily transferred to the coating surface. The thermal stress and intense release of gas products induce microcracks and pores on the coating surface. SiO2 generated by the surface reaction is essentially exhausted.

(2) As ablation continues, a solid‒solution sintering reaction occurs between HfO2 and Ta2O5, forming Hf6Ta2O17. The melting points of Ta2O5 and Hf6Ta2O17 (between the melting points of Ta2O5 and HfO2) are relatively low, resulting in the melting of some oxides and the generation of an Hf‒Ta‒O glassy film. In addition, melting of Ta2O5 and Hf6Ta2O17 is endothermic process, which is beneficial for reducing the surface temperature of high-temperature coating. It has been reported that the Hf–Ta–O glassy film exhibits greater stability than the eutectic mixture of HfO2 and Ta2O5 [50]. The film can fill the pores of the solid oxide HfO2 and act as a barrier to oxygen diffusion, further protecting the interior of the coating from oxidation and thus enhancing its resistance to ablation. Therefore, the oxide film at this stage is expected to exhibit a porous HfO2 framework structure filled with a liquid Hf–Ta–O glass phase. The synergistic effects of Hf–Ta–O glass film and HfO2 in the coating will improve the high-temperature stability of the surface oxide film [51]. Hence, the formation of a Hf‒Ta‒O glassy film in the coating enhances the high-temperature stability of the surface oxide film [52]. Moreover, as the oxygen continues to diffuse to the inner part of the coating, (Hf,Ta)C*x*O*y* oxide forms in the bottom layer.

As the ablation time increases, the plasma flame easily washes away the Hf–Ta–O glassy film from the surface of the coating, leaving only the HfO2 phase on the outer surface of the oxide layer. HfO2 on the outermost layer becomes porous because of the strong airflow, while the HfO2 on the sub-surface layer is relatively dense. The solid oxide acts as a pin to the liquid oxide, reducing the loss of liquid oxide due to mechanical scouring so that some Hf–Ta–O glassy film remains at the bottom layer of the ablated coating (Fig. 15).

## 4 Conclusions

This study combines the technological advantages of HSLC and PC to prepare a unique UHTC composite coating. Through comprehensive analysis of the microstructure, phase composition, and ablation resistance of the coating, we reached the following conclusions:

(1) The HSLC coating, characterized by a dense microstructure and a thickness of approximately 370 μm, is mainly composed of a (Hf,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phase and a Ta(W) metal solid solution phase. The coating exhibits strong metallurgical bonding with the Ta(W) substrate.

(2) The (Hf,Ta)C–TaSi2 composite coating with a three-layer structure was then prepared by the subsequent PC process at 1200 °C for 24 h, achieving our original intention to convert the Ta(W) metal phase in the (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) coating into the corresponding silicide phase. An outer silicon-diffusion layer with a thickness of ~100 μm is mainly composed of (Hf,Ta)C and TaSi2 phases. An intermediate diffusion layer with a thickness of ~4 μm is mainly composed of Ta5Si3 phase, which effectively prevents the longitudinal cracks of the outer diffusion layer from spreading to the inner layer. In addition, the inner layer still exhibits the microstructure characteristics of (Hf,Ta)C‒Ta(W) coating.

(3) After plasma ablation at 2300 °C for 300 s, the linear ablation rate of the coating is −0.67 µm/s, and the ablated coating exhibits three distinct oxide layers: a loose HfO2 top layer, a dense HfO2 middle layer, and a slightly oxidized (Hf,Ta)C*x*O*y*–Hf–Ta–O glassy layer. After long-term ablation, even under the most severe conditions at the center of the ablation zone, the ablation behavior of the coating mainly occurs in the (Hf,Ta)C–TaSi2 outer layer. Most of the (Hf,Ta)C–Ta(W) coating remains largely unoxidized, retaining its structural integrity and exhibiting superior resistance to plasma ablation. In addition, W has little effect on the plasma ablation resistance of this new coating.
