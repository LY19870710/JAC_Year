## 1 Introduction

The hot-end components of aircraft and spacecraft are generally suffered from extreme service environments, which are characterized by thermo-oxygen coupling, high-temperature oxidation, erosion, and vibration [1]. Wireless surface acoustic wave (SAW) sensors, as a new type of intelligent sensors, hold great promise for *in-situ*, real-time monitoring of parameters such as temperature, heat flow, and strain on the surface of these components [2−6]. This capability is crucial for accurately assessing the health status of hot-end components and ensuring flight safety. Regarding the piezoelectric material as substrate including GaPO4, LiNbO3, and AlN, YCa4O(BO3)3 (YCOB) single crystals are the only available piezoelectric ceramics because of the recorded maximum operating temperature of 1250 °C and high-temperature stability [7−10]. Consequently, the thin-film electrode of the sensor core unit must exhibit excellent high-temperature conductivity, stability, and oxidation resistance. However, owing to their poor wettability with the substrate and large atomic diffusion coefficient, metal films such as Pt, Ti, and Cr are prone to agglomeration at high temperatures, which limits their application in harsh environments [11−13]. In contrast, ceramics possess excellent stability and long-term durability at high temperatures. As reported, tin-doped indium oxide (ITO) thin-film electrodes are known for their high-temperature stability, oxidation resistance, and excellent semiconductor behavior [14,15]. Nevertheless, structural damage leading to the failure of conductivity at 1100 °C is the obvious drawback of these ceramic materials because of the limited maximum operating temperature [16], which is disadvantageous for the electrical properties of the ceramics. Moreover, ultra-high temperature ceramics (UHTCs), such as HfC, HfN, and HfB₂, exhibit electrical conductivities on the order of 10⁴ S·cm−¹ [17,18], which makes them very promising as thin-film electrodes for SAW sensing applications in harsh environments. However, it is difficult to obtain stable ceramic coatings because of the possibility of oxidation at low temperatures, such as HfB2, which is easily oxidized at 800 °C [17,19]. Moreover, conventional methods for UHTC coatings, such as chemical vapor deposition (CVD) and magnetron sputtering methods, have problems such as slow growth rates and longtime preparation, limiting their application in harsh environments [20,21].

The polymer-derived ceramic (PDC) method has attracted considerable attention because of its advantages of rapid film preparation and adjustable film thickness [22,23]. Additionally, PDCs have adjustable electrical properties since the chemical constituents, phase compositions, and even microstructures of ceramic nanocomposites can be designed and tailored [24−26], including excellent dielectric properties [27], significant piezoresistive effects [28], and high-temperature semiconductor behavior [29]. Moreover, PDCs possess excellent high-temperature stability [30], oxidation resistance [31], and high-temperature creep resistance [32]. These properties make PDCs promising candidates for high-temperature thin-film electrode applications. However, studies have shown that the conductivity of PDC SiCN and SiBCN ceramics is on the order of 10−⁴ S·cm−1 at 700 °C and the conductivity of PDC SiC ceramics is on the order of 10−1 S·cm−1 at 700 °C [29,33,34]. High electrode resistance usually increases the insertion loss of the sensor. Hence, it is desirable and necessary to improve and research the high-temperature electrical conductivity of PDCs.

PDC SiHfBCN ceramics have been shown to be promising ceramics for ultra-high-temperature applications because of their excellent high-temperature stability and oxidation resistance at temperatures up to 1500 °C [35−37]. Additionally, prior studies [38,39] have reported that PDC SiHfBCN ceramics tend to produce a substantial amount of carbon and HfC nanocrystals, with the formation of a core‒shell structure after high-temperature annealing at 1700 °C. PDC SiHfBCN ceramics exhibit the potential for excellent electrical conductivity due to *in situ* generation of two-dimensional microstructures, such as hafnium-containing conductive phase and graphite carbon, to construct a three-dimensional conductive network [38,39]. Furthermore, the interface/surface effect of PDC films can accelerate the graphitization evolution of carbon with the formation of an enriched structure of free carbon, resulting in the current intensity at the interface/surface being orders of magnitude higher than that in the inner region [23]. SiHfBCN ceramic coatings prepared by the magnetron sputtering method have a good resistivity of 550 Ω·m (tested at 500 °C) [40]; however, the magnetron sputtering method has problems of a slow growth rate and long preparation time, and SiHfBCN ceramic coatings prepared by the PDC method have not been explored. Moreover, the high-temperature electrical conductivity of PDC SiHfBCN ceramic coatings, especially those above 1000 °C, has not yet been reported.

In this work, a smooth and dense SiHfBCN ceramic coating via the PDC approach was successfully fabricated, and its performance was systematically studied. The composition shows interesting microstructural evolution during processing, and the SiHfBCN ceramic coating endows the composite with high-temperature electrical conductivity. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, the relationship between microstructure and electrical conductivity at high temperatures was established to explain the conductive mechanism for the first time.

### 2.1 Materials

YCa4O(BO3)3 (YCOB) single-crystal wafers with a size of 10 mm × 10 mm × 1 mm and a cutting type of X-90° were purchased from the Institute of Crystal Materials, Shandong University, China. Polysilazane was purchased from the Institute of Chemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China. Tetrakis(diethylamido)hafnium(IV) (Hf(N(Et)2)4, TDEAH) and borane dimethyl sulfide complex (BH3·SMe2) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Methylbenzene was purchased from Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China. An argon atmosphere with a purity of 99.99% was used in the pyrolysis process.

### 2.2 Synthesis of SiHfBCN liquid precursor

The synthesis of liquid precursor was carried out in a high-purity argon (99.99%) atmosphere using the Schlenk technique. First, 27 mL polysilazane (PSN) and 27 mL anhydrous toluene were added to a 250 mL Schlenk flask equipped with an argon inlet and magnetic stirrer. Then, 12 mL Hf(N(Et)2)4 was added to the Schlenk flask in an ice bath. After magnetic stirring for 2 h, 30 mL BH3·SMe2 was slowly added dropwise to the mixture with a Schlenk flask in a liquid nitrogen vessel. The obtained solution was then stirred for 24 h at room temperature (RT). The detailed synthesis procedures can be found in Ref. [38]. The chemical structure of SiHfBCN precursor is shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Finally, the liquid precursor was obtained by diluting the resulting precursor and anhydrous toluene at a mass ratio of 1 : 1, denoted as SiHfBCN. Different mass ratios of the resulting precursor and anhydrous toluene were used for comparison. For comparison, SiHfBCN precursors with 6 mL Hf(N(Et)2)4 were prepared under the same conditions and are denoted as SiHfBCN-1.

### 2.3 Preparation of SiHfBCN ceramic coating

The YCOB wafers were ultrasonically cleaned with acetone, distilled water, and ethanol for 1 h, and then dried in an oven before use. The cleaned YCOB wafers were subsequently placed in a chemical deposition furnace to deposit a compact boron nitride (BN) layer. The air source system used was NH3–BCl3–Ar–H2, and the flow rates of NH3, BCl3, H2, and Ar were 30, 10, 50, and 50 standard cubic centimeters per minute (sccm). The deposition temperature was 650 °C, and the deposition time was 3 h.

Preparation of the SiHfBCN ceramic coating, including spin and cross-linking, was carried out in a glovebox. First, the liquid SiHfBCN precursor was dripped onto the YCOB/BN substrate until it spread throughout the substrate. The SiHfBCN precursor was spun for 6 s at 1000 r·min−1. Subsequently, the precursor film was cross-linked at 170 °C for 2 h. Finally, the cross-linked precursors were pyrolyzed under a high-purity argon atmosphere at 1000, 1050, 1100, 1150, and 1200 °C for 2 h at a heating rate of 1 °C·min−1. After pyrolysis, the resulting coating was cooled with furnace to RT. The fabrication procedure of the SiHfBCN ceramic coating is shown in Fig. 1.

### 2.4 Characterizations

The high-temperature electrical conductivity of the SiHfBCN ceramic coatings was tested by using a designed machine (CMT-4304, SANS, China) equipped with a four-point probe setup (ET9000, Eastchanging, China) at a heating rate of 3 °C·min−1 from RT to 1200 °C in Ar. The room temperature electrical conductivity was tested directly by a four-point probe and megger (TH2683A, Tonghui, China). The morphology and thickness of the ceramic coating were observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; S-2700; Hitachi, Japan). The precursors were analyzed by a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR; Nicolet iS50, Thermo Scientific, China). An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Rigaku-D/max-2400, Rigaku, Japan) was used for phase analysis of the samples. The order degree of the carbon phase was analyzed by a micro confocal Raman spectrometer (Raman; Alpha300R, WITec, Germany). The chemical bonding and elemental composition were analyzed by an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Axis Ultra, Kratos Analytical Ltd., UK). The viscosity was tested by a mathematical viscometer (NDJ-1S, Shanghai Yixin, China). The thermal expansion coefficient was tested by a thermal dilatometer (DIL402C, Netzsch, Germany). The Young’s modulus and hardness were tested by a nanoindentation apparatus (TI980, Hysitron, USA).

### 3.1 Micromorphology

To investigate the micromorphology evolution of the liquid precursor during polymer-to-ceramic conversion, the surface morphology of YCOB/BN, the cross-sectional morphologies of YCOB/BN/SiHfBCN, and the morphologies of different concentrations of precursors after ceramization are shown in Fig. 2. In our previous work, under conditions of low oxygen and high temperature (~1000 °C), YCOB decomposes due to the escape of oxygen atoms, resulting in stratification. The pre-deposition of a BN layer on the surface of the substrate can solve this problem [22]. According to the SEM images shown in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b), the surface of the BN layer is smooth and compact, and the surface of the coating prepared with YCOB/BN as the substrate is smooth, with the coating and the substrate closely bonded. This is because the escaping oxygen atoms at high temperatures react with the boron atoms in the BN coating to form boron–oxygen units, preventing further escape of oxygen atoms [22,41]. Owing to the presence of boron‒oxygen units, the BN layer on YCOB has good compatibility with the substrate. Moreover, the thermal expansion coefficient of the SiHfBCN coating is 3.1×10−6 K−1, which is close to the thermal expansion coefficient of CVD BN (2.3×10−6 K−1) [42]. There is no gap between the BN layer and SiHfBCN layer because of the close thermal expansion coefficient. As shown in Fig. 2(c), the surface of the coating prepared with the undiluted precursor exhibits many cracks. The surface of the coating prepared by diluting the precursor with toluene at a mass ratio of 4 : 1, as shown in Fig. 2(d), has relatively few cracks. As shown in Figs. 2(e) and 2(f), the surface of the coating prepared by diluting the precursor with toluene at mass ratios of 1 : 1 and 1 : 4 is smooth and crack-free. In addition, the viscosities of different dilutions of the SiHfBCN precursor are shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. With increasing dilution, the viscosity gradually decreases until it reaches 17.54 mPa·s. As shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM, the thicknesses of the ceramics gradually decrease with increasing dilution ratio. By using the diluted precursor with enhanced fluidity during spin-coating process, a thinner liquid precursor film can be obtained on the substrate. Therefore, during the substantial cross-linking and pyrolysis process, the gaseous products including toluene and small molecule compounds can be released faster and the uniform volume shrinkage can be realized [43,44]. Hence, a smooth SiHfBCN ceramic coating is obtained.

### 3.2 Composition and microstructural evolution

The microstructures of the SiHfBCN ceramic coatings were characterized via FTIR and XRD. According to Refs. [37,38], the FTIR spectrum of the SiHfBCN liquid precursor shows the same characteristic absorption bands, as shown in Fig. 4(a). As shown in Fig. 4(a), the N–H, C–H, Si–H, and Si–CH3 of the cross-linked SiHfBCN precursor almost decompose at 1000 °C since their characteristic bands vanish. This is due to dehydrogenation coupling amino transfer and decomposition of organic side chain groups during the pyrolysis process [45], which indicates that the transition from the polymer to the inorganic ceramics is finished at 1000 °C to form a ceramic coating. The Si–N peak at 1040 cm−1 [46], Hf–N peak at 945 cm−1 [45], and Si–C peak at 748 cm−1 [38] exist in the whole temperature range. With increasing pyrolysis temperature, the Si–C gradually becomes sharp from 1000 to 1200 °C, and the position shifts, indicating that phase separation begins to occur in the system and that the degree of order of the SiHfBCN ceramic coatings improves [24]. As shown in Fig. 4(b), the XRD patterns show that the SiHfBCN coatings remain amorphous in the pyrolysis temperature range of 1000–1200 °C.

*
Fig. 4(a) FTIR spectra of cross-linked SiHfBCN precursor and SiHfBCN coatings pyrolyzed at different temperatures and (b) XRD patterns of SiHfBCN coatings pyrolyzed at different temperatures.

Raman spectroscopy is highly sensitive to the structural evolution of carbon at high temperatures and is a crucial tool for characterizing free carbon. Figure 5(a) shows the Raman spectra of the SiHfBCN ceramic coatings pyrolyzed at different temperatures. All the spectra indicate the presence of both disorder-induced D and graphite-like G bands. The D-band at 1352 cm−1 corresponds to the presence of amorphous carbonaceous products, disordered carbon, or defects in graphite, whereas the G-band at 1580 cm−1 is attributed to the tensile vibration of sp² carbon in an ideal lattice, related to the signal from graphite [47]. Additionally, bands at approximately 2690 and 2920 cm−1 are attributed to overtones or combination modes of the D- and G-bands [48]. With increasing pyrolysis temperature, the intensities of the 2D and G+D bands become more pronounced, indicating progressive structural ordering of carbon and the formation of nanographite clusters. The graphitization grade of a carbon-containing material can be calculated using the I*D/*I*G ratio, where *I*D and *I*G are the intensities of the D- and G-bands, respectively [49]. As shown in Fig. 5(b), the value of *I*D/*I*G decreases with increasing pyrolysis temperature from 1000 to 1200 °C, indicating that the degree of graphitization of carbon increases and confirming the presence of graphitic carbon.

Fig. 5(a) Raman spectra and (b) I*D/*I*G ratios of SiHfBCN coatings pyrolyzed at different temperatures.

XPS was used to analyze the chemical bonding and elemental composition of the SiHfBCN ceramic coatings since it is a surface-sensitive quantitative spectroscopic technique. As shown in Fig. 6, the peaks of Hf 4f, C 1s, and Si 2p are subjected to peak-fitting processing using the Gaussian fitting method. Figure 6(a) shows that the peaks at 20.8 and 22.5 eV are assigned to Hf–O bonds, and the peaks at 13.1 and 15.1 eV are assigned to Hf–N bonds [50]. The peak separation of Si 2p (Fig. 6(b)) shows that the peaks at 102.4 and 101.4 eV are due to Si–N and Si–C [51]. For the high-resolution C 1s spectrum shown in Fig. 6(c), the major peaks appear at 282.8, 283.6, and 284.2 eV, which can be assigned to C–Si (sp³ hybridization), C=C (sp² hybridization), and C–C (sp³ hybridization), respectively [49,52]. According to the Hf 4f, Si 2p, and C 1s spectra, the SiHfBCN ceramic coatings contain SiCN, graphitic C, HfN, and HfO₂ phases, which is consistent with the results of FTIR and Raman spectra. With increasing pyrolysis temperature, the peaks shift to higher binding energies, which may be attributed to the formation of more Hf–N, Si–C, and C=C bonds. Moreover, the chemical bond contents of SiHfBCN coatings calculated by XPS are shown in Table 2. The concentration of Si‒C bonds increases with increasing pyrolysis temperature, revealing micro-scale structural separation processes in the Si matrix phase and the generation of free carbon as the pyrolysis temperature increases, resulting in the formation of more Si‒C bonds with free carbon [29]. The content of C=C and Hf–N bonds increases from 1000 to 1100 °C. From 1100 to 1200 °C, the C=C bond content increases, while the Hf‒N bond content remains nearly unchanged, which is consistent with the increased degree of graphitization characterized by the conversion of sp³ carbon to sp² carbon [51].

### 3.3 Electrical conductivity

The room-temperature resistivities of the SiHfBCN ceramic coatings pyrolyzed at different temperatures, which were tested by a four-point probe and megger, are shown in Fig. 7(a). The resistivity of the SiHfBCN coating decreases with increasing pyrolysis temperature. The SiHfBCN ceramic coatings pyrolyzed at 1200 °C have a room temperature resistivity of 1.516×108 Ω·m, which indicates an insulation behavior. For comparison, SiHfBCN-1 precursors with 6 mL of Hf(N(Et)2)4 were prepared under the same conditions. The room-temperature resistivities of the SiHfBCN-1 ceramic coatings pyrolyzed at different temperatures are shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM, indicating the worse resistivity under the same pyrolysis temperature compared with the room-temperature resistivity of the SiHfBCN ceramic coatings, revealing that the resistivity is closely related to hafnium element. Notably, the coatings prepared by diluting the precursor with toluene at mass ratios of 1 : 1 and 1 : 4 present different resistances and the same resistivities, as shown in Table S1 in the ESM. Furthermore, the resistivity is related only to the composition and not to the thickness. According to Table 2, Fig. 7(b) shows the relationship of the carbon bond content (C–Si (sp³ carbon), C=C (sp² carbon), and C–C (sp³ carbon)) with pyrolysis temperature. The increased C=C bonds and decreased C‒C bonds suggestan sp³‒sp² transition with increasing pyrolysis temperature. According to the Arrhenius relation [53] in Eq. (1), the activation energy of conductivity and the conversion of sp³ carbon to sp² carbon were calculated, and the results are shown in Figs. 7(c) and 7(d). The conductivity activation energy of the SiHfBCN coating is similar to the conversion activation energy of carbon from 1100 to 1200 °C, indicating that the decrease in resistivity in this temperature range is caused by the increase in sp² carbon phase content. The conductivity activation energy of the coating is much higher than the conversion activation energy of carbon from 1000 to 1100 °C, indicating that the growth of the sp² carbon phase is not the only factor leading to the decrease in resistivity in this temperature range.

It is known that the conductivity of semiconductor materials is usually directly related to the band gap of an intrinsic semiconductor. Previous studies have shown that the extinction coefficient of SiHfBCN is consistent with changes in electrical conductivity [40]. Therefore, the resistivity can be analyzed by studying the factors that affect the band gap. Neffati [53] related the band gap of amorphous materials to the chemical bond content and the corresponding band gap of binary systems, as shown in Eq. (2).

(2) Eg=PA−B×Eg(AB)+PB−C×Eg(BC)+PA−C×Eg(AC)

where *E*g (AB), *E*g (BC), and *E*g (AC) are the band gaps of the binary system corresponding to the chemical bond; *E*g is the band gap of the material system; *P*A–B, *P*B–C, and *P*A–C are the proportions of chemical bonds.

According to previous studies, the bandgaps of free carbon, amorphous Si3N4, HfN, and HfO4 could vary between 1.0 and 6.0 eV, depending on the preparation process and concentration [54−57]. The chemical bonds in SiHfBCN ceramic coatings can be roughly divided into two categories: (1) “insulating” bonds, which correspond to binary systems with large band gaps such as Si–N (~4.5 eV) [54] and Hf–O (~5.6 eV) [55], and (2) “conductive” bonds, which correspond to binary systems with small or zero band gaps such as Hf–N (~1.44 eV) [56] and C=C (0.75–1.32 eV) [57]. As shown in Table 2, the content of C=C and Hf–N bonds increases from 1000 to 1100 °C, indicating that the C=C and Hf–N “conductive” chemical bonds in the system contribute to the electrical conductivity of the coating. Furthermore, the C=C bond content increases while the Hf–N bond content remains nearly unchanged from 1100 to 1200 °C, suggesting that the resistivity change in this temperature range is related to C=C, which is consistent with the results obtained by calculating the activation energy. The results show that the increase in the conductivity of the SiHfBCN coating is closely related to the Hf element and some “insulating” bonds in the system are transformed into “conductive” chemical bonds of Hf–N.

The high-temperature electrical conductivity of SiHfBCN ceramic coatings pyrolyzed at different temperatures in Ar, tested using the custom-designed machine equipped with a four-point probe setup, is shown in Fig. 8. As shown in Fig. 8(a), the SiHfBCN ceramic coating pyrolyzed at 1000 °C has an electrical conductivity of 4.26 S·m−¹ at 1000 °C. The coating pyrolyzed at 1100 °C exhibits an electrical conductivity of 7.52 S·m−1 at 1000 °C. In contrast, the SiHfBCN ceramic coating pyrolyzed at 1200 °C exhibits an electrical conductivity of 15.12 S·m−1 at 1000 °C, reaching an exceptional electrical conductivity of up to 291.55 S·m−1 at 1200 °C. The temperature sensitivity of the SiHfBCN ceramic coating pyrolyzed at 1200 °C reaches a maximum of 0.25 S·m−1·K−1 from RT to 1200 °C, indicating excellent temperature sensitivity compared with that of other materials [29,33,34,58]. Therefore, the SiHfBCN ceramic coating pyrolyzed at 1200 °C performs better in terms of electrical conductivity than the other coatings at high temperatures, mainly because of the higher content of C=C and Hf–N bonds. Moreover, the electrical conductivity of SiHfBCN ceramic coatings at different pyrolysis temperatures tends to increase with increasing test temperature, which is consistent with the findings for polymer-derived SiBCN [29], SiC [33], and SiCN [34] ceramics. The results indicate that the increase in electrical conductivity with increasing test temperature demonstrates that SiHfBCN ceramic coatings exhibit a typical semiconducting behavior. As shown in Fig. 8(b), the electrical conductivities of the SiHfBCN ceramic coatings have almost identical and symmetrical wave patterns after 25 heating/cooling procedures, indicating their excellent high-temperature conductivity, outstanding repeatability and durability at high temperatures. The SEM and XRD analyses of the SiHfBCN ceramic coatings pyrolyzed at different temperatures after high-temperature testing are shown in Fig. S5 and S6 in the ESM. The morphology, thickness and phase composition are essentially unchanged, indicating the remarkable high-temperature stability of the SiHfBCN ceramic coatings.

## 4 Conclusions

In conclusion, we have successfully prepared smooth and dense SiHfBCN ceramic coatings on YCa4O(BO3)3/BN substrate using the polymer-derived ceramic method. The obtained SiHfBCN ceramic coatings demonstrate remarkable high-temperature electrical conductivity and typical semiconducting behavior. The SiHfBCN ceramic coating pyrolyzed at 1200 °C achieves an excellent electrical conductivity of up to 291.55 S·m−¹ at a testing temperature of 1200 °C in Ar. Using a combination of techniques such as FT-IR, XPS, and Raman spectroscopy, we confirm that the SiHfBCN ceramic coating pyrolyzed at 1200 °C exhibits a higher content of C=C and Hf–N bonds, resulting in improved electrical conductivity at the testing temperature. Furthermore, the SiHfBCN ceramic coating possesses excellent high-temperature conductivity, and remarkable repeatability and durability. These findings underscore the potential of PDC SiHfBCN ceramic coatings as promising materials for high-temperature intelligent sensors.
