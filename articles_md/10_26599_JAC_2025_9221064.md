## 1 Introduction

Hydrogen and nuclear energies are widely acknowledged as invaluable sources of clean and inexhaustible energy that are capable of resolving the global challenge of human energy scarcity [1]. However, hydrogen isotopes used as fuels can easily penetrate metal materials. For example, tritium permeation through structural components is one of the most cutting-edge technological issues faced by the development of nuclear fusion reactors, which can cause a series of serious problems, including tritium fuel loss, radioactive contamination to the surroundings, and hydrogen embrittlement failure of structural components. The application of ceramic materials as a hydrogen isotope permeation barrier (HPB) on the surface of structural components offers a promising solution to this problem [2].

Low hydrogen permeability, good thermal shock resistance, and irradiation tolerance are pivotal factors for high-performance HPB [3−6]. Single-phase ceramic coatings usually possess relatively limited hydrogen permeation resistance, posing a formidable challenge for the long-term operation of nuclear reactors [7,8]. Although α-Al2O3 has been recognized as a promising HPB candidate with a high theoretical hydrogen permeation resistance level, its thermal mismatch with structural materials and poor irradiation tolerance remain intractable issues [9,10].

Composite coatings, which are composed of more than one phase or multilayer structure, can combine the advantages of each constituent to achieve superior performance [11−13]. Several strategies have been proposed to enhance the performance of these composite coatings in hydrogen permeation reduction, including the introduction of additional grain boundaries through nanograin design, an increase in interfaces by layer structure construction, and the creation of lattice distortions through dopant manipulation. For example, compared with the Er2O3 coating with columnar grains fabricated by magnetron sputtering, the coating consisting of nanograins obtained through the sol-gel method can complicate the orientations of the grain boundaries, thus bending the hydrogen diffusion channels along the boundaries [14]. Hu *et al.* [15] reported that nitride coatings with a multilayer structure increase the deuterium permeation resistance due to the interfaces generated between neighboring layers. The hydrogen barrier efficiency of the ZrO2 coating was enhanced fivefold through 4 mol% Y doping, as revealed by Endoh *et al.* [5]. These strategies focus mainly on crystallized materials; however, some studies have shown that hydrogen diffuses markedly faster at grain boundaries than within crystals and that grain boundaries can act as high-speed channels for hydrogen diffusion [16].

In contrast to crystallized coatings with abundant grain boundaries, amorphous coatings without any grain boundaries have attracted our attention, as hydrogen cannot diffuse through the grain boundaries in this new type of material; thus, can the amorphous material exhibit superior performance? Kim *et al.* [17] investigated the hydrogen permeation behavior of a C-doped TiZrN coating and reported that the doped amorphous carbon helped trap hydrogen atoms at the grain boundaries, improving the hydrogen barrier performance by 80%. Samanta *et al.* [18] also reported that binary amorphous Ni‒P composite coatings prepared by chemical plating displayed better hydrogen permeation resistance than crystalline Ni coatings. The amorphous SiOC coatings developed by Ke *et al.* [19] demonstrated a three-order-of-magnitude increase in hydrogen permeation resistance relative to X70 steel. These achievements implied the significant potential of the amorphous phase to be used for hydrogen permeation barriers. However, to date, amorphous oxide coatings have rarely been reported for hydrogen permeation reduction. In particular, the efficiency of multicomponent amorphous oxides as hydrogen permeation barriers remains a mystery [20].

In this context, we designed and fabricated a novel (AlCrZr)O ternary oxide nanofilm with an overall thickness of only 100 nm to determine the hydrogen barrier performance of amorphous oxide coatings. Al, Cr, and Zr were incorporated into the nanofilm at the atomic scale through a simple and facile sol‒gel technique. The resultant nanofilm exhibited intriguing features and was composed of an amorphous Al‒Zr‒O layer at the top and a crystalline Cr2O3 layer at the bottom. The microstructure of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm was thoroughly studied, and its hydrogen resistance was assessed via a gas-driven deuterium permeation test. The results indicated that the ultrathin (AlCrZr)O nanofilm significantly increased the hydrogen resistance by 2364 times compared with that of steel substrates, comparable to or even surpassing the performance of coatings with thicknesses of up to tens of micrometers. This remarkable increase in hydrogen resistance resulted from the peculiar amorphous/crystalline layered structure of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm. These results not only provide a new platform for the facile fabrication of amorphous oxide films or coatings but also pave the way for achieving excellent hydrogen resistance through the unique amorphous/crystalline structure design of ultrathin ceramic films, which holds great potential for metal component protection in hydrogen-rich surroundings.

### 2.1 Preparation of (AlCrZr)O nanofilms

321 austenitic stainless steel with a composition of Fe–18Cr–9Ni–1Mn–0.5Ti–0.05C (wt%) was opposite to the substrate because of its exceptional high-temperature stability and superior mechanical properties [21,22]. The 321 stainless steel was cut into disk-shaped plates for coating preparation. Prior to film deposition, all these substrates were mirror polished and then cleaned with acetone through ultrasonication to remove the residue on their surface. Zirconium acetate hydroxide ((CH3CO2)*x*Zr(OH)*y*) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Chromium acetate ((CH3COO)3Cr) and aluminum nitrate (Al(NO3)3·9H2O) were obtained from Aladdin. Ethanol, acetone, and acetic acid were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd., China. All reagents were used without further purification.

The (AlCrZr)O nanofilms were fabricated via a sol‒gel method. Zirconium acetate hydroxide was dissolved in alcohol, and a few drops of acetic acid were added to prevent the formation of zirconium hydroxide [23]. The solution was vigorously stirred for 12 h to obtain a clear and transparent sol. Chromium acetate and aluminum nitrate, in an equimolar ratio, were then added to the ZrO2 sol and stirred for 2 h to obtain a homogeneous precursor hybrid sol. The prepared substrates were subsequently immersed in the hybrid sol and removed at a constant speed. After drying, the sol deposition process was repeated three times to achieve a precursor membrane. Finally, the coated samples were annealed at temperatures ranging from 873 to 973 K for 60 min to obtain (AlCrZr)O nanofilms.

### 2.2 Microstructure and morphology characterizations

Grazing incident angle X-ray diffraction (GIXRD) was performed via an XRD (D8 Advance, Bruker, USA) with a Cu Kα radiation source (*λ* = 1.5418 Å). Data were recorded in the 2*θ* range of 20°–90° with an angular step size of 5° and a grazing incidence angle of 0.5°. Selected sample gels were also characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; XRD-7000, Shimadzu Company, Japan) in the same detection range with a sweep rate of 5 (°)/min, which was used to determine their crystallinity. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed under an air atmosphere using a thermogravimetric/differential thermal analyzer (TG/DTA; Diamond, PerkinElmer Instruments, China). Residual stress (RS) measurements were conducted through an X-ray stress analyzer (Xstress-3000, Stresstech Oy, Finland) operating in inclination mode with a Cu Kα cathode (*λ* = 1.5418 Å). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations were obtained via a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Nova NanoSEM 450, FEI, the Netherlands) at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analyzer. An X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) collected XPS data using a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM images were taken via a field emission transmission electron microscope (FE-TEM; Tecnai G2 F30, FEI, the Netherlands). The TEM lamellar section was cut with a focused ion beam (FIB; Helios Nanolab G3 CX, FEI, the Czech Republic). The thermal shock resistance of the nanofilm was evaluated via a thermal cycling test that simulated the pulsed operational profile of the DEMO reactor. The sample was heated in a flowing Ar atmosphere from room temperature to 823 K with dwell time of 2 h, followed by direct cooling to room temperature. The rates for the heating and cooling processes were 275 K·min−1.

### 2.3 Hydrogen resistance measurement

The hydrogen resistance of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilms was evaluated through a deuterium permeation test using a gas-driven deuterium permeation device, as described in Ref. [24]. The device was composed of two sealed vacuum chambers separated by a wafer sample. Before the permeation measurements, the samples were prepolished to remove the oxides on the back side. The two chambers were held at a low pressure below 10−5 Pa. During the deuterium permeation test, the permeation region containing the sample was heated via a stepwise cooling method, with the temperature at the beginning set to 773 K. Then, the test was performed at a temperature ranging from 673 to 773 K under a pressure of 20–80 kPa. This temperature range was determined according to the service temperature of ~773 K for the hydrogen barriers used in the nuclear fusion reactors. The heating time for each testing temperature was above 60 min until the deuterium permeation current reached a steady state. This steady-state current recorded by the quadrupole mass spectrometer (QMS; PrismaPro QMG 250 M1, Pfeiffer Vacuum, Germany) is proportional to the permeation flux (*J* (mol·m−2·s−1)), which can be expressed by Eq. (1) [25]:

(1) J=Ppnd

where *P* (mol·m−1·s−1·Pa−*n*) is the permeability, *d* (m) is the thickness of the sample, *p* (Pa) is the deuterium pressure, and the pressure exponent (*n*) can be estimated experimentally by plotting the permeation flux vs. the driving pressure [26,27].

### 3.1 Fabrication and phase constituents of (AlCrZr)O nanofilms

The (AlCrZr)O nanofilms were fabricated via a simple and facile sol‒gel technique, as depicted in Fig. 1(a). The Al, Cr, and Zr were homogeneously incorporated at the atomic scale within the sol. After dip-coating followed by annealing, ultrathin (AlCrZr)O nanofilms were successfully obtained, as expected. The structural evolution of these nanofilms was investigated by altering the annealing temperature from 873 to 1023 K. As shown in Fig. 1(b), the GIXRD patterns of the nanofilms indicate that Cr2O3 was crystallized into the corundum structure at 923 K, with its crystallization improved with increasing temperature. However, for ZrO2 and Al2O3, no diffraction peaks are observed below 1023 K, indicating an amorphous phase of these two oxides. Moreover, the detection depth of GIXRD can reach at least several hundred nanometers. Therefore, the observed GIXRD peaks indexed to Fe and Cr should originate from the 321 stainless steel substrate, which contains substantial amounts of Fe and Cr that are easily detectable. Figure 1(c) shows the XRD patterns of the powders obtained directly from Zr–O, Cr–Zr–O, Al–Zr–O, and Al–Cr–Zr–O sols after annealing at 973 K. Interestingly, well-defined crystal structures of t-ZrO2 and m-ZrO2 were observed from the Zr–O sol. For the Cr–Zr–O sol, the diffraction peaks of both Cr2O3 and t-ZrO2 appeared, with the intensity of t-ZrO2 weakening and that of m-ZrO2 completely disappearing compared with that of the Zr–O sol. However, for the Al–Zr–O sol, no diffraction peaks are observed, indicating that the incorporation of Al inhibited the crystallization of ZrO2 and resulted in an amorphous phase of Al–Zr–O after calcination. Once Al atoms were added further to form an Al–Cr–Zr–O sol, only the Cr2O3 phase was observed with weakened diffraction peaks, and both the t-ZrO2 and m-ZrO2 phases were also absent. As deduced from these observations, mixing multiple metal elements together may increase the entropy of the sol, leading it to convert toward a more chaotic and disordered state. Consequently, the products from the Al–Cr–Zr–O sol possess an amorphous structure of ZrO2 and Al2O3 with minimal Cr2O3 grains. According to the TGA curves of these three different sols, as displayed in Figs. 1(d)–1(f), the weight loss of Al–Cr–Zr–O sol and Cr–Zr–O sol is nearly twice that of the pure Zr–O sol due to the addition of acetate and nitrate raw materials. The former two kinds of sols can be converted into stable oxides at approximately 600 K.

The chemical states of the Al, Cr, and Zr on the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm surface after annealing at 973 K were characterized via XPS. As shown in Fig. 2, the relative chemical composition of each element was calculated from the fitting peak areas of Al 2p, Cr 2p, Zr 3d, and O 1 s using the Gaussian method [28]. Figure 2(b) shows that the contents of Al and Zr are greater than 14 at%, whereas the content of Cr is much lower, indicating that the predominant components on the nanofilm surface are ZrO2 and Al2O3. The peak of Al 2p at 74.0 eV in Fig. 2(c) is ascribed to the oxidation state of Al2O3 [29]. As shown in Fig. 2(d), the peak at 182.1 eV is assigned to Zr 3d5/2, whereas that at 184.4 eV is related to Zr 3d3/2 [30]. The binding energy difference of 2.3 eV between these two peaks confirms the formation of ZrO2 [31]. The Cr 2p spectra in Fig. 2(e) include a peak at 576.8 eV, assigned to Cr 2p3/2, and the other peak at 586.0 eV corresponds to Cr 2p1/2, indicative of Cr3+ [32]. Additionally, the satellite peaks at 578.3 and 587.6 eV are associated with Cr 2p3/2 and Cr 2p1/2, respectively, indicating the presence of Cr6+ [33,34]. It is assumed that this minor Cr6+ contribution likely arises from the partial oxidation of Cr3+ during high-temperature annealing in oxygen-rich environments, potentially leading to the formation of trace CrO3 or other high-valent species [35]. The O 1s spectra shown in Fig. 2(f) comprise two peaks at 530.0 and 531.4 eV, which can be attributed to the lattice oxygen and the absorbed oxygen, respectively. According to the XRD analyses, the lattice oxygen is rooted in the crystalline Cr2O3, whereas the absorbed oxygen (also referred to as vacancy oxygen) is ascribed to the amorphous phases [36,37].

### 3.2 Peak shift of Cr<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3 </sub>and the internal stress within (AlCrZr)O nanofilm

Compared with those of the standard Cr2O3 ICCD card, the diffraction peaks of Cr2O3 are shifted negatively, as demonstrated in Fig. 3(a). According to Ref. [38], dopants usually lead to broadening of the diffraction peaks of the lattice. In this work, no significant difference was detected in the shape of the Cr2O3 diffraction peaks observed in the XRD patterns. This suggests that the shift in the Cr2O3 diffraction peak might not be caused by doping. This shift may be attributed to tensile stress, which increases the interplanar spacing of crystalline Cr2O3, leading to a corresponding shift of its diffraction peaks toward lower angles. To verify the above speculation, residual stress measurements were conducted on the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm, with the results depicted in Figs. 3(c)–3(f). The (AlCrZr)O nanofilms were diffracted at different measurement angles (*ψ)*, specifically 0°, 5°, 15°, 25°, 35°, and 45°, using a slow-scanning model to obtain single-peak diffraction spectra. A plot of the interplanar spacing at various temperatures vs. the measurement angle (sin2*ψ*) is shown in Fig. 3(b). Furthermore, the strain value was calculated via Eq. (2) [39]:

To relate the strain value with the diffraction peak position (2*θ*), Eqs. (2a) and (2b) can be applied:

(2a) K=Ε(1 +ν)1d0

(2b) M=∂(dθψ)∂(sin2ψ)

The strain value was expressed with Eq. (3):

(3) σφ=K⋅M

In Eqs. (2) and (3), * σφ (MPa) is the residual strain at an angle of φ*. *φ* is the angle corresponding to the diffraction peak with high intensity, no diffusion, and a high diffraction surface index. *E* (GPa) and *ν* are the Young’s modulus and the Poisson’s ratio of Cr2O3, respectively. * d0 represents the strain-free interplanar spacing. ψ* (°) is the tilt angle between the normal diffraction plane and the normal of the sample’s surface, and * dθψ is the interplanar spacing at an angle of ψ*. *K* is a stress constant related solely to the nature of the material. *M* is the gradient of the * dθψ–sin2ψ* curve, which can be obtained from the RS measurements. As shown in Fig. 3(b), the slopes at different annealing temperatures are all negative. The absolute values of the slopes gradually decrease, indicating that Cr2O3 is subjected to tensile stress, which decreases with increasing temperature. This behavior may be attributed to the generation of ZrO2 at higher temperatures, which could induce compressive stress around Cr2O3 and remit the tensile stress. Furthermore, the dislocation motion induced by high temperatures also plays an important role in stress relaxation. At elevated temperatures, the increased atomic mobility facilitates dislocation glide and climb, leading to the rearrangement of the crystal structure and subsequent stress relief [40]. Additionally, a significant decline in the slope value is found at 1023 K, resulting from more ZrO2 particles being generated at this temperature, leading to an increase in compressive stress and a reduction in comprehensive tensile stress on Cr2O3. This finding coincides with the shift in the Cr2O3 peak and accounts for the subsequent transformation in the morphology of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm.

### 3.3 Micromorphology and structure of (AlCrZr)O nanofilm

The micromorphology and elemental mapping of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm are shown in Fig. 4. At 873 K, the nanofilm exhibited a flat and smooth surface without any observable particles (Fig. 4(a)). On the basis of the XRD analyses, the nanofilm is almost entirely amorphous at this temperature. In contrast, at 923 K, oxide particles with a diameter of approximately 1 μm emerge on the surface of the nanofilm (Fig. S1(a) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). These oxide particles continue to grow and reach ~3 μm in size as the temperature further increases (Fig. 4(b)). The particle-free areas maintain a dense and homogeneous structure. The oxide particles are identified as Cr2O3 (Fig. 4(c)), and electron dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping reveals that the Al and Zr are uniformly distributed. The reason for this phenomenon may be the preference of Cr species for crystallization into the oxides, accompanied by structural incompatibility between the Cr species and the amorphous network. The discrete distribution characteristic of these Cr2O3 nanoparticles implies the minimization of the interfacial energy of the amorphous matrix, which is in agreement with the classical heterogeneous nucleation theory. Once the samples are annealed at 1023 K, micropores are observed on the nanofilm (Fig. S1(b) in the ESM), indicating that a temperature above 1000 K is detrimental to the compactness of the nanofilm.

To gain further insight into the microstructure of the nanofilm, we performed cross-sectional TEM characterization on a sample prepared via FIB (Fig. 5(a)). The nanoparticles emerging on the surface are confirmed to be crystalline Cr2O3 (Fig. 5(b)). As shown in the EDS elemental mapping images (Figs. 5(c) and 5(d)), all the metallic atoms in the film were oxidized, with oxygen distributed throughout the whole area of the film. Notably, elemental Cr is mainly distributed at the surface and bottom regions, with the middle area consisting of Al and Zr. The Cr-rich region at the surface is a crystalline structure with a d*-spacing of 0.167 corresponding to the (116) plane of the Cr2O3 lattice (Fig. 5(e)). According to the SEM observations, this region corresponds to the Cr2O3 nanoparticles generated on the surface of the nanofilm. Below the Cr2O3 nanoparticles, the middle region with both Al and Zr is an amorphous phase. A distribution of elemental Si is also observed at the interface between the nanofilm and the substrate (Fig. 5(f)), which is attributed to the precipitation of Si from the 321 steel substrate. On the basis of these analyses, the nanofilm, whose overall thickness is less than 100 nm, is composed of a layered structure (Figs. 5(g) and 5(h)) with Al‒Zr‒O, Cr2O3, and Si layers of ~61, ~29, and ~5 nm, respectively, as shown schematically in Fig. 5(k). The observation of amorphous Si at the interface is attributed primarily to the competitive diffusion kinetics of Si (activation energy ~69 kJ·mol−1) compared with other alloying elements (e.g., Cr and Fe) contained in the substrate under a temperature gradient [41]. During the calcination process, Si precipitated from the substrate, accumulated at the interface, and then formed a continuous amorphous layer, as observed.

Under certain conditions with low oxygen pressure, Cr in stainless steels can diffuse outward and react with oxygen to form Cr2O3 at the surface [42,43]. However, few studies have noted the precipitation of Si in steels, which was first found to impact the growth of Cr2O3. Since the thermal expansion coefficients of Si, Fe, and Cr2O3 are 2.9×10−6, 11.7×10−6, and 9.6×10−6 K−1, respectively [44,45], and that of Si changed very slightly at 973 K, the tensile stress was accordingly produced around Cr2O3 grains to inhibit their outward expansion, resulting in a larger interplanar spacing in the (012) crystal plane and thus enlarging its grain size.

These Cr2O3 grains originate from the following two aspects: On the one hand, the precipitation and oxidation of Cr from the steel substrate could form Cr2O3 grains; on the other hand, Cr ions contained in the precursor nanofilm from the sol may descend to the bottom and form Cr2O3 grains during the annealing process. According to the EDS elemental mapping images (Fig. 5(f)), the regions that appear to be voids exhibit no elemental deficiency of Al, Zr, or O compared with the surrounding areas. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 7(c), the atomic arrangements in these “void-like” regions are consistent with those in the other areas of the amorphous nanofilm, confirming that these regions are not actually voids and that the Al‒Zr‒O amorphous layer has a compact structure. This guarantees a high hydrogen barrier efficiency of the nanofilm.

### 3.4 Hydrogen resistance of (AlCrZr)O nanofilm

The hydrogen resistance of (AlCrZr)O nanofilm was evaluated via deuterium permeation tests using the device described in our previous work [46,47]. The recorded deuterium permeation current during the permeability test for both the 321 stainless steel substrate and the nanofilm is shown in Figs. 8(a) and 8(b). For the substrate, the deuterium permeation current rapidly reached a steady state at each testing temperature because of the fast deuterium diffusion in the steel. In contrast, the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm exhibited an extremely low deuterium permeation current, nearly approaching the detection limit of the device. This causes a disturbance to the recorded data. The fitting line indicates that the permeation current of the nanofilm is above three orders of magnitude lower than that of the substrate, demonstrating the exceptional hydrogen barrier efficiency of the nanofilm. The deuterium permeability of the 321 steel substrate is on the same order of magnitude as the reported value [24], confirming the reliability of this permeation apparatus. Compared with the steel substrate, a significant decrease in deuterium permeation flux was achieved by the (AlCrZr)O nanofilms (Fig. 8(c)). Among these samples, the nanofilm annealed at 973 K has the lowest permeation flux, implying that it possesses the highest hydrogen resistance. Considering the temperature-dependent structural evolution of the nanofilm, the formation of a well-crystallized Cr2O3 layer underneath the amorphous Al‒Zr‒O layer is beneficial for hydrogen resistance. An increase in the annealing temperature from 873 to 973 K can improve Cr2O3 crystallization, thereby decreasing deuterium permeability. However, at a higher temperature of 1023 K, micropores appear on the surface, as seen in the SEM images, which may lead to an increase in the deuterium permeability.

The figure-of-merit for evaluating the performance of (AlCrZr)O nanofilms as a hydrogen permeation barrier is the permeation reduction factor (PRF), which is determined by the ratio of deuterium permeability through the uncoated and coated substrates [5]. Figure 8(d) shows an exponential increase in the PRF with increasing deuterium permeation temperature, highlighting the effective barrier behavior of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilms. As the permeation temperature increases, the deuterium permeability of the substrate sharply increases by several orders of magnitude, whereas that of the nanofilms substantially decreases. The large difference in the changes in deuterium permeability between the substrate and the nanofilm results in an exponential increase in the PRF at higher temperatures. This observation agrees with the findings reported for the Al2O3 coating [20]. The calculated PRFs of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilms annealed at various temperatures are plotted in Fig. 8(e). The performance of the nanofilms annealed at 973 and 1023 K is much better than that of the ones annealed at 873 and 923 K. Strikingly, a PRF as high as 2364 was achieved in the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm annealed at 973 K, indicating that the hydrogen resistance of such an ultrathin nanofilm is 2364-fold greater than that of the steel substrate. For comparison, we fabricated a Cr2O3 nanofilm via the same sol-gel technique, which has a PRF value below 250 (Fig. 11(c)), which is significantly lower than that of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm. The ZrO2 and Al2O3 coatings prepared by the sol‒gel method presented PRF values of ~50 and ~20, respectively [46,48]. These results highlight that the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm developed in this work achieves a significantly higher hydrogen barrier efficiency than the above sol‒gel nanofilms do.

The relationship between the permeation ﬂux and the driving pressure for the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm is shown in Fig. 8(f). At testing temperatures of 773 and 823 K, two sets of data were well-fitted with straight lines, resulting in pressure exponents of 0.97 and 0.81, respectively. At 673 K, the pressure exponent of 0.52 suggests that the deuterium permeation process is governed by atomic diffusion limitations [5]. This phenomenon may arise from the deuterium impeding behavior of the substrate itself at lower temperatures. At 723 K, the pressure exponent increases to 0.7, indicative of a transitional regime where deuterium permeation kinetics are jointly controlled by atomic diffusion and surface reactions [49]. At higher temperatures (≥ 773 K), the deuterium resistance of the substrate becomes negligible, and the nanofilm plays a dominant role in impeding deuterium diffusion. The increased temperature enhances the dissociation kinetics of deuterium molecules, allowing them to more easily adsorb onto the nanofilm surface, dissociate into atomic species, and interact with defects such as oxygen vacancies in the crystalline lattice. For this reason, the deuterium permeation process becomes predominantly prohibited by surface reactions within the nanofilm. For example, the pressure exponent of 0.97 at 823 K approaches 1, indicating surface reaction-dominated permeation kinetics at this temperature.

A comparison of the PRFs of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm and the coatings with different thicknesses, as previously reported, is shown in Fig. 9 and Table S1 in the ESM. Importantly, the PRF of our (AlCrZr)O nanofilm is among the highest values reported previously, even surpassing the performance of micron-thick coatings. An increase in coating thickness is generally considered a straightforward way to increase the hydrogen permeation resistance because of the prolonged permeation pathway in thicker coatings. However, as revealed by our previous study [27], thicker coatings also generate increasing internal stress, which easily leads to microcracks and delamination of the coatings and thus deteriorates their performance. In this study, we achieved superior hydrogen permeation resistance in an (AlCrZr)O nanofilm with a thickness below 100 nm. This ultrathin structure is conducive to reducing the internal stress within the nanofilm, especially under thermal conditions, and therefore can help maintain its performance with a long lifespan. Therefore, the high hydrogen resistance of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm may provide new guidelines for developing high-performance and long-lived hydrogen permeation barriers.

### 3.5 Thermal shock resistance of (AlCrZr)O nanofilm

The thermal stability of hydrogen permeation barriers is important for their practical application. The thermal shock resistance of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm was further evaluated via a thermal cycling test under “823 K-room temperature” conditions, which simulated the pulsed operational profile of the DEMO reactor. As shown in Fig. 10, after 40 thermal cycles, the PRF decreased by 20% compared with that of the pristine nanofilm. Despite this decrease, however, the nanofilm could maintain a high PRF of 1266 after 60 thermal cycles. This finding indicates that the nanofilm can still decrease the deuterium permeability by more than one order of magnitude compared with that of the substrate, meeting the demand of the DEMO operational threshold (PRF ≥ 1000) [20]. The good thermal stability of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm results from the following two factors: (1) The Cr2O3 layer generated under the amorphous film can help mitigate the thermal expansion mismatch between the nanofilm and the substrate due to its larger thermal expansion coefficient [50]; (2) In contrast to micron-thick coatings that easily form cracks under thermal conditions, the ultrathin structure of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm favors its integrity under thermal shock conditions.

## 4 Discussion

To understand the origin of the superior performance of the nanofilm, we prepared a Cr2O3 nanofilm via the sol‒gel technique via the same calcination procedure. The deuterium permeation current and flux of the single Cr2O3 nanofilm are shown in Figs. 11(a) and 11(b). As calculated, the PRF of the Cr2O3 nanofilm is below 250 at various permeation temperatures (Fig. 11(c)), which is significantly lower than that of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm. This result verifies that crystalline Cr2O3 has limited hydrogen resistance. Thus, the high hydrogen barrier efficiency of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm is dominated by the Al‒Zr‒O amorphous layer. We repeated the deuterium permeation test on the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm for 10 cycles using the same sample to reveal its stability, with the cycled deuterium permeation flux shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. As shown in Fig. 11(d), the permeation flux of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm is quite stable, with fluctuations of less than 11% during all the permeation cycles. The deuterium permeation flux still remains at the same level as that of the pristine sample, without any signs of performance degradation even after 10 permeation cycles. Throughout the cycling experiments, the PRF of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm consistently remained at the level of 103 and maintained a high value above 2000. The stability of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm is superior to that of the Cr2O3 coating, for which the PRF decreases by 58.3% after only one permeation cycle [50]. This result excludes the function of hydrogen traps, which account for the high hydrogen resistance of the nanofilm. If deuterium diffusion is prevented by traps, the performance of the nanofilm degrades after each permeation cycle, as the traps gradually become saturated during the permeation process. Therefore, the outstanding barrier performance of the (AlCrZr)O nanofilm can be attributed to its unique amorphous structure, without grain boundaries acting as hydrogen diffusion channels. This finding provides valuable insights for the design of efficient hydrogen barriers with high stability.

For conventional crystalline hydrogen permeation barriers, strong covalent and ionic bonds in the lattice are reported to create stable sites for hydrogen adsorption, thereby trapping hydrogen atoms to restrain their diffusion [16,51]. However, in the crystalline structure, abundant grain boundaries with weaker hydrogen adsorption capacities may act as fast pathways for hydrogen diffusion, significantly reducing the barrier effectiveness [14]. In contrast, the amorphous phase has no interior grain boundaries. Its disordered atomic arrangements can effectively block stable migration pathways of hydrogen atoms, forcing them to take longer, more erratic paths [19]. Therefore, the introduction of an amorphous phase into hydrogen permeation barriers is an effective way to improve their performance. For example, the hydrogen permeability of ZrTiN coatings is reduced by 80% through the incorporation of amorphous carbon rings at the grain boundaries [17]. In this work, we attributed the outstanding hydrogen barrier efficiency of our (AlCrZr)O nanofilm to the following two factors. First, the Al‒Zr‒O amorphous layer without grain boundaries prevents hydrogen from finding continuous diffusion pathways and thus effectively increases the hydrogen resistance. Second, the interface between the Al‒Zr‒O amorphous layer and the crystalline Cr2O3 layer may act as a “hydrogen trap” to restrain hydrogen permeation, on the basis of previous studies that demonstrated an increase in barrier performance via a dual-phase design [52,53]. For these reasons, nanofilms with ultrathin structures exhibit high hydrogen resistance comparable to or even superior to that of microthick coatings.

## 5 Conclusions

In this work, we designed and fabricated a novel (AlCrZr)O ternary oxide nanofilm as a new type of hydrogen permeation barrier. This (AlCrZr)O nanofilm, with a thickness of only 100 nm, was synthesized via a simple and facile sol‒gel method, consisting of an upper layer of amorphous Al‒Zr‒O and an inner layer of crystalline Cr2O3. The temperature-dependent structural evolution of the nanofilm was sufficiently investigated. After annealing at 973 K, the nanofilm was rather dense, with its PRF reaching as high as 2364. This remarkable PRF value of the nanofilm is comparable to or even surpasses that of the microthick coatings. Notably, the high hydrogen resistance of the nanofilm was maintained after 10 permeation cycles. This exceptional hydrogen barrier performance was attributed mainly to the unique Al‒Zr‒O amorphous layer at the top and the interface between the amorphous Al‒Zr‒O layer and crystalline Cr2O3. The former could complicate hydrogen diffusion pathways by eliminating grain boundaries, and the latter might act as “hydrogen traps” to impede hydrogen diffusion. Both of these factors are beneficial to hydrogen resistance. Furthermore, the crystalline Cr2O3 layer mitigated the thermal expansion mismatch between the nanofilm and the steel substrate, ensuring good thermal shock resistance of the nanofilm. These superior performances not only demonstrate the potential of this (AlCrZr)O nanofilm for a wide variety of hydrogen-resistant applications but also provide new guidelines for developing high-performance and long-lasting hydrogen permeation barriers.
