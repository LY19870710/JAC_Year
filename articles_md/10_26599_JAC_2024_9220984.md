## 1 Introduction

Spinel-type aluminum oxynitride (γ-AlON) is an important single-phase ceramic with a stable structure belonging to the Al2O3–AlN system. Owing to its cubic crystal structure, it has excellent transparency for wavelengths ranging from ultraviolet to mid-wave infrared [1−3]. These AlON transparent ceramics have various unique properties, such as good mechanical strength, high thermal shock resistance, and good chemical resistance [4−6]. Hence, AlON transparent ceramics are widely applied in transparent armor, missile domes, smartphone tablet panels, and infrared/visible windows [7−11]. Therefore, various methods have been developed to synthesize AlON powder and manufacture transparent ceramics.

Generally, two manufacturing processes are used to obtain AlON transparent ceramics: A one-step synthesis method and a two-step synthesis method. The one-step approach uses commercial Al2O3 and AlN as raw materials for direct shaping and sintering [12−19], whereas the two-step method involves synthesizing γ-AlON powder first and then conducting shaping and sintering [20−27]. However, for the one-step method, the current AlON powder often has unstable properties because of the instability of the preparation process, which makes the whole AlON transparent ceramic preparation process difficult to control [7]. To date, highly transparent AlON ceramics have usually been produced only via a two-step synthesis process, and the properties of the powder itself directly influence the microstructure and performance of the final transparent ceramics. According to previous reports, three different methods have been extensively used to synthesize single-phase AlON powder: carbothermal reduction nitridation (CRN) [28−33], direct nitridation (DN) [34−39], and solid-state reactions (SSRs) [40−45]. To ensure a complete reaction, these methods typically require high temperatures (≥ 1750 °C) and extended reaction time (≥ 2 h). However, after soaking in a high-temperature environment for several hours, AlON powder often becomes coarse and aggregate, which greatly restricts the application prospects of such materials.

To reduce the synthesis temperature and improve the sintering activity of the powder, the synthesis of core‒shell precursors via a wet chemical method combined with the CRN method for the low-temperature synthesis of AlON powder has been widely studied [46−55]. For example, Jim *et al*. [47] successfully synthesized well-dispersed AlON powder with a bimodal particle size distribution (PSD) at 0.2 and 0.7 μm by using a core–shell structured Al2O3/C mixture derived from the pyrolysis of a Al2O3/urea-formaldehyde resin nanocomposite precursor as the starting material. Xue *et al*. [48] used a silane coupling agent as a dispersant to optimize the distribution uniformity of raw materials of Al2O3 and C, and the addition of a silane coupling agent improved the reactivity of the raw materials and decreased the synthesis temperature (1670–1730 °C) of AlON, resulting in the preparation of AlON powder with an AlN content of 27.5 mol%. Sabaghi *et al*. [54] synthesized AlON powder with a particle size of ~1 μm by reacting at 1750 °C for 1 h in N2 flow with an ultrasonic-assisted CRN method. The alumina/polyacrylonitrile (PAN) precursor was prepared via ultrasonication at room temperature, and the Al2O3/C core‒shell nanoparticles were prepared via pyrolysis of the Al2O3/PAN nanocomposite precursor at 800 °C for 2 h in an argon atmosphere. However, these methods necessitate the synthesis of a minimum of two or more raw materials into pure-phase powder through high-temperature treatment. The presence of multiple raw materials increases the complexity of the mixing process, and the efficacy of material mixing significantly impacts subsequent procedures and the ultimate performance of the powder.

In this study, an innovative approach was employed to synthesize a special core‒shell structure with a hollow outer core composite precursor through the incomplete hydrolysis of Al powder. This unique core‒shell structure not only shortens the diffusion distance between raw materials and reduces the reaction temperature but also results in some of the synthesized AlON powder having a hollow structure, which is conducive to powder crushing. This method effectively combines hydrogen production from Al hydrolysis with innovative recycling applications for byproducts. Additionally, AlON powder can be synthesized from a single raw material at low temperatures, avoiding the cumbersome process and energy waste associated with traditional methods involving uneven mixing of multiple raw materials and high-temperature calcination.

### 2.1 Materials and methods

High-purity metal Al powder (99.9%, *D*50 = 2.4 μm; BuWei Chemical Co., Ltd., China) was used as the sole starting material for synthesizing the precursor and AlON powder. The experiments were divided into two main groups. One group explored the hydrolysis of metal Al powder at room temperature, and the other group used heating to hydrolyze metal Al powder to quickly obtain incompletely hydrolyzed composite precursors for the synthesis of AlON powder. Initially, the first set of experiments studied the hydrolysis process of raw metal Al powder at room temperature, 55 g of metal Al powder was dispersed in 1000 mL of deionized water to form a slurry with a volume solid content of 2 vol%. By magnetic stirring, groups of samples were labeled every 10 h, filtered, dried, and sieved through a 100-mesh sieve. The second set of experiments involved heating the slurry with a volume solid content of 2 vol% at 70 °C. A group of samples were labeled at an interval of 40 min, and each sample was filtered, dried, and sieved through a 100-mesh sieve. Finally, a suitable proportion of the mixed precursor was heated in a flowing N2 atmosphere at 800–1700 °C for 2 h.

### 2.2 Characterization

The phase compositions of the products were identified via a powder X-ray diffractometer (PXRD; miniflex600, Rigaku, Japan) via Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5406 Å, step size = 0.01°, 1 (°)/min) operated at 30 kV and 10 mA. The thermal behavior was studied by a thermal analysis device (NETZSCH STA449F3, Selb, Germany) with a heating rate of 10 °C/min under flowing nitrogen. PSD of AlON powder was measured by a laser size analyzer (LT-2200, Zhenyi Technology Co., Ltd., China) with a wet laser beam. The morphologies of the as-obtained powder were examined by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU8010, Hitachi, Japan). The microstructure and chemical composition of the composite precursor were characterized via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2F20, FEI, USA). High-angle annular dark-field (HADDF) and mapping images were obtained on a TEM in scanning mode equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS; Talos F200X, Thermo scientific, USA) for element analysis. Notably, the sintering temperature was monitored and controlled via a W–Re thermocouple.

Given the difficulty in characterizing the structure of the solid-state byproduct on the composite precursor, we selected a solid-state hydrolyzed byproduct with a greater degree of hydrolysis at 30 h for microscopic morphology observation and further structural characterization. As shown in Fig. 2(c), the morphology of the hydrolyzed byproduct at this stage differs significantly from that of the original Al powder. The enlarged area reveals a uniform coverage of laminate interlacing substances on the surface of the powder particles. When the Al–H2O reaction ends, at this point, the solid-state hydrolyzed byproducts at this stage are only boehmite and bayerite. Figure 2(d) shows the microscopic morphology of these solid-state byproducts after hydrolysis for 70 h. The solid-state hydrolyzed byproduct is a combination of rod-like substances and particles. The appearance of rod-like substances is different from the lamellar morphology. Combined with the previous XRD analysis, the rod-like material represents further growth of bayerite, whereas the particulate matter corresponds to interleaved lamellar boehmite.

Figure 2(a) shows a schematic diagram of metal Al hydrolysis. On the basis of the characterization analysis of the phase and micromorphology of the solid-state hydrolysis byproduct, the hydrolysis process can be roughly divided into three stages: the early hydrolysis induction period, the middle rapid reaction period, and the late reaction termination period. In the first stage, the surface of the metal Al powder becomes rough in terms of its microscopic morphology, the surface integrity of the powder is destroyed, and trace second-phase substances begin to appear. As the degree of hydrolysis increases, the reaction rapidly progresses. At this time, the metal Al powder gradually dissolves from the surface, and the metal Al and the solid-state hydrolysis byproduct exist simultaneously. In terms of microstructure, it is a form of hydrolyzed product coated with metal Al. Finally, in the third stage, the metal Al powder is completely hydrolyzed.

Combined with the XRD diffraction pattern in Fig. 1(b), it is evident that the solid-state hydrolyzed byproduct still remains in the raw material metal Al powder at this stage. To observe the relationship between the residual metal powder and the solid-state hydrolysis byproduct, HAADF combined with EDS analysis was performed on the byproduct at 30 h, as shown in Figs. 3(a)–3(e). Some particles within the hydrolyzed aggregates appear to be hollow. EDS analysis reveals that the incomplete raw material, i.e., the Al powder, is coated with hydrolyzed byproducts in the center at this stage, and some certain small particles of the raw material, i.e., the Al powder, have undergone complete hydrolysis, resulting in empty shells. These unique structures exhibit hollow and core‒shell characteristics in the reaction products of Al‒H2O.

To investigate the formation of a shell structure during the hydrolysis of metal Al, we characterized the net strength spectrum of the unique composite precursor in the HAADF image of the hydrolysis byproduct, as shown in Figs. 3(f) and 3(g). A hollow composite precursor was chosen in the HAADF diagram for characterization, with an arrow spanning a length of 3.5 μm indicating a single composite precursor. The net strength spectrum reveals a consistent trend in the lines of Al and O, with both showing an initial increase followed by a decrease. Two spectral peaks indicate the presence of a hollow structure within the composite precursor particle, as indicated by the arrow. The position where the arrow crosses and the net strength spectrum aligns can be determined. The dark region in the middle of the composite precursor particles represents its hollow nature. The formation of empty shells can be explained by the fact that the hydrolysis of metal Al powder begins at the surface and releases hydrogen. The amount of solid-state hydrolysis byproduct gradually increases with increasing degree of hydrolysis, and the generated hydrogen is released from the surface of the metal Al powder. With the continuous consumption of metal Al powder hydrolysis and the release of hydrogen, a hollow layer gradually forms in the middle of the core‒shell structure.

In summary, during the hydrolysis process of metal Al powder, the particle size of the raw material metal Al gradually decreases as the degree of hydrolysis increases. A hollow structure gradually forms between the solid-state hydrolyzed byproduct and raw materials until complete hydrolysis of the metal Al occurs, resulting in a hollow structure at the center of the hydrolyzed byproduct. In cases where hydrolysis is incomplete, a unique core‒shell structure with a hollow outer core is formed.

To further investigate the interrelationship and structure of various hydrolyzed byproducts, scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) combined with selected area electron diffraction (SAED) was employed to analyze the solid-state hydrolyzed byproducts, as depicted in Fig. 4. As shown in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b), the solid-state byproducts obtained after a hydrolysis period of 30 h can be classified into three main morphological types: near-spherical material covered by lamellar interlacing, rod-like material, and single lamellar material, as shown in the enlarged area of Fig. 4(b).

The diffraction patterns in Figs. 4(d) and 4(f) show that the interlacing thin lamellar substances are boehmite, whereas the short rod-like substances are bayerite. Owing to the instability of the monolayer material in the enlarged region shown in Fig. 4(b), its structure will undergo changes when exposed to high-energy electron beams. Therefore, accurately determining the specific structure of a material through SAED is currently not possible.

### 3.3 Direct nitridation reaction procedure analysis

Hydrolysis analysis of the raw Al powder reveals that the presence of a passivation film on its surface hinders both the hydrolysis and oxidation processes, resulting in slow reaction rates at room temperature. To increase the hydrolysis rate without introducing impurities, the hydrolysis solution was heated. In view of the exothermic reaction of Al–H2O, to avoid excessive reactions and ensure a controllable hydrolysis reaction, the solution was heated to 70 °C in this experiment to shorten the induction time of hydrolysis of the metallic Al powder.

The solid-state hydrolyzed byproduct was then characterized via XRD patterns. As shown in Fig. 5(a), after a hydrolysis time of 20 min, there are two phase diffraction peaks corresponding to the diffraction peak of the raw metal Al powder and the diffraction peak of AlOOH. At this time, the diffraction peak corresponds mainly to the raw Al powder. With increasing hydrolysis time, as observed for the samples after 60, 100, and 140 min, the intensity of Al powder phase gradually decreases, whereas that of the corresponding solid-state hydrolysis byproduct increases. When the hydrolysis time reaches 180 min, the main diffraction peak corresponds to the solid-state hydrolysis byproduct, whereas that associated with the raw material metal Al becomes significantly weak.

An appropriate amount of metal Al powder is crucial for synthesizing AlON powder via DN method. Therefore, to approximate the amount of Al in the composite precursor after hydrolysis of the raw material, the thermogravimetric data were used for characterization, as shown in Fig. 5(b). At 600 °C, AlOOH and Al(OH)3 in the hydrolysis product undergo a transformation into a phase of (Al2O3)1.333. The majority of the sample curves clearly exhibit a gradual increase in weight when the temperature exceeds 700 °C. This can be attributed to the melting of the metal Al powder and its reaction with nitrogen under a nitrogen atmosphere. Therefore, the weight gained by the sample corresponds to the mass of nitrogen involved in AlN synthesis. The approximate calculation of the metal Al powder content in the composite precursor under different hydrolysis time reveals that as the hydrolysis time increases, the weight gain of the sample at a thermogravimetric temperature of 600 °C gradually decreases from 10.22% to 2.03%. This indicates a gradual decrease in the content of synthesized AlN in the sample with prolonged hydrolysis time and suggests that as the hydrolysis time increases, both the content of metal Al in the sample and its completeness gradually decrease.

When the temperature exceeds 1200 °C, the low-temperature phase of alumina in the hydrolysis product undergoes a transformation into a stable phase of Al2O3. By calculating the Al content in the composite precursor, one can estimate the approximate amount of low-temperature phase alumina. Therefore, the ratio of Al2O3/Al in the composite precursor can be roughly determined. As shown in Fig. 5(c), the content of metal Al in the composite precursor gradually decreases with increasing hydrolysis time, whereas the ratio of Al2O3/Al gradually increases from 2.7 to 16.12. This finding indicates that as the hydrolysis time increases, the solid-state byproduct of Al–H2O reaction gradually increases, and the amount of the raw material metal Al decreases. AlON phase can be synthesized within the range of the ratio of Al2O3/Al from 8.19 to 15.64. When heated at 70 °C, the ratio of the solid-state byproduct in the composite precursor to the raw material metal Al powder remains within this theoretical range for a hydrolysis time of 140 min. Therefore, the hydrolysis conditions selected for this experiment are as follows: Hydrolysis is conducted at 70 °C for 140 min.

After determining the hydrolysis conditions, the composite precursor obtained from hydrolysis was subjected to nitridation at different temperatures. The resulting nitride products were characterized via XRD, as shown in Fig. 5(d). At a nitridation temperature of 800 °C, the synthesized product was (Al2O3)1.333, which was the raw material of the Al powder phase. The results suggest that (Al2O3)1.333 is formed through a phase transformation of boehmite and bayerite. At a nitridation temperature of 1300 °C, the synthesized products primarily consisted of α-Al2O3 and AlN phases. At this point, the metallic aluminum present in the composite precursor has undergone complete nitridation, resulting in the formation of an AlN substance. At a nitridation temperature of 1670 °C, the resulting products mainly consist of three phases: AlON, α-Al2O3, and AlN, which indicates that at this temperature, solid solution reactions occur between α-Al2O3 and AlN phases. Furthermore, upon further increasing the nitridation temperature to 1700 °C, only a single phase of AlON is observed.

The solid-state byproduct resulting from incomplete hydrolysis of metal Al powder can be effectively synthesized into pure-phase AlON powder, as demonstrated in the above analysis. To investigate the morphological evolution process of the composite precursor with increasing nitriding temperature, the products synthesized at different temperatures were characterized via SEM, as depicted in Fig. 6. Sample in Fig. 6(a) has a morphology consistent with the aforementioned analysis, revealing a unique core‒shell structure with a hollow outer core. At a nitridation temperature of 800 °C, the overall morphology of the synthesized product does not change much. However, there is a slight alteration, as indicated by arrow 1 and arrow 2 in Fig. 6(b), and the surface lamellar hydrolysate in the area marked by arrow 2 tends to coarsen, which is consistent with the results of XRD analysis above. At a nitridation temperature of 1300 °C, the overall morphology of the synthesized product significantly changes. Points 1 and 2 in sample in Figs. 6(c1)–6(c3) present large lamellar particles with noticeable coarsening on the surface of the composite precursor. Interestingly, different morphologies at various nitridation temperatures can be observed within the same sample, as shown in Fig. 6(c2). The region indicated by arrow 1 exhibits a lamellar particle sensation, whereas the region indicated by arrow 2 displays a distinct particle sensation, which may correspond to the phase transition of alumina at this temperature and the synthesis of aluminum nitride.

At a nitridation temperature of 1670 °C, the morphology of the synthesized product differs significantly from that of the composite precursor. Figure 6(d) clearly shows that the synthesized product consists of large, nearly spherical particles formed by the aggregation of numerous small particles. This can be attributed to both an increase in the calcination temperature and the synthesis of the AlON phase. At a nitridation temperature of 1700 °C, as indicated at points 1 and 2 in Fig. 6(e), the large, nearly spherical particles expand further, whereas the small surface particles gradually increase in size. Additionally, some large particles that are nearly spherical break up and exhibit hollow structures. By grinding and sieving the sample in Fig. 6(e), one can obtain a uniform nanometer-sized (D*50 = 292 nm) AlON pure-phase powder. This is quite different from the particles in the enlarged region in Fig. 6(f), which may be due to the poor dispersion and severe agglomeration of the powder.

## 4 Conclusions

Through further study of Al hydrolysis, Al–H2O reaction can be divided into three processes: The first stage involves the induction of hydrolysis, during which the complete structure of the Al surface is destroyed, lamellar boehmite products emerge, a small number of bubbles appear in the solution, and a small amount of hydrogen is produced. The second stage is characterized by a rapid hydrolysis reaction, during which Al is rapidly consumed, the metal Al content decreases, the hydrolysis byproduct content increases, many bubbles emerge in the solution, and a significant amount of hydrogen is released. The third stage represents the termination of the hydrolysis reaction, characterized by the complete consumption of Al and the presence of only hydrolysis byproducts in the composite precursor. During this stage, no bubbles are observed in the solution, and no hydrogen is generated.

The composite precursor in the second stage of Al‒H2O reaction exhibited a unique core‒shell structure, where the metal Al was coated by the hydrolysis byproduct during incomplete hydrolysis. By utilizing this property, AlON powder can be synthesized in a nitrogen atmosphere through theoretical calculations of the appropriate ratio between the desired byproduct and the remaining metal Al (Al2O3/Al = 10.27). The core‒shell structure decreases the diffusion distance between raw materials and reduces the nitridation temperature (1700 °C). Furthermore, the unique hollow structure of the composite precursor results in some of the synthesized AlON powders also having a hollow structure, which is conducive to powder crushing, and fine nanoscale (*D*50 = 292 nm) powders can be obtained only via a grinding process.

In summary, this study significantly advances our understanding of the hydrolysis process of metal Al, enhances the synthesis method for producing fine AlON powder, and provides a new way to recover the solid-state hydrolysis byproduct of metal Al powder.
