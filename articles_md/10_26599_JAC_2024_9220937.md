## 1 Introduction

Dielectric ceramic capacitors for pulsing power systems exhibit broad applications in national defense, medical equipment, aerospace, and other fields due to their distinctive features of high power density and ultrafast charge/discharge performance [1,2]. As a crucial part of dielectric capacitors, dielectric ceramic materials can be divided into linear dielectrics (LDs), ferroelectrics (FEs), relaxor ferroelectrics (RFEs), and antiferroelectrics (AFEs) based on their electrical properties under an electric field [3−5]. Among them, AFEs possess almost no remanent polarization (*P*r) because the antiparallel dipole moments between two adjacent crystalline lattices cancel each other out after the external electric field is withdrawn [4,6]. Moreover, AFEs also exhibit superior maximum polarization (*P*max) because the electric field-induced AFE–FE phase transition causes a significant jump in polarization [7]. Therefore, AFEs exhibit outstanding energy storage properties and hold great promise for practical applications. The corresponding key energy storage performance parameters include the recoverable energy storage density (*W*rec), total energy storage density (*W*total), and energy efficiency (*η*), which can be calculated by Eqs. (1)–(3) [8−14]:

(1) Wrec=∫PrPmaxPdE

(2) Wtotal=∫0PmaxPdE

(3) η=WrecWtotal×100%

where *P* (µC·cm−2) is the total electric dipole moment per unit volume in a material under the influence of an electric field; *E* (kV·cm−1) is the strength of the electric field, which is the force experienced by a charge per unit length; remanent polarization (*P*r) refers to the polarization intensity that remains in the material after the electric field intensity has returned to zero. Lead-based ceramics are the main kind of AFE materials for commercial dielectric ceramic capacitors. However, lead-based ceramic materials inevitably cause severe harm to the environment and human health [15,16]. Therefore, it is urgent to develop lead-free dielectric ceramic materials to replace lead-based ceramic materials.

Relaxor antiferroelectric (RAFE) ceramics for dielectric capacitor applications have been studied in several lead-free dielectric ceramic systems, such as NaNbO3-based systems [17−21]. Introducing multiple ions to the A-site and B-site can improve the degree of lattice disorder and the degree of configuration entropy. Then, the long-range order of the AFE weakens, and the domain size decreases to the nanoscale, thus converting the AFE phase to the RAFE phase. Compared with those of AFE ceramics, the *P*–*E* hysteresis loops of RAFE ceramics exhibit a smaller hysteresis, thereby greatly increasing *η*. Moreover, the smoother AFE–FE phase transition of RAFE ceramics than AFE ceramics effectively mitigates the generation of internal stresses, which improves their stability under electric fields and results in a higher dielectric breakdown strength (*E*b). Thus, the *W*rec of the RAFE ceramics significantly increases. For example, 0.76NaNbO3–0.24(Bi0.5Na0.5)TiO3 RAFE ceramics with nanoscale domains of 30–50 nm in size realized an ultrahigh *W*rec of 12.2 J·cm−3 because of the high *E*b (690 kV·cm−1) and large Δ*P* (*P*max − *P*r, ~50 μC·cm−2) [19]. A high *η* of 81.3% and an ultrahigh *W*rec of 7.59 J·cm−3 were also achieved for the 0.88(0.85NaNbO3–0.15Sr0.7Bi0.2TiO3)–0.12Bi(Mg0.5Zr0.5)O3 ceramics. Sr0.7Bi0.2TiO3–Bi(Mg0.5Zr0.5)O3 improved the degree of A/B-site disorder, broke former macrodomains into nanodomains, and enhanced the relaxor characteristics [18]. Thus, inducing relaxation by constructing a solid solution of multiple compositions in AFE ceramics is an effective method for improving energy storage performance.

AgNbO3 (AN), one of the most promising lead-free AFE materials, has been extensively studied and reported due to its double *P*–*E* hysteresis loops and high *P*max [22]. As the temperature increases, the pure AgNbO3 possesses various phase transitions of M1 (orthorhombic, *Pmc*21) * →67°C M2 (orthorhombic, Pbcm*) * →267°C M3 (orthorhombic, Pbcm*) * →353°C O (orthorhombic, Cmcm*) * →378°CT (orthorhombic, P*4/*mbm*) [23]. The M1 phase exhibits ferrielectric (FIE) behavior and has double *P*–*E* hysteresis loops with high *P*r (~8 μC·cm−2) [24]. M2 and M3 phases exhibit AFE behavior, while the O and T phases exhibit paraelectric (PE) behavior. The pure AgNbO3 ceramics exhibited a low *W*rec of 1.6 J·cm−3 with a low *η* of 37.0% because of its FIE behavior at room temperature (RT) [10]. Therefore, most of the research efforts in the past several years have focused on A-site or/and B-site chemical modifications to improve energy storage performance. By adding a dopant, the RT FIE phase of the AgNbO3-based ceramics can transform into the AFE phase, which reduces the *P*r and improves the *E*b [25−33]. Due to the limited number and proportion of dopant ions on the A-site or B-site of existing AgNbO3-based compositions, it is difficult to form disordered local structures and polar nanoregions (PNRs) [4]. As a result, the obtained *P*–*E* hysteresis loops are usually square and exhibit a large hysteresis, resulting in a low *W*rec and *η*. Compared with those of lead-based ceramics, the energy storage performances of existing AgNbO3-based ceramics are temporarily unable to meet practical application requirements. For this reason, it is essential to apply new and feasible material design strategies to obtain AgNbO3-based ceramic compositions that realize the simultaneous improvement of *W*rec and *η*.

In this work, we designed a promising AgNbO3–NaNbO3–Sr0.7Bi0.2TiO3 (AN–NN–SBT) ternary solid solution with the aim of attaining enhanced relaxation in AFEs. Both AN and NN are AFEs with orthorhombic phase structures and close A-site ionic radii (Ag+, 1.48 Å, CN = 12; Na+, 1.39 Å, CN = 12), which are able to form an infinite AN–NN solid solution for the introduction of other end-members [34−36]. The larger band gap (*E*g) of NN (3.18 eV) compared to that of AN (2.78 eV) can also increase the *E*b of the system [37]. In addition, the replacement of Ag+ with Na+ can significantly reduce the manufacturing cost and is more compatible with the commercial application of Ag+ in dielectric ceramic capacitors. Sr0.7Bi0.2TiO3 (SBT) is a typical RFE with strong relaxor characteristics originating from the presence of Sr vacancies and the off-center migration of Bi [6]. Therefore, SBT possesses negligible ferroelectric switching hysteresis, exhibiting an ultrahigh *η* of 92.5% and a relatively low *W*rec of 2.6 J·cm−3 [38]. The introduction of SBT in the AN-based system can possibly improve the degree of A/B-site local disorder and break long-range macrodomains into short-range nanodomains, thereby achieving a slim relaxor-like *P*–*E* loop. Additionally, the addition of SBT can lower the sintering temperature of AN-based ceramics and, therefore, refine the grain size, which is beneficial for improving the *E*b [39−41]. As a result,  relaxor antiferroelectric feature was successfully realized, and a high *W*rec of 7.53 J·cm−3 and *η* of 74.0% were obtained for the optimized composition of 0.8(0.7AgNbO3–0.3NaNbO3)–0.2Sr0.7Bi0.2TiO3 under a high *E*b of 478 kV·cm−1. This work not only presented promising AN–NN–SBT ceramics with excellent energy storage performance but also underscored a novel ternary solid solution material design strategy to induce the RAFE phase in AN-based ceramics for energy storage applications.

## 2 Experimental

(1−*x*)(0.7AgNbO3–0.3NaNbO3)–*x*Sr0.7Bi0.2TiO3 (abbreviated as AN–NN–SBT100*x*; *x* = 0.00, 0.10, 0.15, 0.18, 0.20, and 0.25) ceramics were prepared by a conventional solid-state reaction process. The starting materials, including Ag2O (99.7%), Nb2O5 (99.99%), Na2CO3 (99.8%), Sr2CO3 (99.99%), Bi2O3 (99.999%), and TiO2 (99.9%), were weighed and mixed according to the stoichiometric proportions. The raw materials were ball milled for 4 h using zirconium balls in anhydrous ethanol. After drying, the mixtures were calcined at 870 °C for 4 h in an oxygen atmosphere. The calcined powders were ball-milled again with smaller zirconium balls to refine the grain size. After that, the powders were pressed into pellets 13 mm in diameter with 7.0 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) as the binder. After burning the PVA at 700 °C for 2 h, the samples were sintered at 1010–1110 °C for 2 h.

The phase structures of the AN–NN–SBT100*x* ceramics were studied by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/max-2550V, Rigaku UK) using Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5406 Å). Raman scattering spectra were obtained using a Raman spectrometer (inVia, Renishaw, UK). The microstructures of the ceramics were investigated by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; S-4800, Hitachi, Japan). The domain morphology and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns were obtained via FE-TEM (JEM-F200, JEOL, Japan). The polished samples were used for spectroscopic ellipsometry (SE) measurements, which were carried out on a visible–ultraviolet spectroscopic ellipsometer (V-VASE, J. A. Woollam Co., Ltd., USA) in the photon energy range of 1.24–4.96 eV (1000–250 nm) with a fixed incident angle of 70° and a spectral resolution of 5 nm. Through ion sputtering, Au electrodes with a diameter of 1 mm were made on both sides of the ceramic samples with a thickness of ~100 μm. The polarization–electric (*P*–*E*) field hysteresis loops were measured using ceramic samples with a ferroelectric measurement system at 10 Hz (TF Analyzer 2000E, aixACCT, Germany). A broad-frequency dielectric spectrometer (Concept 80, Novocontrol GmbH, Germany) was used to measure the temperature/frequency-dependent dielectric properties. The underdamped and overdamped charge‒discharge properties of the prepared ceramics were characterized using a commercial charge‒discharge platform (CFD-001, Gogo Instruments Technology, China). The ceramic samples for the charge‒discharge test were 1 mm in diameter and ~100 μm in thickness.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of AN–NN–SBT100*x* ceramics, which possess a pure perovskite structure without impurities. The clear splitting of the (220) and (008) diffraction peaks at approximately 46° at *x* = 0.00 implies the presence of the M1 or M2 phase at RT. The merging of the (220) and (008) diffraction peaks at *x* = 0.10 indicates the direct composition-driven phase transition to the M3 phase [42], which stabilizes the AFE phase [43,44]. The ionic radii of Na+ (1.39 Å, CN = 12), Sr2+ (1.44 Å, CN = 12), and Bi3+ (1.38 Å, CN = 12) are slightly less than that of A-site Ag+ (1.48 Å, CN = 12), while the ionic radius of Ti4+ (0.605 Å, CN = 6) is slightly less than that of B-site Nb5+ (0.64 Å, CN = 6) [36]. Thus, Na+, Sr2+, and Bi3+ tend to substitute for Ag+, while Ti4+ prefers to replace Nb5+. In Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), the XRD patterns in the range of 30°–33° indicate that the (114) diffraction peak shifts to higher 2*θ* angles with increasing *x*, which is attributed to the increase in the content of smaller radius ions introduced at the A/B site. The phase structures of the AN–NN–SBT100*x* ceramics at RT were further quantitatively determined through Rietveld refinement of the XRD data, as depicted in Figs. 1(d) and 1(e), and Fig. S2 in the ESM. The corresponding low-reliability factors of the weighted patterns (*R*wp) and patterns (*R*p), as well as the goodness-of-fit indicator (*χ*2), confirm satisfactory fitting results between the experimental and calculated profiles. Furthermore, the *R*wp, *R*p, and *χ*2 based on the *Pbcm* and *Cmcm* space groups in Fig. 1(e) are lower than those based on the *Cmcm* space group in Fig. S3 in the ESM, indicating that our results based on the M3 (*Pbcm*) and O (*Cmcm*) phases are more convincing than those based on the O phase. Specifically, the Rietveld refinement results further demonstrate that the addition of SBT causes a phase transition from the M1 phase (*Pmc*21) at *x* = 0.00 to the M3 phase (*Pbcm*) at *x* = 0.10–0.15, while the mixed phases of M3 (*Pbcm*) and O (*Cmcm*) are illustrated at *x* = 0.18–0.20, which finally evolve into the pure O phase (*Cmcm*) at *x* = 0.25. The detailed RT phases of the AN–NN–SBT100*x* ceramics are summarized in Table S1 in the ESM. The coexistence of the AFE M3 phase and PE O phase supports the feasibility of realizing RAFE characteristics with slim polarization switching behavior for *x* = 0.18 and *x* = 0.20.

Figures 2(a)–2(f) show SEM images of the AN–NN–SBT100*x* ceramic samples, which exhibit dense microstructures for all the compositions. Due to the decrease in sintering temperature from 1110 °C at *x* = 0.00 to 1010 °C at *x* = 0.25 caused by the addition of SBT, the average grain size gradually reduces from 1.40 μm at *x* = 0.00 to 0.84 μm at *x* = 0.25. The smaller grain sizes of the higher SBT component lead to higher grain boundary densities, resulting in more depletion regions that can prevent charge carrier delivery in the electrical breakdown process, which contributes to the higher *E*b values of AN–NN–SBT100*x* ceramics [55−57]. As Fig. 2(g) shows, the corresponding *E*b, which is derived from the intersection of the fitted line with the horizontal axis, increases from 253 kV·cm−1 at *x* = 0.00 to 501 kV·cm−1 at *x* = 0.25. It is accepted that the increase in *E*b is not only related to the small grain size but also influenced by the large *E*g, which can impede the electronic transition from the valence band maximum to the conduction band minimum [60−63]. Therefore, we also measured the bandgap of the AN–NN–SBT100*x* ceramics by SE, as shown in Fig. 2(h), which shows the relationship between the phonon energy and *ε*1 and *ε*2. The imaginary part *ε*2 of all AN–NN–SBT ceramics is almost zero below the absorption edges, indicating that no additional electronic transitions occur in the low-energy range [64]. Then, the electronic transition leads to an abrupt increase in *ε*2 above the absorption edges, and the blueshift of the absorption edge with increasing *x* implies the growth of *E*g. Thus, *E*g gradually increases from 2.98 eV at *x* = 0.00 to 3.07 eV at *x* = 0.25, with reliable fitting results as exemplified by the consistency between the experimental spectra and simulation spectra at *x* = 0.20 in the inset (Fig. 2(i)). Therefore, it can be concluded that the introduction of SBT not only refines the grain size but also enlarges the *E*g, which greatly enhances the *E*b and favors the energy storage performance.

Figure 3(a) shows the unipolar *P*–*E* loops of AN–NN–SBT100*x* ceramics under an electric field of 250 kV·cm−1 and a frequency of 10 Hz. As the SBT content increases, the hysteresis and *P*max of the corresponding *P*–*E* loops continuously decrease, accompanied by a gradual transformation from square *P*–*E* loops to slim *P*–*E* loops. Figures 3(b) and 3(c) show the unipolar *P*–*E* and *I*–*E* loops of the AN–NN–SBT100*x* ceramics at RT under their respective *E*b. The *x* = 0.00 composition displays a square-like *P*–*E* loop with large hysteresis, which is ascribed to the FIE properties of the M1 phase and has an adverse influence on its energy storage performance. Two current peaks resulting from the FIE/AFE properties in *I‒E* loops of *x* = 0.00‒0.15, labeled *E*F and *E*A, represent the electric field-induced AFE‒FE and FE‒AFE phase transitions, respectively [65−67], and the rightward shift of the two peaks indicates that the AFE phase at *x* = 0.15 is more stable than that at *x* = 0.10 [68]. The *E*F and *E*A peaks become wider and flatter at *x* = 0.18‒0.25, which is attributed to the formation of the O phase. Moreover, the existence of current peaks at *x* = 0.18 and *x* = 0.20 is confirmed by the d*I*/d*E* curves under positive and negative electric fields in Fig. S5 in the ESM. Under positive electric fields, the transition of d*I*/d*E* from positive to negative corresponds to the *E*F, while under negative electric fields, the transition of d*I*/d*E* from negative to positive corresponds to the *E*A. Figures 3(d) and 3(e) provide *P*max, *P*r, Δ*P* (*P*max − *P*r), *W*rec, and *η* of the AN–NN–SBT100*x* ceramics as a function of the SBT concentration under the respective *E*b. *P*r decreases from 16.2 μC·cm−2 at *x* = 0.00 to 3.9 μC·cm−2 at *x* = 0.25 owing to FIE–AFE and AFE–PE phase transitions driven by continuously increasing *x*. Consequently, Δ*P* increases from 30.9 μC·cm−2 at *x* = 0.00 to 43.6 μC·cm−2 at *x* = 0.20, which is beneficial for improving the energy storage performance. Therefore, simultaneous improvements in *W*rec and *η* from *x* = 0.00 to *x* = 0.20 are successfully achieved, as shown in Fig. 3(d). However, because the polarization behavior is completely dominated by the PE phase at *x* = 0.25, *P*max decreases sharply from 48.9 μC·cm−2 at *x* = 0.20 to 36.0 μC·cm−2 at *x* = 0.25, directly resulting in a sudden decrease in *W*rec. For *x* = 0.20 with RAFE properties, the coexistence of the AFE and PE phases ensures the simultaneous achievement of relatively high *P*max and small hysteresis in *P*–*E* loops, achieving the highest *W*rec of 7.53 J·cm−3 and *η* of 74.0% among the AN–NN–SBT100*x* ceramics. Figure 3(f) illustrates that the *x* = 0.20 ceramics in this work achieve a concurrent increase in both *W*rec and *η* [16,25,27−31,63,68−78], and the obtained energy storage performance is relatively high among the recently reported AN-based bulk ceramics. The high energy storage parameters in the RAFE demonstrate that constructing complex ternary solid solutions is an effective method for enhancing the energy storage performance of AN-based ceramics.

Figures 4(a)–4(f) display temperature-dependent *ε*r and tan*δ* curves of AN–NN–SBT100*x* ceramics at different frequencies. Several dielectric anomalies are observed in the *ε*r curves, and the evolution of the dielectric properties is consistent with the analysis of the XRD and Raman spectra. The dielectric anomalies in the tan*δ* curve for *x* = 0.00 and *x* = 0.10 are attributed to the M2–M3 phase transition. In concrete terms, *x* = 0.00 undergoes continuous M1–M2, M2–M3, M3–O, and O–T phase transitions over the temperature range from −120 to 600 °C. As the SBT content increases, the M3–O phase transition temperature decreases from 413 °C (*x* = 0.00) to 198 °C (*x* = 0.10) and then decreases to 131 °C (*x* = 0.15). However, the M3–O phase transition peak at *x* = 0.18–0.20 is dispersed due to the coexistence of M3 and O phases over a wide temperature range, and increasing *x* promotes flatter *ε*r curves (Fig. S6(a) in the ESM). It can be seen that *x* = 0.20 has the best dielectric temperature stability within the temperature range of ((−55)–125 °C) among the tested AN–NN–SBT100*x* ceramics. Figure S6(b) in the ESM shows the temperature-dependent *ε*r curve at 1 kHz for *x* = 0.20 with a variation rate of *ε*r. A variation rate of 6.5% in *ε*r ensures the stability of the energy storage characteristics over a wide temperature range, showing high promise in meeting the practical requirements for X7R-type dielectric ceramic capacitors.

To further investigate the underlying mechanism of the enhanced energy storage performance, the domain morphology and SAED patterns at *x* = 0.00 and *x* = 0.20 were characterized and compared, as shown in Figs. 4(g)–4(j). Generally, the (00*l*) reflections with *l* = 2*n* + 1 indicate the presence of the low-symmetry space group *Pmc*21 [65]. Therefore, the appearance of the (* 003¯) reflection, marked by the red arrow in Fig. 4(h), is attributed to the M1 phase at x* = 0.00 at RT, resulting in a macrodomain with a width greater than 500 nm at *x* = 0.00. Furthermore, the existence of the macrodomain strongly inhibits domain orientation switching, which leads to a large polarization hysteresis of the *P*–*E* loop and hinders the improvement of *W*rec and *η*. After the addition of SBT, the suppressed M1 phase illustrates the disappearance of the (* 003¯) reflection, while the formation of the O phase with space group Cmcm* facilitates the emergence of (* 011¯) reflection at x* = 0.20 [28], which is closely related to the relaxor characteristics at *x* = 0.20. Moreover, adding SBT to the AN-based system significantly increases the number of element species, thereby greatly improving the degree of local disorder at the A/B sites. Therefore, the long-range ordered macrodomains of *x* = 0.00 break into nanodomains of *x* = 0.20 in Fig. 4(i1), and the elliptical regions with different contrasts derived from Fig. 4(i1) through the inverse Fourier transform are identified as the nanodomains of 10–20 nm in Fig. 4(i2) [79]. The formation of nanodomains causes rapid domain orientation switching in response to the external electric field, greatly improving the energy storage properties.

The stabilities of the energy storage performance at various temperatures, frequencies, and cycle numbers are also critical requirements for realizing real pulse power applications in dielectric ceramics. Figures 5(a) and 5(b) illustrate the temperature-dependent *P*–*E* loops and energy storage performance of *x* = 0.20 in the temperature range of (−120)–120 °C at 300 kV·cm−1 (~60% of *E*b) and a frequency of 10 Hz. As the temperature increases, *P*–*E* loops at *x* = 0.20 remain slim, and *P*max and *P*r exhibit a slight increasing tendency. Nonetheless, *W*rec nearly remains constant at ~4.02 J·cm−3 with a variation of less than ±3.1%, while *η* decreases from 80.4% at −120 °C to 75.2% at 120 °C, which demonstrates a relatively satisfactory temperature stability of *x* = 0.20. To determine the origin of the temperature stability, the Raman spectra at *x* = 0.20 were obtained from (−120) to 120 °C, as shown in Fig. 5(c), and the corresponding peaks were fitted using the Lorentz function, as exemplified by the Raman spectra at −120 and 120 °C in Figs. 5(d) and 5(e). Three peaks, labeled *ν*1, *ν*5, and *ν*6, are consistently present across the temperature range from −120 to 120 °C, indicating stable vibration modes at *x* = 0.20 [54,80]. Figure 5(f) displays the wavenumbers of *ν*1, *ν*5, and *ν*6 modes at various temperatures, which fluctuate slightly at approximately 570, 210, and 110 cm−1, respectively. Therefore, within the broad temperature range, the phase structure at *x* = 0.20 remains stable, and no structural phase transition occurs. Figure 5(g) shows the variation in the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the three peaks as a function of varying temperatures, which continuously increases throughout the test temperature range, indicating inhibited polarity and enhanced structural disorder [81−83]. Although the FWHM of the *ν*1, *ν*5, and *ν*6 modes increase slightly, *x* = 0.20 maintains the relaxor properties with temperature changes, which accounts for the correlated temperature stability.

The variations in *W*rec and *η* are less than ±6.0% and ±3.1%, respectively, in the range of 10–200 Hz, revealing the superior stability of both *W*rec and *η* over a wide frequency range. Figure 6(b) and Fig. S7(b) in the ESM present *P*–*E* loops and an energy storage performance of *x* = 0.20 under 1–106 electrical cycles. Although the maximum cycle number reaches 106, the fluctuations in *W*rec and *η* are only approximately ±1.3% and ±2.3%, respectively, indicating excellent fatigue cycling stability. For typical AFEs, the field-induced phase transition between the AFE and FE phases could lead to the generation of large strains, which is detrimental to the fatigue cycling stability [84]. However, in our case, the coexistence of the AFE and PE phases contributes to the sluggish AFE–FE phase transition in the *x* = 0.20 RAFE ceramics, which greatly diminishes the phase transition-induced strain, contributing to satisfactory fatigue cycling stability.

The charge‒discharge performance of dielectric ceramics, another crucial factor for practical energy storage applications, is investigated using an *RLC* load circuit with resistance and inductance capable of high-speed discharge. When the applied resistance is 200 Ω, the overdamped discharge curves and the discharge energy density (*W*dis) of *x* = 0.20 under evolving electric fields from 40 to 200 kV·cm−1 are displayed in Figs. 6(c) and 6(d). The *W*dis was calculated using Eq. (8) [70]:

(8) Wdis=R∫I2(t)dtV

where *R* and *V* are the resistance and tested volume, respectively. The calculated *W*dis of *x* = 0.20 is 1.39 J·cm−3 at 200 kV·cm−1, which is lower than the *W*rec of 1.99 J·cm−3 at the same electric field. The viscous force of the high-frequency charge‒discharge test exceeds that of the low-frequency *P*‒*E* experiment, resulting in faster domain wall motion [85,86]. Consequently, additional energy loss occurs during charge‒discharge testing, causing *W*dis to be lower than *W*rec. *t*0.9, which refers to the time to release 90% saturated *W*dis, is a vital parameter for evaluating the discharge rate of dielectrics [41]. Within the electric field from 40 to 200 kV·cm−1, the value of *t*0.9 remains consistently low at ~41 ns, indicating an ultrafast discharge rate of *x* = 0.20. The above excellent charge‒discharge performance suggests that *x* = 0.20 is a promising candidate for pulse power system applications.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, we designed and synthesized new lead-free RAFE materials by constructing a (1−*x*)(0.7AgNbO3–0.3NaNbO3)–*x*Sr0.7Bi0.2TiO3 ternary solid solution. The introduction of SBT successfully inhibits FIE features and results in the coexistence of antiferroelectric (M3) and paraelectric (O) phases, resulting in the RAFE characteristics of the 0.8(0.7AgNbO3–0.3NaNbO3)–0.2Sr0.7Bi0.2TiO3 ceramics. Moreover, the refined grains and enlarged *E*g contribute to the enhanced dielectric breakdown strength. Therefore, a large *W*rec of 7.53 J·cm−3 and a high *η* of 74.0% under 478 kV·cm−1 have been achieved in AN–NN–SBT20 RAFE ceramics, which also exhibit excellent stabilities under various temperatures ((−120)–120 °C), frequencies (10–200 Hz), and fatigue cycles (1–106 cycles). Furthermore, AN–NN–SBT20 ceramics exhibit a high power density of 298.7 MW·cm−3 and a fast discharge time of 41.4 ns. The above merits indicate that the AN–NN–SBT20 ternary solid solution designed in this work is a promising candidate for energy storage applications and thereafter highlights the construction of ternary solid solution RAFEs as a novel avenue for designing AN-based energy storage materials.
