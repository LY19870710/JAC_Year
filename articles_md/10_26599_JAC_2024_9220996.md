## 1 Introduction

With the rapid development of industrialization and modernization, large amounts of organic pollutants such as phenols, dyes, pesticides, and antibiotics are usually poured into the water environment. These pollutants have poor biodegradability and easily accumulate in water bodies for a long period of time, which is an increasingly serious threat to the health of the water environment [1,2]. Therefore, extensive efforts have been directed toward the development of advanced technology for the efficient degradation of organic pollutants [3−7]. Advanced catalytic oxidation, as an environmentally friendly and highly efficient method of pollutant degradation, has been widely accepted [8].

However, single catalysts for advanced catalytic oxidation often suffer from problems such as rapid recombination of electron‒hole pairs [9], which has driven many efforts to develop means of metal or nonmetal doping, heterojunction modification, and multifield coupling to improve the catalytic performance. Heterojunction catalysts composed of two semiconductor materials can not only increase their specific surface areas and active sites [10] but also form an interfacial electric field (IEF) that provides a driving force for carrier separation. For example, the Ov–BiOBr/Cu2−*x*S high-low junction induces an enhanced IEF, which is then coupled with its built-in electric field [11]. As a result, the spatial separation and transfer of charges are greatly facilitated. Although the catalytic efficiency was improved, the IEF in the vast majority of the catalysts was not strong enough to resist the limitations of Coulomb interactions. In addition, the IEF in heterojunction catalysts is static and easily saturated by internal and external shielding effects [12,13].

Piezoelectric catalysis is proposed to provide an ideal strategy for improving the dynamic direction alternation of the IEF in heterojunctions. The internal built-in electric field changes direction when subjected to external mechanical action, which facilitates the efficient separation of electrons and enhances the photocatalytic performance. Recently, piezoelectric materials such as BaTiO3@C [14], TiO2/BiFeO3 [15], and BaTiO3/Pr2O3 [16] have shown promising potential for catalytic degradation applications because of their efficient charge separation efficiencies, but these improvements are often attributed to improvements in the piezoelectric coefficients and surface voltages. Instead, Wang’s group [17] proposed piezoelectric photonics, in which the piezoelectric potential in heterojunction semiconductors can drive photogenerated electron–hole interactions across the charge depletion region at the p–n junction. Zhai *et* *al*. [18] prepared ultrathin g-C3N4/basic g-C3N4 and demonstrated that the invariance of the polarization direction promoted a change in the IEF. A transition of the charge-transfer pathway shifts between the type-II and Z regimes, which facilitates electron/hole spatial separation and migration kinetics. Obviously, this piezoelectric potential is not static in practice; therefore, it is important to construct a dynamic IEF to improve the catalytic degradation performance.

Moreover, it is difficult for conventional photocatalytic reactions to continue in low-light environments such as rainy, cloudy, hazy, or nighttime environments [19]. Li *et* *al*. [20] proposed a “photocatalytic memory effect” to store high-energy photogenerated electrons on the basis of the phenomenon of natural photosynthesis. After the light is turned off, these stored electrons can be released to generate reactive groups that can remain active under light-free conditions. Ruan *et al*. [21] utilized TiO*x*/CN Faraday junctions to store photogenerated charges in redox reactions, enabling continuous and efficient hydrogen production under intermittent sunlight conditions. However, these catalytic performances in the dark after illumination were still much lower than those under light. Therefore, constructing a suitable piezoelectric heterojunction has become a new and effective strategy for improving all-weather sustained catalytic degradation performance.

Herein, we propose the integration of BaTiO3 and BiFeO3 to form efficient heterostructured photoassisted piezocatalysis for round-the-clock degradation of organic pollutants in wastewater (Fig. 1). BiFeO3 nanoparticles are immobilized on BaTiO3 nanorods, which is expected to prevent the excessive aggregation of BiFeO3 particles and thus reduce the diffusion path of the photogenerated charge carriers. The IEF formed in the heterojunction provides a driving force for carrier separation and migration. Moreover, it can be activated and repeatedly constructed through rapid changes in the polarization direction of BaTiO3 nanorods under ultrasonic wave irradiation. Unsurprisingly, the active IEF in BiFeO3@BaTiO3 (BFO@BTO) heterojunction can not only promote catalytic activity under light but also realize continuous and efficient catalysis in the dark. BiFeO3 nanoparticles with excellent light-trapping properties were synthesized via a hydrothermal method and anchored on BaTiO3 nanorods to form BFO@BTO heterojunctions. Through the optical band gap and functional constituent regulation, the constructed heterojunction has a sensitive photoresponse in ultraviolet–visible (UV‒Vis) region and a piezoelectric polarization effect, which enhances its ability to transport carriers and generate free radicals and thus contributes to the redox degradation of organic pollutants in wastewater under light/dark conditions. The prepared BFO@BTO-1:1 heterojunction exhibited photoassisted piezocatalytic synergistic degradation activity under simulated light and ultrasonic wave irradiation, with a catalytic degradation efficiency of 0.038 min−1, which was 1.9 and 7.0 times greater than that of photocatalysis and piezoelectric catalysis alone, respectively. The degradation efficiencies were 93.3% and 89.1% under three “light‒dark” cycles for the simulated high concentrations of the pollutants rhodamine B (RhB) and tetracycline hydrochloride (TC), respectively. Photoassisted piezocatalysis provides an energy-sustainability method for the rapid degradation of organic pollutants.

### 2.1 Preparation of BiFeO<sub>3</sub>

First, 50 mL of acetone was added to a mixture of 2.425 g of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O and 0.81 g of FeCl3 at a molar ratio of 1:1. After stirring for 30 min, 150 mL of deionized (DI) water was added, and pH was adjusted to 11 by adding concentrated ammonia dropwise. The resulting reddish-brown precipitate was washed with water until neutral and further dispersed in 40 mL of NaOH (5 M) in a beaker for 30 min. The mixture was transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and reacted at 140 °C for24 h. The collected black powder was washed with DI water several times until the pH reached 7 and then dried in an oven (DZF-6020, Shanghai Jinghong Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd., China) at 80 °C for 12 h. The sample was identified as BFO.

### 2.2 Preparation of BaTiO<sub>3</sub>

BaTiO3 nanorods were synthesized via a two-step approach. First, 1.6 g of P25 was added to 40 mL of NaOH solution (10 M) and subsequently transferred to a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave for hydrothermal reaction at 240 °C for 24 h. The obtained precipitate was added to a 0.2 M HCl solution and slowly stirred for 4 h for ion exchange. The precipitate was washed in deionized water until neutral and then dried in a 60 °C oven for 12 h. The obtained white powder was named H2Ti3O7. Next, 1.1 g of Ba(OH)2·8H2O was dissolved in 70 mL of deionized water. H2Ti3O7 (0.15 g) was added to 70 mL of Ba(OH)2·8H2O solution. The resulting mixture was transferred to the inner lining of a hydrothermal kettle, sonicated for 5 min, and heated at 210 °C for 12 h. After filtration, washing, and drying, a BaTiO3 white powder was prepared and named BTO.

### 2.3 Preparation of BFO@BTO

Bi(NO3)3·5H2O, FeCl3, and BTO with a molar ratio of 1:1:1 were dissolved in 50 mL of acetone and stirred vigorously for 30 min. Subsequently, the pH of the mixed solution was adjusted to 11 by adding a concentrated ammonia solution. The precipitated reddish-brown product was filtered, washed, and dispersed in a 40 mL NaOH (5 M) beaker. Finally, the mixture was transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless steel reactor heated at 180 °C for 12 h. After washing and drying at 80 °C for 12 h, the product was a reddish-brown powder, denoted BFO@BTO-1:1. In addition, the molar ratio of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O to FeCl3 was adjusted to BFO@BTO-1:2 when the ratio was 1:2 and BFO@BTO-2:1 when the ratio was 2:1.

### 2.4 Photoassisted piezocatalytic experiments and characterization of the catalysts

To evaluate the photocatalytic performance of the BFO@BTO catalyst. BFO@BTO (50 mg) was added to 50 mL of RhB solution (5 mg·L−1) and stirred in the dark for 30 min to establish adsorption‒desorption equilibrium. RhB solution degradation experiments were carried out under 180 W and 40 kHz ultrasonic vibration under dark conditions to simulate night conditions, whereas degradation experiments under irradiation (300 W xenon lamp, 100 mW·cm−2, CEL-HXF300, Beijing CE Au-Light Co., Ltd., China) at room temperature were performed to simulate daytime conditions to achieve round-the-clock degradation experiments. The solution concentration was analyzed via a UV‒Vis‒near-infrared (UV‒Vis‒NIR) spectrophotometer (V-570, JASCO, Japan) at intervals of 15 min.

The crystal structure of each sample was analyzed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; PANalytical Empyrean 2, Panalytical, the Netherlands). The Raman shifts were carried out by a Raman laser spectrometer (Raman, LabRAM HR Evolution, France). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed with an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (ESCALAB250Xi, Thermo Fisher, USA). The morphology and structure of the samples were investigated by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Sigma 500, ZEISS, Germany) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2S-Twin, FEI, USA) with an X-ray spectrometer for an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS; Aztec X-MAX 80, Oxford, UK). UV‒Vis diffuse reflectance spectra (UV‒Vis DRS) were obtained by a UV‒Vis‒NIR spectrometer (V-570, JASCO, Japan) using BaSO4 as the standard. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were obtained with a fluorescence spectrometer (FLS1000, Edinburgh, UK). Piezo-response force microscopy (PFM) was performed via an atomic force microscope (AFM; MultiMode 8, Bruker Dimension Icon, Germany) equipped with an SCM-PIT-V2 probe under an alternating current (AC) voltage of 10 V (13 kHz).

### 3.1 Morphology of the sample

A schematic diagram of the three-step hydrothermal self-assembly of BFO@BTO catalyst is shown in Fig. 2(a). SEM images of pure BaTiO3 nanorods revealed a bead-chain structure with a width of 100–150 nm and a length of several microns (Fig. 2(b)), which assisted in mutual adhesion. The individually prepared pure BiFeO3 nanoparticles are in the form of multilayers stacked to a large diameter (Fig. 2(c)). Interestingly, the micron-scale BiFeO3 particles are changed into nanoscale particles through three-step hydrothermal self-assembly (Fig. 2(d)), which facilitates the formation of a BFO@BTO heterojunction with enhanced interfacial combination. TEM images in Fig. 2(e) show that the surface of pure BaTiO3 is smooth, whereas many tiny BiFeO3 nanoparticles are anchored on BaTiO3 nanorods (Fig. 2(f)). Figure 2(h) shows a selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern confirming that BaTiO3 is in the tetragonal phase. Under the action of force, tetragonal BaTiO3 is prone to deform, and the center dipole is displaced, resulting in a polarization electric field. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images (Figs. 2(g) and 2(i)) show that the lattice spacings of BaTiO3 nanorods and BiFeO3 particles are 0.393 and 0.218 nm, respectively, corresponding to the (010) and (202) planes of BaTiO3 and BiFeO3 [22]. The element mapping shown in Fig. 2(k) further proves that the prepared BFO@BTO-1:1 catalyst contains Ba, Bi, Ti, Fe, and O. BiFeO3 nanoparticles are uniformly loaded on the surface of BaTiO3.

Raman spectroscopy was performed to characterize the phase structure (Fig. 3(c)). The Raman spectra of pure BaTiO3 exhibit several characteristic peaks at 304, 515, and 718 cm−1, which are associated with [E, B1(TO+LO)], [E, A1(TO)], and [E, A1(LO)] modes of BaTiO3. The sharp peaks at 304 and 718 cm−1 are clear evidence that BaTiO3 is in the tetragonal phase, indicating that BaTiO3 transforms into the ferroelectric tetragonal structure [23]. For pure BiFeO3, the peaks at 73.2 cm−1 [E-1], 136.4 cm−1 [A1-1], 171.4 cm−1 [A1-2], and 216.9 cm−1 [A1-3] confirm the rhombic distorted perovskite structure [24]. Moreover, the intensities of A1-1, A1-2, and A1-3 in BFO@BTO decrease as the position shifts, which is due to an increase in Fe–O–Fe bond angle [25]. This shifting and broadening of the Raman modes in BFO@BTO indicate the presence of internal distortion and microstrain, which endows it with excellent piezoelectric properties.

The full solar absorption spectra of the samples are shown in Fig. 3(d). Pure BaTiO3 has a limited absorption wavelength under ultraviolet light, whereas pure BiFeO3 has a wide absorption range in the range of 200–800 nm, indicating that BiFeO3 can be excited by visible light to generate electron‒hole pairs. The absorption spectrum shows that BFO@BTO shifts the light absorption edge to the right, which proves that BiFeO3 and BaTiO3 combine successfully.

XPS was employed to analyze the chemical states of the elements. Figure 3(e) shows the high-resolution O 1s spectra of pure BiFeO3, which can be fitted into three peaks at 535.31, 532.82, and 529.49 eV, and the O 1s spectrum of pure BaTiO3 was fitted into three peaks centered at 534.28, 531.75, and 530.03 eV, corresponding to surface adsorbed water, surface oxygen or hydroxyl radicals, and lattice oxygen, respectively [26]. For the O 1s spectrum of BFO@BTO-1:1 heterojunction, two characteristic peaks with binding energies of 532.47 and 530.49 eV can be assigned to surface oxygen and lattice oxygen, respectively. The reduction in adsorbed water on the surface of BFO@BTO-1:1 indicates the formation of a junction between BiFeO3 and BaTiO3 [27].

As shown in Fig. 3(f), the peaks of Ba 3d can be fitted into four peaks at 795.91, 792.51, 780.62, and 777.88 eV, which represent Ba3+ in pure BaTiO3. Figure 3(g) shows the peaks of Ti 2p, which can be fitted into three peaks at 464.07, 460.55, and 458.08 eV, representing Ti4+ in pure BaTiO3 [28]. Compared with those of pure BaTiO3, Ti 2p and Ba 3d peaks in BFO@BTO-1:1 heterojunction shift to higher binding energies, indicating strong chemical interactions between BiFeO3 and BaTiO3. In Fig. 3(h), for pure BiFeO3, the two peaks with binding energies of 170.37, 169.01, 165.05, and 163.07 eV are Bi 4f7/2 and Bi 4f5/2, which indicate the presence of Bi3+ in BiFeO3 and 158.69 eV for Bi0 [29], while peaks at 168.05, 166.29, 162.74, and 160.37 eV indicate the presence of Bi3+ in BFO@BTO heterojunction. Fe 2p spectra are shown in Fig. 3(i), with BiFeO3 having Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 peaks at 715.02 and 728.83 eV, and BFO@BTO-1:1 heterojunction having Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 peaks at 712.79 and 726.39 eV, respectively. The satellite peak, at 9 eV higher than Fe 2p3/2, indicates the spin‒orbit interaction generated by Fe 2p1/2 and Fe 2p3/2, suggesting that Fe is present in the Fe3+ state in both BiFeO3 and BFO@BTO-1:1 heterojunctions. Compared with those of pure BiFeO3, the binding energies of Bi 4f, O 1s, and Fe 2p in BFO@BTO-1:1 heterojunction transfer to higher binding energies, indicating the interaction between BiFeO3 and BaTiO3. These XPS results confirm the successful preparation of BFO@BTO-1:1 heterojunction photocatalysts. In addition, the peaks of Ba 3d (Fig. 3(f)) and Ti 2p (Fig. 3(g)) in the BFO@BTO-1:1 heterostructure are shifted toward higher binding energies than those of BaTiO3. In contrast, we note that Bi 4f (Fig. 3(h)) and Fe 2p (Fig. 3(i)) in the BFO@BTO-1:1 heterostructure move toward lower binding energies. This result suggests that BaTiO3 and BiFeO3 have strong chemical interactions and that free electrons migrate from BaTiO3 to BiFeO3, which is favorable for improving carrier separation [30].

Cl− and CO32− have a slight inhibitory effect on the removal of RhB by BFO@BTO-1:1. The decrease in adsorption performance due to Cl− may be attributed to its limited competition with RhB molecules for adsorption sites, whereas the decline in degradation performance may be due to the easy binding of Cl− to holes and the inhibition of electron transfer by SO42− [29,33]. The presence of CO32− has a greater effect on the adsorption and degradation of RhB by BFO@BTO-1:1. It is possible that the easy hydrolysis of CO32− makes the solution alkaline, thereby weakening the adsorption performance of BFO@BTO-1:1 for RhB, which is consistent with the acid-base experiment where the adsorption performance decreases under alkaline conditions (Fig. 5(a)). The degradation rate of RhB by BFO@BTO-1:1 increases with increasing catalyst concentration. This may be due to the dominant role of the piezoelectric catalysis of BFO@BTO-1:1. The traditional photocatalyst is influenced by the catalyst content and pollutant concentration. When the catalyst concentration or pollutant concentration is high, the light transmittance of the catalyst is reduced, and the photogenerated carrier migration is hindered; thus, the photocatalytic performance is weakened. Moreover, the degradation process also produces many pollutant intermediates, which compete with RhB molecules for limited photocatalytic active sites, resulting in reduced photocatalytic activity. Therefore, high catalyst contents or pollutants are not conducive to photocatalytic activity. Owing to the piezoelectric–photocatalytic effect, BFO@BTO-1:1 still has catalytic degradation performance under the above conditions, which is dominated by piezoelectric catalysis. Therefore, BFO@BTO-1:1 can still have good RhB removal performance under no light or low light intensity conditions.

RhB solution degradation experiments were carried out using BFO@BTO-1:1 under simulated sunlight at different ultrasonic powers (120, 180, and 240 W). As shown in Fig. 5(f), RhB degradation efficiencies at 120, 180, and 240 W are 58.5%, 93.2%, and 96.7%, respectively. The degradation efficiency of BFO@BTO-1:1 increases significantly with increasing ultrasound frequency. This may be due to the increase in the number of cavitation bubbles produced with increasing ultrasonic frequency, which increases the bombardment probability of BFO@BTO-1:1 nanorods, and the further polarization of BFO@BTO-1:1 nanorods enhances the attractive force of free charge in water, thus further improving the degradation efficiency of the RhB solution.

The stability of a catalyst is one of the most important indicators of its usefulness. After each complete degradation, the samples were washed, centrifuged, dried, and the BFO@BTO-1:1 mixture was collected. The degradation experiments were repeated under simulated sunlight. The results are shown in Fig. 5(g). The catalytic degradation efficiency of BFO@BTO-1:1 composite is 90.7%, 82.2%, and 79.8% over 3 consecutive cycles, and is able to maintain a good catalytic effect. Furthermore, SEM image and XRD patterns of BFO@BTO-1:1 after three cycles indicate that the morphology and structure remain unchanged (Fig. S1 in the ESM). The above results further indicate that the heterogeneous structure of BFO@BTO-1:1 has high stability and recyclability.

Since the prepared BFO@BTO-1:1 composite exhibited high catalytic degradation activity under light or ultrasonic irradiation, it has practical applications in terms of its overall sustainability. The continuous catalytic activity of BFO@BTO-1:1 was verified by using a xenon lamp to simulate solar illumination and ultrasonication to simulate tidal lapping. The degradation of high concentrations (25 mg·L−1) of RhB and TC was conducted under dark and simulated sunlight conditions. As shown in Fig. 5(h), RhB and TC in solution were continuously degraded in three light‒dark cycles, with degradation rates of 93.3% and 89.1% for RhB and TC, respectively, in 360 min. In contrast to previous round-the-clock photocatalysts that store photoexcited carriers, coupling piezocatalysis and photocatalysis is a promising alternative strategy for uninterrupted practical applications. BFO@BTO-1:1 degraded RhB by 27.7% in the first hour of light and 10.9% in the second hour of dark, indicating that piezoelectric catalysis contributes comparably to photocatalysis for the degradation of RhB. On the other hand, BFO@BTO-1:1 degraded TC by 56.0% in the first hour of illumination and 2.8% in the second hour of darkness, and this large difference proved that the degradation of TC was due mainly to photocatalysis.

### 3.4 Catalytic mechanism

As shown in Fig. 8(a), in the absence of light, the Fermi energy level in BFO@BTO heterojunction remains in equilibrium. Under the excitation of simulated sunlight, photogenerated electron‒hole pairs are induced, leading to an increase in the carrier concentration. However, the photogenerated electrons and holes are transferred from BaTiO3 to BiFeO3, leading to a decrease in the redox potential. Moreover, carrier compliance is high in BiFeO3, and the IEF is easily saturated.

The mechanism of organic pollutant degradation by BFO@BTO heterojunction under ultrasonic irradiation is shown in Fig. 8(b). Cavitation bubbles formed by ultrasonic wave irradiation in water can generate local pressures of up to 108–109 Pa on the adjacent materials when they burst at the critical size [46,47]. Thus, in theory, BFO@BTO heterojunction is subjected to a stress of at least 108 Pa. The intrinsic free charges excited by the mechanical energy are effectively separated, move to the surface in the direction of the polarized electric field, and thus trigger the reaction to generate ·OH and · O2− [48]. The system returns to equilibrium when the polarized electric field is counteracted by the charges absorbed and accumulated on the surface. When the pressure is tuned in the opposite direction, a change in the polarization direction breaks the potential equilibrium, leading to reverse charge transfer and a new redox reaction. However, the overall charge concentration in piezocatalysis is lower than that of photogenerated carriers.

Electron transfer from the conduction band of BiFeO3 to BaTiO3 can enhance the photocatalytic performance to a certain extent, but some electron‒hole pairs combine in the interfacial or bulk phase. There is an interfacial potential barrier between BaTiO3 and BiFeO3, which inhibits electron migration. When the reaction system is subjected to ultrasound irradiation, an IEF is provided inside BFO@BTO heterojunction under the action of piezoelectric polarization, which can lower the potential barrier of the interface and promote the transfer of photogenerated electrons [17,49]. As shown in Fig. 8(c), the change in the direction of the IEF accelerates photogenerated carrier separation and migration from a low potential to a high potential [48], which in turn promotes the generation of active radicals. Continuously high efficiency is achieved because the IEF can be repeatedly activated and reconstructed through rapid changes in the polarization direction of BaTiO3 nanorods under ultrasound irradiation [50]. Notably, the above pathways may have synergistic effects on the degradation of organic pollutants, which further enhances the photocatalytic activity of the system. Therefore, the excellent photocatalytic degradation performance of BFO@BTO under piezoelectric assistance may be attributed to the combined advantages of the following: (1) piezoelectric catalysis coupled with photocatalysis results in rapid and round-the-clock degradation; (2) in BFO@BTO heterojunction, BiFeO3 nanoparticles are uniformly distributed on the surface of BaTiO3 nanorods, which shortens the carrier migration path to the surface and results in a subsequent rapid reaction; and (3) in piezocatalysis, ultrasonic waves make the polarization direction change quickly, leading to activation and repeated reconstruction of the IEF. In addition to the reduced potential barrier, photogenerated carrier separation and transfer are accelerated, and thus, the catalytic efficiency is enhanced.

## 4 Conclusions

BFO@BTO heterojunctions with BiFeO3 nanoparticles anchored onto BaTiO3 nanorods have been demonstrated to be highly efficient in the round-the-clock degradation of organic pollutants. BFO@BTO heterojunction not only has excellent solar absorption properties and resonance ability for photogenerated electron–hole pairs but also induces a piezoelectric polarization effect, which enhances its ability to initiate radical generation and thus contributes to the redox degradation of organic pollutants in wastewater under light/dark conditions. As a result, BFO@BTO-1:1 exhibits highly efficient photoassisted piezocatalytic activity, and the degradation of RhB dyes reaches 95%, increasing by 46.7% and 13.8% compared with that of pure BiFeO3 and BaTiO3, respectively. BFO@BTO-1:1 degrades RhB dyes mainly by photoassisted piezocatalytic initiation of redox reactions dominated by h+ and ·OH radicals and exhibits excellent recycling performance. This method has the potential to use the mechanical energy generated by oscillation to treat wastewater in the absence of solar illumination, achieving sustainable degradation of high concentrations of pollutants under three light‒dark cycles. This strategy provides an energy-sustainability approach to round-the-clock organic pollutant degradation and expands the application of solar and tidal power.
