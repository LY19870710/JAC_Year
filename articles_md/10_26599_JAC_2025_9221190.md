## 1 Introduction

High-entropy carbide ceramics (HECCs) are solid solution ceramics composed of more than four carbide species in equimolar or near-equimolar proportions, resulting in relatively high configurational entropy [1]. The complex and disordered chemical composition of HECCs imparts unique properties that are absent in single-element materials, such as lattice distortion, high-entropy effects, sluggish diffusion, and cocktail effects [2]. These characteristics give HECCs exceptional properties, including ultrahigh melting points (above 3000 °C), low thermal conductivity (~10 W/(m∙K)), outstanding wear resistance, and excellent high-temperature mechanical properties. As a result, HECCs are highly attractive for extreme applications, such as thermal protection systems for spacecraft, cutting tools, and ultrahigh-temperature coatings for nuclear reactors [3−8]. Among HECCs, (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C (HECC) has been extensively studied because of its superior hardness (25.7±3.5 GPa) [9], high elastic modulus (495 GPa) [10], excellent oxidation resistance [11], and lower thermal conductivity (6.5 W/(m∙K)) [12]. Despite its remarkable properties, (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C retains the intrinsic brittleness characteristic of binary carbide ceramics (TiC: 3.3±0.1 MPa·m1/2, ZrC: 2.1±0.2 MPa·m1/2, HfC: 2.9±0.2 MPa·m1/2, NbC: 2.9±0.2 MPa·m1/2, TaC: 2.7±0.3 MPa·m1/2) [13−17], which significantly limits its potential applications [18]. Previous studies reported fracture toughness values of 3.5±0.3 MPa·m1/2 for (Hf,Zr,Ti,Ta,Nb)C [19] and 3.0±0.2 MPa·m1/2 for (Hf0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)C [20]. Thus, developing effective toughening strategies for HECCs is imperative.

Traditional ceramic toughening methods typically involve the introduction of particles, whiskers, or fibers to deflect crack propagation. For example, Lu *et al*. [21] introduced 20 vol% SiC into (HfNbTaTiZr)C, increasing the fracture toughness from 4.5 to 5.2 MPa∙m1/2. Luo *et al*. [22] incorporated SiCw into (HfNbTaTiZr)C, resulting in a 43.3% increase in fracture toughness, from 3.0±0.2 to 4.3±0.3 MPa∙m1/2. Although conventional toughening methods can improve HECC to some extent, their effectiveness remains limited. Consequently, it is crucial to explore innovative strategies for designing ceramic materials with novel structures rather than relying solely on specialized components.

Natural bamboo is a typical biocomposite material. Its excellent fracture toughness is derived from its unique microscopic multilevel structure, a hierarchical system composed of a high-strength cellulose matrix and a relatively weak intercellular lignin interface. This biomimetic configuration provides valuable insights for the design of advanced ceramic materials [23−25]. On this basis, the bamboo-inspired fibrous monolithic structure was introduced into high-entropy carbide (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C-based ceramics (FMCs) to increase toughness by utilizing fiber cells for load-bearing and cell boundaries to deflect cracks, preventing catastrophic failure [25−27]. Owing to their relatively low strength, lamellar two-dimensional structure, and chemical stability at high-temperatures, boron nitride (h-BN) and graphite (C) are chosen as the primary components of the cell boundary. This study aims to increase fracture toughness by precisely controlling the bonding strength of the cell boundary. Additionally, the anisotropic lamellar structure of h-BN/C is leveraged to construct a vertical thermal barrier network, effectively reducing the thermal conductivity. In our study, fiber precursors are prepared via coextrusion combined with phase inversion. Compared with conventional coaxial extrusion or extrusion-impregnation methods [28−32], this approach enables precise control of the cell boundary uniformity while simplifying the process and improving the subsequent processing efficiency. The ultimate goal is to achieve synergistic optimization of both the mechanical strength and thermal insulation performance of (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C. Additionally, the fabrication process of fibrous monolithic ceramics should be optimized by employing an integrated preparation of the matrix and the cell boundary method.

In this study, fiber precursors were prepared via coextrusion combined with phase inversion, and HECC and FMCs were synthesized via hot-pressed sintering. The effects of different cell boundary compositions on the mechanical properties of FMCs were systematically investigated, and the toughening mechanism was explored through fracture behavior analysis and surface micrographs. Additionally, the indentation strength bending (ISB) technique was employed to evaluate the damage tolerance, and the thermal conductivity was analyzed. This work aims to refine the design of the fibrous monolithic structure to enhance both mechanical and thermal performance, offering new strategies for toughening HECCs and providing insights into their application in extreme environments, particularly for aerospace and automotive thermal protection.

### 2.1 Materials and processing

Commercially available carbide powders (TiC, ZrC, HfC, NbC, and TaC) (99.99%, 1–3 μm, Shanghai Chaowei Nanotechnology Co., Ltd., China), BN (99.9%, 45–55 μm, Shandong Jingyi Co., Ltd., China), C (99.9%, ~1 μm, Beijing Zhongkeyannuo New Materials Technology Co., Ltd., China), Y2O3 (99.9%, ~10 μm, Guangzhou Jianfeng Co., Ltd., China), Al2O3 (99.9%, 1–3 μm, Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), polyether sulfone (PES; E2010, BASF, Germany), and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP; purity > 99.5%, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd., China) were used as raw materials. Equal molar ratios of the five binary carbides were weighed and placed in a ZrO2 container with a ball-to-powder mass ratio of 2 : 1. Anhydrous ethanol was employed as the ball-milling medium. The mixture was mechanically milled at a rotational speed of 260 r/min for 6 h to achieve uniform dispersion. After drying, the matrix powders were obtained by sieving. The fiber precursor slurries were prepared by mixing the ceramic powder with PES/NMP polymer solution, where PES and NMP were used as the binder and the solvent, respectively. The volume fraction of high-entropy carbide powders in the matrix slurry was 64 vol%, whereas that of ceramic powders in the cell boundary slurry was 4 vol%. To prevent agglomeration of the layered h-BN and C, the dispersion of the cell boundary slurry was enhanced by ball-milling at 300 r/min for 36 h after mixing with the PES/NMP polymer solution. The matrix slurry was prepared by directly mixing the carbide powders with the polymer solution and then subjected to uniform dispersion for 20 min via a vacuum degassing machine (TM-2000TT, Shenzhen Smaida Intelligent Equipment Co., Ltd., China). FMCs with different cell boundary compositions were prepared, as listed in Table 1. To facilitate sintering, 10 wt% Al2O3 and 10 wt% Y2O3 were added to the h-BN component. The bulk HECC was also prepared for comparison.

### 2.2 Measurement and characterization methods

The bulk density was measured according to Archimedes’ principle, whereas the theoretical density was calculated by the rule of mixtures, where the theoretical densities of h-BN, C, and HECC are 2.27, 2.25, and 9.38 g/cm3, respectively. Given that the extrusion speeds of the matrix and the cell boundary slurries were identical, the volume ratio of the matrix in the FMCs was calculated on the basis of the ceramic powder volume fractions of the slurries. The theoretical density was then derived accordingly. Phase analysis was performed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Bruker D8, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation. The microstructure was characterized using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; Tescan MAGNA, The Czech Republic) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200X, Thermo Scientific, USA), both of which are equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS; Oxford, UK) for compositional analysis.

The flexural strength and fracture toughness of the HECC and FMCs were determined via the three-point bending method on a mechanical testing machine (C42.104, Xin Sansi, China). The tensile surface of all the samples was perpendicular to the hot-pressed direction. For the flexural strength test, samples with dimensions of 3 mm × 4 mm × 36 mm were used, with a span of 30 mm and a loading speed of 0.5 mm/min. Single-edge notched beam (SENB) samples with dimensions of 3 mm × 4 mm × 36 mm were used for fracture toughness determination. The span is 30 mm, the notched size of the sample is approximately 2 mm, and the crosshead speed is 0.05 mm/min. The ISB method was utilized to assess the damage tolerance by evaluating the residual flexural strength of the samples, prefabricating indentations on their surfaces. A vicker hardness tester (HV-30AT, Weiyi, China) was used on the center of each sample’s tensile surface, applying loads of 9.8, 49, 98, and 294 N for 15 s each. Afterward, the samples were subjected to a three-point bending test to measure the residual strength. Each data point in the mechanical property test was measured on three samples, and the average value was taken as the experimental value.

The thermal performance was evaluated via a laser thermal conductivity analyzer (LFA 467 HT, NETZSCH, Germany). The test samples were disc-shaped and had a diameter of 12.7 mm and a thickness of 2.0 mm. Graphite was evenly sprayed on both the upper and lower surfaces to ensure uniform laser adsorption. Testing was conducted in an Ar atmosphere, with temperatures ranging from room temperature (RT) to 1000 °C. The thermal conductivity, *k* (W/(m·K)), can be calculated via the Eq. (1) [33]:

(1) k=Dρcp

where *k* is the thermal conductivity; *D* is the thermal diffusivity; *ρ* is the bulk density; *c*p is the constant pressure heat capacity. The sample data at different temperatures were measured three times to calculate the standard errors of the measurements.

### 3.1 Microstructure and phase composition

Figure 2(a) displays the macroscopic morphology of the fibrous precursors with various cell boundaries. The fibrous precursors exhibit flexibility and strength, enabling them to adapt to the subsequent processing and arrangement. Figure 2(b) shows the SEM microstructure of the transverse cross section of the FMCs after hot-pressed sintering. A continuous black cell boundary surrounds the gray hexagonal matrix, indicating that the FMCs are successfully prepared. The shape of the hexagonal matrix and the thickness variations in the cell boundary in different directions are caused by the deformation of the fiber during hot-pressed sintering. Figure 2(b) shows the matrix volume ratios of FMCs with various cell boundaries. Since the slurry concentration and extrusion speed are consistent during preparation, the matrix volume ratio in the FMCs is approximately 85%, with the cell boundary thickness maintained at approximately 20 μm. The relative density of HECC sintered at 1900 °C reached 99.1%. In contrast, the FMCs prepared under identical sintering conditions presented lower relative densities. The relative densities of the FMBN, FM73, and FM721 composites are 95.1% (bulk density: 8.5 g/cm3, theoretical density: 8.957 g/cm3), 93.8% (bulk density: 8.4 g/cm3, theoretical density: 8.918 g/cm3), and 93.7% (bulk density: 8.4 g/cm3, theoretical density: 8.998 g/cm3), respectively. This reduction in density may be attributed to the poor sintering behavior of boron nitride and graphite at the grain boundaries, while the presence of pores at the cell boundaries could further weaken interfacial bonding.

### 3.2 Mechanical properties

Figure 5 presents a comparison of the mechanical properties of HECC and FMCs with various cell boundaries. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the flexural strength of the FMCs is generally lower than that of the HECC. Specifically, the flexural strengths of FMBN, FM73, and FM721 were 19.8%, 58.7%, and 26.7% lower than that of HECC, respectively. This decrease is attributed to the lower relative density of FMCs and the introduction of porosity at the weak cell boundaries. The fracture toughness of FM73 and FM721 is greater than that of HECC, with FM721 showing the greatest toughening effect, achieving a fracture toughness of 8.3±1.5 MPa∙m1/2, which is 51.9% greater than that of HECC (5.5±0.1 MPa∙m1/2), while maintaining a high flexural strength. FM73 results in a 15.8% improvement in fracture toughness relative to HECC, whereas FMBN results in a fracture toughness lower than that of HECC, indicating no effective toughening. Since FMCs with different cell boundaries have various interfacial strengths, the ability to deflect cracks and resist crack propagation may also differ, which leads to differences in the toughening effects and flexural strength of FMCs. The impact mechanisms of their different mechanical properties were described through subsequent characterization.

*
Fig. 5(a) Flexural strength and fracture toughness of HECC and FMCs with various cell boundaries and (b) critical crack size.

According to the Griffith fracture criterion [34], catastrophic fracture occurs when the size of the original defect exceeds the critical crack size via Eq. (2):

(2) αcr=KIC2/σ2π

where  αcr is the critical crack size;  σ is the flexural strength; K*IC is the fracture toughness. Figure 5(b) illustrates how the cell boundary impacts the critical crack size of FMCs. The critical crack size for HECC is calculated to be 61.7 μm, whereas for FMBN, it is 68.4 μm, which is only 10.9% larger than that of HECC. In contrast, FM73 has a critical crack size of 484.3 μm, which is 685.0% greater than that of HECC. This considerable difference indicates that the fibrous monolithic structure can significantly increase damage tolerance by effectively controlling the cell boundary composition. On one hand, crack deflection at the weak cell boundary reduces the stress concentration at the crack tip. On the other hand, modifying the composition of the cell boundary improves the crack propagation resistance. In the case of FM721, the inclusion of matrix components strengthens the bonding between the cell boundary and matrix, leading to improved flexural strength (287.2±52.0 MPa), fracture toughness (8.3±1.5 MPa∙m1/2), and critical crack size of 265.0 μm, an increase of 329.6% compared with those of HECC. The “fibrous monolithic structure” toughening mechanism provides a performance trade-off strategy, improving fracture toughness and critical crack size at the expense of a moderate reduction in flexural strength.

Figures 6(a), 6(d), 6(g), and 6(j) show the load–displacement curves during the fracture toughness test. As shown in Fig. 6(a), the HECC demonstrates a typical brittle fracture behavior, and the load increases linearly with displacement until catastrophic failure occurs. In contrast, all FMCs exhibit nonbrittle fracture behavior, characterized by multiple stress drops before reaching the maximum load. These stress drops indicate crack deflections and branching during the crack propagation process, where each drop corresponds to the fracture of one or several layers of cells [24]. Notably, after the maximum load, the FMCs maintain a certain bearing capacity. For example, FM721 can undergo deformation of up to 0.9 mm before fracture failure, which is significantly greater than the deformation of 0.03 mm for HECC. Figure 6(d) shows that the FMBN has a low maximum load and a small area under the curve, indicating limited resistance to crack propagation and energy dissipation. FM73 demonstrates excellent load redistribution, with the load increasing multiple times after fracture, as depicted in Fig. 6(g). However, the weak interfacial strength limits its resistance to crack propagation. As shown in Fig. 6(j), FM721 has the highest maximum load and largest area under the curve, indicating superior load redistribution and energy dissipation during fracture. FM721 shows excellent resistance to crack propagation, providing the best toughening effect among the FMCs.

### 3.3 Damage tolerance

The ISB method was carried out on both HECC and FM721 under varying loads to investigate the influence of the fibrous monolithic structure design on the damage tolerance of ceramics. As shown in Fig. 12(a), indentation defects, combined with the continuous increase in indentation load, result in a decrease in the residual flexural strength of both materials. Specifically, the initial flexural strength of the HECC is 391.8±27.5 MPa, which decreases to 153.4±9.3 MPa under a 294 N indentation load, reflecting a 60.8% reduction in strength, indicating significant sensitivity to defects. In contrast, FM721 exhibited excellent damage tolerance properties. Under the same indentation load of 294 N, its strength decreases by only 22.2%, from 286.1±52.0 to 222.7±19.0 MPa. Notably, although the initial strength of FM721 is 27.0% lower than that of the HECC, its residual strength after a 49 N indentation load exceeds that of the HECC. Furthermore, when the indentation load increased to 294 N, the residual strength of FM721 was 45.2% greater than that of the HECC. The gentler slope of the FM721 curve demonstrates that it retains greater residual strength and undergoes less degradation than HECC under increased indentation loads, further confirming its superior damage tolerance and lower defect sensitivity. This result demonstrated a substantial increase in damage tolerance with the introduction of the fibrous monolithic structure.

At RT, the thermal conductivity of the HECC is calculated to be 14.1 W/(m∙K), which is in agreement with values reported in the literature [9,12,20]. As shown in Fig. 13(b), the thermal conductivity of the HECC increases linearly from RT to 1000 °C, reaching a maximum of 30.0 W/(m∙K) at 1000 °C. At RT, the thermal conductivity of FM721 is 22.9% lower than that of HECC, and at 1000 °C, it is 34.5% lower. The thermal conductivity of solids depends on both phonons and electron carriers that transfer heat. As the temperature increases, electron mobility improves, making electron conduction the dominant heat transfer mechanism at higher temperatures [33]. Consequently, the thermal conductivity of both HECC and FM721 increases with temperature, by 113.6% and 81.3%, respectively, when the temperature increases from RT to 1000 °C.

High-entropy carbide ceramics are known for their inherent degree of atomic disorder at the cation position, which results in compositional complexity and significant lattice distortion [12]. This distortion induces strain in the anion sublattice, which leads to point defects as the anions shift from their ideal positions. These defects reduce the mean free paths of phonons and increase the thermal resistance [43,44]. The thermal resistance (τ*−1) can be expressed by Eq. (4) [45]:

(4) τ−1=δ3ωD34πvm3(ΓM+ΓS)

where *δ*3 is the atomic volume; *ω*D is the Debye frequency; *v*m is the mean velocity of the material; *Γ*M and *Γ*S represent the scattering parameters due to mass fluctuations and strain field fluctuations, respectively. The lattice distortion and mass disorder characteristics of high-entropy ceramics increase by *Γ*M and *Γ*S, thereby improving the thermal resistance and reducing the thermal conductivity [2,46]. Consequently, compared with the binary carbides TiC, ZrC, HfC, NbC, and TaC [33,47], HECC has significantly lower thermal conductivity at RT. In the case of FMCs, the introduction of a cell boundary with a layered structure further decreases the thermal conductivity. At RT, the thermal conductivity of FM721 is 10.9 W/(m∙K), which falls within the range of values reported in the literature but is comparatively low [20,40,48,49]. As shown in the schematic diagram of Fig. 14, the pores and roughness introduced at the cell boundary may disrupt the continuity of the thermal conduction path in the matrix, leading to stronger phonon scattering and creating breaks in heat transfer. Moreover, materials such as h-BN and C with lamellar two-dimensional structures (as shown in Fig. 6(i)) have anisotropic thermal conductivities. Vertically arranged lamellar material in the cell boundary forms thermal barriers that prevent the in-plane thermal conductivity advantage from being transferred to the matrix. Additionally, the low relative density of FM721 hinders heat conduction and promotes phonon scattering, further reducing the thermal conductivity. This effect is particularly pronounced at high-temperatures [50]. Thus, although electron conduction predominates at higher temperatures, leading to an increase in thermal conductivity for both HECC and FM721, FM721’s increase in thermal conductivity is less pronounced than that of HECC because of interfacial thermal resistance and phonon scattering caused by pores. As shown in Fig. 13(c), HECC and FM721 are placed on a heating table, and their temperature changes are recorded via an infrared temperature gun. The results indicate that HECC has a significantly higher heat transfer rate than FM721 does, suggesting that FM721 provides superior thermal insulation, which is consistent with the thermal conductivity measurements. The introduction of the fibrous monolithic structure effectively reduces the thermal conductivity of FM721 over a wide temperature range (from RT to 1000 °C). The low thermal conductivity of the (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C fibrous monolithic ceramic suggests that it is a promising material for thermal protection. It mitigates heat transfer, reduces thermal stress, enhances insulation efficiency, and improves overall thermal performance.

## 4 Conclusions

This study successfully designed and fabricated (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C-based fibrous monolithic ceramics with various cell boundaries through a combination of phase inversion and hot pressing. The introduction of the cell boundaries significantly enhances crack deflection, leading to a marked improvement in fracture resistance. Specifically, the fracture toughness of FM721 (8.3±1.5 MPa∙m1/2) is 51.9% greater than that of the HECC, whereas its work of fracture reaches 784.0±190.8 J/m2, an eleven-fold increase compared with that of the HECC. Although FMCs exhibit lower flexural strength, their high fracture toughness contributes to a substantial increase in the critical crack size. For example, the critical crack size of FM73 increases by 685.0%, reaching 484.3 μm. The bonding strength of the cell boundary plays a crucial role in the toughening mechanism of FMCs. By optimizing the ratios of the h-BN, C, and HECC phases, the bonding strength can be fine-tuned, enhancing the crack propagation resistance and energy dissipation. Furthermore, FMCs exhibit excellent damage tolerance and reduced defect sensitivity. FM721, for example, maintains 77.8% of its initial flexural strength even after a 294 N indentation, owing to the multilevel dissipation mechanism at the cell boundary. The fibrous monolithic structure also introduces pores and roughness to the cell boundaries, which hinder heat conduction and promote phonon scattering. Compared with HECC, the layered materials at the cell boundary exhibit anisotropic thermal conductivity, resulting in the thermal conductivity of FM721 being 22.9% lower at room temperature and 34.5% lower at 1000 °C. In conclusion, the fibrous monolithic structure significantly enhances the fracture toughness, damage tolerance, and thermal properties of HECCs, making HECCs promising candidates for use as thermal protection materials in extreme environments.
