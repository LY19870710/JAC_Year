## 1 Introduction

Temperature is a fundamental physical quantity in chemistry, physics, and biological processes. Conventional thermometers measure temperature by physically contacting the surface or interior of an object to achieve temperature conduction. However, in some specialized scenarios—such as within living biological cells, micro/nanoelectronic components, rapidly moving systems, and environments with high pressure, strong magnetic fields, or corrosive conditions—contact thermometers often fail to provide accurate temperature measurements [1−3]. In contrast, noncontact optical temperature sensors have garnered significant attention because of their advantages: noncontact measurement, ease of detection, high sensitivity, and ability to achieve high temperature and spatial resolution [4−6]. Noncontact optical temperature sensors can detect temperature changes by monitoring optical signals, including wavelength shifts, fluorescence lifetimes, emission bandwidths, and fluorescence intensity ratios (FIRs) [7−9]. Among these techniques, the FIR method is particularly advantageous, as it is less dependent on measurement conditions and less susceptible to errors from other factors, such as fluctuations in excitation source power, fluorescence detection losses, and variations in atmospheric pressure [10]. Although temperature measurements in daily life and industrial production have generally been well handled [11], accurately obtaining temperature readings under certain extreme conditions, such as strong acids, strong alkalis, thermal shock, and high humidity, remains a formidable challenge.

Luminescent materials for optical temperature sensors must be carefully selected, with particular attention given to the choice of matrix materials and luminescent centers, which are crucial for achieving high sensitivity and stability [12]. An appropriate matrix material not only enhances the tunability of the material’s luminescence but also ensures stable temperature sensitivity in harsh application environments. The garnet structure offers several advantages, including isotropy, high mechanical strength, high thermal conductivity, good chemical stability, and optical uniformity, making it an ideal choice for a matrix material [13]. The general chemical formula for garnet structure crystals is A3B2C3O12, where A, B, C, and O atoms form dodecahedra, octahedra, and tetrahedra, respectively, within the cubic Oh10-*Ia*3*d* space group [14]. This cubic crystal system, combined with an appropriate preparation process, allows for the fabrication of highly transparent ceramics, which improves both the optical properties and practical application performance [15]. Gd3Al3Ga2O12 (GAGG) transparent ceramics, a type of garnet structure material, show significant potential in applications such as scintillation [16], fluorescence [17], and lasers [18]. Luo *et al*. [19] modified the GAGG:Ce material by doping with Y3+ ions, resulting in a relative optical output approximately 1.3 times higher than that of the commercial GOS ceramic scintillator, suggesting promising prospects for medical CT scanner detectors. Liu *et al*. [20] prepared highly transparent Gd3Al4GaO12:Ce3+ ceramics via a solid-state method, which emit warm white light and exhibit superior luminous performance compared with traditional YAG:Ce transparent ceramics. Mishra *et al*. [21] investigated the ultrawide emission band (490–850 nm) of Ce3+ and Cr3+ codoped with Gd3Al4GaO12 and reported maximum absolute and relative sensitivities of 0.0066 and 0.266% K−1 at 503 K, respectively. This material also shows potential as a temperature-dependent pc-WLED [21]. GAGG ceramics, with their unique structural framework and excellent chemical stability, demonstrate great promise for optical and temperature sensing applications.

In addition, selecting the appropriate luminescent center is crucial for achieving high-sensitivity optical thermometers. In recent years, trivalent lanthanide ions (Ln3+) have garnered significant attention as luminescent centers because of their excellent sensing capabilities in harsh environments, such as corrosive conditions, high-speed systems, and environments with intense electromagnetic interference [22]. Among these Ln3+ ions, the ionic radius of Pr3+ is similar to that of Gd3+, which facilitates the substitution of Gd3+ with Pr3+ in GAGG. The 4f5d → 4f interconfigurational energy level transition of rare earth Pr3+ ions has been explored and applied in ultraviolet light applications, whereas transitions within the 4f → 4f configuration are widely utilized in sensors, lasers, long afterglow materials, scintillators, and white LEDs [23−25]. The 4f → 4f internal configuration transition of the Pr3+ ion occurs between the 3P0,1 and 1D2 energy states, producing cyan–blue and red luminescence, respectively. As the ambient temperature changes, cross-relaxation between the 3P0 and 1D2 energy states (or between the 3P0 and 3P1 excited states) facilitates temperature sensing. The unique energy transition process of Pr3+ is valuable for self-calibrated temperature sensing [26−28].

In this study, Pr3+-doped GAGG garnet-type ceramics with various Pr3+ doping concentrations were synthesized via a high-temperature solid-state method. The effects of Pr3+ doping on the microstructure and luminescence properties of GAGG:Pr3+ ceramics were systematically investigated through comprehensive material characterization. The photoluminescence (PL) spectra of GAGG ceramics doped with Pr3+ at different concentrations and temperatures were studied in detail. On the basis of the PL data collected from 300 to 500 K, the temperature sensing characteristics of GAGG:Pr3+ were examined via the fluorescence intensity ratio (FIR) method, with a focus on thermally coupled and nonthermally coupled energy level pairs. These results indicate that the GAGG:Pr3+ fluorescent material is a promising candidate for noncontact optical temperature measurements.

## 2 Experimental

Gd3−*x*Pr*x*Al3Ga2O12 (GAGG:*x*Pr3+, *x* = 0.06, 0.08, 0.10, 0.12) ceramics were synthesized via the high-temperature solid-state method. High-purity raw materials, including Gd₂O₃ (99.99%), Ga₂O₃ (99.99%), Al₂O₃ (99.99%), and Pr2O3 (99.9%), were accurately weighed according to the stoichiometric ratio. Anhydrous ethanol was used as the milling medium, with a mass ratio of raw material: zirconia balls: ethanol = 1 : 6 : 1.2. The mixture was ball milled in a planetary mill at 300 r/min for 12 h to achieve uniform dispersion. After drying at 60 °C for 10 h in the oven, the precursor powder was sieved to a particle size of less than 80 μm. Then, the powder was subsequently cold isostatically pressed at 250 MPa for 10 min to form dense green bodies, which were then presintered in a muffle furnace at temperatures ranging from 1000–1300 °C for 2–6 h. Finally, the ceramics were sintered at 1550–1700 °C for 10–20 h in an oxygen atmosphere.

The density of the ceramics was determined via the Archimedes method by immersing the samples in distilled water. The phase composition was analyzed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker) at a scanning speed of 0.02 (°)/step within a 2*θ* range of 10°–90° using Cu-Kα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA). The surface morphology was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; SU 8600, Hitachi). The average grain size was determined by analyzing ~100 grains with Nano Measurer software. The microstructure was analyzed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Talos F200S, Thermo Fisher). The transmittance was measured in the 200–800 nm range via an ultraviolet‒visible near-infrared spectrophotometer (Lambda 1050+, PerkinElmer). The surface composition and elemental states were analyzed via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Axis Supra, Kratos). The photoluminescence (PL) spectra of the ceramics were measured with a steady-state transient fluorescence spectrometer (FSL1000, Edinburgh Instruments Ltd.) excited at 450 nm, along with temperature-dependent PL spectra (300–500 K). The temperature sensing characteristics were evaluated via the fluorescence intensity ratio (FIR) method.

### 3.1 Phase and microstructure

Figures 1(a)–1(d) show SEM images of the natural surface of the GAGG:*x*Pr3+ (*x* = 0.06, 0.08, 0.10, 0.12) ceramics. Prior to SEM observation, gold was sprayed onto the sample surface as a conductive adhesive to increase the conductivity of the ceramics, thereby improving the imaging signal. The SEM images revealed clear and uniform grain boundaries on the natural ceramic surface, with dense crystal growth and no evidence of a second phase. The average grain size of the ceramics was analyzed on the basis of their microscopic morphology. The average grain size (*G*avg) ranged from 5.7 to 6.8 μm, with the GAGG:0.08Pr3+ ceramic having an average grain size of 6.8 μm. Under the same sintering conditions, the GAGG:0.08Pr3+ ceramic exhibited more significant crystal grain growth. Figure 1(e) shows the high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) characterization results of the GAGG:0.08Pr3+ ceramic. The high-resolution lattice fringes are clearly visible, confirming that the material has a highly ordered crystal structure. Further analysis of the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Fig. 1(f)) revealed that when observed along the [210] zone axis, the sample exhibited an equidistant lattice fringe of 0.62 nm, which was completely consistent with the theoretical interplanar spacing of the (002) crystal plane of the GAGG crystal. This precise lattice matching not only verifies the intrinsic crystal characteristics of the material but also indicates that the ceramics maintain a well-ordered lattice arrangement at the atomic scale.

The phase structure of the GAGG:*x*Pr3+ ceramics was analyzed via X-ray diffraction. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the XRD patterns of the ceramics match the standard PDF Card#46-0448 for Gd3Al3Ga2O12. With increasing Pr3+ content, the diffraction peaks of the ceramics indicate the presence of a single phase, with no second phase detected. It has been demonstrated that sintering in an oxygen atmosphere effectively inhibits the decomposition of gallium oxide at high temperatures, which favors the formation of single-phase ceramics with better quality [29,30]. The lattice parameters of GAGG:*x*Pr3+ were determined via XRD Rietveld refinement, as shown for GAGG:0.08Pr3+ in Fig. 2(b). Figure 2(c) presents the crystal structure diagram of this material system, where Gd and O atoms form a dodecahedral structure, whereas Ga and Al atoms form octahedral and tetrahedral structures with O atoms. This behavior is attributed to Pr3+ ions replacing Gd3+ ions in the lattice, as the ionic radius of Pr3+ (1.013 Å) is larger than that of Gd3+ (0.938 Å), in accordance with Vegard’s law. The theoretical density of the samples decreases with increasing doping content, as Pr3+ ions occupy the lattice position of Gd3+, and the atomic mass of Pr (*M*Pr = 140.91) is smaller than that of Gd (*M*Gd= 157.25). The bulk density of the ceramics was measured via the Archimedes drainage method. The ceramics exhibited good bulk density, with values ranging from 6.39 to 6.41 g/cm3, reaching over 99% of the theoretical density. The maximum relative density of the GAGG:0.08 Pr3+ ceramic is 99.93%. Under the same sintering conditions, when the Pr3+ doping content is 0.08, the grain growth is more complete, with fewer grain boundaries and pores in the ceramic, resulting in the highest relative density.

### 3.2 Elemental analysis

XPS analysis was performed with GAGG:0.08Pr3+ as the representative sample. Figures 3(a)–3(f) show the XPS spectra for the Gd 4d, Ga 2p, Al 2p, O 1s, and Pr 3d orbitals and the overall XPS survey spectrum, respectively. In Fig. 3(a), the peak at 147.6 eV corresponds to the 4d3/2 orbital of Gd, whereas the peak at 142 eV corresponds to the 4d5/2 orbital of Gd. The Gd 4d5/2 peak is more intense and broader than the Gd 4d3/2 peak is, confirming the predominant presence of the Gd3+ oxidation state [31]. The Gd 4d5/2 peak is further deconvoluted into two peaks at 141.7 and 144.1 eV. This triplet state in the Gd 4d spectrum arises from the strong electrostatic interaction between the Gd 4d and 4f orbitals, as predicted by the Gaussian model [32]. In Fig. 3(b), Ga 2p peaks are observed at 1117.7 and 1145.5 eV, corresponding to the 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 orbitals of Ga, respectively. These values confirm that Ga is in the +3 valence state [33]. In Fig. 3(c), the Al 2p1/2 orbital is located at 74.3 eV, indicating the presence of Al3+ ions. Figure 3(d) shows the O 1s spectrum, which can be deconvoluted into three peaks: the peak at 530.4 eV corresponds to lattice oxygen, accounting for 82.6%; the peak at 532.2 eV is attributed to oxygen vacancies, contributing 12.3%; and the peak at 533.2 eV corresponds to adsorbed oxygen, accounting for 5.1% [6,34]. To determine the oxidation state of the Pr ions, the Pr 3d orbital spectrum was examined. As shown in Fig. 3(e), the peaks of the Pr 3d orbitals are located at 954.0 and 933.3 eV, corresponding to Pr 3d3/2 and 3d5/2, respectively. The energy difference between the Pr 3d3/2 and Pr 3d5/2 peaks is 20.7 eV. Additionally, strong satellite peaks are observed on the lower binding energy side of the Pr 3d5/2 and Pr 3d3/2 peaks at 949.6 and 928.8 eV, respectively. The energy differences between the satellite peaks and the main peaks are 4.4 and 4.5 eV, respectively. Previous studies reported similar features for Pr3+, with a Pr 3d5/2 core level peak at 933.4 eV and an energy difference between the Pr 3d3/2 and Pr 3d5/2 peaks of 20.7 eV. The satellite peaks, which appear 4–4.5 eV lower in binding energy [35,36], confirm the presence of Pr3+ in GAGG.

### 3.3 Optical properties at room temperature

To investigate the optical properties of the GAGG:*x*Pr3+ ceramics, the transmittance and photoluminescence (PL) spectra were measured. Figure 4(a) presents the transmittance curves of the GAGG:*x*Pr3+ ceramics (the physical ceramics shown in the illustration are wafers with a diameter of 14 mm and a thickness of 0.60 mm). The transmittance of all the ceramics exceeds 50%, with GAGG:0.08Pr3+ achieving a maximum transmittance of 83% at 350 nm. The high transmittance could be attributed to the single-phase nature and dense microstructure of the ceramics, which minimize refraction and scattering. The larger average grain size and higher relative density of GAGG:0.08Pr3+ contribute to its superior optical transparency. High transparency also enhances the energy transfer efficiency between the energy levels of rare-earth ions, allowing more energy to be converted from the excited state to the emission state, thereby improving the fluorescence emission intensity [37].

*
Fig. 4(a) Transmittance curves of the GAGG:x*Pr3+ ceramics and (b) PL emission spectra under 450 nm excitation.

Additionally, some absorption peaks are observed in the transmittance curve (Fig. 4(a)) within the 450–500 nm and 550–600 nm regions [38]. These absorption features correspond to the 4f–4f electron transition of Pr3+ ions. Figures 4(b) and 5(a) show the PL emission spectra under 450 nm excitation at room temperature and the energy level diagram of Pr3+, respectively. The PL spectrum consists of multiple narrow emission peaks. The observed emission peaks and their corresponding transitions are as follows: 489 nm (3P0 → 3H4), 501 nm (1I6 → 3H5), 534 nm (3P1 → 3H5), 548 nm (3P0 → 3H5), 565 nm (3I6 → 3H6), 599 nm (1D2 → 3H4), 621 nm (3P0 → 3H6), 637 nm (3P1 → 3F3), 663 nm (3P0 → 3F3), 715 nm (3P1 → 3F4), and 743 nm (3P0 → 3F4). The emission intensity initially increases and then decreases with increasing Pr3+ concentration. The PL intensity of GAGG:0.08Pr3+ is comparable to that of GAGG:0.10Pr3+, with the GAGG:0.10Pr3+ ceramic exhibiting the highest PL intensity. However, further increasing the doped Pr3+ concentration to 0.12 resulted in a significant reduction in the emission intensity due to concentration quenching. At lower doping concentrations, the large interionic distances between activator ions minimize their mutual interactions, allowing the emission intensity to increase linearly with increasing doping concentration as the number of effective luminescent centers per unit volume increases [39]. However, when the doping concentration exceeds the critical threshold, the decreased interionic distances lead to three predominant energy dissipation pathways: i) cross-relaxation processes between adjacent ions via resonant energy transfer, ii) nonradiative multiphonon transitions induced by excited-state absorption, and iii) energy migration to quenching centers such as surface defects. These competing nonradiative processes significantly increase the probability of excitation energy dissipation via lattice vibrations (phonon emission) rather than radiative transitions [40,41]. Consequently, the macroscopic luminescence intensity is nonmonotonic dependent on the dopant concentration, characterized by initial enhancement followed by quenching at higher doping levels [42−44]. Figure 5(b) presents the CIE chromaticity diagram related to PL emission, showing that the luminescent colors of the ceramics under 450 nm excitation fall within the yellow‒green region.

*
Fig. 5(a) Energy level diagram of Pr3+, (b) CIE chromaticity diagram related to PL of GAGG:x*Pr3+, (c) diagram of the relationship between lg(*I*/*x*) and lg*x*.

The nonradiative energy transfer between Pr3+ ions can be further analyzed. Nonradiative energy transfer among rare earth ions generally occurs via exchange interactions or electrical multilevel interactions. The Blasse formula [45] can be used to analyze the critical transfer distance (*R*c) (Eq. (1)):

(1) Rc≈2(3V4πxcN)1/3

where *R*c is the critical transfer distance, *x*c is the critical doping concentration, *V* is the unit cell volume, and *N* is the number of sites that can be replaced in each cell. For the GAGG ceramics, when *x*c = 0.10, *V* = 1830.3 Å3, and *N* = 16, *R*c is calculated to be approximately 12.97 Å. This value significantly exceeds the critical transfer distance of the exchange interaction (5 Å), indicating that the multipolar‒multipolar interaction between Pr3+ ions mainly drives concentration quenching. The relationship between the fluorescence intensity (*I*) and the concentration (*x*) follows Dexter‒Reisfeld theory [46], expressed as (Eq. (2)):

(2) lg(Ix)=C−(θ3)lgx

where *I* is the emission intensity of 3I6 → 3H6, *x* is the concentration of Pr3+ ions, *C* is a coefficient, and *θ* can be 6, 8, or 10, corresponding to dipole‒dipole, dipole‒quadrupole and quadrupole‒quadrupole interactions, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5(c), the slope of the fitted curve is −3.50, and the calculated value of *θ* is 10.5, indicating that the concentration quenching in GAGG:Pr3+ is mainly controlled by quadrupole‒quadrupole interactions.

### 3.4 Optical temperature sensing properties

To investigate the temperature dependence of the luminescence intensity of the GAGG:*x*Pr3+ ceramics, the PL spectra of the GAGG:0.08Pr3+ ceramic were recorded at temperature intervals of 50 K between 300 and 500 K. As shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b), when the temperature reached 500 K, the luminescence intensity decreased to 56.6% of its initial value at 300 K. This decrease was attributed to thermal quenching, a phenomenon where nonradiative processes, such as multiphonon relaxation, dominated at elevated temperatures, leading to reduced fluorescence intensity [41].

*
Fig. 6Temperature-dependent luminescence characteristics of GAGG:0.08Pr3+: (a) PL spectra and (b) relative percentage of luminescence intensity.

Figure 7(a) presents the corresponding CIE chromaticity diagram, which illustrates the color variation of the ceramic with increasing temperature. As the temperature increases, the emission color shifts from yellow–green to green, indicating a change in the spectral distribution. With increasing temperature, the luminescence intensity of most energy levels (such as 3P0 → 3H6, 3P0 → 3H4, 3I6 → 3H6, and 3I6 → 3F3) continuously decreases. However, as observed in Fig. 7(b), the luminescence behavior of certain energy levels deviates from this trend because of thermal coupling effects. Specifically, the luminescence intensity of the 3P1 → 3H5 and 1D2 → 3H4 transitions initially increases with temperature but then decreases. This is due to the thermal coupling effect between the 3P0 and 3P1 energy levels, which follow a Boltzmann distribution. As the temperature increases, some electrons in the 3P0 level are thermally excited to the 3P1 level, leading to an increased electron population and luminescence intensity of the 3P1 energy level. However, as the temperature continues to rise, the thermal coupling effect becomes insufficient to compensate for the energy losses through nonradiative processes, resulting in a decrease in luminous intensity [47]. A similar trend is observed for the 1D2 → 3H4 transition, where the luminescence intensity first increases but then decreases with temperature. In contrast, the luminescence intensity of the 3P0 → 3H4 transition continuously decreases with temperature but at a faster rate than those of the other energy level transitions. These distinct thermal responses of multiple energy levels enable the use of GAGG:x*Pr3+ ceramics for temperature sensing applications on the basis of the fluorescence intensity ratio (FIR) method. By analyzing the relative intensity changes between thermally coupled levels, FIR-based temperature sensing can provide accurate thermal readings in various applications.

*

To quantitatively analyze the luminescent thermal stability of the material, the Arrhenius equation [48] is used to fit the temperature dependence of luminescence intensity, and the activation energy (*E*a) that causes the temperature quenching can be determined (Eq. (3)).

(3) I(T)=I01+Be(−EakT)

where *I*0 is the initial luminescence intensity, *I*(*T*) is the luminescence intensity at the temperature *T*, *B* is the frequency factor, and *k* is the Boltzmann constant. The activation energy (*E*a) for thermal quenching was determined from the slope of the linear fit of the ln (*I*0/*I*−1) versus 1/(*kT*) curves, as shown in Fig. 7(c). The activation energy of GAGG:Pr3+ was calculated to be *E*a = 0.16 eV.

To further evaluate the optical temperature sensing properties of the GAGG:Pr3+ ceramics, the relationship between the fluorescence intensity ratio (FIR) and temperature was analyzed for both the thermal coupling energy levels (3P0 and 3P1) and the nonthermal coupling energy levels (3P0 and 1D2). The temperature-dependent plots of the intensity ratios (*I*534/*I*489) and (*I*599/*I*489) for the GAGG:Pr3+ ceramic are shown in Figs. 8(a) and 8(b). The sensitivity of the temperature sensors can be quantified by the FIR value associated with the 3P1→3H5 and 3P0→3H4 transitions. The FIR value can be derived via Eq. (4) [49]:

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, GAGG:*x*Pr3+ transparent ceramics were successfully prepared via a high-temperature solid-state method. The synthesized ceramics exhibited excellent luminescent properties. The effects of Pr3+ doping on the phase structure, morphology and optical properties of GAGG ceramics were thoroughly investigated. The emission intensity was found to be concentration- and temperature-dependent, with quenching occurring at high concentrations and temperatures. The luminescence color of the ceramics lies within the green region. Furthermore, according to the distinct thermal responses of the multiple energy levels, based on the fluorescence intensity ratio (FIR) method, the maximum relative sensitivities for the thermally coupled energy level pair (3P1 → 3H5 and 3P0 → 3H4) and the nonthermally coupled energy level pair (1D2 → 3H4 and 3P0 → 3H4) were calculated to be 0.81 %·K−1 at 300 K and 0.37 %·K−1 at 350 K, respectively. These results indicate that GAGG:Pr3+ ceramic materials not only exhibit promising photoluminescent properties but also have potential for optical temperature sensing applications. Moving forward, bandgap engineering or defect engineering strategies may be applied to further enhance the luminescence properties and temperature sensing sensitivity of the material.
