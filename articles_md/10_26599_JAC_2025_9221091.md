## 1 Introduction

The value of ceramics relies on the material, shape, and surface, and machining plays a crucial role in shaping ceramics and refining their dimensions and surface quality to meet application requirements [1]. Advanced ceramics are widely used in high-tech applications, including aerospace, electronics, and biomedical engineering, owing to their exceptional strength, wear resistance, and thermal stability [2−4]. However, high strength and hardness also make machining challenging, often leading to surface damage, dimensional inaccuracies, and high tooling costs [5]. The inherent brittleness and high hardness of ceramics further complicate the fabrication of complex shapes with high precision [6,7].

To mitigate these challenges, machining in the green state has been widely adopted as an alternative to processing fully sintered ceramics [8,9], as green bodies exhibit lower hardness and are easier to machine [10]. However, their high porosity, weak mechanical strength, and lack of cohesion make them highly susceptible to cracking, chipping, and delamination under machining forces [11−13]. Improper cutting parameters can further exacerbate damage, requiring precise control to prevent structural degradation before sintering [14].

To reduce machining requirements, net-shaped forming techniques, such as slip casting, injection molding, and three-dimensional (3D) printing, have been explored to minimize material removal and lower overall production costs [15−17]. However, precise dimensional control remains a challenge owing to shrinkage and warpage during sintering [18]. Additionally, the surface finish of net-shaped ceramics often fails to meet industry specifications, necessitating post-processing [19]. Moreover, traditional mechanical machining methods struggle with intricate features such as non-communicating slots and holes [20,21]. Given these limitations, there is a pressing need for reliable, rapid, and cost-effective ceramic processing techniques [22].

To address these challenges, this paper proposes machining ceramic gel in its “wet body” state, which is formed via spontaneous coagulation casting (SCC). This technique has gained increasing attention and has been successfully applied to structural [23], porous [24,25], electronic [26], and transparent ceramics [27,28]. The ceramic gel is created by incorporating a copolymer of isobutylene and maleic anhydride (PIBM) as both dispersant and gelling agent [29]. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy analysis by Marsico *et al.* [30] revealed that no covalent network formED during gelation, with the process relying solely on weak interactions. Wang [31] further identified that this ceramic gel was a physical gel stabilized by hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding.

A key characteristic of this gel is its ability to re-fluidize slurry under non-intrusive shear forces and subsequently coagulate again [32]. Experiments have shown that the reflow phenomenon can also occur under various shear stress conditions, such as vibration, stirring, and ball milling, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). This stress-induced reflow behavior provides a theoretical foundation for manipulating ceramic bodies in their gel state [33,34]. Achieving high precision in machining requires localized energy input into a small area of the ceramic gel. Ultrasonic vibration technology offers a viable solution by generating high-frequency vertical oscillations to avoid damage to the sidewall, which precisely controls the distribution and intensity of local shear stress. This ensures the accuracy and stability throughout the machining process.

By integrating ultrasonic vibration with ceramic gel processing, a novel wet-body machining technology can be developed to overcome the drawbacks of conventional cutting methods. In this study, we explore the feasibility of ultrasonic vibration-assisted reflow machining (URM) of ceramic gels, which incorporates PIBM and can be re-fluidized by ultrasonic vibration. Our objective is to demonstrate that this innovative approach significantly enhances machining precision and surface quality, enabling the fabrication of ceramic parts with special structures, high dimensional accuracy, and minimal surface defects.

## 2 Experimental

Commercial alumina powder (AES-11, Sumitomo Chemical Co., Ltd., Japan, median particle size (*D*50) = 0.45 μm, Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area= 6.4 m2/g) was used as the starting material. PIBM agents, specifically Isobam 104 and Isobam 600AF, with molecular weights of 55,000–65,000 and 5500–5600 (Kuraray, Japan), respectively, were used as both dispersants and gelling agents [29]. The ceramic gels were prepared with a solid loading of 48–56 vol% by attrition milling of the alumina powder with 0.1 wt% Isobam 104, 0.25 wt% Isobam 600AF, and deionized water. The resulting slurries were poured into molds to form ceramic gels with predefined geometries. The ceramic gels were then machined using an ultrasonic vibration-assisted cutting system, as depicted in Fig. 1(b). The system consisted of a power supply and a high-frequency converter to generate high-frequency oscillations, a booster to amplify the ultrasonic waves, a horn to transmit ultrasonic vibrations to the ceramic gel surface for machining, a fixation unit to secure the ceramic gel and prevent displacement or deformation during machining, and an *XYZ*-axis moving platform for precise positioning and multi-dimensional processing. Following machining, the processed gels were dried, demolded (800 °C×2 h in air), and sintered (1550 °C×6 h in air) to produce the final ceramic components.

The rheological behaviors of the slurries, including viscosity and storage modulus, were analyzed using a rotational rheometer (Haake Viscotester iQ Air, Thermo Electron GmbH, Germany). Measurements were conducted using a 20 mm diameter parallel plate at 25 °C. The storage modulus, which reflects the gelling characteristics of the slurries, was assessed in oscillatory mode at a frequency of 1.0 Hz and a constant strain of 0.4%.

Indentation tests were performed using an universal testing machine (INSTRON-1195, Instron Co., USA) equipped with a *ϕ*5 mm ruby ball indenter. The indenter penetrated the gelled samples at a constant rate of 0.5 mm/min. During each cycle, the indentation load (*P*) and the corresponding penetration depth (*h*) were recorded [35]. The indentation hardness (*H*), which represents the plastic yield characteristic of the ceramic gel, was calculated using Eq. (1):

where *A* is the projected indentation area.

A digital microscope (VHX-7100, Keyence, USA) was used to observe the machining details of the dried ceramic gels. The surface roughness after drying was measured using a 3D optical profiler (Sneox A90, Sensofar, Spain). The microstructure of dried ceramic gels and sintered ceramics was examined with a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SU8220, Hitachi, Japan). The Vickers hardness was measured on the polished surface using a Vickers hardness tester (TUKON-2100B, Instron Co., USA), in accordance with the GB/T 16534-2009 standard. A test load of 9.8 N was applied with dwell time of 10 s.

## 3 Results and discussion

The efficiency of URM is influenced by ceramic gel properties, ultrasonic power, vibration frequency, and contact pressure of the vibration tool. Owing to the constraint of the designed system with fixed frequency and power, this study focused on understanding the physical properties of ceramic gels and their interactions with ultrasonic machining conditions.

The effects of solid loading and gelling time on the slurry viscosity, storage modulus, indentation hardness, and machining performance of alumina ceramic gels are shown in Figs. 2 and 3. Higher solid loading resulted in increased viscosity, as shown in Fig. 2(a). When the solid loading increased from 48 to 56 vol%, the viscosity rose from 0.01 to 0.57 Pa·s. Despite the increase, all slurries maintained sufficient flowability for the casting process. A trend was observed in the storage modulus (Fig. 2(b)), where higher solid loading led to faster gelation.

To further evaluate the structural evolution of ceramic gels, indentation hardness measurements were conducted, providing insights into network tightness. As shown in Fig. 2(c), the hardness increased significantly with both solid loading and gelling time. At gelling time of 24 h, the hardness value increased from 4.9 kPa (48 vol%) to 18.9 kPa (56 vol%). For the 56 vol% sample, the hardness further increased from 6.9 to 126.5 kPa as the gelling time extended from 12 to 84 h, while the hardness of the 48 vol% sample increased from 2.3 to 12.1 kPa. This progressive increase in hardness indicates that higher solid loading and prolonged gelling time enhance the structural rigidity of ceramic gels, improving their ability to withstand greater external stress.

A 500-μm-wide ultrasonic horn was used for machining, and the relationships among the machining channel width, solid loading, and gelling time are shown in Fig. 2(d). As the solid loading increased from 48 to 56 vol%, the machining channel width decreased. For example, the green body from the 48 vol% solid loading slurry gelled for 12 h exhibited deeper and wider machining channels, exceeding 1000 μm. In contrast, the body from the 56 vol% solid loading slurry gelled for 12 h had a 435 μm machining channel width. This suggested that the integrity of the gel from low solid loading and short gelling time cannot be maintained under high-frequency shear force, resulting in irregular removal channels, as shown in Fig. 3(a).

In fact, beyond a certain solid loading (52 vol%), the machining channel width tended to stabilize. Further increasing the solid loading did not yield significant improvements in the machinability. Second, the gelling time also had a notable influence. For the green bodies from the 48 and 50 vol% solid loading slurries, the machining channel width exhibited similar behaviors, and the gelling time significantly affected the width. The 12 h gelling time resulted in widths of 1096 and 1117 μm, and the 72 h gelling time resulted in widths of 348 and 367 μm, respectively. In contrast, for the body from the 52 vol% solid loading slurry, the machining channel width became stable and controllable after gelling for 24 h. Furthermore, for solid loading higher than 52 vol%, such as 53 vol% and above, 12 h is necessary to obtain a controllable machining channel width. Figure 3(c) shows a stable machining channel for the gel from 54 vol% solid loading slurry gelled for 24 h.

However, for the ceramic gel from the 56 vol% solid loading slurry gelled for 72 h, although the machining channel width can be controlled and the channel is clean and narrow, other undesirable cracks emerged, as shown in Fig. 3(d). This state indicated that while the machining process effectively maintained channel integrity, the induced stresses could compromise structural integrity, leading to crack propagation and potential failure. These observations suggest a complex interplay between the physical properties of ceramic gels and machining parameters, highlighting the necessity of tailored machining conditions. A deeper investigation of these observations was conducted, and the results are summarized in Fig. 4(a).

A ceramic gel with a low hardness in the purple zone of Fig. 4, resulting from short gelling time and/or low solid loading, is particularly vulnerable to shear forces induced by ultrasonic vibration. Under these conditions, the rupture or slippage of connection points leads to the destruction of its three-dimensional structure (Fig. 3(a)), which compromises the ceramic gel stability and its ability to withstand ultrasonic treatment. Increasing both gelling time and solid loading can improve ceramic gel’s resistance to deformation under external forces.

A ceramic gel with a relatively high hardness, resulting from long gelling time and/or high solid loading, is rigid and stable when processed by ultrasonic vibration, as shown in the blue zone in Fig. 4. However, this rigidity reduces the toughness and flexibility, making the material more prone to brittle fracture under ultrasonic stress (Fig. 3(d)).

The optimal processing zone is identified as the “yellow zone” in Fig. 4, which represents conditions where the ceramic gel exhibits a relatively homogeneous structure capable of withstanding the ultrasonic energy without significant collapse or cracking. This balance enables the formation of clean, thin machining channels (Fig. 3(c)). However, this balance is sensitive. By carefully optimizing these variables, the machining performance can be further refined, ensuring that the ceramic gel retains its integrity under ultrasonic treatment while achieving efficient material removal.

Figure 5(a) shows the natural surface of the ceramic gel from the 54 vol% solid loading slurry and gelling for 24 h. After machining by URM, the surface appears noticeably smoother, with fewer air pockets, as shown in Fig. 5(b). However, cracks and other surface imperfections are generated during conventional cutting with a 500 μm thick knife (Figs. 5(c) and 5(d)). This phenomenon is quite different from that of URM (Fig. 3(c)), highlighting that URM has potential for applications where surface quality and defect-free finishes are critical.

To assess the surface roughness after URM, a 3D optical profiler was used to scan and obtain a roughness representation of the surface. Figures 6(a) and 6(b) show the natural surface roughness and URM machined surface roughness of the ceramic gels, respectively. The natural surface of the ceramic gel has a roughness of 3.2 μm. However, that of the URM machined one was reduced to 0.3 μm. That is, the surface quality increased by 10 folds. By using the same horn of this equipment without ultrasonic vibration to cut the ceramic gel, the cut surface has a roughness of 0.7 μm, which is greater than that of the URM machined surface. This comparison illustrates that the introduction of external ultrasonic energy enhances the surface quality. This dramatic improvement is attributed to the unique advantages of URM. Specifically, ultrasonic treatment promotes reflow in the contact areas of ceramic gel, allowing the re-fluidized slurry to be removed and reshaped, ultimately producing a smoother and higher-quality surface.

Additionally, the microstructures of the machined surfaces were analyzed to assess potential microscopic defects. As shown in Fig. 7, the URM-treated surfaces exhibited higher density and fewer imperfections than the natural ones, confirming the improved surface flatness. The results suggest that ultrasonic treatment introduces reflow, leading to a more homogeneous microstructure. A high-density surface is particularly beneficial for enhancing the machinability of ceramic gels, especially for applications requiring precise aesthetics or functional performance.

In addition, URM has been successfully applied to several typical tasks in ceramic fabrication, including drilling, concave surface preparation, slotting, preparation of special-shaped ceramics, and body bonding. Figure 8(a) shows a URM drilled hole of ϕ*500 μm × 4000 μm by a needle-like horn. Figure 8(b) shows the fracture surface of the hole. Figures 8(c)–8(e) show concaves, slots, and “SIC CAS” letters achieved by URM, respectively. Figure 8(f) shows the bonding of two cut gels. No macroscopic cracks, warping, or other discernible surface defects were detected in the bodies mentioned above, which demonstrated that the ceramic gel network can reassemble and restore its structure once the external shear force is removed [33,36]. Traditional methods often require multiple steps to achieve the desired features, whereas URM enables faster and more precise shaping and refinement, minimizing material waste and reducing the need for extensive post-processing. Moreover, conventional gel-casting systems typically rely on covalent crosslinking via radical polymerization, which requires toxic monomers and initiators (e.g., acrylamide). This raise concerns related to toxicity, residual monomer contamination, and limited biodegradability. In contrast, SCC utilizes physical gelation driven by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions originating from the PIBM copolymer, as summarized in Table 2. The SCC system eliminates the need for external initiators and offers a safer and more environmentally friendly alternative. Immediately after gelation, SCC facilitates the preparation of ceramic gels and allows for the direct application of ultrasonic vibration. More importantly, SCC enables the formation of alumina gels that exhibit reflow behavior under shear stress—a property absent in chemically crosslinked systems. This reflow capability is crucial to the success of ultrasonic vibration processing, as it allows localized material removal without compromising the overall mechanical integrity of the green body.

Finally, the ultrasonic machining method based for the spontaneous coagulation–casting gel system is likely applicable to other ceramic gels, provided that they do not possess a covalent network. By adjusting parameters such as ultrasonic frequency, amplitude, and exposure time, ceramic gels can be machined, offering more efficient and precise processing conditions and opening new possibilities for the machining of advanced ceramics.

## 4 Conclusions

This study demonstrates that ultrasonic vibration-assisted processing is a highly effective method for machining alumina ceramic gels formed through SCC, which results in a shear-induced reflow behavior. By leveraging high-frequency ultrasonic vibrations, this technique enables surface refinement, drilling, slotting, and concave surface preparation, overcoming the limitations of traditional mechanical cutting methods. URM offers a more efficient, precise, and non-destructive alternative for advanced ceramic manufacturing. Additionally, the ultrasonic vibration method has the potential to reduce material waste and production time, making it a cost-effective solution for large-scale ceramics.

Future research will focus on optimizing processing parameters for various ceramic materials, expanding the versatility of this method, and exploring new applications in the production of special-shaped ceramic components across different industry applications.

| Gelation mechanism | Chemical crosslinking (polymerization) | Physical crosslinking (hydrophobic interactions) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Processing conditions | Requires initiators, oxygen control, temperature, etc. | Mild conditions, and no chemical initiators needed |
| Toxicity & environmental impact | Toxic monomers and crosslinkers, potential health/environmental concerns | Non-toxic, and environmentally friendly |
