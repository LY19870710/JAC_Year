## 1 Introduction

In response to the development concepts of “carbon neutrality” and “carbon peak”, radiative cooling technology has been widely discussed as a potential energy-saving choice in recent years [1]. Compared with traditional cooling technology, such as air conditioners and motor fans, radiative cooling technology emits heat to the outside world through an atmospheric transparency window (ATW; 8–13 μm) and reflects the vast majority of the heat of near-infrared (NIR) solar radiation, which can effectively improve thermal comfort with zero energy consumption and reduced carbon emissions [2]. To achieve this goal, developing radiative cooling materials is crucial for advancing this technology [3]. Radiative cooling materials must meet four key requirements: high ATW emissivity, high NIR solar reflectivity, low thermal conductivity, and good chemical stability [4]. Traditional radiative cooling materials include multilayer organic polymers [5], multilayer phonon crystals [6], metal-based metamaterials [7], ceramic materials [8], etc. Specifically, radiative cooling ceramic materials without complex structural designs, such as SiO2 [9], TiO2 [10], CaCO3 [11], and NaH2PO2 [12] have gained significant attention. However, current radiative cooling ceramic materials are limited by low ATW emissivity, insufficient NIR solar reflectivity, or poor chemical stability. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel ceramic materials that meet the requirements of radiative cooling applications.

The rare earth stannate (RE2Sn2O7) with a pyrochlore structure exhibits excellent optical and dielectric properties [13,14], good chemical stability [15], and low thermal conductivity [16] and may have the potential to be radiative cooling ceramic materials. La2Sn2O7 [17] possesses a wide band gap (*E*g = 3.2 eV) and will maintain high NIR solar reflectivity based on band structure and electron transition theory [18]. RE2Sn2O7 exhibits 26 different phonon modes [14,19], including RE/Sn–O stretching and bending vibrations with notably high intensities. In addition, RE2Sn2O7 presents high asymmetry due to the presence of an oxygen vacancy. Therefore, RE2Sn2O7 has great potential for the high emissivity of ATW. At the same time, rare earth (RE) elements exhibit particular electronic structures with a surplus f electrons. The formation of discrete 4f energy levels promotes these electrons to absorb energy and transit to higher energy levels, resulting in improved infrared emissivity [20]. The synergistic regulation of the ATW emission and NIR solar reflection properties is essential for achieving the radiative cooling potential of RE2Sn2O7.

In recent years, there has been growing interest in high-entropy ceramics (HECs), which are defined as solid solutions of inorganic compounds with one or more Wyckoff sites shared by equal or near-equal atomic ratios of multiple principal elements [21−23]. The emergence of HECs supplies more strategies to overcome bottlenecks in the application of materials [24,25]. The lattice distortion effect of HECs significantly influences the properties of infrared emissivity and thermal conductivity. In particular, lattice distortion could modify the asymmetry of the structural unit, resulting in a notable alteration in the electric dipole moment and ultimately leading to improved ATW emissivity. The infrared emissivity of (V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2W0.2)Si2 is approximately 60% greater than that of MoSi2 [26]. Compared to LaMgAl11O19, (La0.2Nd0.2Gd0.2Sm0.2Dy0.2)MgAl11O19 exhibited an approximately 10% increase in infrared emissivity in the 3–14 µm range [27]. Moreover, the lattice distortion effect increases the anharmonic phonon scattering, significantly reducing the thermal conductivity. The thermal conductivity of (Dy1/7Ho1/7Er1/7Tm1/7Yb1/7Lu1/7Y1/7)PO4 is approximately 50% lower than that of YPO4 [28]. In addition, the “cocktail” effect of HECs can lead to the emergence of unexpected superior properties. For example, Zheng *et al*. [29] developed (Nd0.25Y0.25Er0.25Ho0.25)6MoO12 ceramics with high NIR solar reflectivity (92.50%) and exceptional chemical stability. Consequently, the high-entropy strategy is expected to be an effective solution for achieving coordinated regulation with various properties. However, high-entropy rare earth stannate ceramics (HE-RE2Sn2O7) with pyrochlore structures have rarely been reported. Therefore, developing novel HE-RE2Sn2O7 radiative cooling materials would be beneficial for promoting diversity in the application of high-entropy ceramics.

In this work, a series of high-entropy rare earth stannate ceramics ((Y0.2La0.2Nd0.2Eu0.2Gd0.2)2Sn2O7 (HE-RE1), (Y0.2La0.2Sm0.2Eu0.2Lu0.2)2Sn2O7 (HE-RE2), and (Y0.2La0.2Gd0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)2Sn2O7 (HE-RE3)) were prepared by the solid phase method. The effects of composition and lattice distortion on the band gap, ATW emissivity, NIR solar reflectivity, thermal conductivity, and chemical stability were investigated comprehensively. Significantly, the regulatory mechanism between the ATW emission and the NIR reflectance by multi-component design was systematically surveyed. The results indicate that HE-RE2Sn2O7 exhibits high ATW emissivity, high NIR solar reflectivity, low thermal conductivity, and excellent chemical stability, which demonstrates that multi-component design provides a novel approach for the development of radiative cooling materials.

### 2.1 Materials and methods

Commercially available powders of SnO2 (99.9%), Y2O3 (99.99%), La2O3 (99.99%), Nd2O3 (99.99%), Sm2O3 (99.99%), Eu2O3 (99.99%), Gd2O3 (99.99%), Yb2O3 (99.99%), and Lu2O3 (99.99%) (Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) were used as raw materials. SnO2 and RE2O3 (RE = Y3+, La3+, Nd3+, Eu3+, and Gd3+) were mixed at a molar ratio of 2 : 1 for 24 h in a planetary mill (MSK-SFM-1, Hefei Kejing Materials Technology Co., Ltd., China) with ethanol as the medium. Then, the mixed powders were uniaxially pressed into green bodies and heated at 1600 °C for 6 h in air. The calcining products were ball-milled in ethyl alcohol and then thoroughly dried. The obtained powders were sieved through a 400-mesh sieve to obtain fine and homogeneous (Y0.2La0.2Nd0.2Eu0.2Gd0.2)2Sn2O7 (HE-RE1) powders. Then, the powders were pressed into green bodies (20 mm in diameter and 3 mm in height) under a force of 300 MPa. Finally, the green bodies were sintered at 1700 °C for 6 h to obtain dense bulk ceramics. La2Sn2O7 (LA), (Y0.2La0.2Sm0.2Eu0.2Lu0.2)2Sn2O7 (HE-RE2), and (Y0.2La0.2Gd0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)2Sn2O7 (HE-RE3) powders and corresponding bulk compacts were fabricated via the same process for comparison.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The phase composition was identified by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Miniflex 600 instrument, Rigaku, Japan) using Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5406 Å) with a scanning speed of 2 (°)/min from 20° to 90°. The crystal structure parameters were determined via the Rietveld refinement method (GSAS II) based on the obtained XRD patterns. The density of the bulk samples was determined using the Archimedes method. The microstructure and elemental distribution were investigated by a transmission electron microscope (TEM; FEI Tecnai F20, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) combined with an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS; Talos F200x, Super X, USA). The vibrational absorption of the groups in the structure was characterized by a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR; Thermo Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and a Raman spectrometer (LabRAM, Horiba, Japan). The thermal conductivity was evaluated by a thermal conductivity meter (TPS 2200S, Hot Disk, Sweden). The emittance of the mid-infrared band (ATW 8–13 μm) was determined by an FTIR (Nicolet iS50R, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a gold integrating sphere (L1272405, PIKE Technologies, USA) by the reflection method, and the test window was a zinc selenide window. According to Kirchoff’s law, due to the opacity of the object, the emissivity = 1 − reflectivity. An ultraviolet–visible‒NIR (UV‒Vis‒NIR) spectrophotometer (Carry 5000, Agilent, USA) was calibrated using polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) before testing to ensure accurate reflectance measurements. The preparation process of the powder samples was uniform to obtain samples with similar particle sizes. In addition, before the test, the powder samples were poured into a special tray and compacted to reduce the effect of light scattering on the measurements.

To evaluate the chemical stability, the synthesized ceramic powders were immersed in separate solutions of 5 wt% HCl and 5 wt% H2SO4 and stirred for 24 h. Afterwards, the samples were thoroughly rinsed with ultrapure water until a neutral pH was achieved. Then, the samples were air-dried in an oven, and the NIR reflectivity was measured. The stability of the ceramics could be determined by measuring the changes in the NIR solar reflectivity.

### 3.1 Phase compositions and microstructures

Figure 1 shows the crystal structure of HE-RE2Sn2O7 pyrochlores [30]. RE ions and Sn ions occupy 16d sites (1/2, 1/2, 1/2) and 16c sites (0, 0, 0), respectively. The O1 and O2 oxygen anions are located at the 48f (*x*, 1/8, 1/8) and 8b (3/8, 3/8, 3/8) sites. Moreover, there is an ordered inherent 8a oxygen vacancy in its 1/8 unit cell because the 8a (1/8, 1/8, 1/8) site is not occupied. The crystal structure of the RE2Sn2O7 compounds was determined by the ratio of the radii of the RE3+ and Sn4+ ions (*γ* = *r*RE/*r*Sn). Typically, RE2Sn2O7 crystallizes an ordered pyrochlore phase when *γ* is within the range of 1.46–1.78 [31]. According to the radius ratio rule, it can be identified that LA (*γ* = 1.681), HE-RE1 (*γ* = 1.565), HE-RE2 (*γ* = 1.537), and HE-RE3 (*γ* = 1.506) have pyrochlore structures with space groups of *Fd** 3¯m*. The XRD patterns of the LA and HE-RE2Sn2O7 powders, together with the La2Sn2O7 powders from the PDF cards, are shown in Fig. 2(a). Four strong pyrochlore characteristic peaks (222), (400), (440), and (622) are observed in the XRD patterns of the La2Sn2O7 and HE-RE2Sn2O7 samples. Thus, it can be confirmed that LA, HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 solid solutions were successfully synthesized [32]. The peak corresponding to the (222) crystal plane is enlarged in Fig. 2(b). Compared with those of the single-component LA, the characteristic peaks of HE-RE2Sn2O7 shift in the high-angle direction. Significantly, the extent of the peak shift becomes more significant with increasing 2*θ*, mainly due to the shrinkage of the lattice structure [33]. Compared with single-component LA, HE-RE2Sn2O7 shows lattice shrinkage. Because La2Sn2O7 has the largest lattice constant among the single components of RE2Sn2O7, it is derived from having the largest ionic radius of La3+. The order of the ionic radii of the rare earth elements selected in this paper is La3+ (1.160 Å) > Nd3+ (1.109 Å) > Sm3+ (1.079 Å) > Eu3+ (1.066 Å) > Gd3+ (1.053 Å) > Y3+ (1.019 Å) > Yb3+ (0.985 Å) > Lu3+ (0.977 Å). When La3+ is replaced by RE3+ with a smaller radius, it will inevitably cause lattice contraction. As a result, the interplanar spacing is reduced, resulting in the peak position moving to a higher angle, which conforms to Bragg’s law [33].

### 3.2 Lattice distortion analysis

In most publications, the displacement of the constituent atoms away from their ideal locations has been defined as lattice distortions. It has been confirmed that lattice distortion will change the microstructure of the crystal to a certain extent, thus significantly affecting the characteristic properties of the materials [37,38]. To enhance the lattice distortion, the influence of the ion size and mass was considered in the design and synthesis of HE-RE2Sn2O7.

The average cation radius difference (*δ*) for the formation of HE-RE2Sn2O7 is shown in Eq. (1) [27]:

(1) δ=∑i=1NXi[1−ri/(∑i=1NXiri)]2

where *N* is the number of components; *X**i* and *r**i* are the molar fraction and the cation radius of the *i*th element in the La3+ 16d sites (1/2, 1/2, 1/2), respectively. At the same time, the average cation mass difference can be determined by employing the conceptual equation for the radius difference. The average mass difference (*σ*) for the formation of HE-RE2Sn2O7 is shown in Eq. (2):

(2) σ=∑i=1NXi[1−mi/(∑i=1NXimi)]2

where *X**i* and *m**i* are the molar fraction and the cation masses of the *i*th element in the La3+ 16d sites (1/2, 1/2, 1/2), respectively. *δ* and *σ* of HE-RE2Sn2O7 are shown in Table 2.

Table 2 shows that the radius difference and mass differences of HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 gradually increase, indicating that the degree of lattice distortion in HE-RE2Sn2O7 is enhanced [22]. On the one hand, lattice strain based on the increase in the XRD peak shift is an effective method for measuring the degree of lattice distortion [39,40]. On the other hand, the fluctuation of ionic bond properties in crystalline structures can also be considered part of lattice distortion, such as the magnitude of changes in bond angles and bond lengths [41].

The magnitude of the lattice strain is quantified using Williamson–Hall equation (Eq. (3))[40]:

(3) βcosθ=KλD+4εsinθ

where *β* is the half-peak height and width; *θ* is the diffraction angle; *D* is the grain size; *λ* is the wavelength of the Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5406 Å); *ε* is the lattice strain. As shown in Fig. 5, there is a gradual increase in the lattice strain for LA, HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3, with values of 0.005%, 0.131%, 0.211%, and 0.263%, respectively. Obviously, the lattice strain values follow the same trend as the XRD peak shape shifts to a large angle, as shown in Fig. 2(b). The appearance of lattice strain can be attributed to the progressively increasing radius difference and mass difference of rare earth ions due to the multi-component design (Table 2). The different degrees of lattice distortion of HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 were proven.

Previous work has shown that the chemical environment of anions can be altered by introducing cations with different radii and masses [41]. Hence, an efficient approach for evaluating the degree of lattice distortion is to examine the differences in the bond angles and bond lengths of the anions. Figure 6(a) shows the Raman spectra of LA, HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 in the wavenumber range of 200–600 cm−1. The peak observed at approximately 300 cm−1 is attributed to the bending vibration of the O–Sn–O bond [42]. The O–Sn–O bond angles for LA, HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 are measured to be 83.90°, 82.01°, 81.77°, and 81.67°, respectively, as calculated by GSAS II, as shown in Table 1. Moreover, the shift of the peaks toward higher wavenumbers confirms the decrease in the O–Sn–O bond angle. Consequently, the deviations of HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 from the LA bond angles exhibit a progressive augmentation in the level of lattice distortion.

### 3.3 ATW emission property

The ATW emissivity is one of the key factors in determining the radiative cooling ability. As shown in Fig. 7, the average ATW emissivity of LA, HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 is 90.43%, 91.38%, 93.01%, and 95.41%, respectively. The high ATW emissivity of La2Sn2O7 can be attributed to its crystal structure with a variety of different phonon vibration modes, such as La/Sn–O tensile vibration and O–Sn–O bending vibration. In addition, La2Sn2O7 exhibits high asymmetry due to the presence of 1/8 oxygen vacancies. Previous work has proven that the ATW emissivity is directly related to the magnitude of the lattice distortion [27]. Notably, the average ATW emissivity of HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 increases with the degree of lattice distortion [39].

Figure 8 illustrates a consistent and significant correlation among lattice strain (ε*), lattice distortion (*ξ*), and ATW emissivity. The increase in the lattice strain and lattice distortion is directly associated with the increase in the ATW emissivity of LA, HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3. The high-entropy design paradigm is crucial for maintaining the crystal structure and inducing severe lattice distortion. To enhance lattice distortion, it is essential to select rare earth ions with significant differences in cation radius and mass based on a multi-component design strategy. Substitution of light rare earth elements (Sm3+ and Eu3+) with heavy rare earth elements (Yb3+ and Lu3+) induces a localized contraction of the unit cell, thereby initiating nondirectional atom migration and resulting in a decrease in metal–oxygen bond lengths. For the ATW range (8–13 μm), the energy can be calculated by Eq. (5) [27]:

The infrared emission of HE-RE2Sn2O7 originates from lattice vibrations within the crystal. The intensity of lattice vibrations is linked to changes in the electric dipole moment between positively and negatively charged ions, which is influenced by the symmetry of the lattice structure. A higher lattice asymmetry caused by lattice distortion leads to greater fluctuations in the electric dipole moment, thereby increasing the infrared emissivity.

(6) p=ql

This can enhance the fluctuations in the group spacing of positive and negative ions, which in turn results in significant changes in the electric dipole moments [46]. In conclusion, the lattice distortion induced by high-entropy technology substantially enhances the asymmetry of the crystal structure unit, thereby causing a notable alteration in the electric dipole moment and consequently greatly enhancing the ATW emissivity.

### 3.4 NIR solar reflection properties

NIR solar reflectivity is another crucial factor of radiative cooling ability. NIR radiation in the range of 0.70−2.5 μm accounts for more than half of the total heat from solar radiation. Therefore, extensive research has focused on solar reflectivity in this region [47]. Radiative cooling materials rely heavily on high NIR solar reflectivity to efficiently deflect substantial solar radiation. The NIR solar reflectivity (* R∗) [48] was calculated according to ASTM Standard Nos. E891-87, G159-878, and G173-03 (Eq. (7)):

(7) R∗=∫7002500r(λ)i(λ)dλ∫7002500i(λ)dλ

where r*(*λ*) is the experimentally obtained spectral reflectance (W·m−2), and *i*(*λ*) is the solar spectral irradiance (W·m−2·nm−1). As shown in Fig. 9(a), the average NIR solar reflectivity of LA, HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 was 99.70%, 92.74%, 93.76%, and 97.62%, respectively. Compared to single-component LA, HE-RE2Sn2O7 exhibited a marginal decrease in the NIR solar reflectivity. It is generally accepted that the NIR solar reflectivity is directly related to the band gap [27].

*
Fig. 9(a) NIR solar reflectivity and (b) E*g of LA, HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3.

The band gap is calculated by Eq. (8):

(8) (αhv)0.5=A(hv−Eg)

where *α* is the absorption coefficient; *h* is the Plank’s constant; *ν* is the phonic frequency; *A* is a constant. As shown in Fig. 9(b), LA, HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 possess band gaps of 3.01, 2.26, 2.34, and 2.68 eV, respectively. These band gaps fall outside the NIR radiation energy range, typically from 0.5 to 1.8 eV [27]. It is evident that a reduction in the band gap results in a corresponding decrease in the NIR solar reflectivity.

Notably, the NIR solar reflectivity of HE-RE3 is greater than that of HE-RE1 and HE-RE2, as shown in Fig. 9(a), which can be attributed to the increased difficulty in electron excitation. According to the energy level diagrams of rare earth ions [49], ions with high energy levels have weak electron transition capacities and show good solar reflectivity. The valence band of La is fully occupied, resulting in inadequate light absorption upon excitation. While Nd and Eu in HE-RE1 show easy electron absorption, Sm and Eu in HE-RE2 have relatively low energy levels and tend toward easier absorption. Moreover, Y, La, and Lu in HE-RE3 are completely or half-filled, leading to difficulty in electron excitation. Notably, HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 surpass the threshold of 90% NIR solar reflectivity, thereby emphasizing the remarkable capacity to effectively reflect solar heat. Hence, the multi-component design paradigm can modify the band gap and electron transition capability to equip HE-RE2Sn2O7 with exceptional NIR solar reflection performance.

According to the phonon point defect scattering theory, the mass difference and radius difference of cations strongly influence the thermal conductivity [51,52]. Based on the theoretical model proposed by Slack [53] and Abeles [54], the phonon scattering coefficient (*Г*) associated with the mass and radius can be expressed as Eq. (10):

(10) Γ=∑xi[(mim)2Γm+ε(rir)2Γr]

where *x**i*, *m**i*, and *r**i* represent the atomic proportion, the atomic mass, and the ionic radius of the substitutional element, respectively; *ε* is the strain field factor; *Г*m and *Г*r are the phonon scattering coefficients induced by differences in the atomic mass and the ionic radius, respectively. The data presented in Table 2 illustrate that the ion radius deviations for HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 are 4.507%, 5.809%, and 6.385%, respectively. Moreover, the differences in atomic mass are 18.00%, 20.26%, and 21.58%, which are primarily attributed to significant variations in atomic mass across the substituted cations. The phonon scattering coefficients of HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 show a progressive increase in correlation with the increase in both the ionic radius difference and atomic mass difference. Hence, the multi-component design progressively amplifies the radius difference and mass difference of HE-RE2Sn2O7, consequently increasing the overall phonon scattering coefficient. This indicates a progressive enhancement of the phonon scattering phenomenon, resulting in a decrease in thermal conductivity.

Superior chemical stability is essential for the long-term and consistent operation of radiative cooling. It has been demonstrated that high-entropy ceramics exhibit superior chemical stability compared to single-component ceramics [55]. To assess chemical stability, comprehensive experiments were meticulously devised and carried out, focusing specifically on the comparison between HE-RE3 and LA. It is important to note that the mass loss resulting from acid corrosion was determined to be negligible [29]. The change in NIR solar reflectivity (∆*R*) is calculated by Eq. (11):

(11) ΔR=|R0−RH|R0

where *R*0 is the NIR solar reflectivity of uncorroded LA and HE-RE3; *R*H is the NIR solar reflectivity of acid-corroded LA and HE-RE3; Δ*R* is the change value of the near-infrared solar reflectance before and after acid soaking. Figure 10 shows the NIR solar reflectivity of uncorroded and acid-corroded LA and HE-RE3. The ∆*R* values of LA are 5.05% and 2.45% after acid treatment with 5% HCl and 5% H2SO4, respectively. Similarly, ∆*R* of HE-RE3 is 0.779% and 2.16%, respectively. Compared to LA, HE-RE3 exhibited better chemical stability. The basicity of rare earth oxides ranges from the smallest, Sc2O3, to the largest, La2O3, showing a direct correlation with the cation radius. With decreasing RE3+ radius and increasing ion charge density, the change from La3+ to Lu3+ leads to a relatively stable decrease in the basicity of the oxide [56]. Therefore, La3+, the most basic element, is gradually replaced by RE3+, which has weaker alkalinity. This process eventually leads to HE-RE2Sn2O7 showing weaker alkalinity than single-component LA, thus showing better acid corrosion resistance. Traditional radiative cooling ceramic materials, such as CaCO3 [11] and NaH2PO2 [12], are limited by insufficient chemical stability. In contrast, HE-RE2Sn2O7 exhibits exceptional chemical stability, making it suitable for prolonged utilization in challenging environments such as acid rain.

### 3.6 Balance of ATW emissivity and NIR solar reflectivity

The lattice distortion caused by the multi-component design significantly increases the ATW emissivity, but it inevitably reduces the band gap, leading to a decrease in the NIR solar reflectivity. Therefore, the simultaneous achievement of high ATW emissivity and high NIR solar reflectivity is a serious challenge. To achieve the balance of different properties, rare earth cations with significant differences in radius and mass are selected to generate severe lattice distortion, and subsequently, rare earth ions that are difficult to excite are selected to maintain a wide band gap. Thus, combining a multi-component design strategy and rational elemental screening, the as-prepared HE-RE2Sn2O7 exhibited high ATW emissivity and NIR solar reflectivity. ## 4 Conclusions

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the multi-component design in radiative cooling, a series of high-entropy rare earth stannate ceramics (Y0.2La0.2Nd0.2Eu0.2Gd0.2)2Sn2O7, (Y0.2La0.2Sm0.2Eu0.2Lu0.2)2Sn2O7, and (Y0.2La0.2Gd0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)2Sn2O7) were prepared by a solid-phase reaction followed by a pressureless sintering method. The prepared samples possess a pyrochlore structure with a space group of *Fd** 3−m*. The multi-component design enhances the lattice distortion, which directly affects the characteristic performance of radiative cooling. The ATW emissivity of LA, HE-RE1, HE-RE2, and HE-RE3 is 90.43%, 91.38%, 93.07%, and 95.41%, respectively, which indicate a high infrared radiation capacity. Moreover, the NIR solar reflectivity reaches 99.70%, 92.74%, 93.76%, and 97.62%, respectively, indicating that the HE-RE2Sn2O7 ceramics have good solar reflection properties. In addition, the thermal conductivities are 2.99, 1.62, 1.40, and 1.08 W·m−1·K−1, respectively, indicating excellent thermal insulation. The synergistic effect of lattice distortion modulation and multi-component design not only modulates the ATW emission properties and NIR solar reflection properties but also significantly reduces the thermal conductivity and improves the chemical stability. Finally, this work pioneers the application of a high-entropy design paradigm in the domain of radiative cooling and provides a novel strategy for developing radiative cooling materials.
