## 1 Introduction

In recent years, the semiconductor industry has rapidly escalated the demand for higher-performing chips through further miniaturization of line widths and high aspect ratios in wafer fabrication. The rapid expansion of the innovative generative artificial intelligence (AI) market has driven significant growth in non-volatile three-dimensional (3D) NAND flash memory, which retains data in a grid of memory cells without requiring a continuous power supply, and recent advancements, such as four-dimensional (4D) NAND memory with more than 300 layers, have been unveiled [1]. The shift toward high-bandwidth memory (HBM), which involves multiple vertically stacked thin wafer layers interconnected by through-silicon vias (TSVs), has gained significant momentum [2]. For fabrication of such high-bandwidth memories, plasma environments are required for both reactive ion etching during the front-end process and Bosch dicing at the back-end. This requires a high-density, high-energy plasma environment exceeding 100 kW, which affects not only the chips but also the ceramic components such as the dielectrics, confinement rings, and inner walls within semiconductor manufacturing chambers [3,4]. Extending the life span of these ceramic components can directly improve semiconductor production yields because high etching rates accelerate the replacement cycle, necessitating equipment downtime for maintenance [5]. Furthermore, in plasma environments containing fluorine gas, nonvolatile particles such as YF3 and AlF3, which form on the surface of typical ceramic component materials, tend to float in sheaths during equipment operation and settle on the wafers when the equipment is stopped [6]. This contamination degrades the control properties of the bandgap donors and acceptors in the wafer, ultimately reducing the yield of large-scale integrated circuits. Consequently, it is necessary to develop materials with excellent etching resistance in plasma environments to minimize the generation of contaminating particles [7,8]. Residual pores in the ceramic microstructure are a major cause of large crater formation, and they affect the etching rate of components [9]. Crater formation can be suppressed by effectively eliminating these pores, improving the plasma resistance of ceramics while ensuring optical transparency in the visible light region [10]. New classes of advanced plasma-resistant ceramics have been developed to address these challenges.

Ceramic components, including the bulk and coatings used in plasma-etching equipment, are predominantly composed of single-composition systems such as Al2O3, Y2O3, and yttrium aluminum garnet (Y3Al5O12, YAG). Recently, there has been a growing focus on nanocomposite materials such as Y2O3–MgO, Y2O3–YAM (Y4Al2O9), and Gd2O3–MgO, which are being explored for their ability to minimize surface roughness changes and offer specific advantages in various plasma environments through the pinning effect [11−15]. Studies have also been conducted to improve the plasma resistance by doping with Zr ions or by incorporating rare-earth ions [16,17]. Despite these advances, the development of plasma-resistant materials has been limited to a narrow range of compositions. Given that the plasma-resistant properties of ceramic components are influenced by factors such as gas type, mixing ratio, and bias power during etching, it is crucial to broaden the spectrum of available materials to enable the selection of the most suitable material for a specific environment.

Developing novel materials capable of exhibiting unique properties or resistance to external environments, such as plasma, is essential. Recently, high-entropy materials have been introduced, and their configurational entropy provides multifaceted benefits owing to their desirable properties. In general, high-entropy ceramics refer to ceramics composed of five or more elements in near-equiatomic proportions, with a configuration entropy * (ΔSconfig) of at least 1.5R*, where *R* is the gas constant [18]. With the increasing number of multiple principal components, configurational entropy promotes the homogenization of a singular phase. The enthalpy of interactions between multiple elements in the multi-element composition was optimized to be near zero to minimize both repulsive and attractive forces, thereby discouraging phase segregation and the formation of intermetallic compounds [19]. Entropy-stabilized materials exhibit high phase stabilization by lowering the Gibbs free energy of the system. Stability predictions for high-entropy ceramic phases are most commonly conducted through Gibbs free energy calculations using density functional theory (DFT) [20]. Methods such as molecular dynamics simulations, computer coupling of phase diagrams and thermochemistry (CALPHAD), Goldschmidt’s tolerance factor, and entropy-forming ability (EFA) have also been utilized as descriptors in designing high-entropy phases [21−24].

In high-entropy ceramics, high chemical disorder induces various beneficial core effects. First, it is possible to form a novel stable solid solution that surpasses the solubility limits of conventional materials. Significant lattice distortions can enhance both mechanical and chemical properties while resulting in sluggish diffusion characteristics. Finally, a unique combination of elements can produce an unexpected “cocktail effect”, creating synergistic properties that enable applications in new fields [25−27]. Numerous high-entropy ceramic materials, including carbides and nitrides, are the subject of ongoing research. High-entropy oxides are particularly notable because of their exceptional compositional flexibility and various crystallographic structures [28]. Owing to the aforementioned core effects, these materials have been investigated for a broad spectrum of potential applications, including thermal barriers, catalysts, energy storage, and optical materials [29−33]. In particular, high-entropy oxides that utilize rare-earth ions have been investigated. Trivalent rare-earth ions possess an [Xe]4f*n*−1 electron configuration, which results in lanthanide contraction. These distinctive characteristics reduce the enthalpy of mixing rare-earth elements in high-entropy ceramics, thereby subtly influencing their properties [34]. Sun *et al.* [35] fabricated high-entropy oxides containing rare-earth elements with a cubic bixbyite structure to reduce the thermal conductivity and enhance the resistance to calcium-magnesium-aluminosilicate, thereby facilitating the application of these materials as environmental barrier coatings. Through both *in situ* and *ex situ* analyses, Tseng *et al*. [36] demonstrated that phase transformations were suppressed in lanthanide sesquioxide high-entropy oxides. Djerdj *et al*. [37] successfully produced hydrogen via photocatalytic water splitting using high-entropy ceramics containing rare-earth elements. Although high-entropy oxides incorporating lanthanides have been studied for various applications, their plasma resistance properties remain unexplored. The formation of a layer on the ceramic surface through reactions with reactive gas species in the plasma environment is closely related to the plasma etching resistance. Hence, it is essential to investigate how the unique properties of high-entropy ceramics, such as lattice distortion and diffusion inhibition, affect materials in plasma environments.

In this study, we designed and fabricated a novel high-entropy sesquioxide that exhibited both transparency and plasma resistance to explore new application fields for high-entropy materials. Initially, high-entropy ceramics with rare-earth oxides with a single-phase structure and minimal porosity were produced via hot-press sintering. The plasma-resistant properties of these materials, including their etching rates and surface roughness changes, were assessed for the first time, to the best of our knowledge, in a robust plasma environment containing CF4/Ar/O2. This assessment aimed to evaluate their potential use as components in semiconductor processing equipment. Moreover, we discuss the transmittance and optical properties of high-entropy ceramics for applications in transparent windows in plasma environments.

### 2.1 Preparation and sintering of transparent high-entropy oxides

Commercial Y2O3 (≥ 99.9%, Cenotec, Republic of Korea), Yb2O3, Gd2O3, Dy2O3, Lu2O3, Er2O3 (all ≥ 99.9%, Sigma Aldrich), and Eu2O3 (≥ 99.5%, Sigma Aldrich) powders were used as raw materials. All powders were mixed in the following stoichiometric ratio via ball milling at 200 r/min for 24 h at room temperature ((Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4X0.4)O3, where X = Lu, Er, or Eu). First, 200 g of ZrO2 balls (diameter: 5 mm) were used with anhydrous ethanol as the solvent. Subsequently, the ball-milled slurry was dried at 200 °C and calcined in an alumina crucible at 1000 °C for 5 h in a box chamber furnace to eliminate any remaining residue. The sample was then sieved through a 200-mesh sieve. The sieved powder was uniaxially pressed into a steel mold with a diameter of 14 mm to form a green body, followed by cold isostatic pressing at 200 MPa for 5 min. The green body was sintered using the hot-press method in a vacuum atmosphere at 1450–1550 °C and 20 MPa for 2 h, followed by cooling. To prevent adhesion between the green body, mold, and spacers during sintering, the inside of the graphite mold was coated with boron nitride, and a graphite foil was placed between the spacers and green body. To remove residual pores and carbon, post-annealing was performed at 1100 °C for 12 h in a box furnace under an air atmosphere. The heating and cooling rates were 5°C/min.

### 2.2 Plasma etching

To investigate the plasma resistance of the high-entropy oxides, samples with a diameter of 14 mm and thickness of 0.8 mm were prepared. Commercial *c*-axis sapphire and Y2O3 polycrystalline ceramics were prepared as reference materials. A transparent Y2O3 sample was purchased from FineTech Co., Ltd. (Republic of Korea). The measured relative density of the Y2O3 ceramics was 99.9%. The surfaces of the sintered samples were polished and chemically mechanically planarized (CMP) to reduce the initial surface roughness. A shadow mask frame made of a nickel‒cobalt alloy was used to cover the sample surface, allowing only a specific area with a width of 1 mm and length of 6 mm for etching. The thickness of the shadow mask was 0.05 mm. The remaining areas were masked using Kapton tape. The plasma etching test was conducted in a radiofrequency (RF) biased inductively coupled plasma (ICP) chamber [38]. A gas mixture comprising CF4 (40 standard cubic centimeters per minute (sccm)), Ar (10 sccm), and O2 (10 sccm) was used. The ICP power and RF bias voltage were set as 1500 W (13.56 MHz) and 600 V (2 MHz), respectively. These conditions, known as the inductive mode, maintain a high plasma density, which is crucial in the semiconductor industry [39]. The plasma density was measured to be 9×1010 cm−3 using a microwave cut-off probe [40,41]. The pressure was maintained at 20 mTorr, and the distance from the ICP antenna to the substrate was 152 nm. All the samples were exposed to the plasma environment for 1 h. Following plasma etching, the samples were subjected to ultrasonic cleaning with ethanol and acetone to eliminate masking tape residues, enabling precise assessment of the etching depth and surface roughness.

### 2.3 Characterization

Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; JSM-7800F, JEOL, Japan) was employed to observe the morphology of the powder and surface of the high-entropy ceramics before and after the plasma etching test. Phase analysis of raw materials and sintered high-entropy ceramics was performed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/Max 2500, Rigaku, Japan) using Cu Kα radiation. A scan rate of 5 (°)/min and a scan range of 15°–90° were employed. High-resolution XRD patterns of the sintered high-entropy oxide ceramics were obtained using synchrotron XRD at the 9B high-resolution powder diffractometer (HRPD) beamline (* λ= 1.5464 Å) at the Pohang Accelerator Laboratory (PAL), Republic of Korea. Data were collected with a six-detector system with Ge(111) analyzer crystals over an angular  2θ range of 15°–135.5° with a step size of 0.005°. The VESTA program was used to determine the crystal structure [42]. The measured densities of all sintered samples were determined using the Archimedes method. The average grain size was obtained using the linear intercept of 150 grains [43]. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; AXIS SUPRA+, Kratos Analytical Ltd., UK) was used to determine the binding energies of the high-entropy ceramics. The binding of the obtained spectra was calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.6 eV. Atomic probe tomography (APT) samples were prepared using a focused ion beam system (FIB; Helios 5 UX, Thermo Fisher, USA). APT (LEAP 5000, Cameca, France) analysis was performed using a 355 nm ultraviolet (UV) laser with 50 pJ pulse energy and 200 kHz pulse rate. The chamber pressure was maintained at 3.0×10−11 Torr. The APT acquisition temperature was set to 50 K. CAMECA Integrated Visualization and Analysis Software (IVAS) was used for 3D atomic reconstruction. X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis of the high-entropy ceramics was conducted to estimate their chemical compositions (ZSX Primus IVi, Rigaku, Japan). The operating conditions were set to 4 kW, 50 kV, and 48 mA. The average etching depths of the samples were measured at three different positions for each sample using a surface profiler (Tencor P-7 Stylus Profiler, KLA Co., USA). The scan rate was set to 200 Hz, and the scan length was set to 1 mm. The surface morphology and roughness (R*a) of the samples were investigated using an atomic force microscope (AFM; XE-100, Park Systems, Republic of Korea). The scan size was 25 μm × 25 μm, and the scan rate was 0.5 Hz. After FIB milling (FIB; Scios, Thermo Fisher, USA), a transmission electron microscope (TEM; TALOS F2000X, FEI, USA) was used to analyze the etching behavior of the high-entropy oxide. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed to facilitate elemental analysis and chemical composition line mapping. Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis)/near infrared (NIR) spectra were used to evaluate the in-line transmittance, diffuse transmittance, and diffuse reflectance of the high-entropy ceramics in the range of 250–3000 nm (Lambda 1050, Perkin Elmer, USA).

### 3.1 Design and sintering of high-entropy oxides

This study focuses on designing sesquioxide high-entropy ceramics by considering factors such as atomic and ionic radii, valency, and cation electronegativity [44]. High-entropy ceramics were developed using cubic Y₂O₃, known for their exceptional plasma-resistant properties, as the base material, with lanthanide ions of varying ionic radii substituted at the Y cation site. According to the empirical Hume–Rothery rules, which are widely applied for the formation of solid solutions in alloys, the difference in the atomic radius between cations should not exceed 15% [45]. Additionally, the solubility is enhanced when cations have similar crystal structures and valences and the difference in electronegativity is minimal. The metallic radii and Pauling electronegativities of lanthanides at coordination number 12 are shown in the Darken–Gurry map in Fig. 1(a) [46,47]. Except for Yb, which has no available Pauling electronegativity value, all rare-earth elements are indicated on the map. The Darken–Gurry map is primarily applied to solid solutions in alloys; however, recent studies have extended its application to the design of high-entropy ceramics [48]. Except for Eu, the metallic radii of most lanthanides satisfy the Hume–Rothery rule, remaining within 15% of Y and exhibiting a small electronegativity difference of less than 0.4 [49]. Figure 1(b) shows the ionic radii and electronegativities of the lanthanides. (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4X0.4)O3 compositions were designed based on the Y3+ ions; X = Lu, Er, or Eu. A composition with a combination of Gd3+ (0.94 Å), Dy3+ (0.91 Å), and Yb3+ (0.86 Å) was chosen to achieve an average ionic radius of 0.90 Å, which is similar to the ionic radius of Y3+ (0.89 Å). This composition enhanced the stability of the cubic phase at high temperatures [50]. For this base composition, we selected three variations: Lu3+ (0.85 Å), which has a smaller ionic radius than Y3+; Er3+ (0.88 Å), which has a similar ionic radius to Y3+; Eu3+ (0.95 Å), which has a larger ionic radius than Y3+ [46]. The polymorphic behavior of single-phase high-entropy ceramics is influenced by the average ionic radius of their complex compositions, which is intrinsically linked to their structural stability [51]. Although Eu deviates from the 15% size rule in terms of its metallic radius, its similarity to Y in valency and electronegativity justifies its selection for evaluating the properties of high-entropy ceramics based on ionic radius. An SEM image of the powder containing Lu2O3 that was ball milled and calcined is shown in Fig. 1(c). The particle size was 100–300 nm, indicating even crushing. The XRD patterns of the powders before sintering are shown in Fig. 1(d). Despite calcination at 1000 °C, the single-phase high-entropy ceramics with a cubic structure did not form; instead, a mixture of multi-phase raw powders was observed. Among the three compositions, differences in peak intensity were observed only at the positions corresponding to Lu2O3 (JCPDS#12-0728), Er2O3 (JCPDS#77-0464), and Eu2O3 (JCPDS#34-0392) compositions, with peaks detected at lower angles when the ionic radii of the raw material compositions were larger.

The results of the XRD phase analysis on sintered ceramic bodies fabricated through hot pressing at various temperatures (1450, 1500, and 1550 °C) in a vacuum atmosphere, followed by post-heat treatment in an air atmosphere, are presented in Figs. 2(a)–2(c). An analysis of the sample produced at 1450 °C revealed a single-phase cubic structure, with no secondary phases or reaction products detected among the three high-entropy ceramics comprising Lu, Er, and Eu. The XRD peak positions closely matched those of cubic Y2O3 (JCPDS#86-1326), confirming the successful fabrication of the new high-entropy ceramics, with all constituent elements sharing a single crystallographic site, as initially intended. Notably, compositions such as Gd2O3 and Eu2O3, which typically undergo a phase transition from cubic to monoclinic when sintered at temperatures exceeding 1200 °C, did not exhibit such a transition in this study [52,53]. This stability is likely due to the random arrangement of multiple cations and relatively small average ionic radii of the elements combined in the high-entropy ceramics compared with the radii of Gd ions, which suppresses the phase transition at elevated temperatures in the phase diagram [50]. The enlarged XRD results in the 2θ* range of 28°–30° for the three compositions of samples sintered at 1450 °C are shown in Fig. 2(d). As the ionic radius increases in the order of Lu, Er, and Eu, the average ionic radius of the fabricated high-entropy ceramics also increases, causing the XRD peak to shift to lower angles. Figure 2(e) shows the measured relative densities of the sintered high-entropy oxide bodies as a function of the sintering temperature. An increase in the consolidation temperature corresponded to an overall increase in the ceramic density. The relative density of the samples sintered at 1450 °C ranged from 98.2% to 99.1%. At 1550 °C, they achieved a high relative density exceeding 99.7%. Notably, the compositions containing Lu and Eu were close to the theoretical densities. The measured average grain sizes and SEM microstructural images of the high-entropy ceramics sintered at different temperatures are shown in Fig. 2(f) and Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Despite utilizing raw powders with sizes in the range of several hundreds of nanometers, increasing the sintering temperature to 1550 °C resulted in rapid grain growth, with grain sizes exceeding twice those at 1450 °C for all compositions. When the sample was sintered at 1550 °C, the grain size increased sequentially in the compositions containing Er, Lu, and Eu. Notably, the composition containing Eu exhibited a large average grain size of 56.7 μm, accompanied by a significant standard deviation. A plausible explanation is that when Eu ions are incorporated into a high-entropy ceramic composition, they can exist in multiple valence states (Eu3+ and Eu2+), which may increase the number of oxygen vacancies within the matrix. XPS analysis was conducted on the (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Er0.4)O3 composition sintered at 1550 °C, and the results are presented in Fig. S2 in the ESM. An analysis of Eu 3d XPS spectra revealed the presence of Eu2+ with binding energies of 1124 and 1155 eV and Eu3+ with binding energies of 1134 and 1164 eV. The presence of Eu2+ disrupts the charge balance and increases the number of oxygen vacancies, potentially enhancing mass transfer and leading to excessive grain growth [54]. SEM images of the fracture surfaces of (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Er0.4)O3 ceramics sintered at different temperatures are shown in Figs. 2(g)–2(i). Overall, the fracture modes of the samples displayed transgranular fracture characteristics, with C-shaped curved lines and cleavage steps observed within the transgranular regions [55]. As the sintering temperature increased from 1450 to 1550 °C, both grain growth and density enhancement were evident, as noted in the fracture surface. In the sample sintered at 1450 °C (Fig. 2(g)), residual pores that were not fully eliminated were observed along the grain boundaries and at triple points. These pores can potentially contribute to crater formation during plasma etching. In contrast, in the sample sintered at 1550 °C (Fig. 2(i)), only a few small pores remained along the surfaces of adjacent grains due to grain growth and densification [56]. High-temperature sintering effectively minimized the residual pores in the microstructure, thereby realizing high-density compacts.

Figure 3 and Table 1 present the results and structural parameters obtained from Rietveld refinements of high-entropy ceramics (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Lu0.4)O3, (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Er0.4)O3, and (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Eu0.4)O3. The cubic Y2O3 unit cell used as the base material featured two non-equivalent Y atoms, namely, Y1 and Y2, at the Wyckoff positions of 8b* (Wyck 8*b*; 0.25, 0.25, 0.25) and 24*d* (Wyck 24*d*; u, 0, 0.25), respectively, with oxygen atoms in octahedral coordination [57,58]. All the diffraction peaks could be indexed to the cubic *Ia** 3¯ space group, with no detectable secondary phases. The refinement parameters ( χ2) for the three compositions were 2.47, 2.19, and 3.39, respectively. The incorporation of Lu, Er, and Eu into the structure resulted in an increase in the lattice constant, reflecting the average ionic radius, with values of 10.5829, 10.6151, and 10.6782 Å, respectively. Selected interatomic distance data for the fabricated high-entropy oxides are listed in Table S1 in the ESM. In cubic Y2O3, the Y1–O1 bond distance is approximately 2.29 Å; however, in high-entropy ceramics, this distance deviates within the range of 2.271–2.301 Å, reflecting the entropy stabilization effect when various elements occupy the rare-earth (RE) site [57]. The octahedra surrounding RE2 atom exhibited significant distortion, with RE2–O1 average bond distances varying widely from 2.232 to 2.356 Å. Notably, in compositions containing Er, the RE1–O1 and RE2–O1 interatomic distances were larger, leading to greater polyhedral distortions [59]. Despite the local lattice distortions associated with increased configurational entropy, a phase analysis confirmed the successful production of single-phase high-entropy ceramics without secondary phase formation.

### 3.2 Plasma etching resistance of high-entropy oxides

To assess the potential of novel high-entropy ceramics as a plasma-resistant material for semiconductor manufacturing equipment, the plasma etching resistances of the fabricated samples were evaluated. As depicted in Fig. 5(a), a shadow mask made of a Ni–Co alloy was applied to the samples, limiting plasma exposure to a narrow area. A strong bias power of 1.5 kW was applied in an environment containing a CF4/Ar/O2 gas mixture; the detailed plasma etching conditions are provided in Table S2 in the ESM. After 1 h of plasma etching, the average etching rates of the reference (sapphire and high-density Y2O3) and high-entropy ceramics were measured, as shown in Fig. 5(b). The *c*-axis sapphire exhibited a rapid etching rate of 707 nm/h, primarily because of the combined effect of the high sputtering yield of AlF3 formed on the sapphire surface by the dissociation of F radicals generated from CF4 gas in the physicochemical etching environment [60]. The non-volatile fluorine-rich particles formed on the sapphire surface tend to detach easily and remain within the sheath. Subsequently, they deposit onto the Si wafer, leading to contamination. High-density transparent Y2O3 polycrystalline ceramics with a relative density of 99.9%, a representative plasma-resistant material, exhibited excellent etching resistance, with an etching rate of 97 nm/h under identical conditions. In a previous study conducted using the same equipment and plasma test conditions, Y2O3 with a relative density of 99.4% exhibited a fast etching rate of 435 nm/h [15]. In contrast, the fabricated high-entropy ceramic exhibited a significantly lower etching rate than the reference materials, with most etching rates remaining below 100 nm/h. Overall, the etching rate decreased with increasing sintering temperature. All high-entropy ceramics sintered at temperatures above 1500 °C exhibited plasma etching rates equal to or lower than those of high-density Y2O3. As indicated by the density results, higher sintering temperatures led to the elimination of residual pores within the grain boundaries and grains owing to densification. When exposed to a plasma environment, the ceramic tends to be etched initially at the edges of these residual pores, resulting in the formation of large craters. Thus, controlling porosity through densification can increase etching resistance [9]. This is further supported by the observed differences in the etching rates of Y2O3 samples with varying densities. Notably, the composition containing Er exhibited a relatively low etching rate. The (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Er0.4)O3 composition, which was sintered at 1550 °C and characterized by a high density and small grain size, demonstrated an etching rate of only 8 nm/h, which is merely 1.13% of the etching rate observed for sapphire. This rate is exceptionally low, even compared with those of Al2O3, YAG, and Y2O3 polycrystalline ceramics, which are commonly used components in semiconductor processing equipment and known for their excellent plasma resistance [14,61]. The boiling point of YF3 is 2230 °C, which is much higher than the sublimation temperature of AlF3 (approximately 1275 °C), indicating that it is difficult to vaporize [8]. Thus, forming a fluoride layer of a suitable thickness on the surface can enhance the etching resistance by shielding it from direct Ar+ ion bombardment. LuF3, ErF3, and EuF3, generated through the reactions of Lu, Er, and Eu with F radicals, have boiling points of approximately 2000 °C, effectively protecting the sample from ion sputtering. When evaluating the plasma etching resistance of ceramic materials, it is important to determine the change in surface roughness with increasing etching depth. Significant alterations in the surface roughness can be associated primarily with the ejection of sputtered neutral atoms and generation of contaminants, which negatively impact the yield in semiconductor manufacturing processes [60]. Figure 5(c) illustrates the surface roughness (*R*a) of Y2O3 and high-entropy ceramics sintered at various temperatures, both before and after plasma etching. The initial surfaces of all the samples were polished to a roughness of 1.5 nm or less. There was a minimal change in surface roughness across all the high-entropy ceramics and high-density Y2O3 ceramics, with *R*a values remaining uniformly below 3 nm even after etching. This represents a markedly smaller change in the surface roughness compared with the results of Y2O3 and the nanocomposite plasma-resistant ceramics reported in previous studies under the same etching conditions [15]. High-entropy ceramics and high-density Y2O3, characterized by an almost pore-free microstructure and measured density, did not form large craters, and their large micrometer-sized grains were uniformly etched. The observed surface roughness change was comparable to that of plasma-resistant glass (PRG); the minimal change in the actual surface roughness minimized the contamination [5]. Figures 5(d)–5(g) show the surface morphology of high-entropy ceramics both before and after etching. As illustrated in Fig. 5(e), the (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Er0.4)O3 composition sintered at 1450 °C exhibited crater formation around the grain boundaries, and residual pores remained at the triple points. Additionally, high-entropy ceramics undergo preferential etching (based on grain orientation) akin to Y2O3, where the (001) or (011) planes are preferentially etched [11]. The surface morphologies of (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Eu0.4)O3 sintered at 1550 °C before and after etching are presented in Figs. 5(f) and 5(g), respectively. After plasma etching, the surface exhibited a smoother profile, with the surface roughness decreasing from 1.03 to 0.55 nm. This improvement was likely due to the uniform etching of large grains, with a size of 56.7 μm, which is larger than the AFM scan area of 25 μm × 25 μm, resulting in minimal crater formation. In contrast, the conventional plasma-resistant coating method often results in significant surface roughness changes owing to its lower density compared with that of bulk materials. Thus, the minimal surface roughness change observed in high-entropy ceramics provides a substantial advantage in maintaining high semiconductor production yields under a physicochemical etching atmosphere. These entropy-stabilized oxides combine the excellent etching resistance of typical plasma-resistant ceramics with the minimal surface roughness variation in plasma-resistant glass.

The surface changes of plasma-etched (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Er0.4)O3 high-entropy oxide sintered at 1550 °C were analyzed through high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM), and the results are shown in Fig. 7. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and EDS mapping analyses of the samples were conducted (Fig. 7(a)). The HAADF-STEM image revealed a well-defined boundary between the matrix and thin surface-adjacent layer. Subsequent analysis of the same region via EDS mapping verified that Y, lanthanide, and O elements constituting the high-entropy ceramic were uniformly distributed throughout the matrix. Conversely, F originating from the CF4 plasma gas penetrated the surface but was detected only within a limited depth of several tens of nanometers. From the surface to the boundary layer, a MOx*F*y* fluorinated layer formed due to the interaction of Y, lanthanide, O, and F. HR-TEM images (Figs. 7(b) and 7(c)) clearly illustrate the interface between the high-entropy ceramic matrix and the damage zone resulting from fluorine penetration. In the damaged zone near the surface, partially dark contrast regions were observed, likely owing to the stress induced by the reaction with fluorine [62]. To more clearly define the boundary between the damaged zone and the substrate, a high-magnification HAADF-STEM image is presented in Fig. 7(d). This image reveals that a distinct layer, approximately 20 nm thick, formed between the Pt coating and the high-entropy oxide substrate. Notably, defects such as pores and wave-like patterns were observed within this zone. The stress at the interface between the fluorinated layer and substrate contributed to cracking, exfoliation, and particle contamination. Line mapping was performed to conduct depth-dependent elemental analyses. As shown in Fig. 7(e), the concentration of fluorine was significantly high up to a defined boundary located approximately 20 nm from the surface, with the highest fluorine content detected near the boundary where defects were prevalent. The fluoride layer formed on the surface was directly attacked and preferentially removed, resulting in a relatively low F concentration compared with that at the boundary of the damaged zone [11]. Beyond this boundary, the fluorine content gradually decreased and became undetectable beyond approximately 40 nm. The boundary observed in the TEM images was 20 nm, but fluorine was detected at depths up to approximately 40 nm, which is nearly twice the boundary depth. In contrast, the oxygen levels increased progressively from the surface to the oxide substrate, becoming saturated beyond a depth of 40 nm, where they exhibited no difference from the substrate. In a CF4/Ar/O2 plasma environment, ion bombardment causes oxygen bonds to break, forming dangling bonds on the oxide surface, which significantly increases surface reactions with fluorine [63]. The reacted surface layer is removed by physical sputtering, leading to a lower oxygen fraction near the surface than in the interior of the sample. High-entropy ceramics are characterized by severe lattice distortion owing to the incorporation of multiple principal elements, which enhances the diffusion resistance [20]. The etching kinetics of the fluorocarbon layer are closely related to the fluorine diffusion transport mechanism [64]. The limited diffusion or penetration path of fluorine results in the formation of a relatively thin fluorination layer. This prevents the expansion of the layer, facilitating easy removal by physical sputtering in harsh plasma environments. This can reduce the overall plasma-etching rate. In a mild environment, the fluorinated layer protects the substrate from direct physical sputtering by Ar ions. However, within the damaged zone, residual stresses between the fluorinated layer and substrate, combined with direct ion sputtering and the removal of fluorine-containing phases, can lead to the formation of defects such as cracks and pores. The weak boundary could be rapidly eliminated under conditions of a high bias voltage or when physical etching dominates, particularly with a large amount of Ar gas [62,65]. Therefore, it is necessary to maintain a layer of appropriate thickness (neither too thick nor too thin). As is evident from the Rietveld analysis, the strong polyhedral distortion in the high-entropy oxides containing Er is considered to have suppressed deep fluorine penetration, thereby reducing the plasma etching rate. The plasma etching behavior of high-entropy oxides under physicochemical erosion conditions containing CF4/Ar/O2 mixed gases is shown in Fig. 7(f). Initially, the sample was exposed to reactive species from a gas containing CF4, which led to the deposition of a fluorocarbon layer on the surface. Owing to reactions with the substrate and the internal penetration of fluorine, the fluorinated layer formed on the surface exhibited a sputtering yield over four times greater than that of the oxide substrate when exposed to Ar+ ions. This layer was rapidly removed by physical etching, thereby protecting the original ceramic matrix, a process that was repeated from the deposition stage [60]. Although various defects, such as pores and cracks, existed within the damaged zone, the produced high-density transparent high-entropy ceramics exhibited a thin layer that resulted in a slower etching rate. This material demonstrated a relatively smooth etching behavior without craters within the grains, thereby minimizing the presence of contaminants. Additional studies using various elemental combinations, including medium-entropy oxides and compositions with six or more elements, are needed to investigate the correlation between the constituent elements of high-entropy ceramics and their etching rates.

### 3.3 Optical properties of high-entropy oxides

The optical properties of the transparent plasma-resistant high-entropy ceramics were examined, and the results are shown in Fig. 8. Figures 8(a)–8(c) show photographs of high-entropy ceramics with three different compositions, each of which was fabricated at various sintering temperatures. Across all the temperature conditions, the letters behind the samples appear increasingly distinct in the following sequence: Er, Lu, and Eu. The residual pores in the ceramic microstructure act as scattering centers because of the substantial difference in the refractive index of the surrounding material, which can significantly decrease the optical transmittance via pronounced scattering [66,67]. Because pores are crucial for the formation of craters during plasma etching, it is essential to eliminate them through optimized ceramic processing and sintering to ensure that high-entropy materials are suitable for use as transparent and plasma-resistant ceramics. As evidenced in Fig. S1 in the ESM and Figs. 2(g)–2(i), residual pores were scarcely observed in the fabricated ceramics, indicating that they were transparent to the naked eye. The in-line transmittance values of the high-entropy ceramics sintered at different temperatures are shown in Figs. 8(d)–8(f). In the infrared region spanning 1.5–3 μm, the Lu-containing high-entropy ceramics exhibited the highest transmittance, characterized by a near absence of absorption peaks. In the short-wavelength region, including the near-infrared and visible light regions, the Eu-containing high-entropy ceramics demonstrated the highest transmittance, corroborating the findings observed in previous photographs. The Er-containing high-entropy ceramics exhibited lower transmittance across all wavelength ranges, despite exhibiting a minimal plasma etching depth. Figure S5 in the ESM illustrates the diffuse reflectance and transmittance results obtained using an integrating sphere for high-entropy (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Er0.4)O3 ceramics sintered at different temperatures. In all the samples, the total reflected and transmitted light from the sample remained below 70% in the visible region owing to the intrinsic light absorption of the samples rather than the influence of the internal pores. For transparent Er2O3 ceramics, unintended absorption in the visible wavelength range leads to a lower transmittance than the theoretical values, which can potentially be improved by optimizing the sintering conditions [68]. Although increasing the sintering temperature from 1450 to 1550 °C would typically enhance the transmittance by improving the density, in this study we observed a significant decrease in the transmittance and diffuse reflectance along with increased absorption. This may be associated with the post-sintering processes. Following vacuum hot-pressing under a highly reducing atmosphere, post-annealing was employed to remove oxygen vacancies and carbon contamination. While optimizing post-heat treatment conditions based on sintering temperature is ideal, in this study, the treatment was performed at a relatively low temperature of 1100 °C. This may explain why the samples sintered at higher temperatures exhibited no improvement in transmittance [69]. As this phenomenon is not due to residual pore scattering, it is unlikely to affect the plasma-resistant properties. Further investigation is required to explore the light absorption mechanism in the visible range of Er-ion-containing high-entropy ceramics and optimize the sintering and post-annealing conditions to enhance transparency.

## 4 Conclusions

Herein, we report the successful fabrication of single-phase high-entropy sesquioxide ceramics (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Lu0.4)O3, (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Er0.4)O3, and (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Eu0.4)O3 through solid-state reactions. These ceramics were confirmed to adopt an *Ia* 3¯-structured cubic phase, free of impurities, with a uniform nanoscale distribution of all the constituent elements. For the first time, we systematically evaluated the plasma etching resistance of the fabricated high-density, high-entropy ceramics in a challenging inductively coupled plasma-reactive ion etching (ICP-RIE) environment, utilizing a CF4 : Ar : O2 gas mixture. Notably, the (Y0.4Yb0.4Gd0.4Dy0.4Er0.4)O3 composition sintered at 1550 °C exhibited exceptional resistance, with an etch depth of only 8 nm after 1 h of exposure, outperforming conventional plasma-resistant ceramics such as Y2O3. The minimal change in surface roughness highlights the potential of the material to reduce contaminant generation, which is a critical factor in improving the wafer production yield during semiconductor etching processes. The significant lattice distortion in the high-entropy ceramics inhibited the penetration of fluorine into the matrix. Furthermore, the dense microstructure of the ceramics, devoid of residual pores, facilitated excellent visible and near-infrared light transmittance. This study underscores the potential of high-entropy oxides to enhance the tunability of their optical properties, which can be attributed to the diverse energy levels of their constituent elements. The findings of this study suggest that high-entropy oxides are promising candidates for use in transparent plasma-resistant windows and next-generation active optical ceramics, including lasers, phosphors, and scintillators.
