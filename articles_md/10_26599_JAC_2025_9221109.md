## 1 Introduction

Alumina is widely used as an oxidant barrier in aeroengines and gas turbines at elevated temperatures [1,2]. Preventing the transport of oxidants through alumina at high temperatures can significantly increase the durability of the underlying aluminum-containing superalloy components. In polycrystalline alumina, grain boundary (Gb) plays a crucial role in oxidant diffusion [3], serving as rapid diffusion pathways [4−6]. If the diffusion of oxygen at the alumina grain boundaries can be effectively hindered, the oxidation resistance and durability of alumina can be substantially improved. However, numerous factors influence grain boundary diffusion via complex mechanisms [7,8], and the relative significance of these factors remains unclear, resulting in less than optimal experimental outcomes regarding grain boundary regulation.

Researchers have invested significant effort in developing computational strategies to control the diffusion of alumina grain boundaries. First-principles computational methods, particularly those based on density functional theory (DFT), have been widely used to study the mean square displacement (MSD) and diffusion coefficients related to alumina grain boundary diffusion [9−13]. For example, Futazuka *et al*. [14] established a correlation between the Σ13 tilt grain boundary and diffusion rates using scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) technology combined with DFT calculations. Yokoi *et al*. [15], on the basis of STEM imaging, compared two computational methods, artificial neural networks (ANNs) and DFT, further validating the accuracy of simulation calculations in predicting properties related to Al2O3 grain boundaries. In terms of experimental techniques, electron backscatter diffractometer (EBSD)-guided grain boundary design [16] and laser-induced periodic interface structuring [17] have become effective means for detecting and regulating the orientation arrangement in polycrystalline alumina. The diffusion rates of alumina grain boundaries with various orientations vary significantly [18−20]. It was reported that the [0001] orientation of alumina can serve as a favorable crystal direction to inhibit grain boundary diffusion [21]. However, the abovementioned grain boundaries composed of the advantageous crystal direction [0001] in alumina are obtained under different and inconsistent conditions [15,22,23], indicating a lack of comparative analysis of the alumina grain boundary diffusion rates under unified conditions. This methodological inconsistency has created fundamental barriers to establishing reliable structure–property relationships, significantly impeding the rational design of oxidation-resistant alumina microstructures.

To address these critical issues, we propose a comprehensive computational strategy that involves the construction of multiple alumina grain boundaries [24], enabling direct comparisons of diffusion behaviors under unified conditions. The study initially screened for dominant crystal planes with low diffusion rates by combining various crystal planes with the preferred crystal orientation of alumina[0001]. We discovered that grain boundaries associated with the (001) and (110) planes were expected to exhibit low oxygen permeability. Consequently, we constructed the Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) grain boundary families and reported that their diffusion rates were 2–4 orders of magnitude lower than those of conventional grain boundaries. We subsequently summarized the factors influencing grain boundary diffusion and analyzed the reasons for the low diffusion rates of the Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) grain boundary families. Furthermore, we derived the influence weights of each factor on the regulation of grain boundary diffusion. Our findings indicate that, in addition to the grain boundary angle, the average bond length and band gap also play primary roles in impeding oxidant transport. Finally, the [0001]-oriented alumina block was prepared via step sintering; the selective nucleation of alumina was realized; the accuracy of the model and calculation results were verified. This study presents the first statistical analysis on the influence weights of factors affecting grain boundary diffusion under unified conditions, highlights directions for designing grain boundaries with low oxidant permeation, and provides innovative ideas for enhancing the oxidation resistance of aluminum-containing superalloys.

### 2.1 Simulation and calculation

To investigate the diffusion characteristics of the oxidation of Al2O3 grain boundaries, on the basis of DFT, 19 grain boundary models and 12 crystal faces were built via Materials Studio software. For quantitative screening, twelve parameters were systematically calculated via the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP): grain boundary angle (±0.1° resolution), thickness (Å), atomic distance (Å), bond length (Å), band gap (eV), charge density difference (e/Å³), grain boundary energy (J/m²), surface energy (J/m²), diffusion activation energy (eV), diffusion rate (m²/s), and total density of states (from −50 to 50 eV). The calculated differential charge density was visualized via Visualization for Electronic and Structural Analysis (VESTA) software, and the atomic spacing and grain boundary width are measured directly via VESTA’s length tool.

The electronic exchange–correlation interaction of all the structures was handled with a projector augmented wave (PAW) and general gradient approximation (GGA) via the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) pseudopotential. The plane wave function cutoff energy was set to 420 eV. For both transition state and dynamic simulations, geometry optimizations were performed via conjugate gradient minimization until all the forces acting on ions were less than 0.01 eV/Å per atom. The k-point was set to 2 × 2 × 1. The energy range for the density of states calculation was set from −50 to 50 eV. The diffusion rate calculation used the canonical ensemble (NVT), and the temperature control was set to 900–1600 °C via the Nosé–Hoover thermostat.

### 2.2 Sample preparation and characterization

To verify the Al2O3 grain boundaries, Al2O3 bulk samples were prepared through a two-step sintering process. Commercial alumina nanopowder (Qingdao Yubao Fine Chemical Co., Ltd., with a particle size of 40–100 nm) was used as the raw material. Hydraulic pressing at a pressure of 300 MPa was employed to form green bodies with a diameter of 20 mm and a thickness of 5 mm. Then, the Al2O3 bulk samples were prepared through a two-step sintering process in a commercial muffle furnace (SX-G02103, Tianjin Zhonghuan Furnace, China). The initial sintering was conducted at 1200 °C for 5 h, followed by a second stage at 1550 °C for 20 h, with heating and cooling rates of 2 and 1 °C/min, respectively.

The surface and cross-sectional microstructures of the composites were examined by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; VEGA II-XMU, TESCAN, Czech Republic). Moreover, the crystal orientations on the alumina surface were characterized via an EBSD (AZTEC, OXFORD INSTRUMENTS, UK) at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV, beam intensity of 15 mA, and tilt angle of 70°. In addition, the resolution of EBSD is ~10 nm. In addition, the scanning step and time step were 0.4 μm and 24.7 ms, respectively. The EBSD results were then processed via CHANNEL 5 software (HKL Technology, Inc., UK). Noise reduction was carried out with level 5 noise. The atomic structures were investigated through a scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM; ARM200F, JEOL, Japan, operating at 200 kV). The TEM samples were prepared via a focused ion beam system (FIB; NX5000, Hitachi, Japan). The amorphous layers generated during FIB preparation were removed via a nanomill (M1040 Mill, Fischione, USA) with argon ions (Ar+) as the cleaning agent at an ion energy of 700 eV and a milling angle of 30°. The faces of the samples were milled for 30 min.

### 3.1 Finding low-oxygen diffusion crystal planes

To find crystal planes with a low diffusion advantage for grain boundaries, the low-energy stable grain boundary direction [0001] was fixed [23], and grain boundary models were constructed for Gb(xxx)/[0001]. Specifically, we selected 8 of the 12 crystal faces with lower surface energies (Fig. 1(a)) and built (104)/[0001], (110)/[0001], (210)/[0001], (230)/[0001], (310)/[0001], (410)/[0001], (450)/[0001], and (001)/[0001] models. The grain boundary energy, band gap, diffusion rate, and diffusion activation energy were calculated on the basis of these models via first-principles calculations.

Figure 1(b) shows that the predicted grain boundary energies of Gb(410)/[0001] and Gb(450)/[0001] are 1.85 and 1.87 J/m², respectively. The energy is consistent with the reported value of 1.85 J/m² for the same grain boundaries [15], indicating the effectiveness of the current model. Among all the combinations, Gb(001)/[0001] and Gb(110)/[0001] have significantly lower grain boundary energies, indicating lower grain boundary diffusion rates. This is further supported by their greater peaks at the Fermi (0 eV) energy level (Fig. 1(c)). Figure 1(d) shows the band gap of Gb(xxx)/[0001]. Previous studies have confirmed that the size of the band gap is related to the coordination number [25,26]. A larger band gap indicates a greater coordination number of Al and O at the grain boundary, resulting in slower diffusion [27,28]. Compared with the measured Al2O3 bulk bandgap (8.8 eV) [29], the bandgap at Gb(xxx)/[0001] is significantly lower, confirming more rapid diffusion at the grain boundaries [30,31]. Among all the combinations, the Gb(001)/[0001] and Gb(110)/[0001] samples present greater band gaps, indicating lower permeability, which is consistent with their lower grain boundary energies (Fig. 1(b)) and greater density of states (DOS) (Fig. 1(c)).

Figure 1(e) presents the diffusion activation energies of Gb(xxx)/[0001]. A greater activation energy indicates a slower grain boundary diffusion coefficient. The predicted values are consistent with the reported energies, which range from 4 to 8 eV [29], further confirming the effectiveness of the current model. Among all the combinations, Gb(001)/[0001] and Gb(110)/[0001] exhibit the greatest diffusion activation energies of 5.53 and 5.56 eV, respectively. These results confirm that Gb(001)/[0001] and Gb(110)/[0001] present the lowest diffusion trends, which is consistent with their lowest grain boundary energies (Fig. 1(b)), greatest DOS (Fig. 1(c)), and greatest band gaps (Fig. 1(d)). Figure 1(f) shows the predicted diffusion rates of Gb(xxx)/[0001]. At temperatures ranging from 1400 to 1500 °C, the diffusion rates of Gb(210)/[0001], Gb(230)/[0001], Gb(310)/[0001], Gb(410)/[0001], and Gb(450)/[0001] are comparable to the reported values of Gb{2 3¯10}/[0001] and Gb{4 5¯10}/[0001] [32]. As anticipated, compared with all other combinations, Gb(001)/[0001] and Gb(110)/[0001] demonstrated significantly lower diffusion rates. These results confirm that the formation of Gb(001)/[0001] and Gb(110)/[0001] is particularly advantageous for impeding oxidant permeation in Al2O3.

### 3.2 Grain boundary diffusion rates for (001) and (110) planes

On the basis of the searched low-diffusion (001) and (110) surfaces, we built multiple combinations of the Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) models. These models include Gb(110)/(001), Gb(104)/(001), Gb(210)/(001), Gb(230)/(001), Gb(310)/(001), Gb(450)/(001) and Gb(001)/(110), Gb(104)/(110), Gb(210)/(110), Gb(230)/(110), Gb(310)/(110), and Gb(450)/(110). To further verify that Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) are low-oxygen-diffusion grain boundaries, their diffusion rates were calculated.

Figure 2 compares the diffusion rates of Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) with the reported values. At Gb(xxx)/(001), the diffusion rates from fast to slow are (210) > (104) > (310) > (230) > (450) > (110). At Gb(xxx)/(110), the diffusion rates from fast to slow are (310) > (210) > (230) > (450) > (001) > (104). These results indicate that achieving precise control over grain boundaries such as Gb(110)/(001) and Gb(104)/(110) is expected to significantly impede oxidant permeation in Al2O3. Additionally, Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) demonstrate significantly lower oxygen diffusion rates than the reported values [33−44]. At 1500 °C, the reported rate at its lowest value ranges from 10−25 to 10−21 m³/s [35,41−43]. In contrast, the diffusion rates of Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) range from 10−29 to 10−23 m³/s at this temperature, which are 2–4 orders of magnitude lower than the reported values. Among all the combinations, Gb(104)/(110) results in the lowest diffusion rate (10−29 m³/s), and its equivalent oxygen barrier effectiveness is 4 orders of magnitude greater than that of the reported structure. This finding highlights that preferentially forming Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110), especially Gb(001)/(110) and Gb(104)/(110), can significantly reduce the diffusion of oxygen in Al2O3.

### 3.3 Factors affecting grain boundary permeation

Grain boundary diffusion is influenced by many factors, including grain boundary angle, average bond length, grain boundary width, atomic distance, grain boundary energy, defect volume, band gap, diffusion activation energy, charge density, and DOS. However, the relative importance of these factors for grain boundary diffusion remains unclear. For Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110), which have ultralow oxygen diffusion rates (Fig. 2), further research on these factors was conducted to verify the mechanism for hindering oxidant permeation.

#### 3.3.1 Grain boundary structure

Figures 3 and 4 show the structures of Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) with various grain boundary angles and widths. A lower boundary width and angle indicate a lower diffusion rate [45]. In this study, the grain boundary widths of Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) are approximately 4–6 Å, which is approximately 3–4 atomic layers thick. These values are consistent with reported data of 5–10 Å for Al2O3 [46], confirming the effectiveness of the current model. Additionally, the atoms at the (001) and (110) planes are parallel to each other, resulting in a grain boundary angle of 0° between these two crystal planes (Figs. 3 and 4), indicating low diffusion defects and rates. In Gb(xxx)/(001) (Fig. 3), the grain boundary width from large to small is (210) > (104) > (310) > (230) > (450) > (110), which positively correlates with the diffusion rates presented in Fig. 2. Similarly, in Gb(xxx)/(110) (Fig. 4), the grain boundary thickness from large to small is (310) > (210) > (230) > (450) > (001) > (104), which is consistent with the diffusion rates shown in Fig. 2. These results indicate that minimizing the grain boundary width can significantly reduce the diffusion rate.

Small-angle grain boundary regulation is the main scheme used to reduce the alumina grain boundary diffusion rate [47−49]. In Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) (Figs. 3 and 4), the grain boundary angle is ordered as (310) > (210) > (230) > (450) > (110) = (104). Notably, Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) have the same grain boundary angles. However, the diffusion rates of Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) at the same angles are quite different (Fig. 2). These findings indicate that the influence of the grain boundary angle on the diffusion rate may also be constrained by other factors. Therefore, we evaluate the influence weights of the factors affecting the grain boundary diffusion rate in Section 3.3.3.

To reveal the effect of bonding length on diffusion, we compared the local bonding environment along the O cleavage paths at Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110), as shown in Figs. 5 and 6. Each unit contains 2–4 atomic layers, and the Al–O bond lengths in the Al2O3 bulk region without distortion are 1.87 and 1.99 Å (Fig. 5(a)), which are well consistent with the reported values of 1.88 and 1.97 Å [50]. Except for Gb(410)/(001), the average Al–O bond lengths of Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) are significantly shorter than those of the bulk sample (Fig. 5(a)). Among these, the average Al–O bond length at the Gb(110)/(001) interface is 1.64 Å (Fig. 5(b)), whereas that at the Gb(104)/(110) interface is 1.42 Å (Fig. 6(b)), representing reductions of 12.8% and 24.5%, respectively, compared with the bulk Al–O bond length. The shortened Al–O bonds at these grain boundaries create a significant energy barrier for oxygen diffusion, thereby establishing Gb(110)/(001) and Gb(104)/(110) as low-diffusivity grain boundary configurations.

Since the grain boundary angles of Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110) are consistent across different orientations (Figs. 3 and 4), the influence weights of the two grain boundary groups are the same under the influence of the grain boundary angle. These results imply that in addition to the grain boundary angle, the bond length and band gap also play important roles in reducing grain boundary permeation. This is the first study to calculate the weight of factors affecting grain boundary diffusion under unified conditions.

### 3.4 Experimental verification of Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub> grain boundaries

The Al2O3 bulk samples were fabricated through two-step sintering at 1200 and 1550 °C, with the sintered surfaces exhibiting high flatness and being free of discernible porosity (Fig. 13(a)). The two-stage sintering process was specifically designed to obtain low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs, 2°–15° misorientation) through controlled grain growth kinetics. This method utilizes the relationship between crystal growth and temperature. After the alumina powder is pressed into pellets, they are sintered at 1200 °C for 5 h to achieve uniform nucleation of grains. The temperature was subsequently slowly increased to 1550 °C at a heating rate of 1 °C/min and held for 20 h, allowing sufficient time for grain growth. Moreover, high-temperature sintering promotes coarsening of the grains to reduce the grain boundary density.

## 4 Conclusions

Alumina (Al2O3) is extensively utilized as an oxidant barrier in aeroengines and gas turbines at elevated temperatures, and small-angle grain boundaries significantly reduce its oxygen permeation. In this study, calculations of the various grain boundaries related to the (001) and (110) planes were performed to obtain ultralow oxygen diffusion. The main conclusions are as follows:

1) Preferentially forming Gb(xxx)/(001) and Gb(xxx)/(110), especially Gb(001)/(110) and Gb(104)/(110), can significantly reduce the diffusion of oxygen in Al2O3. The oxygen diffusion coefficient of Gb(104)/(110) at 1500 °C (10−29 m3/s) is 6 orders of magnitude lower than that of the large-angle boundary.

2) The low diffusion rates of Gb(104)/(110) and Gb(110)/(001) are attributed primarily to their small grain boundary angles, low bond lengths, and high bandgaps.

3) The weights of the factors positively affecting grain boundary diffusion are ranked as follows: grain boundary angle > average bond length > grain boundary width > atomic distance > grain boundary energy > defect volume. The weights of the negative factors are ordered as follows: band gap > diffusion activation energy > charge density > total density of states.

4) Gb(104)/(110) was successfully obtained from Al2O3 samples prepared via a two-step sintering process, confirming the effectiveness of the current prediction.
