## 1 Introduction

As a fundamental pillar for advancing global strategic technologies and high-end equipment, the performance of aerospace structural materials is critical to ensuring the long-term service reliability of aircraft and vehicles operating in extreme environments—characterized by ultra-high temperatures, severe oxidation, high irradiation, and reciprocating friction. Building on traditional ultra-high temperature ceramic structural materials, we developed a series of novel transition metal non-oxide high-entropy ultra-high temperature ceramics by leveraging the “high-entropy” material design concept from the alloy field [1−4]. Among these innovations, high-entropy boride (HEB) ceramics exhibit remarkable properties, including high strength (> 20 GPa), ultra-high melting point (> 3000 °C), excellent oxidation resistance, and tunable tribological characteristics enabled by their unique multi-component design strategy [2,5,6]. These characteristics render them promising candidates for next-generation ultra-high temperature structural materials.

Previous research has demonstrated that the fracture toughness of HEB ceramics is generally low, typically ranging between 2 and 4 MPa·m1/2 [7−9], which severely limits their practical applications. Thus, enhancing the fracture toughness of HEB ceramics represents a critical challenge that demands urgent attention. To address the issue of low fracture toughness in HEB ceramics, researchers both domestically and internationally have conducted extensive investigations and achieved significant results. These studies encompass various strategies, such as grain refinement and toughening, increasing the volume fraction of grain boundaries, thereby improving the fracture toughness by leveraging the hindering effect of these boundaries on crack propagation [9,10]. Additionally, second-phase dispersion toughening involves the introduction of secondary phases such as SiC [11,12], HEC [13], Cr [14], Ni [15], and Co [16] to form a dispersion phase, thereby increasing the fracture toughness. Unlike particle toughening approaches, incorporating a toughening phase with a high aspect ratio can increase the material defect tolerance and effectively increase the resistance to crack propagation, yielding superior toughening effects. Moreover, it is often more convenient to directly substitute SiC particles with SiC fibers, whiskers [17], or other materials such as graphite [18], Ta fibers [19], or carbon nanotubes [20]. Although introducing a second phase can promote mechanisms such as crack bridging and crack deflection at their interfaces to improve performance, this approach still has limitations. Consequently, despite these advancements in methodology and material design efforts aimed at improving performance characteristics, the overall fracture toughness is still restricted to the range of 4–8 MPa·m1/2, making substantial improvements challenging to achieve. In addition to the traditional idea of component regulation, the spatial distribution of the toughening phase, such as the texture structure [21], fiber monolith structure [22], core-shell structure [23,24], and bionic layered structure [25,26], has been optimized. Among them, the weak interface layer is introduced into the layered structure, such as boron nitride (BN) and Ta foil, to achieve multi-scale synergistic strengthening so that the fracture toughness of the material is greatly improved, exceeding 12 MPa·m1/2. Both theoretical and practical investigations have demonstrated that high-aspect-ratio toughening phases, when combined with optimized toughening and structural design strategies, can achieve superior improvements in the fracture toughness. By adopting a synergistic toughening mechanism, a second phase with a high aspect ratio is incorporated as the toughening agent, and its spatial distribution is further optimized according to structural design principles (such as directional arrangement, layered composites, etc.). This synergistic strengthening mechanism can be realized through crack deflection, crack bridging, and stress transfer at phase interfaces, which significantly enhances the fracture toughness of HEB ceramics. The comprehensive strengthening effect significantly surpasses that of any single toughening strategy.

In addition, previous research has shown that the tribological properties of HEB ceramics demonstrate a pronounced temperature dependency. HEB ceramics exhibit excellent wear resistance at room temperature, with the wear rate maintaining a stable order of magnitude below 10−6 mm3/(N·m) [27−30]. However, when the temperature increases above 800 °C, the wear rate increases to the order of 10−5 mm3/(N·m) [31−33]. The current research temperature for the tribological properties of HEB ceramics is generally lower than 1000 °C; however, there are few studies on their high-temperature tribological properties, and their performance needs to be improved. Therefore, it is urgent to study the high-temperature tribological properties of HEB ceramics and realize performance regulation through a self-lubricating design [34,35]. At high temperatures, friction energy consumption and wear damage are inhibited by the in situ oxidation tribofilm and lubrication phase at the same time, and the high-temperature tribological properties of HEB ceramics are effectively improved when the high-temperature tribological properties of HEB ceramics are explored [36].

Hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) has a unique toughness enhancement mechanism for fracture behavior, which is attributed to its two-dimensional layered structure and high aspect ratio. The elastic response at the crack tip and geometric asymmetry induce crack deflection and branched propagation, and ductile fracture mode transformation is realized by stress redistribution [37−39]. Notably, this material overcomes the limitations of traditional uniaxial strengthening systems and achieves multi-dimensional strengthening and toughening through the synergistic effect of high-aspect-ratio structure toughening and lamellar design toughening. The weak van der Waals interaction between h-BN layers endows excellent shear slip ability [40,41]. In combination with its excellent high-temperature stability (oxidation resistance temperature > 1000 °C [42]) and multi-environment adaptability, h-BN demonstrates superior tribological properties in both oxidizing/humid environments and vacuum conditions [43,44]. In the composite ceramic system, h-BN can effectively reduce the friction coefficient through the interface slip mechanism and achieve long-term wear protection via *in situ* formation of a continuous tribofilm, which significantly improves the self-lubricating properties of the material [45]. In summary, h-BN provides a new idea for improving the fracture toughness and high-temperature tribological properties of HEB ceramics through a microscopic toughening mechanism and macroscopic lubrication function, providing theoretical support for exploring the application of HEB ceramics in high-temperature friction environments.

Therefore, h-BN is uniformly blended with (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2 powder to serve as a toughening and lubricating phase. Thereafter, high-entropy (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2-hBN composite ceramics are fabricated via spark plasma sintering (SPS). The influence of varying h-BN content on the phase composition, microstructure, mechanical properties, and high-temperature tribological behavior of these composite ceramics is investigated, and differences in their tribological performance at elevated temperatures ranging from 1000 to 1200 °C. Furthermore, how the h-BN content affects both fracture processes and crack propagation modes within composite ceramics is explored to clarify the fracture mechanisms and toughening strategies. The impact of the h-BN content on the high-temperature tribological properties is also elucidated, together with an analysis of the wear mechanisms, to reveal the underlying lubrication principles.

### 2.1 Material preparation

The raw materials used in this study included TiO2 powder (~0.3 μm, purity ≥ 99.90%, Qinhuangdao ENO High-Tech Material Development Co., Ltd., China), ZrO2 powder (1–3 μm, purity ≥ 99.90%, Qinhuangdao ENO High-Tech Material Development Co., Ltd., China), HfO2 powder (1–3 μm, purity ≥ 99.95%, Beijing Fangdexing Technology Co., Ltd., China), and Nb2O5 powder (5–10 μm, purity ≥ 99.90%, Qinhuangdao ENO High-Tech Material Development Co., Ltd., China). Ta2O5 powder (~0.5 μm, purity ≥ 99.90%, Shanghai Naio Nanotechnology Co., Ltd., China), B4C powder (1–3 μm, purity ≥ 99.90%, Shanghai Naio Nanotechnology Co., Ltd., China), C powder (~2.0 μm, purity ≥ 99.50%, HighChem (ShangHai) International Trading Co., Ltd., China), and h-BN (diameter 1 μm, thickness 30–40 nm, lamellar, purity ≥ 99.90%, Beijing Huawei Ruike Chemical Co., Ltd., China).

In this study, high-entropy (Ti0.2Hf0.2Zr0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2 ceramic powders are prepared via the boron/carbon thermal reduction method of metal oxides such as TiO2, HfO2, ZrO2, Nb2O5, and Ta2O5 with B4C and the C boron/carbon thermal reduction method in a vacuum furnace (ZT-50-20Y, Shanghai Chenhua, China), which was completed in previous work [19]. The high-entropy (Ti0.2Hf0.2Zr0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2 ceramic and h-BN powder were dispersed via ultrasonication (SB-5200D, Ningbo Scientz, China) and subsequently mixed with anhydrous ethanol in a planetary ball mill (BQM-1 L, Changsha Mitr, China) at a speed of 200 r/min for 10 h to prepare a high-entropy (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2-hBN composite ceramic mixed powder.

The h-BN is uniformly mixed with HEB powder at total volume fractions of 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 vol% and pressed into a circular HEB-hBN composite ceramic green body with a diameter of *φ*25 mm. It is placed in a graphite mold and sintered in an SPS furnace (RP-S, Shanghai Chenhua, China) at 2000 °C and 30 MPa for 10 min to prepare HEB-hBN composite ceramics.

### 2.2 Characterization

The phase components of the composite ceramics are characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; EMPYREAN, PANalytical, the Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA). The microstructure of the composite ceramics was characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; EVO18, Carl Zeiss, UK) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200, Thermo Scientific, USA), both of which are equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDS INCA X-MAX, Oxford, UK) for element mapping. The phase composition of the worn surface of the composite ceramics after friction testing was analyzed via a high-resolution X-ray diffractometer (HRXRD; D8 DISCOVER, Bruker, Germany). The wear morphology on the surface of the composite ceramics after the friction test was examined via a super-depth-of-field three-dimensional surface profiler (VHX-6000, Keyence, Japan), from which the wear volume was calculated. X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific, USA) was employed to analyze the surface elements and phase composition of the wear scars on the composite ceramics after friction. Additionally, Imade-Pro Plus 6.0 software facilitated measurements of the grain size and thicknesses of the tribofilms and oxide layers in multiphase ceramic materials. MDI Jade 9 software was used to analyze the XRD data, and Avantage 6.6.0 software was used to interpret the XPS data. Origin 2022 software was used for graphical plotting purposes.

The open porosity and density of the composite ceramics were tested and calculated via the Archimedes drainage method.

The formula for calculating the open porosity of the composite ceramics is shown in Eq. (1):

(1) πa=m3−m1m3−m2×100%

where πa is the open porosity of the composite ceramics (%), *m*1, *m*2, and *m*3 are the dry, floating, and wet weights of the composite ceramics (g), respectively.

The formula for calculating the actual density of the composite ceramics is shown in Eq. (2):

(2) ρb=m1m3−m2×ρl

where * ρb is the bulk density (g/cm3), and  ρl is the density (g/cm3) of the liquid immersed at the test temperature.

The formula for calculating the theoretical density of the composite ceramics is shown in Eq. (3):

(3) ρt=∑ρi×Vi

where  ρt is the theoretical density (g/cm3) of the composite ceramics,  ρi is the theoretical density (g/cm3) of a single component in the composite ceramics, and  Vi is the volume fraction of a single component in the composite ceramics (vol%).

The formula for calculating the density of the composite ceramics is shown in Eq. (4):

(4) ρr=ρbρt×100%

where  ρr is the density of the composite ceramics (%).

Vickers hardness tester (HV-50, Optical Instrument No.5 Factory, China) was used to determine the hardness and fracture toughness of the composite ceramics. A load of 49 N was selected for the measurement of the Vickers hardness, which was maintained for 15 s and then unloaded. The indentation and crack lengths were then measured. The calculation formula for the Vickers hardness of the composite ceramics is shown in Eq. (5):

(5) HV=2Fsin⁡(136∘2)d2×103=1.8544×Fd2×103

where H*V is the Vickers hardness (GPa), *F* is the magnitude of the test force (N), and *d* is the average value of the indentation diagonal length (μm).

The calculation formula for the fracture toughness of the composite ceramics is shown in Eq. (6):

(6) KIC=F(π(C1+C24×106))−32(tan⁡(136∘2)−1)×10−6

where *K*IC is the fracture toughness (MPa·m1/2), *F* is the magnitude of the test force (N), *C*1 and *C*2 are the diagonal crack lengths (μm).

The tribological properties of the composite ceramics were tested via a high-temperature reciprocating friction tester (GF-1200, Zhongke Kaihua, China). The test conditions were as follows: the composite ceramic was used as the lower sample, whereas the upper sample of the friction pair was an Al2O3 (*φ*6.0 mm) ceramic ball [46]. The test temperatures were 1000, 1100, and 1200 °C, and the test method was contact reciprocating motion. The load applied was 10 N, sliding speed was 50 mm/s, and test duration was 30 min. The surface roughness of the composite ceramics must be less than 0.1 μm. To ensure the accuracy of the results, the test data for each group is repeated at least three times.

The wear rate of the composite ceramics can be calculated as shown in Eq. (7):

(7) W=ΔVPL

where *W* is the wear rate (mm3/(N∙m)), ∆*V* is the wear volume (mm3), *P* is the applied load (N), and *L* is the sliding distance (m).

### 3.1 Effect of h-BN content on preparation of composite ceramics

Figure 1 shows the SEM images of HEB-hBN composite ceramic mixed powder with different h-BN contents and the corresponding enlarged EDS images at 20 vol%. The diagram shows that the HEB powder remains irregularly granular and that the h-BN powder remains irregularly lamellar. Both show a uniform spatial distribution in the mixed system, and there is no agglomeration or structural damage to the h-BN, indicating that ultrasonic dispersion combined with the mechanical mixing process can effectively achieve a uniform composite of the HEB and h-BN powder. The h-BN observed in Figs. 1(b)–1(e) gradually increases, and h-BN maintains a good two-dimensional layered morphology, which is consistent with the design concept. The corresponding enlarged EDS image in Fig. 1(e) shows that the elements Ti, Zr, Hf, Nb, and Ta are evenly distributed and that there is no obvious element segregation, indicating that the high-entropy HEB powder is a good solid solution. However, the corresponding N signals at the white lamellar h-BN are limited by the detection sensitivity of light elements, and the EDS probes are difficult to capture, so they are not obvious via EDS. Studies have confirmed that HEB-hBN composite ceramic mixed powder with controllable h-BN contents and uniform dispersions was successfully constructed in this study.

Studies have confirmed that HEB and h-BN coexist stably in the form of a single phase in the composite ceramics and that they have good chemical compatibility.

Figure 3 shows the SEM images of HEB-hBN composite ceramics with different h-BN contents and the EDS images corresponding to the EPMA images at 20 vol%. The figure shows that before the addition of h-BN, the HEB ceramics exhibit single-phase characteristics, forming a dense solid solution with obvious pores. The surface flatness of the composite ceramics significantly improved with the incorporation of h-BN, whereas the density of the composite ceramics is effectively enhanced with decreasing pore size. With increasing h-BN content, the observed black h-BN phase shows a gradient increase, as the two-dimensional lamellar structure shows a uniform dispersion distribution in the matrix. After sintering, h-BN does not undergo obvious deformation and still maintains the original irregular lamellar morphology, which proves that its structural stability is excellent. The EDS images corresponding to the EPMA in Fig. 3(f) show that the elements Ti, Zr, Hf, Nb, and Ta are evenly distributed, and there is no obvious element segregation, indicating that there is no significant interfacial reaction between the HEB matrix and h-BN during the sintering process and that the matrix structure remains intact. The enrichment of N in the black lamellar phase confirms that the black phase was h-BN. Studies have confirmed that HEB-hBN composite ceramics with a uniform and dense structure can be successfully prepared via the mechanical mixing of HEB and h-BN powders combined with the sintering process, which effectively realizes the quantitative regulation and uniform dispersion of the h-BN second phase.

Figure 4 shows the TEM analysis of HEB-hBN composite ceramics. Through the selected area electron diffraction analysis in Figs. 4(a) and 4(c), the fast Fourier transform (FFT) images of the HEB phase and h-BN phase show symmetrical and sharp diffraction spots, confirming that both phases have long-range ordered crystal structures. The high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images in Figs. 4(b) and 4(d) further reveal the periodic lattice arrangement characteristics of the two components: the unit cell parameters (a* = *b* = 0.311 nm, *c* = 0.337 nm) of the HEB phase are completely consistent with the XRD test results, showing a typical simple hexagonal structure; the measured cell parameters of the h-BN phase (*a* = *b* = 0.250 nm, *c* = 0.664 nm) are highly consistent with the XRD data of standard h-BN (*a* = *b* = 0.250 nm, *c* = 0.666 nm), showing a hexagonal layered structure. Notably, the 0.210 nm interplanar spacing observed in the HEB phase is highly matched with the theoretical value (2.10 Å) of the hexagonal (101) crystal plane, and the characteristic lattice fringes of 0.332 nm in the h-BN phase are accurately matched with the theoretical spacing (3.33 Å) of the (002) crystal plane, which provides dual experimental evidence of cell parameters and interplanar spacing for the structural confirmation of the two phases. The BF image in Fig. 4(e) shows that the h-BN phase (bright region) is uniformly dispersed in the HEB matrix (dark region). The high-angle annular dark field image (HAADF) in Fig. 4(f) and the corresponding element surface distribution analysis show that the high-atomic-number elements, such as Ti, Zr, Hf, Nb, and Ta, are evenly distributed in the matrix phase, and no element segregation is observed, which confirms that the region is an HEB single phase formed from a solid solution. The N element is significantly enriched in the second phase region, and combined with the crystal structure analysis results, it is confirmed to be the h-BN phase. Studies have confirmed that both the HEB and h-BN phases in HEB-hBN composite ceramics maintain their complete hexagonal crystal structures. The introduction of h-BN did not affect the crystal structure of the HEB matrix, and the two phases formed a stable nanoscale composite structure.

### 3.2 Effects of h-BN content on mechanical properties of composite ceramics

Table 1 shows the open porosities and densities of HEB-hBN multiphase ceramics with various h-BN contents. It can be observed from the table that with increasing h-BN content, the grain size of the composite ceramics first decreases but then increases. When the h-BN content is 10 vol%, the grain size is the smallest, and the value is 1.90 μm, which indicates that the addition of h-BN can refine the grain size of HEB ceramics. The open porosities of the composite ceramics with different h-BN contents are less than 0.50%. Before the addition of h-BN, the density of HEB ceramics was only 93.77%, and the density of the composite ceramics significantly improved with the incorporation of h-BN. When the content of h-BN is 15 vol%, the density is the highest at 97.20%, which is nearly 3.50% greater than that without h-BN. The aforementioned phenomenon is consistent with the uniform and dense surface appearance shown in the SEM image in Fig. 3. The density of the composite ceramics gradually decreases with increasing h-BN content, which is similar to the trend predicted by the rule of mixing [26]. When the content of h-BN is greater than 15 vol%, the lamellar structure forms a “platelet-chamber” structure in the matrix [47], which leads to the obstruction of grain movement and a decrease in sintering densification power, resulting in an increase in porosity and a decrease in density. Studies have confirmed that the addition of an appropriate amount of h-BN (≤ 15 vol%) can refine the grain size by optimizing the particle distribution to promote densification, whereas excessive addition inhibits the sintering process due to structural heterogeneity.

Moreover, its low hardness characteristics lead to a decrease in matrix hardness, which synergistically reduces fracture toughness. Studies have confirmed that synergistic improvement in the mechanical properties of HEB-based composite ceramics can be effectively achieved by adjusting the content of h-BN.

### 3.3 Effect of h-BN content on the tribological properties of the composite ceramics at 1000 °C

Figure 8 shows the tribological properties of HEB-hBN composite ceramics with different h-BN contents at 1000 °C. The diagram shows that the friction coefficients of the composite ceramics with different h-BN contents fluctuate between 0.15 and 0.35. The friction coefficient is low, and the fluctuation is stable. Before the addition of h-BN, the friction coefficient of the ceramic increases, and the fluctuations are obvious. After the addition of h-BN, the friction coefficient of the composite ceramic decreases, and the fluctuation is more stable, which is related to the interlayer sliding caused by h-BN. With increasing h-BN content, the average friction coefficient and wear rate first decreased but then increased. When the h-BN content is 5 vol%, the average friction coefficient and wear rate are the smallest, and the average friction coefficient is 0.149±0.017. The layered structure of h-BN forms a continuous tribofilm at the friction interface by inducing an interlayer sliding mechanism, which is the main reason for the decrease in the friction coefficient and wear rate. Studies have confirmed that the friction coefficient and wear rate of HEB-hBN composite ceramics with different h-BN contents are low, and the high-temperature tribological properties are better. The addition of h-BN can effectively improve the high-temperature tribological properties of HEB-hBN composite ceramics.

The formation of an oxide film on the surface of the composite ceramics at a high temperature of 1000 °C leads to oxidative wear [51]. After the addition of h-BN, the surface integrity is improved by the lubrication effect, the wear surface becomes relatively smooth, and the high-temperature tribological performance is enhanced. However, with a further increase in the h-BN content, grain precipitation is aggravated due to the interphase inhibition of h-BN and the decrease in the hardness of the composite ceramics. Studies have confirmed that the wear mechanism of HEB-hBN composite ceramics is mainly abrasive and oxidative wear, and the addition of an appropriate amount of h-BN can effectively influence the lubrication performance and mechanical strength of the composite ceramics, significantly improving their wear resistance at high temperatures.

### 3.4 Effects of different friction temperatures on tribological properties of HEB-hBN composite ceramics with 5 vol% h-BN

The peak area of some metal oxides (such as ZrO2) gradually decreases; the content of oxides decreases, and the friction and lubrication effects decrease, corresponding to the increase in the friction coefficient in Fig. 12, whereas the peak area of B2O3 increases significantly, which indicates that with increasing temperature, the high-temperature tribological properties of the composite ceramics are more dependent on the friction and lubrication effects of B2O3. The in-depth XPS analysis further reveals that the surface lubrication mechanism of the composite ceramic under temperature gradient changes presents a dynamic evolution. The low-temperature range (1000 and 1100 °C) depends on the synergistic effect of h-BN, B2O3, and metal oxides, whereas B2O3 gradually becomes the dominant lubrication phase under high-temperature conditions (≥ 1200 °C). Studies have confirmed that the high-temperature tribological properties of HEB-hBN composite ceramics mainly depend on the synergistic lubrication effect of h-BN, B2O3, and various metal oxides. The lubrication contribution of B2O3 has a significant temperature dependence, and its leading role becomes more significant with increasing temperature.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, h-BN is incorporated into high-entropy (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2 powder to serve as a toughening and lubricating phase, resulting in the development of high-entropy (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2-hBN composite ceramics. The influences of varying h-BN contents on the mechanical properties and high-temperature tribological performance of the composite ceramics are systematically investigated. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:

(1) An analysis of the preparation process and fracture toughness revealed that the density of the composite ceramics is the highest with the incorporation of 15 vol% h-BN, which is nearly 3.5% greater than that of the HEB ceramic without h-BN. Notably, when the h-BN content is set at 10 vol%, the fracture toughness reached its peak value of 4.50±0.20 MPa·m1/2, which is approximately 70% greater than that of the HEB ceramic without h-BN, indicating a significant toughening effect. The fracture mechanism involved a combination of numerous transgranular fractures alongside a limited number of intergranular fractures; toughening mechanisms such as crack deflection, crack bridging, and stress dissipation due to h-BN contributed to this increase.

(2) High-temperature tribological performance assessments indicated that the optimal tribological behavior at elevated temperatures (1000 °C) occurred with an h-BN content of 5 vol%. Under these conditions, the average friction coefficient is 0.149±0.017, which is approximately 40% lower than that of the HEB ceramic without h-BN, and it has a wear rate as low as (1.551±0.177)×10−5 mm3/(N·m), which is approximately 60% lower than that of the HEB ceramic without h-BN. The friction coefficient remained consistently below 0.300 within the temperature range from 1000 to 1200 °C, whereas the wear rates remained stable at approximately 10−5 mm3/(N·m). The predominant wear mechanisms are abrasive wear and oxidation wear, whereas effective high-temperature self-lubrication is achieved through synergistic lubrication interactions among h-BN, B2O3, and metal oxides.

(3) In the design of the material system, h-BN has successfully achieved multifunctional integration within composite ceramics. It serves as a toughening phase to increase fracture toughness through crack control mechanisms. In addition, this type of phase is able to facilitate self-lubricating functions as a solid lubricant. The B2O3 liquid phase generated *in situ* during high-temperature friction processes forms a solid‒liquid multiphase synergistic lubrication system in conjunction with h-BN and various metal oxides. This results in the construction of a gradient composite tribofilm alongside optimized interfaces. Consequently, the toughening and high-temperature self-lubricating properties of HEB-hBN composite ceramics have been realized.
