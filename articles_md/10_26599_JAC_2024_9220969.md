## 1 Introduction

Owing to their high specific strength and mechanical stability at high temperatures, ceramic matrix composites (CMCs) are considered the preferred material for turbine hot-section components for next-generation aeroengines with high thrust-to-weight ratios [1−4]. However, structural degeneration occurs when CMCs are exposed to high-temperature turbine corrosive environments containing water vapor, oxygen, and molten corrosion [4−9]. To ensure the application of CMCs, environmental barrier coatings (EBCs) with both thermal insulation and corrosion resistance play an important role [10−13].

The EBC system has evolved from Si/mullite/(1−*x*)BaO·*x*SrO·Al2O3·2SiO2 (BSAS) system to Si/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 system [5,11,12,14−16] owing to limitations in the former, such as mullite degradation by water vapor because of high silicon oxide (SiO2) reactivity [11,16], crystallization-induced cracks in metastable mullite due to volume changes accompanying the crystallization of residual amounts of metastable amorphous mullite formed during the coating preparation process when exposed to temperatures above 1000 °C [11], and the reduced adhesion above 1300 °C resulted from a low-melting glass phase formed by BSAS and thermally grown oxide (TGO) SiO2 [11,16]. Yb2Si2O7, which has a stable phase, lower SiO2 reactivity than mullite, and a closer coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) to that of CMC ((3.6–4.5)×10−6 K−1 for Yb2Si2O7, (4.5–5.5)×10−6 K−1 for CMC) [5,11,16,17]; and Yb2SiO5, which has superior water vapor resistance, chemical compatibility with TGO, and high phase stability [11,15,18], has become the preferred choice. However, directly using Yb2Si2O7 as an external EBC layer might cause decomposition because of prolonged exposure to water vapor (Yb2Si2O7 + 2H2O(g) → Yb2SiO5 + Si(OH)4(g)) [16], or employing Yb2SiO5 (with a CTE of ~7.2×10−6 K−1) directly could result in thermal cycling cracks due to that CTEs mismatch with CMC substrates [16]. Hence, the use of Yb2Si2O7 as an intermediate layer to alleviate CTE mismatches and Yb2SiO5 as a protective top layer against water vapor makes the Si/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 system the next-generation EBC system for higher-temperature applications [14,19].

In Si/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 system, pure Si acts as a bond to promote coating adhesion and generate a TGO layer that resists oxygen [20]. Unfortunately, TGO in the form of SiO2 cristobalite undergoes an α  ⇌[] β displacive phase transformation (~270 °C) during thermal cycling [21], causing a volume change (~2.8%) and a significant CTE difference (~9×10−6 K−1) between α- and β-cristobalite [22,23], leading to delamination at TGO/bond coating interface and premature failure of the external EBC. For example, (1) Si/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 coatings prepared on SiC surfaces showed significant spalling after 40 thermal cycles at 1350 °C [14]; (2) Si/mullite/Yb2SiO5 system exhibited severe transverse cracking at SiO2‒TGO interface after 250 h of steam cycling [21]; and (3) after 1300 °C thermal cycling, a similar phenomenon occurred in Si/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5/Y3Al5O12 system [12]. Moreover, the low melting point (~1414 °C) and brittle‒ductile transition (~627 °C) properties of Si [24] also limit the service temperature and lifespan of Si/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 system.

More recent attention has focused on the potential of HfO2-doped Si bond coating to overcome the aforementioned challenges [10,17,24−27]. Owing to the high melting point (~2780 °C) and phase stability (below 1700 °C) of HfO2, along with its ability to reduce SiO2 cristobalite content through a high-temperature reaction and form a stable, high-melting HfSiO4 phase, it offers significant improvements in the Si bond coating and holds promise for further elevating the operating temperature limit [10,24,25]. Additionally, the uniformly dispersed HfSiO4 phase, with an increase in molar volume during growth, not only aids in microcrack closure [28] but also has a toughening effect on Si–HfO2 bond coating [10,27]. These advantages allow the Si‒HfO2 bond to coat the preferred system to replace the Si bond coating in Si/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2Si2O7 system. Although research into single Si‒HfO2 bond coating is extensive, especially in terms of failure mechanisms during thermal cycling [10,25,26], the oxidation mechanism of Si‒HfO2 bond coating remains insufficiently understood [17,25]. Importantly, the potential changes in its oxidation kinetics model, including the forms of oxygen transport and activation energy, remain unclear when it is integrated with external Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 EBC layers. In addition, the thermal growth rate of Si–HfO2 bond coating at high temperatures and its impact on the stability of the entire Si–HfO2/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 tri-layer EBC require further exploration. In particular, under aeroengine conditions, where EBC faces challenges such as rapid heating and cooling, the thermomechanical compatibility between Si–HfO2 bond coating and adjacent layers and the effect of mixed thermally grown oxide (m-TGO, the overall Si–HfO2 bond coating composed of a mixture of phases including SiO2, HfSiO4, and HfO2 after high-temperature exposure) growth on adjacent interfaces need to be verified before it can be effectively applied in actual EBC systems. In addition, continuously initiated vertical mud cracks (narrow and deep fissures caused by thermal stress within Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 layers) during thermal cycling create rapid channels for turbine corrosive media [21,29], limiting the protective effect of the external Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 layers. Current research has identified only the initiation of vertical mud cracks, but the underlying mechanism of their lateral expansion at Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 interface [12,14] and the risk of eventual spalling remain unknown. Exploring these key scientific issues and countermeasures through destructive experimental methods, such as thermal cycling [14,30], is vital for improving performance stability and accelerating the practical implementation of promising new Si–HfO2/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 systems.

The oxidation kinetics model and m-TGO growth equation of Si–HfO2 bond coating in the case of external Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 layers are clarified by modifying the Arrhenius equation during thermal cycling tests at 1400, 1450, and 1500 °C in this work. Through calculations of the thermal mismatch stress, interfacial residual stress, and strain energy during thermal cycling, this study provides novel insights into the thermomechanical compatibility between Si–HfO2 and adjacent layers, as well as the impact of m-TGO growth on neighboring interfaces for the first time. Furthermore, this work refines the currently incomplete understanding of the evolution mechanism of vertical mud cracks through micro defect analysis and macro finite element simulations, offering improvement strategies for advanced EBC development.

### 2.1 Experimental procedure

Si–HfO2/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 EBC samples were deposited on the surface of 15 mm × 15 mm bulk SiCf/SiC substrates (surface roughness, *R*a = 5 μm) via plasma spraying physical vapor deposition (PS-PVD) method. This method is distinguished for its ability to codeposit diverse phases, which enhances the efficiency and quality of advanced coating applications [31−33]. Commercial Yb2O3 and SiO2 powders (99.99% purity) were mixed at 1:1 and 1:2 ratios, respectively, and subjected to wet milling (450 r/min, 7 h) in a ball mill equipped with zirconia balls. After drying, the mixtures were placed in a high-temperature furnace for a solid-state reaction (1400 °C, 2 h) to synthesize Yb2SiO5 and Yb2Si2O7 initial powders, respectively. Initial Si–HfO2 powders were produced by blending 15 mol% HfO2 (99.95% purity) with 85 mol% Si (99.99% purity), ball-milling at 500 r/min for 12 h, and then drying. Three initial powders were subsequently processed into feedstocks with good sphericity through the spray granulation process, and the process parameters are listed in Table 1. Finally, novel Si–HfO2/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 trilayer EBC was fabricated on the CMC substrate via PS-PVD process, with process parameters detailed in Ref. [34].

After deposition, thermal cycling tests were conducted on EBC samples via the following steps: (1) isothermal heating for 1 h in a furnace at test temperatures of 1400, 1450, and 1500 °C, respectively; and (2) rapid cooling in a stream of air to reach room temperature for 10 min. These temperatures were specifically chosen to challenge this EBC system under progressively harsher conditions.

The phase compositions of the powders were identified via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Cu Kα; D8-ADVANCE, Bruker, USA). Using XRD patterns, the phase fractions were determined through the Rietveld method implemented in the GSAS-II software. Crystallographic information files (CIFs) for Yb2Si2O7 (74780-ICSD), Yb2SiO5 (4446-ICSD), Si (43403-ICSD), and HfO2 (27313-ICSD) were utilized. Background fitting was performed with linear interpolation between designated background points, and the pseudo-Voigt function was applied to model the peak shapes. The reliability factors from the Rietveld refinement were all less than 10%, confirming that the structural refinement was reasonable. The micromorphology of the surfaces and cross-sections (prepared with a diamond wire cutting machine), as well as the elemental composition distributions of all the samples, were characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Scios 2 DualBeam, Thermo Scientific, USA) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy system. To measure the porosity of the coating surface, ImageJ was employed for digital image quantification on the basis of SEM images. In addition, ImageJ software was also used to measure the average thickness data of m-TGO at different thermal cycling test temperatures and durations, with measurements taken twelve times at equal intervals. The average thickness was calculated by excluding the maximum and minimum values. Figures S1–S3 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), which provide clearer visual evidence for these measurements, have been added to the ESM. Lamellar samples for detailed microstructure analysis were produced via a focused ion beam (FIB; Scios 2 DualBeam, Thermo Scientific, USA) and were observed by a scanning/transmission electron microscopy (SEM/TEM; Talos F200X, FEI, USA). Geometric phase analysis (GPA) was performed via the Strain++ open-source program [35].

### 2.2 M-TGO growth model

During high-temperature exposure, the oxygen diffusing inward reacts with Si–HfO2 bond coating to form a m-TGO composite structure, which consists of dispersed HfO2/HfSiO4 particles embedded in the β-cristobalite SiO2 phase. This is the result of a two-step reaction within Si–HfO2 bond coating, where oxygen initially reacts with Si to form SiO2, followed by HfSiO4 formation at O2 interface [36], as illustrated in Eqs. (1) and (2):

(1) Si+O2→SiO2

(2) SiO2+HfO2→HfSiO4

Previous research have indicated that the growth of SiO2 follows a parabolic model with respect to time [37]. HfSiO4 formation undergoes an initial linear growth phase when SiO2 and HfO2 are not in full contact, known as the incubation period before transitioning to a parabolic growth rate once full contact is established. [36,38]. In this work, the use of densely packed, uniformly composed Si–HfO2 spherical powders (Fig. 1(c)) enhanced HfO2 and SiO2 contact during thermal cycling, effectively shortening the incubation period for HfSiO4 growth. This period diminishes further with increasing temperature [36], indicating that at the elevated temperatures applied in our tests (1400, 1450, and 1500 °C), the incubation time was notably brief. Considering these factors, this work assumed that the growth of SiO2 and HfSiO4 within the m-TGO layer follows a parabolic relationship over time, and it was reasonable to neglect the very short linear growth phase of HfSiO4. Consequently, utilizing Deal–Grove model for oxidation kinetics, the thickness of m-TGO was defined as a parabolic function of the high-temperature holding time (Eq. (3)) [39]:

### 2.3 Finite element model

Finite element method simulations were performed via ANSYS program. The generalized plane strain equation was employed to conduct a linear static structural analysis. On the basis of the principle of normal crack propagation, a crack propagates in a direction normal to that of the maximum local principal stress, particularly during the cooling stage of a thermal cycle when stress arises from a CTE mismatch. Therefore, since the goal of this simulation was to qualitatively analyze the effects of changes in the stress field caused by differences in the material properties of the layers during the cooling process on the evolution of vertical mud cracks, it must be emphasized that (1) stresses that develop due to the volume expansion of m-TGO were not considered in this model; (2) the material properties were assumed to be linear and constant across the entire temperature range during the simulation; (3) plasticity or creep was not considered in the material model; and (4) each material layer in this model was assumed to be homogeneous, without any pores or oxide inclusions, to reduce computational demands and facilitate easier interpretation of the results. Moreover, the material properties closer to those of actual coatings were used in the model, as listed in Table 2 (based on Refs. [14,27,40]).

### 3.1 Microstructure and composition of feedstock and as-sprayed coating

Figure 1 displays the micromorphology and XRD patterns of Si–HfO2, Yb2Si2O7, and Yb2SiO5 feedstocks. The three feedstocks (Fig. 1(a) for Si–HfO2, Fig. 1(b) for Yb2Si2O7, and Fig. 1(c) for Yb2SiO5) exhibited high sphericity and a particle size distribution of approximately 11–55 μm (reported in our previous work, Ref. [34]), which is suitable for PS-PVD process to prepare dense coatings. As shown in the EDS results in Figs. 1(d) and 1(e), the darker contrast part was the Si phase (85 mol%), whereas the remaining part was the HfO2 phase (15 mol%), which was homogeneously mixed with the Si phase. This feedstock was advantageous for achieving composition uniformity in Si–HfO2 bond coating after undergoing PS-PVD process, thereby preventing component segregation. Furthermore, XRD results in Fig. 1(f) reveal that the Yb2SiO5 feedstock was indexed as the pure X2-Yb2SiO5 phase. Quantitative phase analysis revealed that Yb2Si2O7 feedstock consists of 94.4 mol% β-Yb2Si2O7 and 5.6 mol% X2-Yb2SiO5 phases, likely because SiO2 ablation causes a small fraction of X2-Yb2SiO5 phase to remain during the Yb2Si2O7 synthesis process [2]. Si–HfO2 feedstock was composed of 85.3 mol% cubic Si and 14.7 mol% m-HfO2 phases, which aligns with the EDS results mentioned above.

The cross-sectional microstructure features, surface morphology features, and EDS results of the tri-layer Si–HfO2/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 coating sprayed with PS-PVD are shown in Fig. 2. The thicknesses of Si–HfO2, Yb2Si2O7, and Yb2SiO5 layers were ~40, ~119, and ~75 μm, respectively, as shown in Fig. 2(a). The thickness of trilayer EBC is strategically chosen to maximize the corrosion resistance of Yb2SiO5 top layer while preventing delamination caused by thermal mismatch stresses due to excessive thickness. Additionally, it optimizes Yb2Si2O7 intermediate layer to effectively alleviate thermal mismatches between the top layer and the substrate and leverages the bond coating to provide essential adhesion and mechanical support. This tri-layer coating presented a dense structure, tight adhesion to CMC substrate, and no apparent cracks appeared in the coating layers and layer interfaces (as confirmed by our earlier work [34], the internal stress within the as-sprayed coating was a compressive stress of 257 MPa), except for a few encapsulated pores in Yb2Si2O7 layer caused by the inability of gas to completely escape during the deposition process of PS-PVD [41]. Additionally, from the global view of the composition contrast difference in the coating, the mixture of Si and HfO2 phases within the bond coating was largely uniform. While there are minor areas showing composition segregation, these instances do not detract from the overall uniformity, which corresponds to the composition in Si–HfO2 feedstock. Yb2Si2O7 and Yb2SiO5 layers displayed a typical layered microstructure. Within Yb2Si2O7 layer, a small amount of light gray lamellar secondary phase can be observed. This is likely due to the presence of a minor Yb2SiO5 phase in Yb2Si2O7 feedstock (Fig. 1(f)). For Yb2SiO5 layer, a small amount of dark gray lamellar secondary phase is noticeable. Prior researches [29,41,42] have demonstrated that Yb2SiO5 feedstock partially dissociates into liquid Yb2O3 and gaseous SiO2 in high-temperature plasma (> 10,000 °C) and that the degree of dissociation is not the same. Through the periodic movement of the plasma gun, which deposits both gas and liquid phases layer by layer, Yb2SiO5 coating is composed of Yb2O3 and SiO2 striped bands and microsized Yb2SiO5 splats alternately. Yb2O3 and SiO2 also rapidly formed ytterbium silicates in subsequent high-temperature service [34]. Furthermore, the surface morphology (Fig. 2(b)) revealed that the coating was composed of fully melted and partially melted particles. The coating surface exhibited few pores, and only partial areas of the coating were crystallized in the high-magnification SEM image, as confirmed by XRD results of previous work [34]. Additionally, the porosity of the coating surface was determined to be 3.68% via image analysis with ImageJ, as shown in Fig. 2(b).

#### 3.2.1 Thermal cycling tests at 1400 °C

The microstructure evolution of the trilayer Si–HfO2/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 coating was investigated after the thermal cycling test at 1400 °C, as shown in Fig. 3. It is apparent from Figs. 3(a1)–3(a4), after 50 h of thermal cycling, the coating crystallized on the surface. The coating remained intact and bonded with CMC substrates, and no obvious microcracks were generated within or between layers. From the microstructure of the bond coating in Fig. 3(a3) and EDS analysis in Fig. 3(a4), it can be seen that the light gray hafnium-rich precipitates were primarily distributed within the dark gray Si-rich phase. On the basis of EDS analysis and previous XRD results [27], the dark gray part is SiO2 cristobalite phase, and the light gray part is HfSiO4 phase, forming a unique dual-state duplex composite structure with HfSiO4 particles “bricks” dispersed within the SiO2 “mortar” [10,27]. Although minor agglomeration of these precipitates into masses can be observed in certain areas at high magnification morphology (Fig. 3(a3)), owing to a phase separation phenomenon that occurs because the surface energy of HfSiO4 is significantly lower than the interface energy between HfSiO4 and SiO2 [10], a broader examination at lower magnification (Fig. S1(a) in the ESM) reveals that these agglomerated masses themselves exhibit a uniform distribution across the entire bond coating. Consequently, despite the presence of localized disparities within the m-TGO bond coating, the aggregate distribution maintains notable uniformity. Moreover, the average thickness of the bond coating reached 44.87 μm after 50 h of thermal cycling at 1400 °C, as summarized in Table 3 and visually demonstrated in Fig. S1(a) in the ESM. This growth could be attributed to Si reacting with HfO2 in the presence of an oxidant to form HfSiO4 [10,27], making the bond coating an m-TGO layer consisting of SiO2, HfO2, and HfSiO4. Interestingly, unlike the distinct SiO2 scale commonly observed in pure Si bond coatings, no prominent SiO2 scale was detected in this bond coating.

When the crack front contacts the dispersed HfSiO4 phase, the residual stress causes the cracks to segregate and branch (forming oriented cracks), which prolongs the crack propagation path and hinders the interconnection of cracks. Additionally, prior research revealed that a small quantity of Si in Si–HfO2 bond coating tends to melt and migrate into EBC layer during thermal cycling at 1426 °C [24]. This phenomenon was caused by two main factors in prior research: (1) The non-uniform Si–HfO2 feedstock led to a prepared Si–HfO2 bond coating with poor uniformity and visible large Si blocks, encouraging Si melting; (2) the less dense EBC layer had many pores, offering channels for Si migration. However, examination of Si–HfO2 and Yb2Si2O7 layers in this work revealed no occurrence of Si melting or flow during thermal cycling at 1400, 1450, and 1500 °C (Figs. 3−5). This improvement is attributable to the high uniformity and sphericity of Si–HfO2 feedstock prepared in this study (Figs. 1(c)–1(e)), which increased the compositional uniformity of the bond coating and prevented the formation of large Si blocks. Prior research [24] has indicated that Si can rapidly oxidize within 10 h at 1426 °C [24]. Therefore, as the contact area between the uniformly dispersed Si and oxygen increases, it significantly prevents its melting and flow. Moreover, utilizing PS-PVD technology and optimized parameters [29,41], the prepared denser Yb2Si2O7 and Yb2SiO5 layers in this work inhibited Si migration. In terms of compositional uniformity and density, Si–HfO2/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 effectively mitigated the void formation induced by Si migration, thereby ensuring protection of CMC.

### 3.3 TEM analysis of trilayer EBC after thermal cycling

In this work, samples from different stages of the 1450 °C thermal cycling test were selected for phase composition and microdefect analysis across the trilayer coating. Building on the aforementioned analysis of oxygen transport, it is essential to delve into the reaction mechanisms by which permeated oxygen molecules control the growth of SiO2 and HfSiO4 phases within m-TGO layer, as illustrated in Fig. 9. These mechanisms not only influence the growth of m-TGO but also impact the overall integrity and protective capability of the novel Si–HfO2 bond coating. First, the external molecular oxygen penetrates Si–HfO2 bond coating through Yb2SiO5 and Yb2Si2O7 layers, where it initially reacts at Si phase in Si–HfO2 bond coating to grow SiO2 phase, evolving into a Si/SiO2 phase interface structure. Second, once the protrusions of SiO2 phase contact HfO2 phase, HfSiO4 phase is formed at SiO2/HfO2 phase interface, evolving into a Si/SiO2/HfSiO4/HfO2 phase interface structure [25,27]. Previous studies [36,38] have indicated that the continuous increase in the thickness of the SiO2 and HfSiO4 phases is due to the decomposition of SiO2 near the HfSiO4 side into Si4+ and 2O2−. Under high temperatures, 2O2− ions migrate to Si/SiO2 phase interface, react with Si to form new SiO2, and release 4e− ions (Si + 2O2− → SiO2 + 4e−). Si4+ diffuses to HfSiO4/HfO2 phase interface, and to ensure charge conservation, the released 4e− are also transported to HfSiO4/HfO2 interface. The permeated oxygen molecules are first reduced at HfSiO4/HfO2 phase interface by 4e− to 2O2− ions, which then react with Si4+ and HfO2 molecules to form new HfSiO4. As each HfSiO4 phase progressively grows, the gaps between HfSiO4 particles gradually decrease, leading to the agglomeration or interconnection of HfSiO4 particles, as demonstrated in Fig. S4 in the ESM.

### 4.2 Theoretical thermomechanical compatibility of m-TGO growth

Learning more about the compatibility between Si–HfO2 bond coating with the underlying CMC substrate and the overlying Yb2Si2O7 layer from a thermomechanical perspective is crucial since EBC system undergoes a significant temperature change during operation. This study focuses on three essential theoretical calculation parameters during thermal cycling to assess the reliability and compatibility of Si–HfO2 bond coating in CMC surface tri-layer EBC system: (1) thermal mismatch stress * (σt), which results from differences in CTE between the coating and substrate and leads to mud-cracking during thermal cycling; (2) interfacial residual stress  (σr), which originates from differences in contraction or expansion between neighboring layers during thermal cycling, affects adhesion at the interface and increases the risk of delamination; and (3) stored elastic strain energy (G*SS), which accumulates due to external and thermal stresses during thermal cycling, triggers failure mechanisms such as cracking in the coating when excessive. Notably, before evaluation, a prior study demonstrated that SiO2 rapidly transitions to the cristobalite phase at higher operating temperatures, a process that is completed in just a few minutes at 1450 °C [36]. This cristobalite undergoes an α * ⇌[] β phase transition (~270 °C) during the thermal cycling process, resulting in a sudden change in CTE [25,27]. Therefore, CTE of m-TGO cannot be treated as a constant independent of temperature by a simple multiphase mixing rule. To assess the three essential thermomechanical parameters related to the linear CTE (LCTE) more accurately, it is important to determine the effective LCTE of m-TGO, as it varies with the temperature and time of thermal cycling. Figures 6(c)–6(e) and Fig. S4 in the ESM show that Si–HfO2 bond coating in this work existed in the form of m-TGO from the early stage of thermal cycling, consisting of HfSiO4 ellipsoidal particles that were randomly distributed within β-cristobalite matrix. Moreover, during other stages of thermal cycling, although localized HfSiO4 particles grew and agglomerated into masses, on the whole, the larger HfSiO4 ellipsoidal masses were also randomly distributed within β-cristobalite matrix, as depicted in Figs. 3−5. This microstructure evolution is essentially consistent with that observed during the thermal cycling of a single Si–HfO2 bond coating prepared via PVD method in our previous study, including the shape and distribution of HfSiO4 particles [27]. Consequently, given that the molar mass of Hf in m-TGO was determined to be 15% in this work, the effective LCTE of m-TGO in this work was derived via a linear regression method based on the equivalent micromechanics model we previously developed [27]. As depicted in Fig. 10, m-TGO exhibited a relatively high LCTE at a lower temperature (~9.6×10−6 K−1) but a lower one at higher temperatures. These results arise from the fact that α-cristobalite, which is stable at lower temperatures, has a higher CTE than β-cristobalite at higher temperatures. Additionally, as the thermal cycling progresses, there is a slight decrease in LCTE at lower temperatures and a rise at higher temperatures. This phenomenon can be attributed to the increased content of elliptical HfSiO4 particles and the increase in the major/minor ratio in proportion to the Eshelby tensor [27,48].

The actual thermal mismatch stress generated in multilayer coating systems during thermal cycling arises from several factors, including a mismatch in the coefficient of CTE between layers, volume changes due to chemical reactions or phase transitions, and residual stresses introduced during the manufacturing process. Among these, the thermal stress induced by the differences in CTE between layers is the main source of the actual thermal mismatch stress [49], and this specific part (theoretically calculated  σt) can be derived via Eq. (7) [14,49]:

(7) σt=(αc−αs)EcΔT/(1−vC)

where  αc,  Ec, and  νC are the average CTE, elastic modulus, and Poisson’s ratio of the coating, respectively;  αs is the CTE of the substrate; and  ΔT is the temperature change. To investigate the degree of thermal matching between m-TGO and CMC,  σt as a function of the thermal cycling time after cooling to room temperature from various testing temperatures was analyzed and is shown in Fig. 11, with the average LCTE and elastic modulus shown in Fig. 10 and Ref. [27]. Notably, in m-TGO, α  ⇌[] β phase transition of SiO2 cristobalite is accompanied by changes in CTE and volume, which complexly affect the actual thermal mismatch stress during thermal cycling. The existing formulas for evaluating the thermal mismatch stress do not accurately account for this impact. Although this study did not fully consider the impact of volume expansion on the actual thermal mismatch stress, it considered CTE change caused by the cristobalite phase transition when LCTE and elastic modulus for m-TGO layer were selected [27], thereby enhancing the credibility of  σt. Furthermore, although Yb2Si2O7 layer imposes a constraint force on the expansion of m-TGO during thermal cycling, which affects the results of Eq. (7), its ability to estimate  σt between m-TGO and CMC is supported by several factors: (1) The bonding strength between the substrate and bond coating is generally greater than that between the top coat and bond coating, leading to a more significant constraint force by CMC substrate to Yb2Si2O7 layer; (2) the distribution of grains and voids within m-TGO influences the constraint force, which is more pronounced near m-TGO/Yb2Si2O7 interface but weaker overall. Therefore, as with its use by other researchers in analyzing various multilayer systems [14,21], Eq. (7) is applicable for providing an upper bound estimation of  σt between the bond coating and CMC substrate in a trilayer EBC system.

In addition to the initial assessment of thermal stress accumulation within a multilayer coating system utilizing thermal mismatch stress, an in-depth evaluation of the interface stability between m-TGO and both the bottom CMC and the top Yb2Si2O7 layer is needed. This involves a thorough investigation of the interfacial residual stress ( |σr|) generated by the mutual compression/tension of adjacent layers during heating/cooling cycles, which is shown in Eq. (8) [51]:

(8) |σr|=EmaxEmin(εL-max−εL-min)/(Emax+Emin)

where  Emax and  Emin are the maximum and minimum elastic moduli in the two layers, respectively,  εL-max and  εL-min are the maximum and minimum linear strains of the two layers, respectively, which are obtained by multiplying the temperature by LCTE ( εL=αLT), where α*L is linear thermal expansion coefficient. Notably, despite the presence of compressive stress after coating processing, numerous experiments and simulations have shown that this stress is released during the heating and holding processes of ceramic coating materials due to high creep rates [21]. Therefore, its impact on the assessment of interfacial residual stress can be disregarded.

In a multilayer coating system, deformation (compression or tension) between each layer during thermal cycling not only leads to interface instability but also stores elastic strain energy under the influence of thermal stress, becoming a potential source of surface energy required for crack propagation. To gain a deep understanding of the interlaminar cracks at CMC/m-TGO or m-TGO/Yb2Si2O7 interfaces shown in Figs. 5(d2) and 5(d3), this study further calculated G*SS (strain energy release rate (ERR)) of m-TGO with adjacent layers as a function of temperature and cycling time via Eq. (9) [43]:

(9) GSS=(1+v)Eh[(α−α′)ΔT+εL]/16(1−v)

where * ν, E*, *h*, and *α* are Poisson’s ratio, elastic modulus, thickness, and LCTE of m-TGO; * α′ is LCTE of CMC or Yb2Si2O7;  ΔT represents the temperature variation during thermal cycling;  εL is the linear strain linked to any phase deformation that takes place during thermal cycling. By substituting m-TGO thickness growth model Eq. (6) into Eq. (9), the specific expression for G*SS can be derived as Eq. (10):

(10) GSS={(1+v)E[(α−α′)ΔT+εL]16(1−v)[h0+(824.16exp⁡(−133.69RT)τ(t))1/2],1400∘CT1450∘C(1+v)E[(α−α′)ΔT+εL]16(1−v)[h0+(824.16exp⁡(−101.47RT)τ(t))1/2],1450∘CT1500∘C

where *h*0 is the initial thickness of Si–HfO2 bond coating of 40 μm. Importantly, the strain in Eq. (9) does not consider the constraining effects of adjacent layers on a specific layer. For example, when calculating ERR between CMC/m-TGO, the constraint imposed by Yb2Si2O7 layer on m-TGO is not considered. During thermal cycling, the constraint also changes with the variation in the interlayer binding strength and internal structure of the coating, making precise evaluation through experiments or other theoretical calculations difficult. Therefore, despite these limitations, Eq. (9) can still be used as a rough method for assessing ERR, which has been applied in numerous multilayer EBC system studies [21,43,46,52]. Similarly, in Si–HfO2 bond coating, changes in elastic modulus, volume, and CTE from SiO2 cristobalite phase transition alter the ability to store elastic energy, deformation behavior, and internal stress distribution, all of which affect the elastic strain energy, especially during thermal cycling. Although Eq. (10) may not capture every detail of phase transition effects, the primary source of elastic strain energy in multilayer coatings arises from varying compression or tension rates due to differences in CTE between layers, as well as variations in layer thickness [21,52]. Hence, Eq. (10), which outlines the relationship between ERR and the differences in CTE, as well as m-TGO thickness growth with the thermal cycling time, remains a viable rough method for assessing elastic strain energy. Moreover, this work reflects the changes in CTE before and after the cristobalite phase transition in LCTE parameters for Si–HfO2 bond coating, enhancing the credibility of the ERR calculation between Si–HfO2 bond coating and adjacent layers.

Owing to the lower difference in LCTE between m-TGO and Yb2Si2O7, ERR remained relatively low, providing a minimal driving force for the required surface energy for crack propagation until it began to increase rapidly above 1300 °C. It reached 4.7, 5.2, and 5.8 J/m2 at 1400, 1450, and 1500 °C test temperatures, respectively, becoming a possible stage for crack propagation, as observed in Fig. 5(c2).

### 4.3 Vertical mud-crack evolution in trilayer EBC

In Si–HfO2/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 EBC system, in addition to preventing delamination of TGO formed in the pure Si bond coating at the cost of interlaminar cracks in Si–HfO2 bond coating to maintain protection of CMC substrate, another critical aspect that significantly prolongs the overall EBC protection is the bifurcation of vertical mud cracks at Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 interface. This unique crack evolution can be attributed to the plastic deformation inside Yb2Si2O7 crystals, which partially absorbs the thermal stress generated within Yb2Si2O7 layer during thermal cycling, thereby preventing further vertical propagation of cracks and increasing toughness [14]. Clear evidence of plastic deformation, including plate-shaped twinning and parallel dislocations, was observed in dark field (DF) image (Fig. 14(a), corresponding to BF image in Fig. 7(c)) of Yb2Si2O7 layer after 1450 °C thermal cycling for 100 h. An analysis of SAED pattern in Fig. 14(b) and HRTEM image in Fig. 14(c) indicated that the twinning boundary within the purple circle in Fig. 14(a) was the (001) crystal plane. The anisotropic analysis of the shear modulus (Fig. 14(d)), which was calculated from the second-order elastic constants provided by Tian *et* *al*. [53], revealed that the plane of the lowest shear modulus is (110) in Yb2Si2O7 crystal. Thus, the (110) twinning mode is the mode most likely to occur under stress, and currently, only the (110) twin boundary has been observed [14,53]. However, the appearance of twinning with the (001) plane in this work may be attributed to the elevated thermal stresses resulting from higher thermal cycling test temperatures, thus opening the higher-energy (001) twinning mode, further highlighting that the quasiplastic property of the Yb2Si2O7 ceramic can adapt to higher service temperatures. Interestingly, numerous dislocations were observed interacting with this (001) twinning, a phenomenon not previously reported in other similar studies [14,53], as illustrated in Fig. 14(e). Further observations through HRTEM image reveal lattice distortions inside the twinning due to dislocation slip, as confirmed by the strain distributions of GPA images in Figs. 14(g)–14(i). This interaction phenomenon may be explained by the unique interplay between twin boundaries and dislocations, wherein edge dislocations reacting with twin boundaries generate new edge dislocations to skip during twinning and produce new incomplete dislocations on the twin boundary that can slip along the twin boundary [54]. Consequently, twinning with coherent interfaces can serve as slip planes for dislocations, enabling the absorption and storage of dislocations during deformation, thereby significantly contributing to the quasi plasticity of Yb2Si2O7.

Coating failure induced by crack propagation typically occurs during the cycling stage, with the maximum stress occurring at the end of the cooling cycle [56]. To further analyze the mechanisms governing vertical mud-crack propagation in trilayer coatings, the stress distribution of vertical mud-cracks during the 1450 °C thermal cycle cooling process was elucidated via finite element simulations. On the basis of the cross-sectional microstructure image of the sample after 150 h of thermal cycling at 1450 °C, a trilayer coating model incorporating vertical mud cracks was established, as shown in Fig. 16, in which a vertical mud crack and a bifurcated crack were inserted. The depths of the vertical mud crack and the bifurcated crack, as well as the spacing between them, were aligned with the experimental results to ensure consistency. Owing to the high creep rates within the top coat and bond coating during the heating stage of thermal cycling [56], it can be assumed that the model was in a stress-free initial state at 1450 °C, and then the model was cooled from 1450 to25 °C. This simulation is classified as a prefabricated crack simulation. Its principal aim was to analyze the changes in the stress field between two cracks during the cooling process and to predict the potential direction of crack propagation. Notably, factors such as the stress from volume expansion of m-TGO, plasticity (dislocations and twinning defects), creep behaviors, and the pores within coatings indeed impact the propagation of cracks to varying extents. However, the primary driving factor for crack propagation is the direction of the maximum local principal stress. Owing to computational and methodological limitations, current stress simulations for thermal barrier coating (TBC) and EBC struggle to fully account for all these factors [52,56]. This work aims to preliminarily evaluate the mechanism by which stress variations at the crack tip, due to property differences in each layer during cooling, affect the potential for crack propagation. Although the results of the simulation are qualitative and limited, they also ensure a certain degree of scientific validity.

Figure 17 displays the strain and stress distributions within the trilayer model at various stages during the cooling process. Figure 17(a1) initially shows that the region between the left vertical mud crack and the right bifurcated crack exhibited lower strain in the early stage of cooling. However, it is apparent from the middle and final stages of cooling (Figs. 17(b1) and 17(c1)) that the strain values in this region increased step by step. Similar to the strain distribution, the stress between the two cracks gradually increased during cooling. The von Mises equivalent stress distributions in Figs. 17(a2), 17(b2), and 17(c2) reveal that the left vertical mud-crack tip consistently experienced the highest stress levels because it was located within Yb2SiO5 layer. Notably, the stress levels of the left tip of the bifurcated crack were greater than those of the right tip. The increase in von Mises stress during the cooling process highlights the susceptibility of the coating to changes in thermal conditions. As the stress intensifies, particularly in regions between the two cracks, there is a growing risk of material failure. This stress buildup is crucial for understanding how the trilayer model behaves under thermal loads, indicating areas where the materials may not only yield but also undergo significant structural changes due to cracking. Additionally, as can be observed from the principal stress distribution in Figs. 17(a3), 17(b3), and 17(c3), the principal stress between these two cracks gradually increased during cooling, and there was a trend toward connection (as indicated by the red box). Given the nature of crack propagation, which normally occurs in the direction of the maximum local principal stress, it can be anticipated that during the cooling process, the gradual intensification of the stress field between the tips of the two cracks may prompt the bifurcated crack to extend in a horizontal direction. This could also be one of the reasons for the observed experimental results of bifurcated crack evolution at Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 interface (i.e., Figs. 4(c2), 4(d2), and 5(c2)). Furthermore, additional simulations (including strain, equivalent stress, and principal stress) were conducted under the same conditions but with varying distances between the vertical and bifurcated cracks, as detailed in Figs. S5 and S6 in the ESM. The results indicate that adjusting the proximity of these two cracks during the cooling process still led to a tendency for the stress fields between them to interconnect. This tendency was less pronounced when the cracks were farther apart and more pronounced when they were closer together. This supplementary simulation also substantiates the hypothesis that the stress field at the tip of the bifurcated crack was influenced by the stress field at the tip of the adjacent vertical crack, thereby supporting the possibility of lateral extension as a plausible outcome.

To summarize the evolution of cracks within Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 layers during thermal cycling, a schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 18. For oriented cracks, microcracks initially originate from the cristobalite phase transformation in SiO2 mortar. Since the elastic modulus and CTE between HfSiO4 particles and SiO2 mortar have a complementary relationship during thermal cycling, the edges of HfSiO4 particles are in a compressive stress state relative to the mortar. Therefore, when the microcrack tips within the mortar come into contact with HfSiO4 particles, they are subjected to compressive stress, which causes deflection, gradually evolving into oriented cracks [27]. Furthermore, during the middle stage, the accumulation of interfacial residual stress and elastic strain energy may initiate the formation of interlaminar cracks between m-TGO and the underlying CMC, and such cracks may even appear at m-TGO/Yb2SiO5 interface in the late stage. The initial formation and vertical propagation of vertical mud cracks may result from the thermal mismatch stress between Yb2SiO5 layer and the underlying CMC substrate. As vertical mud cracks subsequently penetrated Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 interface, Yb2Si2O7 layer relied on plastic deformation (twinning and dislocations) to relieve thermal stress, preventing further extension of vertical mud cracks along the thickness direction in Yb2Si2O7 layer and leading to the bifurcation of vertical mud cracks at the interface. Ultimately, the two tips of each bifurcated crack may continually interconnect under the effects of cyclic thermal stress and the influence of stress fields from nearby vertical mud-crack tips, weakening the protection of Yb2SiO5 layer for CMC.

Moreover, according to a comparison of the thermal cycling performance of different EBC systems (Table 4), Si–HfO2/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 system prepared by PS-PVD demonstrates superior thermal cycle temperature-bearing capacity and service life. For instance, (1) Si/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 system, applied to SiC substrates using the atmospheric plasma spraying (APS) method [14], showed obvious edge spalling and cracking after just 40 cycles of heat treatment at 1350 °C (15 min of dwell time followed by cooling to 25 °C); (2) a new 80 wt% HfO2–Si/YbSi1.5/Yb2SiO5/LaMgAl11O19 system prepared using APS technology [57], exhibited severe spalling at both YbSi1.5/HfO2–Si interface and HfO2–Si/substrate interface, as well as vertical cracks that completely penetrated the layers, after 171 cycles of heating to 1300 °C (55 min of dwell time, 5 min of cooling), without showing the bifurcated cracks that could delay the ingress of external corrosive media as observed in this work, failing to provide effective protection to the underlying CMC; (3) a new SiO2–HfO2/Yb2Si2O7 system [58], prepared using APS and subjected to 22 cycles of steam cycling at 1475 °C (10 h of dwell time followed by cooling to room temperature), saw the complete delamination of the 70 mol% HfO2–SiO2/Yb2Si2O7 at the SiC substrate, while the 50 mol% HfO2–SiO2/Yb2Si2O7, although not delaminated, developed cracks in TGO layer, reducing its protective performance. In summary, despite the evolution of various cracks during thermal cycling at 1400, 1450, and 1500 °C, Si–HfO2/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 system consistently protected the underlying CMC within 200 h of cycling. In particular, Si–HfO2 bond coating not only mitigates spalling caused by the phase transformation of SiO2 but also prevents the degradation of oxidation resistance when HfSiO4 is distributed as a dispersed phase within m-TGO. Additionally, the design of incorporating 15 mol% HfO2 doping helps avoid severe mismatches in CTE between m-TGO and CMC substrate. Therefore, these advanced trilayer EBCs rank highly among numerous EBC systems.

## 5 Conclusions and outlook

The findings presented in this study motivate the primary research and applications of the novel trilayer Si–HfO2/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 EBC system prepared by PS-PVD, including the oxidation mechanism and thermomechanical compatibility of Si–HfO2 bond coating and the underlying mechanism of crack propagation. After achieving 200 h of thermal cycling at 1500 °C, the trilayer EBC system faces challenges such as cracks between m-TGO and adjacent layers, as well as the lateral spreading and connection of bifurcated cracks at Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 interface. To mitigate these issues and further enhance the system’s temperature resistance and durability, future efforts should concentrate on improving the compatibility between m-TGO and the CMC substrate, as well as the toughness of Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 layers. Strategies include developing a gradient-composition Si–HfO2 bond coating to prevent interface cracking, incorporating β-SiC fibers into Yb2Si2O7 layer for crack healing, and applying annealing treatments to seal vertical mud‒cracks. These measures aim to improve EBC performance for long-term protection in high-temperature environments.

The primary conclusions of this study are outlined below:

1) Si–HfO2/Yb2Si2O7/Yb2SiO5 trilayer EBC prepared via PS-PVD method has a dense structure and tightly adheres to CMC substrate. During the thermal cycling tests at 1400, 1450, and 1500 °C, Si–HfO2 bond coating existed in the form of m-TGO with HfSiO4 particles embedded in β-cristobalite SiO2 mortar. Notably, for the first time, this work demonstrates that the growth thickness variation of m-TGO with EBC outer layers adheres to the oxidation kinetics parabolic model. The oxygen diffusion activation energy is 133.69 kJ/mol in the range of 1400–1450 °C and 101.47 kJ/mol in the range of 1450–1500 °C, which are associated with molecular transport. Additionally, between 1400 and 1450 °C, m-TGO growth follows  (Δh)2=824.16exp⁡(−133.69/RT)τ(t), and from 1450 to 1500 °C, m-TGO adheres to  (Δh)2=824.16exp⁡(−101.47/RT)τ(t) during thermal cycling.

2) Although the interfacial residual stress and ERR at CMC/m-TGO and m-TGO/Yb2Si2O7 interfaces during thermal cycling significantly increase around the cristobalite phase transition temperature and in the high-temperature range, inducing interlaminar cracks, m-TGO results in a lower thermal mismatch stress and ERR than SiO2–TGO formed by a typical Si bond coating. This makes it less prone to the dangerous delamination phenomena of SiO2–TGO.

3) During thermal cycling, the vertical mud cracks in Yb2SiO5 layer originate from thermal mismatch stress. At higher thermal cycling temperatures, not only are [110] dislocations detected within Yb2Si2O7 but also, for the first time, (001)-type twins requiring higher activation energy than typical (110) twins in Yb2Si2O7 are observed, and the presence of an interaction region between the twins and the dislocations suggests that the quasiplasticity of Yb2Si2O7 can be used at higher temperatures to force vertical mud-crack bifurcation. Additionally, influenced by the accumulation of thermal stress and the stress fields from neighboring vertical mud cracks, bifurcated cracks might extend laterally.
