## 1 Introduction

Because of their excellent piezoelectric properties, lead zirconate titanate (Pb(Zr,Ti)O3, PZT) ceramics have been widely used [1]. However, the Curie temperature (*T*C) of the PZT ceramics is below 380 ℃ [2], which cannot meet the needs of high-temperature piezoelectric applications. Moreover, due to the toxicity of lead elements to both health and the environment, researchers have been pursuing lead-free alternatives [3,4]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to study new piezoelectric ceramics with high *T*C. Bismuth layered-structure ferroelectrics (BLSFs) are one type of lead-free piezoelectric ceramics with high *T*C, including Bi3TiNbO9, Bi4Ti3O12 (BIT), and CaBi4Ti4O15 [5−7], etc. The chemical formula of BLSFs is (Bi2O2)2+(A*m*–1B*m*O3*m*+1)2**−**, and their structure is formed by the regular arrangement of perovskite layers (A*m*−1B*m*O3*m*+1)2**−** and bismuth oxide layers (Bi2O2)2+ [8]. Due to their special structure, BLSFs also have relatively low dielectric constants, aging rate, and temperature coefficients of the resonant frequency [9]. BIT, as one vital type of BLSFs, has great application prospects in high-temperature environments due to its excellent *T*C of 675 ℃ and large spontaneous polarization (*P*s) of 50 μC·cm**−**2. Moreover, it is also widely used in optical and electrical fields due to its anisotropy [10]. However, *P*s of BIT is mainly along the *a*–*b* plane, which leads to low piezoelectric activity (*d*33 = 6–8 pC·N**−**1) due to its strong anisotropic polarization [11−13]. Additionally, the Bi volatilization during sintering in BIT-based ceramics leads to the generation of oxygen vacancy defects (* VO⋅⋅), resulting in the exacerbation of the electrical properties. At present, there are two main ways to solve the above problems and modify the properties of the BIT ceramics. One is modification through component design, such as doping substitution at the A or B-site [14,15]. Element doping is an effective modification method that can significantly affect the microstructure and improve piezoelectric and ferroelectric properties [16−18]; the other is modification through preparation processes, such as the molten salt method [19]. The B-site modification is one of the most effective modification methods, which introduces high-valence cations such as Nb5+, W6+, and Ta5+ [14,20,21] or co-doped cations such as Zn2+/Nb5+, Cu2+/Nb5+, and Cu2+/Ta5+ [22−24] into the B-site of BIT to enhance piezoelectric activity. Furthermore, some studies indicate that non-equivalent doping, where high-valence ions replace low-valence ones, can inhibit the formation of oxygen vacancies and thus enhance electrical resistivity at high temperatures [25].

However, by the B-site modification for the BIT-based ceramics, it is usually hard to obtain both a high piezoelectric coefficient and a high Curie temperature, as well as large resistivity at high temperatures (~500 ℃) [26−28]. There seems to be a mutual restriction between d*33 and *T*C due to the difficulty of simultaneously achieving excellent electrical properties and good structural stability, making it a great challenge to synergistically optimize the two parameters [29]. For example, Chen *et al.* [26] reported a relatively large piezoelectric coefficient of 28 pC·N**−**1 and a low Curie temperature of 638 ℃ by co-doping W/Cr in the BIT ceramics. In addition, W/Mn co-doped BIT ceramics with a low piezoelectric coefficient of 21 pC·N**−**1 and a Curie temperature of 655 ℃ were reported [27].

In this work, we propose a new strategy to improve piezoelectric coefficient and Curie temperature of BIT-based ceramics while maintaining high-temperature resistivity. Cr3+/Ta5+ non-equivalent co-doped Bi4Ti3−*x*(Cr1/3Ta2/3)*x*O12 ceramics were prepared by the solid-state reaction method. Compared with other synthesis methods, the solid-state reaction method is efficient, simple, and has a low production cost [30]. The structure, piezoelectric, dielectric, ferroelectric, and temperature stability properties were systematically studied. In the composition with *x* = 0.03, a high piezoelectric coefficient of 26 pC·N−1 and a high Curie temperature of 687 ℃ were simultaneously achieved while maintaining large high-temperature resistance. Besides, related mechanisms of conduction and enhancement of piezoelectricity were discussed in detail.

## 2 Experimental

Bi4Ti3−*x*(Cr1/3Ta2/3)*x*O12 (BTCT100*x*, *x* = 0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, and 0.04, abbreviated as BTCT0, BTCT0.5, BTCT1, BTCT2, BTCT3, and BTCT4, respectively) piezoelectric ceramics were prepared via the solid-state reaction technique. Bi2O3 (99.99%), TiO2 (99.8%), Cr2O3 (99%), and Ta2O5 (99.99%) were used as raw materials. The metal oxides were weighed according to their stoichiometric compositions and ball milled with ethanol for 4 h. Then the dried mixtures were calcined at 850 ℃ for 2 h. The calcined powders were remilled in the same condition, then dried, and ground. The powders were pressed into pellets using polyvinyl butyral (PVB) as the binder. The pellets were slowly heated to 500 ℃ to burn out PVB and then sintered at 1100 ℃ for 2 h. After sintering, the top and bottom surfaces of the samples were coated with silver paint and then fired at 720 ℃ for 15 min. The samples were polarized for 20 min at 180 ℃ with a direct current field of 6–8 kV·mm**−**1.

The crystalline phase of ceramics was analyzed by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Empyrean, PANalytical). The bonding characteristics were determined at a Raman spectrometer (inVia Reflex, Renishaw). The microstructures were obtained by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; TM4000plus, Hitachi). The valence of the main elements was analyzed by an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB 250XI, Thermo). The dielectric properties and impedance spectra were measured using an impedance analyzer (E4990A, Keysight). The direct current (DC) resistivity was measured by a high resistance meter (6517B, KEITHLEY). The *d*33 values of the BTCT100*x* samples were measured at room temperature by a quasistatic *d*33 meter (ZJ-6BN, Institute of Acoustics Academia). The ferroelectric properties of the samples were measured by a ferroelectric analyzer at 1 Hz (Analyzer 2000E, aix-ACCT).

## 3 Results and discussion

The microstructures of BTCT100*x* are shown in Fig. 1. Because of the anisotropy of the grain growth, the growth rate of crystals along the *a*–*b* plane is greater than that along the *c*-axis, and all the samples show typical plate-like grains with random orientation. It is a typical feature of BLSFs. The introduction of Cr/Ta leads to a notable change in the size and orientation of the grains. With the increase in the Cr/Ta doping content, the average crystal size decreases from 16.3 to 7.5 μm first and then gradually increases to 23.2 μm. Meanwhile, the ratios of plate-like crystal structures parallel to the surface (marked as Par) to plate-like crystal structures perpendicular to the surface (marked as Per) are quantified through image analysis, as shown in Fig. 1(h). When the content of Cr/Ta co-doping increases to 0.03, the ratio of Par to Per gradually decreases from 1.82 to 0.67, which reflects the change in the preferred orientation of surface crystals with the change in doping level. It is evident that Cr3+ and Ta5+ co-doping gradually decreases the rate of the grain growth in the *a*–*b* plane and limits the anisotropy of the grain growth.

The XRD patterns of BTCT100*x* samples in the 2*θ* range of 10°–60° are displayed in Fig. 2. The diffraction peaks of all the samples match well with the main phase of Bi4Ti3O12 (JCPDS No. 72-1019). No secondary phase can be observed after the introduction of Cr/Ta, indicating that both Cr3+ and Ta5+ ions have been successfully incorporated into the crystal lattice of BIT due to the similar ionic radius (Cr3+: 0.615 Å, Ta5+: 0.64 Å, and Ti4+: 0.605 Å) [31]. Besides, the XRD results are in good agreement with the SEM results. With the increase in doping content, the peaks of (00*l*) begin to weaken due to the decreasing ratios of Par to Per. The strongest diffraction peak gradually changes from the (0014) peak to the (117) peak due to the preferential growth of crystal grains on the surface [32]. When *x* = 0.03, the strongest peak corresponds to the (117) crystal face, which is in agreement with the fact that the strongest peak of the layered perovskites belongs to the type of (112*m*+1) [33].

Raman spectra of the BTCT100*x* samples at room temperature are shown in Fig. 4. According to previous research, the orthorhombic BIT has 16 Raman active modes of different symmetries (6A1g + 2B1g + 8Eg) [36], which can be divided into three parts. Due to the possible overlap of vibrations and the broadening of the bands, there are 13 Raman modes (from *v*1 to *v*13) among 50–950 cm−1 as shown in Fig. 4. The Raman active modes are identified at 62, 89, 117, 182, 226, 270, 330, 356, 448, 534, 569, 614, and 849 cm−1 and are well-matched with the previous research. Modes in the low-frequency bands below 200 cm−1 (*v*1, *v*2, *v*3, *v*4) correspond to the vibrations of heavier Bi3+ ions along with a relative motion at the A-site [37,38], and no obvious shifts can be observed in this area, indicating that the Cr/Ta substitution has almost no effect on the Bi3+ vibration. The modes in the region of 200–600 cm−1 correspond to the stretching and torsion vibration modes of Ti–O bending. Furthermore, the high-frequency mode observed at 226 and 270 cm−1 (*v*5 and *v*6) can be attributed to the torsional bending of Ti–O and belongs to B2g + B3g symmetry originating from the lifting of Eg degeneracy [39,40]. The mode *v*6 shifts to lower frequency regions with an increase in *x*, indicating an alteration in the degree of structural distortion. The modes at 330 and 356 cm−1 (*v*7 and *v*8) belong to the O–Ti–O bending mode. With the increase of *x*, the mode *v*7 with B1g character slightly decreases in intensity, indicating that the O–Ti–O bending mode is changed with Cr/Ta substitution in the B-site, and the disorder in the structure changes. Moreover, the modes in the high-frequency bands above 600 cm−1 are associated with TiO6 octahedra vibrations [36]. The intensity of the modes at 614 cm−1 (*v*12) also gradually weakens with an increase in *x*, indicating the stretching change of the TiO6 octahedra. The change in Raman spectra corresponds to the result of XRD patterns, indicating that Cr/Ta complex ions have been doped into the B-site of BTCT100*x* ceramics successfully.

To study the effect of Cr/Ta co-doping on oxygen vacancy concentration and valence states of elements in the BIT ceramics, XPS characterization of the BTCT100*x* ceramics was conducted at room temperature. As shown in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b), the peak of O 1s is split into two parts: the peak with higher binding energy at 531.7 eV corresponds to the oxygen vacancy defect (* VO⋅⋅), and the peak with lower binding energy at 530.1 eV corresponds to the lattice oxygen ( OO×) [41]. By calculating the area ratio of the oxygen vacancy to lattice oxygen of BTCT0.5 and BTCT3, it can be concluded that the peak area ratio decreases from 43.85/56.15 to 15.93/84.07 as the doping level increases. The result indicates that Cr/Ta co-doping can effectively reduce the oxygen vacancy concentration. This is because the valence state of (Cr1/3Ta2/3) complex ions is higher than +4 valence, resulting in the donor doping. Bi 4f7/2 and Bi 4f5/2 binding energy peaks of the samples are located at 159.3 and 164.6 eV, respectively, as shown in Fig. 5(c). The energy difference between the two peaks is 5.3 eV, and the two peaks do not shift, indicating that the valence state of Bi ions in the samples has not changed. In addition, the binding energy locations of Ti 2p3/2 and Ti 2p1/2 peaks for fitting analysis are 485.4 and 464.4 eV, respectively, as shown in Fig. 5(d), which match the Ti–O bond energy, indicating that the valence of Ti in the samples is approximately +4 [42]. Therefore, the doping concentration of Cr/Ta complex ions has no obvious effect on the valence state of Bi3+ and Ti4+ ions. Furthermore, the peaks of O, Bi, and Ti ions shift to higher binding energy compared with the standard peaks, indicating that strongly ferroelectrically active Ta5+ ions have been successfully doped into the B-site of materials. It leads to an enhanced distortion of the crystal unit cell and, hence, high T*C in the samples [43].

where *r*A, *r*B, and *r*O are the ionic radii of the A-site cation, the B-site cation, and the oxygen ion, respectively. In general, a lower *t* value corresponds to a higher *T*C value in BLSFs. The substitutions of Ti4+ (0.605 Å) with Ta5+ (0.64 Å) and Cr3+ (0.615 Å) result in a decrease in the tolerance factor (*t*), which is responsible for an increase in *T*C values for the samples. On the other hand, the *δ* value of lattice constants and the valence bond change of TiO6 octahedra also influence the *T*C value [45]. Combined with XRD and Raman results, it can be found that Cr/Ta co-doping increases the lattice distortion of the BTCT100*x* ceramics, which also causes an increase in *T*C of samples. In addition to the change in *T*C values, there is a dielectric anomaly in the permittivity−temperature curve in the high-temperature region below *T*C, which can be attributed to the dielectric response associated with the * (VBi'''−VO⋅⋅)′ defect dipoles [46]. This dielectric anomaly weakens with the increase in the Cr/Ta doping amount, indicating that the introduction of Cr/Ta complex ions effectively reduces the  (VBi'''−VO⋅⋅)′defect dipole concentration. Figure 6(d) shows the tanδ* values of the BTCT100*x* samples at 500 ℃. As the doping amount increases, the tan*δ* values of BTCT100*x* ceramics decrease significantly. The tan*δ* value of BTCT3 is about one order of magnitude lower than that of BTCT0.5, indicating its potential for high-temperature applications. The decrease in tan*δ* may be due to the reduction of oxygen vacancies caused by Cr/Ta co-doping, which is beneficial to the increase of activation energy in oxygen vacancies. Obviously, the introduction of the Cr/Ta donor additive into the B-site of BIT effectively reduces the oxygen vacancies, which is consistent with the XPS results.

The piezoelectric performance results of BTCT100*x* are shown in Fig. 7. Figure 7(a) shows the change in *d*33 values of BTCT100*x* at room temperature. As the Cr/Ta content increases from *x* = 0.005 to 0.03, the piezoelectric constant (*d*33) increases from 17 to 26 pC·N−1, and then the piezoelectric constant (*d*33) decreases to 23 pC·N−1 when *x* = 0.04. Compared with the pure BIT ceramics (*d*33 = 6–8 pC·N−1), all co-doped ceramics exhibit much higher *d*33 values, indicating that the Cr/Ta substitution effectively improved the piezoelectric performance of the BIT ceramics. In addition, the thermal depolarization is conducted to evaluate the temperature stability of the BTCT100*x* ceramics, as shown in Fig. 7(b). *d*33 of the BTCT100*x* samples shows a small drop step before the annealing temperature reaches *T*C, and then drops rapidly. When *T* = 600 ℃, the *d*33 value of BTCT3 decreases by no more than 15% of the room temperature value, while the *d*33 value of BTCT0.5 decreases by about 30%, indicating that BTCT3 possesses better thermal stability. Figure 7(c) shows a comparison of the *d*33 values and *T*C of BTCT3 ceramics with those of BIT-based ceramics [17,20−23,33−37]. Apparently, the BTCT3 ceramics display significantly high *T*C together with large *d*33, suggesting that the strategy of Cr/Ta non-equivalent co-doping can simultaneously improve the piezoelectric coefficient and the Curie temperature of BIT-based ceramics.

The *P–E* loops at a frequency of 1 Hz under different electric fields for BTCT100*x* are shown in Figs. 8(a)–8(f). With the electric field strengthening, all *P–E* loops and residual polarization (*P*r) values of the samples become larger. For BTCT0 (*x* = 0), the *P–E* loop clearly shows weak ferroelectric loop and high leakage current. BTCT0 has low *P*r of 3.6 μC·cm−2 and high coercive field (*E*c) of 27.5 kV·cm−1 under the maximum applied field strength of 50 kV·cm−1, as shown in Fig. 8(a). Due to the existence of a large number of oxygen vacancies, BTCT0 has a high leakage current, and it is easily broken down under a low electric field [35,38,40]. As shown in Fig. 8(b), a slight increment in *P*r values is observed for *x* = 0.005, and the ferroelectricity is enhanced. Furthermore, for *x* ≥ 0.01, the loops become asymmetrical, and an internal bias field (*E*i) occurs. With the increase in *x*, the *E*c value decreases first and then increases to 27 kV·cm−1, as shown in Fig. 8(h). The remnant polarization (*P*r) of 11.5 μC·cm−2 can be observed in BTCT3, whereas that of BTCT0.5 is only 5 μC·cm−2. The improved ferroelectricity could be attributed to the lattice distortion. According to Rietveld-refined X-ray diffraction data, the increase in the *δ* value leads to distortions of the oxygen octahedral and off-centering of cations [35] and hence the improved ferroelectric properties of the samples. In addition, an increase in the grain size is also conducive to the enhancement of ferroelectricity [45]. Therefore, the change in the remanent polarization is basically consistent with the variation in the piezoelectric constant with increasing doping contents, demonstrating that the enhancement of the ferroelectricity leads to an improvement in the piezoelectric properties. Besides, the asymmetric *P–E* loops may be due to the existence of defects (A-site vacancies, oxygen vacancies, nonequivalent-doped ions, etc.), which usually leads to *E*i [52].

The DC resistivity versus temperature curves of the BTCT100*x* ceramics are shown in Fig. 9(a). As the temperature increases, the DC resistivity gradually decreases, indicating that the high temperature activates the charge carriers [32]. When *T* = 500 ℃, the resistivity value of BTCT3 is 2.8×106 Ω·cm, two orders of magnitude higher than that of the pure BIT ceramics (*ρ* 4 Ω·cm), which indicates the high-temperature functionalization and stability of piezoelectric ceramics. The activation energy is estimated from the resistivity in the range of *T* = 400–600 ℃, and the fitting results are shown in Figs. 9(b) and 9(c). It can be found that *E*a (the activation energy associated with *ρ*DC) value gradually increases as the doping amount increases. *E*a of BTCT3 is 1.35 eV, which is nearly half of the BIT band gap [53]. Besides, the *E*a values of BTCT2, BTCT3, and BTCT4 are almost equal, independent of the Cr/Ta content, which proves that intrinsic conduction dominates at high temperatures. In general, the electrical conduction behavior of BIT materials at high temperatures is dominated by ion conduction due to oxygen vacancies [54]. Oxygen vacancies are usually created due to the volatilization of bismuth during high-temperature sintering (Eq. (2)). The oxygen vacancies can be thermally activated and act as charge carriers. According to the principle of charge compensation, after the introduction of Ta2O5 and Cr2O3, Ta5+ and Cr3+ ions replace Ti4+ ions in BIT and can produce * TaTi· and  CrTi' defects (Eq. (3)). Therefore, the donor doping of (Cr1/3/Ta2/3) can effectively reduce the oxygen vacancy concentration, which is one of the main reasons for the increase in resistivity of the BTCT100x* ceramics.

To further explore the intrinsic conductive mechanism of the BTCT100*x* ceramics, the impedances of BTCT0.5 and BTCT3 were characterized at 400–650 ℃, as shown in Fig. 10. Figures 10(a) and 10(b) show the impedance plot of the BTCT0.5 sample in the measurement frequency region from 100 Hz to 10 MHz. The semicircles in the low-frequency, mid-frequency, and high-frequency regions of the complex impedance plot are generally considered to be contributed by the ceramic-electrode surface, grain boundary, and grain bulk, respectively [49]. When the temperature is 400 ℃, there are two obvious semicircles in the complex impedance plots of BTCT0.5, and thus the semicircle in the higher frequency region can be attributed to the contribution of the grains, and the semicircle in the lower frequency region can be attributed to the grain boundary effect. The grain boundary effect gradually decreases as the temperature rises; the grain contribution dominates, and the resistance decreases rapidly. When the temperature exceeds 450 ℃, a Warburg diffusion line with an inclination angle of 45° appears in the low-frequency region, and it is speculated that there is a weak ion conductance related to oxygen vacancy shift [55]. Fitting was performed using the Zview software, as shown in the illustrations in Figs. 10(a) and 10(b). The impedance patterns of both samples are in good agreement with the experimental data. Therefore, the sample impedance data can be fitted to an equivalent circuit consisting of (*R*//*C*//CPE) series combinations (Fig. 10), where *R* is the resistor, *C* is the capacitance, and CPE is the constant phase element. When the temperature is below 550 ℃, the equivalent circuit is a series of combinations of two parallel (*R*//*C*//CPE) elements (the inset in Fig. 10(a)), and as the temperature increases, the equivalent circuit becomes one (*R*//*C*//CPE) element (the inset in Fig. 10(b)). The logarithmic conductivity of BTCT0.5 obtained from the fitted *Z** data as a function of the measured temperature is shown in Fig. 10(c). The calculated *E*g (the activation energy of grain) of BTCT0.5 is 0.66 eV, and *E*gb (the activation energy of grain boundary) is 1.71 eV. Compared to BTCT0.5, only a semicircle is observed in the complex impedance plot of the BTCT3 ceramics, as shown in Figs. 10(d) and 10(e). Obviously, the single semicircle in the impedance plot can be attributed to the grain response, indicating that only the grain contributed to the conductivity of the ceramics. The transition from two semicircles to a single semicircle implies that the Cr/Ta co-doped BIT-based ceramics become electrically uniform, exhibiting highly insulating grains. The increase in the grain effect is beneficial to the enhancement of piezo-/ferroelectricity and *T*c values of the samples, which is consistent with the *P–E* results. By fitting the *Z** data and Arrhenius equation, *E*g of BTCT3 is 1.38 eV. Compared with BTCT0.5, the grain activation energy increases, and the oxygen vacancy defect is alleviated. These results prove that Cr/Ta non-equivalent co-doping effectively reduces the oxygen vacancy concentration and significantly improves the high-temperature performance.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, a series of BTCT100*x* ceramics with high performance were successfully synthesized by the solid-state reaction method. The results show that (Cr1/3/Ta2/3) non-equivalent co-doping at the B-site of the BIT ceramics can effectively reduce the concentration of oxygen vacancy defects in the BIT ceramics, improving their piezoelectric properties, dielectric properties, and high-temperature resistivity. The BTCT3 piezoceramics exhibit a high piezoelectric coefficient (26 pC·N−1) with high resistivity of 2.8×106 Ω·cm at 500 ℃ and a high residual polarization strength of 11.5 μC·cm−2. The BTCT100*x* lead-free piezoceramics also display a high Curie temperature above 687 ℃, accompanied by good thermal stability, and are expected to be a promising candidate in the field of high-temperature piezoelectricity. More importantly, this study further demonstrates that non-equivalent co-doping by combining both high and low-valence cations is an effective strategy for improving the electrical properties of BIT-based ceramics.
