## 1 Introduction

Piezoelectric materials, which are capable of interconverting mechanical and electrical energy [1], serve as critical components in sensors, oscillators, actuators, and transducers and are extensively used in automotive systems, medical devices, and aerospace technologies [2]. Lead-containing piezoelectric ceramics, represented by lead zirconate titanate (PZT) [3], occupy the majority of the piezoelectric material market because of their excellent thermal stability, high piezoelectric coefficient, and large electrostrain [4]. However, the large amount of lead contained in PZT ceramics (approximately 60 wt%) can cause serious pollution problems during production, usage, and recycling [5,6]. Given the continuous concern for environmental sustainability, governments around the world have implemented strict regulations to limit the use of toxic lead. Therefore, the development of environmentally friendly lead-free piezoelectric materials is particularly important [7]. Currently, lead-free piezoelectric systems such as barium titanate (BT) [8], sodium potassium niobate (KNN) [9], and sodium bismuth titanate (BNT) [10] have demonstrated excellent electrical properties, and some of their electric performance even exceeds that of lead-containing piezoelectric ceramics in specific applications.

BT-based materials, which possess many excellent electric properties, such as a piezoelectric constant *d*33 > 500 pC/ N and an electrically induced-field strain *S* > 0.3%, are among the most promising lead-free alternatives [7,11]. In recent years, BT-based ceramics with superior electric-induced field strain have been successfully prepared [4,12−14], but their strain hysteresis is large, limiting their application. Many efforts have been made to determine the trade-off between strain and hysteresis in BT-based systems. For example, Ce-doped BaTiO3 ceramics have an electric field-induced strain (*S*) of 0.12%, but they exhibit a large strain hysteresis of ~24% [15]. An ultrahigh strain *S* of 0.8% is subsequently achieved by fabricating ultrafine-grained BT ceramics (grain size 16]. More recently, textured-(Ba0.95Ca0.05)(Ti0.94Zr0.05Sn0.01)O3 ceramics with a moderate strain of 0.1% were prepared, but they still retained a large hysteresis of 25% [17]. Although the strain hysteresis of BT-based lead-free ceramics has improved, their large strain hysteresis still fails to meet the application requirements in high-precision devices [3]. Therefore, one of the key issues in the development of BT-based lead-free piezoelectric actuators is how to reduce low hysteresis (18].

Generally, multiphase coexistence can reduce the barrier required for domain switching compared with a composition with only a single phase (Fig. 1) and promote the rapid response of domains to an applied electric field, which improves strain hysteresis. In addition, active polar nanoregions (PNRs) can respond quickly to an external electric field [19], in favor of degrading strain hysteresis (Fig. 1). Based on the theory above, Ba(Sn0.11Ti0.89)O3 with multiphase coexistence is selected as the matrix [20,21]. Moreover, the small amount of Sr2+ ions occupying the A-site is used for crushing long-range ferroelectric order into PNRs [22,23]. Ultimately, the (1−*x*)Ba(Sn0.11Ti0.89)O3–*x*SrTiO3–0.6 wt% MnO2 (BST–*x*ST) compositions are elaborately designed, and ultralow hysteresis (4.8%) is achieved in BST–*x*ST ceramics. Rietveld refinement and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) measurements revealed rhombohedral–orthorhombic–tetragonal–cubic (R–O–T–C) quadr-phase coexistence, and the time-dependent piezoelectric amplitude tested by piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) indicated that ultralow strain hysteresis also stems from active PNRs.

## 2 Experimental

(1−*x*)Ba(Sn0.11Ti0.89)O3–*x*SrTiO3–0.6 wt% MnO2 (abbreviated as BST–*x*ST, *x* = 0.06, 0.08, 0.1, 0.13, 0.16, and 0.19 mol) ceramics were prepared via the conventional solid-state reaction method. BaCO3 (99%), TiO2 (99%), SnO2 (99.5%), and SrCO3 (99%) were weighed in a stoichiometric ratio. The chemical powders were put into a ball-milling jar with alcohol and ball-milled for 12 h. Then, the dried powders were calcined in air at 1423 K for 3 h. The calcined products were ball-milled for 12 h again. Powders with 10% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) were pressed into discs with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 1 mm. Finally, the polycrystalline ceramics were sintered in air at 1613 K for 4 h.

The phase structure of the samples was determined via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Empyrean, PANalytical B.V., the Netherlands) with copper Kα radiation (*λ* = 0.15406 nm). The XRD data were analyzed via Jade software, and Rietveld refinement data were obtained via GSAS-Ⅱ(SVN version 5799) software [24,25]. A Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR Evolution, HORIBA S.A.S., France) equipped with laser excitation at 532 nm was used to detect phonon vibrational modes, and the Raman spectral data were decomposed via Lorentz and Gaussian functions. The microstructure features of the polished and thermally etched surfaces of the ceramic samples were observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-6700F, JEOL, Japan). For electrical property measurements, both sides of the sintered samples were polished and coated with silver paste and then burned at 600 °C for 30 min to form electrodes. Dielectric properties were measured via a precision impedance analyzer (HP4294A, Agilent, USA) at temperatures ranging from −50 to 130 °C and different frequencies (1 kHz, 10 kHz, 100 kHz, and 1 MHz). To measure the ferroelectric and electrostrain properties, the ceramic samples were polished to a thickness of 400 μm and coated with a high-temperature silver electrode on both sides. A ferroelectric test system (FETS-2000, Wuhan Yanhe Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China) was used to test room-temperature *P*‒*E* loops (Polarization–electric field intensity hysteresis loops). Local polar region responses under applied voltage were tested via a PFM (MFP-3D, Oxford Instruments Asylum Research, USA), and PNR, nanodomain, and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) spot analyses were performed via a TEM (F20, FEI, USA).

## 3 Results and discussion

The room temperature and temperature dependence of the *P*–*E* and *S*–*E* hysteresis loops of the BST–*x*ST ceramics are shown in Figs. 2 and S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). As shown in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b) and S1(a) and S1(b) in the ESM, all the ceramic samples take on slender ferroelectric and electrostrain loops. Moreover, the maximum polarization and *S* first increase and then reach the maximum value at *x* = 0.08 and thus decrease linearly. A large grain size, multiphase coexistence, and nanodomains and active PNRs should be responsible for the superior electric properties [26], and they will be discussed in detail below. A comparison of the *S*–*E* curves of the BST and BST–0.13ST ceramics is presented in Figs. S2(c) and S2(d) in the ESM, and it can be seen that the strain hysteresis (*H* = ∆*S*max/*S*max × 100%) of the composition with *x* = 0.13 is significantly reduced at 40 kV/cm. In addition, it can be clearly observed in Fig. S3 in the ESM that the strain hysteresis (*H*) decreases gradually, and *H* is 4.8% at *x* = 0.08 and thus reaches the minimum value *H* of ~3% at *x* = 0.13. However, the electric field-induced strain at *x* = 0.13 is inferior (*S* ≈ 0.081%) to that at *x* = 0.08 (*S* ≈ 0.11%), and the bipolar *S*–*E* loops of the BST–*x*ST ceramic samples are shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM. The comprehensive electrical performance at *x* = 0.08 is better than that of the other compositions.

*

The BST–0.08ST samples were selected to test the temperature-dependent electrostrain and polarization loops, as shown in Figs. 2(c)–2(f). Overall, the maximum strain and polarization of the BST–0.08ST ceramics gradually decrease with increasing temperature, which is attributed to the low *T*m = 14 °C (*T*m corresponds to the temperature at the maximum dielectric constant), resulting in polarization loss and increasing symmetry of the BST–0.08ST composition during the heating process [27]. Moreover, Fig. 2(f) shows that the bipolar strain loops are symmetric and that the electrostrain in the unipolar loops is equal to that of the bipolar loops, revealing the absence of a contribution from the defect dipole. Furthermore, as evidenced in Figs. 2(c)–2(e), the ferroelectric hysteresis loops broaden gradually above 85 °C, resulting from the emergence of nonferroelectric contributions [28], and this behavior may correlate collectively with an elevated leakage current, expansion of the nonpolar cubic phase, and diminished polar domains.

The X-ray diffraction patterns of the BST–*x*ST samples are shown in Fig. 3(a). It can be clearly seen that pure perovskite structures have been successfully achieved, and no secondary phases are detected. Moreover, Fig. 3(b) clearly shows that the magnified XRD patterns near 45° and 56° shift toward high angles, which is attributed to the small radius Sr2+ (0.126 nm) substituting for the large radius Ba2+ (0.142 nm), which induces lattice distortion and a reduction in the interplanar spacing according to Bragg’s formula (2*d*sin*θ* = *nλ, *where *d* is the interplanar spacing, *θ* is the angle between the incident ray, reflected ray and the reflecting crystal plane, *λ* is the wavelength, and* n* is the reflection order) [29,30].

*

To systematically investigate the phase composition of BST–*x*ST ceramics, Rietveld refinement was conducted via the coexistence of C, R, T, and O phases via GSAS-II software. The Rietveld-refined XRD patterns of the BST–*x*ST ceramics are presented in Figs. 3(c) and S5 in the ESM, and the corresponding refinement parameters are listed in Table S1. Low-weighted residual values *w*R 31]. To verify the reliability of the fitting results, the pure C, C and R as well as the T and R–T–C–O phases were separately used to fit the XRD data of the BST–0.08ST ceramic sample via GSAS II software, as shown in Fig. 3(d). Visibly, the calculation data coincide well with the experimental data when the experimental data are fitted with the R–O–T–C phase, and *w*R and the GOF are better than those of the other samples, confirming that the BST–*x*ST sample has four-phase coexistence. Four-phase coexistence can greatly reduce the barrier required for domain switching (Fig. 1(d)) [4,18,32,33] in favor of the domain rapidly responding to external electric field stimuli, thus reducing strain hysteresis [29,30].

To further investigate the evolution of the local phase structure of BST–*x*ST ceramics, Raman spectroscopy was performed. As shown in Fig. 3(d), all the Raman vibration peaks of the BST–*x*ST ceramics are similar, indicating that their phase structures are also analogous, and these results are consistent with the XRD results. The Raman modes were decomposed by the Gaussian Lorentz function, and the fitted Raman plots of the BST–*x*ST ceramics are shown in Fig. 3(e). The Raman modes can be well fitted and decomposed into 13 independent Raman peaks [34–35]. According to the different vibrational modes, the Raman spectra are categorized into three different regions: region I (≤ 200 cm−1) is associated with A-site cations (Ba/Sr–O); region II (200–400 cm−1) is related to B-site ions (Sn/Ti–O); and region III (≥ 400 cm−1) is related to the vibration of the BO6 oxygen octahedron [36–37]. All the Raman active peaks gradually broadened and shifted toward lower wavenumbers with increasing Sr2+, which indicates that the A‒O bonds, B‒O bonds, and BO6 octahedra weakened, namely, PNRs formed. Concurrently, the progressive broadening of the M5‒M6 peaks with increasing Sr content indicates both an increasing cubic (C) phase fraction and enhanced long-range crystalline symmetry [38,39].

Figure S6 in the ESM shows the surface morphology of the BST–*x*ST samples, and it can be seen that all the sintered ceramic samples have a dense microstructure and clear grain boundaries as well as coarse grains. With increasing Sr2+ content, the grain size first increases, reaches a maximum value of ~48.7 μm at *x* = 0.08, and then decreases. A large grain size favors domain switching owing to a decrease in the grain boundary density [30,40], which should also be responsible for low hysteresis.

The temperature-dependent dielectric constant and dielectric loss of the BST–*x*ST ceramics are shown in Fig. 4. The *T*m corresponding to the maximum dielectric constant decreases gradually with increasing Sr2+, indicating that the introduction of Sr2+ induces a decrease in the transition temperature from the ferroelectric to the paraelectric phase, originating from the annihilation of local long-range ferroelectric order into PNRs [41]. Moreover, the *T*m gradually increases with increasing frequency, which indicates obvious frequency dispersion. Moreover, the dielectric constant peaks gradually broaden with increasing frequency, which is a diffuse phase transition phenomenon [42], indicating that BST–*x*ST compositions are relaxor ferroelectric [1]. This is mainly attributed to the local composition fluctuation stemming from the replacement of Ba2+ with Sr2+, leading to lattice distortion and the formation of PNRs.

The dispersion factor *γ* was used to qualitatively investigate the effect of relaxor behavior on ferroelectric ceramics. Here, the modified Curie‒Weiss function is used to fit the relationship between ln(1/*ε*r − 1/*ε*m) and ln(*T* − *T*m) [43]. The fitting results are shown in the inset in Fig. 4, from which it can be clearly observed that the fitting results are in good agreement with the measured results. The dispersion factor *γ* is approximately equal to 1.5 for all compositions, indicating that BST–*x*ST compositions are relaxor ferroelectric rather than ideal relaxors after introducing incipient SrTiO3, which is conducive to building macromultiphase coexistence [44]. Moreover, as shown in Fig. S7 in the ESM, the burn temperature (*T*B) gradually decreases with increasing Sr2+, and the *T*B is 77 °C at *x* = 0.08. Generally, polar regions barely exist beyond the *T*B, which is also the reason why the electrostrain nosedives above 75 °C for *x* = 0.08.

To comprehensively characterize the phase composition and domain architecture of the BST–0.08ST ceramics, TEM analysis was conducted, as illustrated in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b) display SAED patterns indexed via Gatan DigitalMicrograph™ software, revealing a coexisting C (*Pm** 3¯m) phase along the [ 1¯11] zone axis and a T (P*4*mm*) phase along the [* 1¯10] zone axis [45,46]. Furthermore, the diffraction spots from fast Fourier transformation are shown in Fig. 5(c), which is consistent with the phase structure of R (R*3*m*) with a zone axis of [1* 1¯0]. These results are consistent with the Rietveld XRD refinement results for the BST–0.08ST ceramics. The multiphase coexistence is conducive to flattening the barrier, facilitating domain switching [4,14,47,48] and thus reducing strain hysteresis. In addition, PNRs and stripe-like domains are clearly observed in Figs. 5(d)–5(i), and this local disordered region of approximately 2–8 nm in length and 3–6 nm in width is the PNRs resulting from local structural heterogeneity [18,49]. The formation of PNRs lowers the Landau free energy barrier, increasing the speed of ferroelectric domain switching under an external electric field, which is conducive to decreasing strain hysteresis. Moreover, in Figs. 5(e)–5(i), the mixture of stripe-like nanodomains can decrease frictional damping and thus respond quickly to the applied electric field [50], promoting electrostrain and reducing strain hysteresis.

To verify that the polar regions can respond to an applied electric field, the in situ* time-dependent domain evolution is investigated via a vertical piezoresponse force microscope (VPFM; MFP-3D, Oxford Instruments Asylum Research, USA), as presented in Fig. 6. The amplitude and phase angle of the polar regions during loading at an applied voltage of 40 V are shown in Figs. 6(b)–6(d), whereas no significant polar regions switch in amplitude, but the variation in the phase angle adjacent to the polar regions is clearly observed. We believe that the local active PNRs are responsible for ultralow strain hysteresis, whereas the inferior electrostrain is attributed to local inert PNRs, resulting in a reduced concentration of recoverable PNRs that can quickly respond to external electric field stimuli [51−53]. Moreover, the PNRs are highly active and can respond quickly to applied voltage stimuli, resulting in ultralow strain hysteresis.

## 4 Conclusions

BST–*x*ST ceramics with ultralow strain hysteresis (4.8%) are successfully prepared by elaborately designing components to build multiphase, stripe-like nanodomain, and active PNRs coexistence. Introduction of Sr2+ can crush long-range ferroelectric order into active PNRs and nanodomains coexistence due to the local compositional inhomogeneity, reducing the amount of large-size domains, which increases frictional damping. Four-phase coexistence is proved by TEM and Rietveld XRD refinement, and a mixture of stripe-like nanodomain and PNRs is identified by TEM. Formation of active PNRs verified by the temperature dependence of PFM-amplitude has flattened the Landau free energy barrier, and thus reduced frictional damping, promoting polar regions to fast respond to the applied electric field stimuli. Meanwhile, large grain size can also reduce the frictional damping of domain switching due to decreasing grain boundary density. Therefore, ultralow strain hysteresis is achieved in BST–*x*ST ceramics. This novel strategy develops a path for designing the piezoceramics components with ultralow strain hysteresis.
