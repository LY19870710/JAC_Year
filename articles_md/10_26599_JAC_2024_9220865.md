## 1 Introduction

Information security involving banknotes, certificates, artworks, medicines, and electronics, causing enormous losses due to counterfeiting, is becoming a global issue [1−4]. To overcome the problem of counterfeiting, various anti-counterfeiting means have been adopted, such as labels, luminous inks, watermarks, and 2D codes [5−7]. Among these available anti-counterfeiting technologies, pattern design strategies based on light-stimulated luminescent materials have attracted much attention owing to their bright colorfulness and multicolor display. So far, various research advances mainly focus on the selection of different types of luminescent materials, such as quantum dots [8], organic dyes [9], core–shell nanoparticles [10], and rare-earth doped oxides [11,12], to enrich the diversity of luminescent emission colors. However, the traditional single static color output can be easily duplicated and clearly fails to meet the requirements of information encryption technology.

In recent decades, the luminescent pattern has exhibited dynamic changes through a combination of stimulation modes, including thermal stimulation [13], excitation power [14], multiple lasers [15], mechanical force [16], etc. This feature makes the presented encrypted information more vivid, and further improves data security by introducing more anti-counterfeiting dimensions. For example, Tian *et al*. [11] discovered CaAl2Si2O8:Eu2+/Eu3+, which shows multicolor emissions under different excitation wavelengths and variable temperatures, and can be used as multichannel readout anti-counterfeiting materials. Ding *et al*. [17] realized efficient bimodal luminescence with tunable color output by combining upconversion emission and core–shell nanostructuring strategy, making encoded patterns more secure. Nevertheless, stimulus-responsive luminescence patterns change luminescent color by integrating two or more stimulus-responsive mechanisms, which may require more expensive near-infrared lasers and heaters, making it inconvenient for practical applications. Thus, achieving color change in a single stimulus-response mode still faces more challenges. Recently, luminescent modulation behaviors by light-induced Eu3+→Eu2+ or Eu2+→Eu3+ redox reactions have been reported, such as Ba2SiO4:Eu3+ [18], BaNa2MgP2O8:Eu3+ [19], Sr2SiO4:Eu3+ [20], Sr3SiO5:Eu3+ [21], Na2BaSiO4:Eu2+ [22], etc. These results provide some new ideas for tuning luminescent intensity or color by light-induced reduction reactions. If the luminescent colors could change dynamically by controlling laser irradiation duration, this would further improve the security level of information storage by introducing a time dimension. Despite significant advances in stimulus-dependent luminescent behavior on a time scale, such as fluorescent hydrogels and carbon dots [23,24], these materials involving strong acidic reagents or complex preparation methods have precluded their practical applications. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel dynamic anti-counterfeiting materials to improve information encryption security.

In this work, we fabricate novel luminescent modulation materials of Na2Ba2Si2O7:Eu based on our previously studied results [22], and realize the time-dependent photoluminescence under 365 nm light stimulation. The Eu2+→Eu3+ energy transfer mechanism is unraveled by light-induced oxidation. In particular, the potential applications of light-induced luminescent modulations are systematically investigated and evaluated in the information anti-counterfeiting and invisible optical storage aspects. By utilizing the unique feature of Na2Ba2Si2O7:Eu, the designed luminescent patterns or the stored data can be clearly displayed under 365 nm light irradiation, and this information would gradually disappear with increasing irradiation time, as shown in Fig. 1. Moreover, the coding and decoding processes are invisible in the bright field. The security of information encryption and storage can be greatly improved. The time-dependent luminescent modulation behavior gives new guidance for anti-counterfeiting and storage designs.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

A series of Na2Ba2-*x*Si2O7:*x*Eu (*x* = 0, 0.001, 0.003, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, and 0.04) (NBS:*x*Eu) compositions were synthesized by a high-temperature solid-state reaction method. The starting materials were Na2CO3 (Aladdin, 99.99%), BaCO3 (Aladdin, 99.99%), SiO2 (Alfa Aesar, 99.5%), and Eu2O3 (Aladdin, 99.99%). The raw materials were weighed according to stoichiometric ratios, mixed and ground in an agate mortar with anhydrous ethanol for 3 times, and then pre-sintered in air at 800 ℃ for 4 h. The pre-sintered powders were again placed in a mortar and mixed with anhydrous ethanol and ground for 2 times. 10 wt% of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) was added for granulation. The powders were pressed into a disk of 12 mm in diameter and 1 mm in thickness, and then heated in air at 550 ℃ for 6 h to remove PVA. Finally, the samples were sintered at 970 ℃ for 4 h in a reductive atmosphere (5% H2/95% N2). The powder samples were sintered directly after grinding under the same conditions as above.

### 2.2 Structure and property characterizations

The phase structure of the samples was characterized using a Cu target Kα radiation powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD; MiniFlex600, Rigaku, Japan) with a scanning step of 0.01°. The surface micromorphology and elemental composition of the samples were characterized using a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; G300, Zeiss, Germany). Elemental binding energy and chemical compositions were measured using an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; VG, ESCALAB 250XI, Thermo Scientific, UK). The diffuse reflectance spectra were measured using a UV/Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2700, SHIMADZU, Japan). The photoluminescent excitation (PLE) and emission (PL) spectra were measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF-5301, SHIMADZU, Japan). Electron paramagnetic resonance spectra were obtained by an electron paramagnetic resonance spectrometer (EPR; A300, Bruker, Germany). Thermoluminescence (TL) spectra are detected with the SL18 TL analyzer in the temperature range of 30–400 ℃. The fluorescence lifetime decay curves of the samples were measured using a transient steady-state fluorescence spectrometer (FLS1000, Edinburgh Instruments, UK). A commercial diode laser (SHE-T8, SHREPA, China) at 365 nm (172 mW·cm−2) was used to irradiate samples.

As demonstrated in Fig. 2(a), various compounds can be selectively achieved according to the Na2O–BaO–SiO2 ternary phase diagram [25]. Herein, silicate compounds (chemical formula of Na2Ba2Si2O7) with the nominal chemical composition of Na2O–2BaO–2SiO2 are specially designed and prepared by a high-temperature solid phase method. Figure 2(b) provides XRD patterns of NBS:*x*Eu. All samples exhibit a main phase of Na2Ba2Si2O7 (PDF#28-1044) and a small amount of Na2SiO3 (PDF#16-0818) phase (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). Figures 2(c) and 2(d) present wide-scanning XPS spectra and high-resolution Eu 3d XPS spectra of the NBS:0.01Eu compositions, respectively. Eu2+ and Eu3+ ions coexist in the NBS matrix (Fig. 2(d)). Also, it could be qualitatively analyzed that the Eu2+ content is less compared to Eu3+. SEM images of the representative sample of NBS:0.003Eu are shown in Figs. 2(e) and 2(f). Some cracks occur on the sample surface due to high-temperature sintering. Many large grains are produced, and embedded in the NBS matrix. Also, Na, Ba, Si, O, and Eu elements uniformly disperse into the NBS matrix according to elemental mappings in Fig. 2(g).

### 3.2 Reversible luminescence modulation

The normalized PLE and PL spectra for NBS:0.003Eu are shown in Fig. S2(a) in the ESM. The PLE spectrum (*λ*Em = 444 nm) shows a broad peak at 355 nm and a strong absorption at 254 nm, which can be attributed to Eu2+ activator absorption and electron transition from the valence band to the conduction band, respectively [26]. The weak 594 nm (5D0→7F1) and 611 nm (5D0→7F2) emissions of Eu3+ can be observed by 393 nm excitation (Fig. S2(b) in the ESM). Under 355 nm excitation, all samples exhibit broadband emission centered at 444 nm, corresponding to the typical 4f–5d energy level of Eu2+ [27]. In Figs. 3(a) and 3(b), it is found that pure Na2Ba2Si2O7 shows a weak purple emission under UV irradiation, which may be caused by oxygen-related defects [28]. The PL intensity gradually increases and then decreases with increasing the Eu doping concentration, where the optimal doping concentration is *x* = 0.003. The color changes of the blue emission can be clearly seen by naked eye, as shown in the inset of Fig. 3(b).

When the NBS:0.003Eu sample is irradiated by a diode laser of 365 nm for 2 min (172 mW·cm−2), the PL intensity notably decreases, as shown in Fig. 3(c) and Fig. S3 in the ESM. The irradiated sample shows a large color contrast (the inset of Fig. 3(c)). To characterize the degree of PL intensity quenching, the luminescent modulation rate (Δ*R*t) is defined by Eq. (1):

(1) ΔRt=(R0−R1)/R0×100%

where *R*0 and *R*1 are the emission intensity before and after irradiation, respectively. The calculated Δ*R*t values are shown in Fig. 3(d). When the Eu concentration is below 0.01, the samples maintain higher Δ*R*t values (85%–89%). With further increasing Eu content, Δ*R*t starts to decrease. In particular, the luminescent modulation is also present in the undoped sample (Δ*R*t = 14.4%), which may be caused by photogeneration defects in the matrix [29]. Besides, the luminescent quenching behavior is a reversible process. As shown in Fig. 3(e) and Fig. S4 in the ESM, the Δ*R*t value can be well recovered by alternating 365 nm irradiation and thermal treatment in a reductive atmosphere (600 ℃ for 30 min). After undergoing five reversible cycles, the Δ*R*t value only shows a slight change, suggesting its excellent repeatability. This unique feature makes the NBS:*x*Eu samples suitable for the visualization display of optical information in dark field. When the irradiated sample is excited or read out by a 365 nm laser (Fig. S2(c) in the ESM), the emission peaks at 611 nm progressively become more distinct with increasing irradiation time, resulting in a blue to weak purple color change after irradiation. These effects are consistent with the results of BMA:Eu reported by Amidani *et al*. [30]. As shown in Fig. 3(f), the irradiated samples show a distinct color contrast, which can realize many kinds of pattern displays, such as a “star” pattern. Nevertheless, these patterns are invisible in bright field. This feature provides an excellent solution for designing “invisible optical information storage”.

To further investigate the dynamic processes of the light-induced luminescent modulation, PL spectral changes are measured under 365 nm irradiation at different irradiation time, as shown in Fig. 4. With increasing the irradiation time, the PL intensity gradually decreases (Figs. 4(a)–4(c)). The dynamic color change from the luminescent quenching can be clearly observed in the insets of Figs. 4(a)–4(c). The strong blue emission at the initial state becomes dark with 365 nm irradiation time ranging from 0 to 120 s. Except for the dynamic luminescence quenching behavior, it is found that the full width at half maxima (FWHM) of PL spectra alters significantly with irradiation time.

*

Figures 4(d) and 4(f) show normalized PL spectra with different irradiation time. Although the 365 nm irradiation does not cause any shifts of PL peak positions, FWHM gradually increases with the irradiation time (Figs. 4(g)–4(i)). To deeply analyze the dynamic luminescent quenching processes with the irradiation time, a double-exponential function is adopted to intuitively describe this behavior, as Eq. (2):

(2) y=A1×exp(−x/t1)+A2×exp(−x/t2)+y0

where *A*1, *A*2, and *y*0 are the constants, *y* is the PL intensity, *x* is the irradiation time, *t*1 and *t*2 is the time required to decay to the original 1/e. The fitting curves with Eq. (2) are well consistent with the experimental data (Figs. 4(g)–4(i)). The detailed fitting parameters are listed in Table S1 in the ESM. The PL intensity reaches half of the original intensity in the first 10 s, and nearly reaches the saturation within 60 s. From this, the quenching process of the blue emission is obviously divided into two different degradation stages: the fast decrease process (depending on *t*1) and the slow decrease process (depending on *t*2). In the fast decrease process, the degradation rate of the sample (*x* = 0.005) is significantly higher than the others, which can be reflected in *t*1 (*t*1 is about one-fifth of the other concentration). While in the slow decrease process, three components degrade to a similar extent (*t*2 is comparable). Such a nonlinear variation also further suggests that the blue quenching processes may be coming from different mechanisms for the fitted degradation stages.

### 3.3 Luminescence modulation mechanism

The associated mechanisms with luminescent degradation in NBS:*x*Eu are revealed by microscale characterizations (EPR and TL, etc.). Figure 5(a) gives the low temperature (*T* = 77 K) *in-situ* EPR spectra of Eu2+ for NBS:0.003Eu before and after 365 nm irradiation (5 min), with the magnetic field frequency (*F*) = 9.069 GHz. It is notably found that the peak intensity of the Eu2+ EPR signal distinctly decreases, indicating the reduction of Eu2+ contents in the NBS host [31]. Meanwhile, these Eu2+ ions would be oxidized to Eu3+ ions after 365 nm light irradiation, thereby leading to the blue emission quenching. These results are in agreement with the quenching behavior of BaMgAl10O17:Eu2+ phosphors after vacuum UV irradiation [30]. Unfortunately, we did not observe the enhanced emission of Eu3+ ions (Fig. S2(b) in the ESM), which may be correlated to non-radiative relaxation behaviors of Eu3+ ions [32]. The relative content of the Eu2+ oxidation can be qualitatively analyzed by the corresponding area ratios of Eu2+ EPR peaks before and after 365 nm irradiation (∆*R*Eu). The calculated peak area ratio of the Eu2+ signal is only about 54.5%. In view of such high luminescent modulation ratio (Δ*R*t > 80%), we can conclude that the Eu2+ oxidation is not the only mechanism involved, implying that there may be additional mechanisms responsible for the luminescent quenching.

According to previously reported results in luminescent materials [30,33,34], some defects created during the high-temperature sintering or light irradiation may act as killer centers to inhibit luminescent emission. In this work, NBS:*x*Eu materials are fabricated by the high-temperature sintering in a reductive atmosphere, and some vacancy-related defects unavoidably appear in the host. Among these defects, oxygen vacancy defects play an important role in trapping carriers [35]. In NBS:0.01Eu, the EPR signals of the oxygen vacancy of are clearly detected before and after 365 nm irradiation, as shown in Fig. 5(b). The oxygen vacancy EPR signal before irradiation is mainly caused by the high-temperature sintering in a reducing atmosphere. After 365 nm irradiation, the EPR intensity shows a marked increase, indicating that the UV irradiation easily induces more single ionized oxygen vacancies (* VO·) [33]. The photo-generated defects combined with intrinsic defects would act as traps for capturing electrons and contribute to the Eu2+ emission quenching. As observed in the reflectivity spectra of Fig. 5(c) and Fig. S5 in the ESM, the appearance of a small absorption band (400–500 nm) also demonstrates the electron capture process of light-generated defects, which finally brings about the luminescent modulation (ΔR*t = 14.4%) in pure NBS (Fig. 3(d)). In Fig. 5(d), the fitted TL spectra show that there are multiple defect energy levels with different depths in NBS:0.01Eu. The trap depth distribution is in the range of 0.71–0.98 eV. Moreover, the decrease in the average decay lifetime after irradiation further proves the energy transfer between defects and electrons, as shown in Figs. 5(e) and 5(f) [36]. The detailed calculation can be seen in the ESM.

Based on these results mentioned above, two factors are in responsible for the significant luminescence quenching: (i) light-induced oxidation of Eu2+; (ii) defect-induced nonradiative energy transfer. The luminescence quenching schematic is shown in Fig. 5(g). Eu2+ ions are oxidized to Eu3+ by the electron transfer between Eu2+ and the defect after UV irradiation. The oxidation process can be expressed by Eqs. (3) and (4):

(3) OO∗→HightemperatureVO⋅⋅+12O2↑

(4) Eu2++VO⋅⋅→hvEu3++VO⋅

At the same time, the light-induced defects are regarded as killer centers that have a quenching effect on the nearby Eu2+ emission [30]. In Fig. 5(h), the electrons of Eu2+ are excited to the 5d state and subsequently captured by the traps, resulting in the oxidation of Eu2+ to Eu3+ [21], which is a major contribution to the luminescent quenching. Besides, the electrons from the valence band (VB) are excited to the conduction band (CB), which would rapidly relax to the metastable energy level, and then induce the emission quenching by the non-radiative energy transfer between electrons and traps (Fig. 5(i)).

### 3.4 Anti-counterfeiting and invisible optical storage applications

In the field of luminescent anti-counterfeiting technologies, effective readout of the stored information is very essential. Compared with the availably static anti-counterfeiting means, dynamic anti-counterfeiting under single-mode reading brings more convenience and security. Based on the time-dependent luminescence tuning behavior of NBS:*x*Eu materials, the high-security-level anti-counterfeiting and optical information storage can be well realized by facile patterned designs. As proof of concept, we demonstrate the feasibility as displays using the fabricated NBS:0.01Eu powders. Any 2D patterns (such as QR code) can be realized by irradiating 365 nm light from a pre-defined photomask, as shown in Fig. 6(a). By comparing exposed and unexposed areas, these patterns can be read out, and clearly observed in the dark field by a portable 365 nm lamp. However, it is invisible under natural light, in favor of improving the security levels of the stored information.

*
Fig. 6Visualization displays of dynamic anti-counterfeiting and invisible optical storage in NBS:0.01Eu. (a) Schematic illustration of optical storage applications. (b) Time-dependent dynamic anti-counterfeiting design. (c) Writing, reading, and erasing processes for invisible optical information storage.

Furthermore, the luminescent intensity can be dynamically adjusted by controlling the irradiation time, which can realize the really dynamic anti-counterfeiting. In Fig. 6(b), a QR code is produced using UV photolithography. The written code information can be easily read out with 365 nm light like those printed on paper with invisible inks [37]. In particular, this information gradually disappears with increasing the irradiation time from 0 to 180 s, and the effective information can be accessed only within a specific length of time, which prevents it from being counterfeited by the secondary reading, and reaches the purpose of dynamic anti-counterfeiting.

The high luminescent modulation performance enables NBS:x*Eu materials to possess high-resolution patterning ability. This feature indicates the feasibility of storing binary data as well as pattern information. As shown in Fig. 6(c), the two states before and after light irradiation can be defined as “1” and “0”, respectively. The written data is carried out using a common UV lamp with a wavelength of 365 nm and lower power density of 34 mW·cm−2. However, the stored binary data is invisible in bright field. Only when exposed to 365 nm light, the written information can be effectively read out. Also, it is spontaneously erased by increasing the irradiation time. Upon the thermal stimulus of the NBS:0.01Eu powders under a moderate temperature in a reductive atmosphere, the sample would once restore its initial state, showing excellent reversibility and repeatability.

## 4 Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed novel luminescence modulation materials of Na2Ba2Si2O7:Eu. When exposed to 365 nm light, the samples exhibit the significant luminescence quenching of Eu2+, and change emission color from blue to purple. By controlling the irradiation time, the luminescent modulation degree can be dynamically tuned, showing a time-dependent behavior. The unique features were attributed to the combination of the oxidation of Eu2+ ions and non-radiative energy transfer of light-induced defects. As a proof-of-concept experiment, the visualization displays based on the time-dependent luminescence modulation were demonstrated. Such photo-sensitive samples can be well-written, read, and erased by a portable UV lamp. The unique optical property of the NBS:*x*Eu promises potential advantages in the dynamic anti-counterfeiting and invisible optical storage.
