## 1 Introduction

The work function, defined as the minimum energy required to transfer an electron from the interior of a solid to its surface, is a fundamental parameter in various technological applications [1,2]. It plays a crucial role in catalysis [3], solar cells [4−7], thermionic energy conversion [8], and other applications. Materials with low work functions, distinguished by their strong electron-donating ability, are particularly desirable owing to their widespread utility. They are extensively used as electron emitters, electron-injection materials, and electron donors in diverse applications. Moreover, low-work-function materials play a pivotal role in catalytic processes by increasing the reaction rates and selectivity. Notably, they facilitate the activation of inert molecules such as N2 and CO2, thereby enabling key chemical transformations [9−15].

Owing to their structural adaptability, compositional diversity, tunable physical properties, and chemical stability, perovskites constitute an exceptionally versatile class of materials that have attracted considerable attention in recent years [16−19]. This inherent flexibility enables perovskites to exhibit a wide range of adjustable characteristics, including the modulation of work functions [1,2]. Moreover, perovskites have been widely applied in the fields of electrochemistry [20,21], catalysis [22], and other fields. However, the vast complexity of the data associated with perovskites (Figs. 1(a)–1(c)) poses a significant challenge to conventional trial-and-error approaches for material discovery, rendering them highly inefficient.

With the rapid progress of machine learning (ML) and density functional theory (DFT), their use has become a powerful paradigm in contemporary materials design [23−38]. These methodologies have enabled the rational exploration of materials for diverse applications, such as catalysts [39], molecular organic light-emitting diodes [40], and photovoltaic technologies, including solar cells [41−44]. Notably, the ML–DFT synergy holds great promise for accurately predicting the work functions of perovskite oxides, offering crucial insights into the targeted design of functional materials.

In this study, a data-driven approach was developed to efficiently identify stable perovskite oxides with low work functions by integrating ML with DFT calculations. The motivation for this approach originated from the discovery of inorganic electrides, a novel class of materials characterized by exceptionally low work functions (~2.5–4.0 eV) and demonstrated effectiveness in catalysis and ion battery electrode applications [9−12,19,39,45−48].

Experimentally synthesized perovskites exhibit a variety of crystal structures, including cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, and other phases. These materials also expose multiple crystallographic surfaces, and the measured work function represents a weighted average of the contributions from all exposed facets. However, owing to the accessibility and simplicity of the input features in available ML datasets for work function prediction [1,2,49], this study focuses on cubic perovskites, specifically ABO3-type single perovskites and A2BB'O6-type double perovskites. For the latter, we constructed slab models with AO-terminated (001) surfaces. A screening criterion was applied to identify candidates with AO-terminated (001) surfaces exhibiting work functions below 2.5 eV [2]. To initiate the screening process, a dataset comprising the computed work functions of AO-terminated (001) surfaces (Fig. 1(d) and Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) for 683 known perovskite oxides, including both single and double perovskites, was used to train the ML model. The trained model was then employed to predict the work functions of 23,822 perovskite oxides. Based on these predictions, a refined selection of 27 stable perovskite oxides with low work functions was validated through thermodynamic stability ML prediction, high-precision DFT calculations, and experimental validations. This integrated ML-DFT framework significantly enhanced the efficiency of discovering promising perovskite materials, offering valuable insights for their potential applications.

### 2.1 Construction of perovskite structures

The cubic ABO3-type perovskite crystal structure (Fig. 1(a)) features a framework where corner-sharing BO6 octahedra encompass a larger A site at the center. By substituting B sites with different ions, an A2BB'O6-type double perovskite structure is formed, as illustrated in Fig. 1(b). The elements under consideration for this study are highlighted in Fig. 1(c). Each square represents a specific element, with pink indicating occurrences at the A sites, green indicating occurrences at the B and B' sites, orange indicating oxygen, and gray indicating no occurrences [1,50]. To facilitate calculation, the structure of ABO3-type perovskites was expanded to maintain unity with the A2BB'O6-type double perovskite structure in this work.

### 2.2 Design framework

The multistage design framework (Fig. 2) for identifying perovskite oxides with work functions of AO-terminated (001) surfaces below 2.5 eV involves separate ML predictions for work function and thermodynamic stability, followed by DFT and experimental validations. Notably, two distinct ML models were employed—one for work function prediction and the other for thermodynamic stability assessment. Both models were applied to the same prediction space, and only candidates identified by both models were selected for subsequent DFT validation.

### 2.3 Theoretical calculation

Work function input dataset preparation: Initially, an input dataset containing work function values of perovskite oxide AO-terminated (001) surfaces was compiled to train and test the work function ML model. Slab structures with AO-terminated (001) surfaces of A2BB'O6 perovskite (Fig. 1(d) and Fig. S1 in the ESM) were constructed. The slab model was constructed using 9 layers of atoms with a 1×1 unit cell of A2BB'O6-type perovskites. The periodic slabs were separated by 15 Å of vacuum to avoid interference between layers along the [001] direction. Only the atoms in the outermost two layers of the slab were allowed to move during geometry optimization. After the perovskite slab model was optimized, the work function was calculated via Eq. (1) Φ=Φ0−EF

The tolerance factor (*t*) and octahedral factor (* μ) are commonly used to evaluate the stability of perovskites. The calculation formulae for the tolerance factor (t*) and the octahedral factor (*μ*) are outlined via Eqs. Work function prediction based on ML: To construct an appropriate ML model of the work functions of perovskites, three different ML regression algorithms were tested, including gradient boosting regression tree (GBRT) [51], random forest regression (RFR) [52], and support vector regression (SVR) [53]. Scikit-learn (version 1.3.2) [54] implementations of these ML algorithms were used. Finally, it was confirmed that the successful utilization of the GBRT algorithm could enable the construction of an efficient work function ML model. Then, feature engineering techniques were utilized to identify relevant features crucial for accurate work function prediction. The work function ML model was subsequently applied to the extensive perovskite dataset to predict their work functions, with a focus on isolating those below the threshold of 2.5 eV.

In this study, the input dataset was randomly split into two parts: 90% and 10% of the dataset. 10% of the dataset was the test dataset, whereas 90% of the dataset was the sum of the training dataset and the validation dataset, which was simply referred to as the training dataset in this study.

To eliminate the dimensional influence between data of different features, the input dataset of the work function was normalized to make different features comparable via the standard deviation standardization method (Eq. (4)), where X*i and Normalized(*X*i) represent the original and normalized values of features, respectively; *X*mean represents the mean value of the features and Std(*X*) represents the standard deviation value of the features.

(4) Normalized(Xi)=Xi−XmeanStd(X)

Two indicators, the mean squared error (MSE) and determination coefficient (*R*2), were used to evaluate the performance of the work function ML regression models, which were obtained via Eqs. (5) and (6), respectively, where *n* represents the amount of data in the dataset; *y**i* represents the targeted value; * y¯ represents the average value of all the targeted values; y*pred represents the value predicted by the ML model.

(5) MSE=1n∑i=1n(ypred−yi)2

(6) R2=1−∑i=1n(ypred−yi)2∑i=1n(y¯−yi)2

Bootstrapping sensitivity analysis (1000 iterations) implemented in the Scikit-learn Python package was adopted to analyze the robustness of the ML work function model. A confidence interval (CI) estimation of the sample mean based on a *t* distribution was used, and the confidence interval was 95%. The interquartile range (IQR) method was used to identify outliers (values beyond Q3±1.5 × IQR). The IQR was calculated via Eq. (7), where Q3 was the third quartile, and Q1 was the first quartile.

(7) IQR=Q3−Q1

Thermodynamic stability input dataset preparation: There are several open-source material computing database platforms that are built from DFT calculations, such as the Open Quantum Materials Database (OQMD) [55] and Materials Project [56], which include the energy above the convex hull value (*E*hull). *E*hull was used as a criterion to judge the thermodynamic stability of the materials in this work. *E*hull was defined via Eq. (8) [57]:

(8) EhullA2BB′O6=EfA2BB′O6−Ech

where * EhullA2BB′O6 is the E*hull of the A2BB'O6 perovskite; *E*ch is the convex hull energy of the A2BB'O6 perovskite. The formation energy * EfA2BB′O6 of each structure was calculated via Eq. (9):

(9) EfA2BB′O6=EA2BB′O6−2μA−μB−μB′−6μO

where  EA2BB′O6 represents the total energy of the A2BB'O6 perovskite; μ*A, *μ*B, *μ*B', and *μ*o represent the chemical potentials of A, B, B', and oxygen, respectively.

Many researchers have directly used these *E*hull data in these databases for analysis. Talapatra *et al*. [57] built ML models using perovskite data from the Materials Project to screen for thermodynamically stable single and double perovskites. Balachandran *et al*. [58] constructed classification ML models and used *E*hull values from OQMD to identify thermodynamically stable compounds.

In this study, the *E*hull input dataset for the thermodynamic stability ML model was obtained from the Materials Project and OQMD databases. During the high-precision DFT verification in this study, Eqs. (8) and (9) were used to calculate the *E*hull values for the compounds.

Thermodynamic stability prediction based on ML: The random forest classification (RFC) [52] algorithm was used to develop a model for the thermodynamic stability of perovskites. The input dataset of ML was randomly split into a training dataset (90%) and a test dataset (10%). The input data of thermodynamic stability were also normalized via the standard deviation standardization method, as expressed in Eq. (4) above, before performing model training.

Validation of candidate structures via DFT calculations: High-precision DFT calculations were conducted to validate the thermodynamic stability, dynamic stability, and work functions. Dynamic stability was verified by the phonon dispersion of perovskites, which was calculated via Phonopy software (version 1.10.9) implemented in VASP (version 5.4.4) [59]. Perovskite structures that exhibited no imaginary frequencies in their phonon spectra were considered dynamically stable.

All DFT calculations for selected perovskite oxides were carried out via the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) (version 5.4.4) [59]. The projector-augmented wave method (PAW) [60] and generalized gradient approximation (GGA) [61], in the form of Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE), were used for the DFT calculations, and Hubbard U correction was not considered in this study [2,57]. For the structural optimization of the bulk and slab, the plane-wave basis-set cutoff energy, convergence criterion of self-consistent iterations, and Brillouin zone sampling on a grid of spacing were set to 500 eV, 1×10−5 eV, and 2π×0.03 Å−1, respectively. The absolute value of the force on each atom was set to 0.03 eV∙Å−1 [14]. Spin polarization was accounted for in the DFT calculations. These parameters can meet the requirements of structural optimization while ensuring the consistency of the input data of the ML models.

### 2.4 Experimental methods

Compound synthesis and characterization: Ba2TiWO8 was prepared via a hydrothermal synthesis method. First, Ba(NO3)2, C16H36O4Ti, and (NH4)6H2W12O40 were mixed at a stoichiometric ratio of *n*(Ba) : *n*(Ti) : *n*(W) = 2 : 1 : 1. The above ingredients were dissolved in deionized water. Then, 30 mL of ethylene glycol (EG) and 5 mL of 5 mol∙L-1 NaOH solution were added to the deionized water, and the resulting solution had a total volume of 120 mL. The solution was stirred evenly and then transferred to a Teflon-lined reaction vessel. The Teflon-lined reaction vessel was directly placed in an oven at 180 °C for 24 h, removed directly, and cooled to room temperature in air. After that, the reaction product was cleaned with deionized water and ethanol, dried at 400 °C for 8 h, cooled to room temperature, and then milled in a high-speed ball mill for use.

Ba2FeMoO6 was prepared via a solid-state synthesis method. BaO, Fe2O3, and MoO3 were mixed at a stoichiometric ratio of *n*(Ba) : *n*(Fe) : *n*(Mo) = 2 : 1 : 1. The mixture was thoroughly milled in a high-speed ball mill for 5 h. The mixture was subsequently calcined in air at 600 °C for 5 h. After another 5 h of ball milling in a high-speed ball mill, the mixture was calcined again at 1000 °C for 10 h under an Ar/H2 (10%) atmosphere. The mixture was cooled to room temperature and then ground for use.

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed via an X-ray powder diffractometer with a Cu Kα radiation source (PANalytical X’Pert PRO, the Netherlands). The compound morphology was obtained by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; TESCAN CLARA, Czech Republic). The elemental distributions of the compounds were obtained via an energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS; Xplore 30, Oxford Instruments, UK). The work function of each sample was measured via an ultraviolet photoelectron spectrometer (ESCALAB XI+, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The element concentration was evaluated via an inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscope (ICP-AES; Agilent 5110, USA).

Synthesis of Ru-loaded catalysts: Ru-loaded Ba2TiWO8 catalysts were prepared via an impregnation method. First, 30 mL of tetrahydrofuran (THF) was added to a beaker, followed by the uniform dispersion of the desired amount of Ru3(CO)12 into the solution. The mixture was stirred at room temperature until the THF completely evaporated. The resulting material was subsequently subjected to a controlled heating process: It was heated at a rate of 5 °C∙min−1 to 80 °C and maintained at this temperature for 12 h, followed by further heating at the same rate to 110 °C, where it was held for 6 h before being cooled to ambient temperature. The obtained catalysts were then directly used for performance evaluation and characterization without any additional treatment.

Ammonia synthesis performance evaluation: NH3 synthesis catalytic performance was evaluated in a fixed-bed reactor with a quartz tube reactor (for activity evaluation at 0.1 MPa) or a stainless-steel reactor (for activity evaluation at high pressures). In a typical run, 0.1 g of catalyst was loaded into the reactor, and a flow of 60 mL∙min−1 (N2 : H2 = 1 : 3) was introduced into the reactor. The catalyst was heated to 400 °C and held at this temperature for 2 h, after which the sampling was started. The produced NH3 was trapped with H2SO4 and analyzed by ion chromatography (IC). The reaction activities were calculated based on the NH3 produced in a certain time interval. The activation energies were calculated via the Arrhenius equation.

Ammonia decomposition performance evaluation: NH3 decomposition catalytic performance was evaluated in a fixed-bed reactor with a quartz tube reactor for activity evaluation at 0.1 MPa. In a typical run, 0.05 g of catalyst was loaded into the reactor. The catalyst was treated with the following program: 5 °C∙min−1 up to 400 °C, held for 3 h in a H2 atmosphere (30 mL∙min−1), and then cooled to 300 °C in an argon atmosphere (30 mL∙min−1). The gas flow was subsequently switched to NH3 (15 mL∙min−1), corresponding to a weight hourly space velocity (WHSV) of 18,000 mL∙gcat−1∙h−1. The reaction was carried out in the temperature range of 300–600 °C. The concentrations of the outlet gases were analyzed via an online gas chromatograph (GC). NH3 conversion ( XNH3) was calculated via Eq. (10), where [NH3]in and [NH3]out are the molar flow rates of ammonia in the feed and exit gases, respectively. The activation energies were calculated via the Arrhenius equation.

(10) XNH3=([NH3]in−[NH3]out)/[NH3]in×100%

Electrochemical energy storage measurements: Electrochemical performance was evaluated via CR2032-type coin cells [62,63], with Ba2FeMoO6 as the working electrode and lithium foil as the counter electrode. The coin cells were assembled in an argon-filled glove box, maintaining O2 and H2O concentrations below 10 ppm. For electrode preparation, a slurry was prepared by thoroughly mixing 60 wt% of active material, 30 wt% of carbon black, and 10 wt% of polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) in 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP). The resulting slurry was uniformly coated onto a copper foil and subsequently dried under vacuum at 60 °C for 24 h. The dried copper foil was then punched into circular electrodes with a diameter of 12 mm, yielding electrodes with an active material loading of approximately 1.0 mg∙cm−2.

The electrochemical cell configuration included lithium foil as the counter electrode, 1 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate (EC) and diethyl carbonate (DEC) (1 : 1, v/v) as the electrolyte, and a Celgard 2400 membrane as the separator. Galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) measurements and galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) analyses were conducted via a Neware battery test system (China) at various current densities within a voltage window of 0.01–3.0 V. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were performed via a CHI660E electrochemical workstation (CH Instruments, China).

### 3.1 Dataset preparation

The quality of the input dataset plays a critical role in constructing an effective ML model. An ideal dataset should be representative, diverse, sufficiently large, and highly precise. In this study, an input dataset for the work function of perovskites with AO-termination (001) surfaces was developed. It consists of 683 interface work function values, including 593 entries corresponding to ABO3-type single perovskites and 90 entries corresponding to A2BB'O6-type double perovskites. The work functions of the 593 single perovskites were sourced from the dataset calculated by Xiong *et al*. [1] via DFT calculations, whereas the work functions of the 90 double perovskites were computed in this study via the same DFT calculation parameters.

To facilitate data analysis, the input dataset was visualized through graphical representations (Fig. 3(a) and Figs. S2 and S3 in the ESM), illustrating the relationships between the tolerance factor (*t*), octahedral factor (* μ), and work function of the perovskites. Data distribution analysis revealed that the work function values ranged from 0.8 to 10.0 eV, with corresponding t* values between 0.7 and 1.3, and * μ values between 0.3 and 0.7. The dataset exhibited a relatively uniform distribution, ensuring its suitability for ML model training. Overall, the input dataset meets the fundamental criteria required for effective ML-based predictions.

### 3.2 Algorithm selection and feature engineering

The input dataset included not only the work function values of the perovskites but also values of features associated with their A and B atoms in the case of ABO3-type single perovskites and A, B, and B' atoms for A2BB'O6-type double perovskites. For the double perovskites, symmetric and antisymmetric compound features were used [57]. For a given feature *P*, the symmetric compound feature was calculated as * PB+ = (P*B *+ P*B')/2, and the antisymmetric compound feature was * PB− = |P*B *− P*B'*|*/2, where *P*B and *P*B' are the elemental properties of the B and B' sites of the perovskites.

The 55 intrinsic elemental properties were selected across multiple dimensions, including geometric parameters, electronic structures, and other fundamental characteristics. This eliminates the need for additional DFT calculations during feature construction and allows for efficient high-throughput screening across large chemical spaces. Prior to performing model training, missing values in the training dataset were imputed using the mean of the available values for each feature, and then 55 features were normalized via the standard deviation standardization method. The input dataset of ML was randomly split into a training dataset (90%) and a test dataset (10%).

Selecting an appropriate ML algorithm is crucial for developing an accurate ML model. Currently, various ML regression algorithms have been successfully applied in materials discovery. In this study, three different ML algorithms—GBRT, SVR, and RFR—were employed for training the work function ML model. To ensure a fair comparison, all three algorithms were trained and tested on the same dataset of perovskite work functions. Hyperparameter optimization for the three ML models was conducted via the randomized-search-cross-validation (randomized-search-CV) method, implemented in the Scikit-learn Python package. The R*2 and MSE values of the test dataset for each algorithm are presented in Fig. 3(b). Among the three models, the GBRT exhibited the highest *R*2 value (96.11%) and the lowest MSE value (0.14 eV), indicating superior predictive performance. Therefore, the GBRT was selected to establish the mapping between the input features and work function values, enabling the prediction of work functions for unexplored perovskites.

Feature selection was also a critical step in ML model development, as the target property was influenced by multiple features. Effective features should comprehensively characterize a given material while distinguishing it from others. A well-structured feature selection process removes redundant features and preserves the relevant features, ultimately improving machine learning algorithm performance [64]. Fifty-five features were used to train the work function ML regression model with the GBRT algorithm. The recursive feature elimination method (RFE) [65,66] implemented in the Scikit-learn Python package was subsequently used to select appropriate features. RFE was an embedded backward elimination strategy that iteratively built a feature ranking by removing the least important features at each step. One feature was eliminated at each step until the number of features reached one. According to the results of RFE, the appropriate features and the number of features can be determined. The GBRT algorithm incorporated with RFE was used to efficiently exclude the features that have less impact on the work function. To improve the effectiveness of feature selection, each step of RFE was calculated on a randomly selected test dataset over 10 independent runs.

The mean *R*2 and MSE values as a function of the number of features are shown in Fig. 3(c), with the shaded region indicating the minimum-to-maximum variation across 10 independent runs. When the number of features reached 23, both the mean *R*2 and MSE began to plateau, with further additions yielding 2 and rspdA, EAA,  RatomA, LUMOA, Z*A, MNA, * RionA,  IPB+, HOMOB', CSB, IPA,  LUMOB+, HOMOA, CSA,  MNB+,  HOMOB+, IPB', χ*A, CSB+, * RionB+, Z*B', MNB, and * RatomB+. The number of features was reduced from 55 to 23, effectively improving the efficiency of the ML model while maintaining its accuracy.

Xiong et al*. [1] used the ML method to investigate the key factors influencing the work function of perovskites and reported that the work function of the surface was highly sensitive to the energy of the oxygen *p*-band center. Similarly, Ma *et al*. [2] used the oxygen p-band center as the primary descriptor for screening the work functions of perovskites. However, calculating the oxygen p-band center for each perovskite requires extensive computational resources. In this work, an effective ML model for predicting the work function of perovskites was constructed using fundamental elemental properties, eliminating the need for resource-intensive calculations of the oxygen p-band centers of each perovskite. The model achieved comparable accuracy to previously reported methods while reducing computational cost [1]. Furthermore, it enables the identification of new intrinsic features governing the work function of perovskites, offering deeper insights into their electronic properties.

### 3.3 Work function ML model inference

The GBRT algorithm was employed to develop the work function ML model via a dataset comprising 23 selected features. The hyperparameters of the work function model are shown in Table S1 in the ESM. The model achieved an *R*2 value of 96.73% and an MSE of 0.11 eV on the test dataset (Fig. 3(d)) and an *R*2 value of 93.30% and an MSE of 0.21 eV on the validation dataset (Table S2 in the ESM), demonstrating high predictive accuracy and reliability without signs of overfitting or underfitting.

A bootstrapping sensitivity analysis with 1000 iterations was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the work function prediction model. The model’s performance metrics exhibited limited variability across iterations. Specifically, the mean *R*2 was 96.07%, with 95% CI of [95.98%, 96.16%], and only 3.2% of the iterations were identified as outliers (defined as values beyond Q3±1.5×IQR) (Fig. S4 in the ESM). The mean MSE was 0.12 (95% CI: [0.1146, 0.1197]), with 2.2% of the iterations classified as outliers (Fig. S5 in the ESM). These results indicated that the model exhibited strong predictive robustness and limited sensitivity to the training dataset.

To explore the underlying trends and periodicity within the work function dataset, data analysis and visualization were conducted. The feature importance rankings were derived via the built-in feature importance function of the GBRT algorithm, as shown in Fig. 3(e). The analysis revealed that the sum of the s, p, and d valence orbital radii of the A-site element ( rspdA) was the most influential feature in determining the work function. Furthermore, the top seven most important features were related to A-site properties, indicating that the work function of the AO-terminated (001) surface was predominantly influenced by the characteristics of the A site.

To further elucidate the impact of  rspdA on the work function, a scatter plot of  rspdA versus the work function (Fig. 3(f)) was constructed, with corresponding elemental classifications annotated. The plot is distinctly divided into two regions: (1)  rspdA > 3 Bohr, which corresponds primarily to alkali and alkaline-earth metals, and (2)  rspdA  rspdA captured periodic trends across A-site elements, with the exception of Li, owing to its unusually small radius. As shown in Fig. 3(f), a clear trend was observed in which the work function decreased with increasing  rspdA. This behavior can be rationalized in terms of the first ionization potential (IPA) of the A-site element. As  rspdA increased, IPA decreased, reducing the minimum energy required to remove an electron from the interior of the solid to the surface, consequently resulting in a lower work function. In summary, a negative correlation was observed between  rspdA and the work function. Consequently, perovskites with alkali or alkaline-earth metals at the A site tend to exhibit lower work functions, making them promising candidates for applications requiring materials with high electron-donating capacity.

### 3.4 Prediction of new perovskites

The process of constructing the prediction dataset followed a systematic approach. Given the vast number of possible elemental combinations for the A, B, and B' sites in A2BB'O6 perovskites, careful selection was necessary. Drawing on previous reports, 26 elements were selected for the A site, as illustrated in Fig. 1(c). These elements include mainly alkali metals, alkaline-earth metals, and several transition metals [1,50]. These elements were considered because of their greater likelihood of forming stable perovskite structures. Moreover, the B and B' sites were each substituted with 43 different elements from across the periodic table [1,50]. This combinatorial approach initially yielded 48,074 possible perovskite compounds (26 A-site cations × 43 B-site cations × 43 B'-site cations). However, because the A2BB'O6 perovskite structure maintained a 1 : 1 ratio between the B and B' elements, the formulae A2BB'O6 and A2B'BO6 represented the same compound, necessitating the removal of duplicate entries. Additionally, any composition in which the A-site element coincided with either the B or B' element was excluded, as it did not conform to the perovskite structure considered in this study. After applying these constraints, the final prediction dataset consisted of 23,822 unique perovskite compounds.

The trained GBRT ML model was then employed to predict the work functions of these 23,822 perovskites. The predicted work function values closely matched those of the original input data, ranging from 1.0 to 10.0 eV (Figs. S6 and S7 in the ESM). This strong correlation validated the rationality and reliability of the ML model, reinforcing its predictive ability for further analysis. The ML approach enables the rapid and efficient estimation of work functions, significantly reducing the computational time and enhancing workflow efficiency.

The primary objective of this study was to identify perovskite oxides with work functions of AO-terminated (001) surfaces below 2.5 eV. Perovskites that did not meet this criterion were filtered out, leaving 1468 candidate materials. Further analysis of these low work function perovskites revealed that their A-site elements predominantly consisted of alkali and alkaline-earth metals. This finding was consistent with the results obtained from the feature importance analysis of the work function, further validating the selection process.

### 3.5 Thermodynamic stability ML screening

Thermodynamic stability is a crucial criterion for evaluating the feasibility of synthesizing predicted perovskites and their potential for practical applications. Convex hull analysis has been widely employed in materials screening studies to qualitatively assess material stability. The energy above the convex hull (*E*hull) serves as a standard metric for determining thermodynamic stability. Wu *et al.* [71] obtained a DFT error value for convex hull analysis by examining the DFT-calculated *E*hull values of experimentally synthesized compounds contained in the Inorganic Crystal Structure Database (ICSD) [72]. More than 80% of the compounds in the ICSD had *E*hull values of less than 36 meV∙atom−1. To encompass metastable compounds that may be experimentally accessible, a threshold of *E*hull = 60 meV∙atom−1 was adopted in this study. Perovskite materials with *E*hull ≤ 60 meV∙atom−1 were classified as thermodynamically stable, whereas those exceeding this value were considered thermodynamically unstable.

To increase the efficiency of thermodynamic stability assessment, another ML model was developed to predict the thermodynamic stability of perovskites. As depicted in the design framework (Fig. 2), an RFC algorithm was employed. The input dataset for *E*hull values was compiled from the Materials Project and the OQMD, consisting of 3228 data points—1292 for ABO3-type single perovskites and 1936 for A2BB'O6-type double perovskites. Although the perovskites in the *E*hull dataset did not completely overlap with those in the work function dataset, the A-, B-, and B'-site elements in both datasets were selected from the highlighted regions of the periodic table (Fig. 1(c)).

Using the *E*hull = 60 meV∙atom−1 threshold, the perovskites in the dataset were labeled either stable or unstable. Initially, 58 features (Table 1) were considered for the thermodynamic stability ML model. Prior to training, these features were normalized via standard deviation standardization. The RFE implemented in the Scikit-learn Python package was then applied to optimize feature selection. The relationship between model accuracy and the number of features is shown in Fig. 4(a). When the feature count was reduced to 13, the model achieved relatively high accuracy. Considering both model performance and computational cost, 13 optimal features were selected: *t*, CSA, * RatomA, χ*A, MNA,  rspdB−,  χB+,  ZB−,  EAB−,  LUMOB+,  MNB+, rspdB+, and  CSB+.

### 3.6 DFT validation

High-precision DFT calculations were performed to validate the thermodynamic stability, dynamic stability, and work functions of the predicted compounds. The thermodynamic stability of the 124 selected perovskites was assessed via DFT-calculated *E*hull values, and the results are shown in Table S3 in the ESM. The results indicated that only 28 out of 124 compounds had *E*hull values greater than 60 meV∙atom−1, confirming the effectiveness of the ML method for predicting thermodynamic stability to a certain extent. After these 28 compounds were excluded, 96 thermodynamically stable compounds remained.

The dynamic stability of the remaining 96 perovskites was subsequently evaluated by calculating their phonon dispersion spectra. Compounds exhibiting no imaginary frequencies were identified as dynamically stable. Ultimately, 37 perovskite structures were confirmed to be dynamically stable (Fig. S9 in the ESM). After removing the structures that overlapped with the work function ML training dataset, 31 promising stable perovskites were selected.

The work functions of these 31 perovskites were then calculated via DFT. Among them, 27 materials exhibited DFT-calculated work functions of AO-terminated (001) surfaces below 2.5 eV, thereby computationally validating the ML predictions. A comparison between the ML-predicted work function values and the DFT-calculated values is presented in Table 2. The MSE was employed to measure the discrepancy between the predicted and calculated values for these 27 materials, yielding an MSE of 0.2 eV, which demonstrated the reliability of the method. The observed discrepancies may be attributed to the following factors. ML models contain intrinsic biases, and DFT calculations rely on approximations; both factors may contribute to the observed deviations. ### 4.2 Catalytic performance of 2 wt% Ru@Ba2TiWO8

Driven by the promisingly low work function of Ba2TiWO8 (4.11 eV), which was lower than that of Ru (4.7 eV), we synthesized Ru-loaded Ba2TiWO8 and systematically evaluated its catalytic performance. The Ru loading amount was set at 2 wt%. The catalytic activity of 2 wt% Ru@Ba2TiWO8 for NH3 synthesis was assessed under various reaction conditions (Figs. 7(a)–7(c)). At 400 °C and 0.1 MPa, the NH3 synthesis rate reached 2249 μmol∙gcat−1∙h−1, exhibiting a monotonic increase with increasing pressure, ultimately reaching 4581 μmol∙gcat−1∙h−1 at 1 MPa (Fig. 7(a)). The long-term stability of 2 wt% Ru@Ba2TiWO8 was tested at 400 °C and 0.1 MPa (Fig. 7(b)), and the results revealed stable NH3 synthesis activity (~2200 μmol∙gcat−1∙h−1) without any noticeable deactivation after 50 h of continuous operation. Additionally, the apparent activation energy (*E*a) for NH3 synthesis was determined to be 106 kJ∙mol−1 (Fig. 7(c)), underscoring the catalytic potential of the material. Compared with previously reported perovskite materials [75,76], 2 wt% Ru@Ba2TiWO8 showed relatively good thermal catalytic activity for NH3 synthesis. (Table S5 in the ESM)

Beyond NH3 synthesis, the catalytic performance of 2 wt% Ru@Ba2TiWO8 for NH3 decomposition was also examined (Figs. 7(d)–7(f)). As the reaction temperature increased from 300 °C, the NH3 conversion rate steadily increased, reaching 100% at 515 °C under a WHSV of 18,000 mL∙gcat−1∙h−1 (Fig. 7(d)). The catalyst demonstrated excellent stability under NH3 decomposition conditions at 450 °C and 0.1 MPa, maintaining 48% conversion without any noticeable deactivation over a 50-h time-on-stream test (Fig. 7(e)). Compared with some previously reported typical Ru-loaded catalysts for NH3 decomposition reactions in Table S6 in the ESM [77−80], the E*a of 2 wt% Ba2TiWO8 was only 60 kJ∙mol−1 (Fig. 7(f)) under the conditions of a low load mass of Ru, further highlighting its potential for practical applications.

In general, 2 wt% Ru@Ba2TiWO8 could be used as a catalyst for both NH3 synthesis and NH3 decomposition reactions. The catalytic activities of both NH3 synthesis and NH3 decomposition were highly sensitive to the work function of the catalyst. The work function of Ru was 4.7 eV, whereas Ba2TiWO8 exhibited a lower measured work function of 4.11 eV, facilitating electron transfer from Ba2TiWO8 to Ru [81]. This efficient electron donation enhanced the activation of N2 during NH3 synthesis and promoted NH3 dissociation in the NH3 decomposition reaction, ultimately contributing to the high catalytic activity observed. The synergistic effect between Ba2TiWO8 and Ru provided a compelling strategy for designing next-generation catalysts with enhanced efficiency for nitrogen-related reactions.

### 4.3 Electrochemical energy storage measurements

To evaluate the Li+ storage performance of the Ba2FeMoO6-based electrode, CR2032-type coin cells were assembled using Ba2FeMoO6 as the working electrode and lithium foil as the counter electrode. The CV curves of the Ba2FeMoO6-based electrode, recorded within the voltage range of 0.01–3 V (vs. Li/Li+) at a scanning rate of 0.1 mV∙s−1, are shown in Fig. 8(a). The CV curves of the first three cycles were compared with those of the 601st and 602nd cycles after 600 cycles at a current density of 1 A∙g−1. In the first cycle, distinct irreversible peaks appeared at approximately 0.68 V and within the range of 0.35–0.58 V, which could be attributed to the formation of a solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer and electrolyte decomposition. However, from the second cycle onward, the CV curves exhibited high repeatability, indicating excellent reversibility and stability of Li+ storage in the Ba2FeMoO6-based electrode.

*
Fig. 8Electrochemical performance of Ba2FeMoO6-based electrodes. (a) CV curves at different cycles. (b) Cycling stability at specific current densities of 0.1 and 1 A∙g−1. (c) Rate capability at various current densities. (d) Long-term cycling stability over extended cycles at a specific current density of 10 A∙g−1. (e) CV curves at different scanning rates. The short-term cycling performance of the Ba2FeMoO6 electrode is depicted in Fig. 8(b). At a current density of 0.1 A∙g−1, the initial discharge-specific capacity was measured at 377 mAh∙g−1. A capacity decline was observed during the first 15 cycles, primarily due to irreversible SEI layer formation. However, following this initial decrease, the specific capacity gradually increased with continued cycling. After 400 cycles, the capacity stabilized at approximately 438 mAh∙g−1. At a higher current density of 1 A∙g−1, the discharge-specific capacity stabilized at ~ 180 mAh∙g−1 after 600 cycles, demonstrating excellent long-term cycling performance. Cross-sectional SEM images of the Ba2FeMoO6 electrode before and after 600 cycles (Fig. S15 in the ESM) revealed significant volumetric expansion, which was likely due to lithium-ion penetration into the electrode material.

The rate performance of the Ba2FeMoO6-based electrode was evaluated at various current densities ranging from 0.1 to 2 A∙g−1 (Fig. 8(c)). The electrode exhibited reversible capacities of 318, 169, 151, 132, 112, and 90 mAh∙g−1 at current densities of 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 1, and 2 A∙g−1, respectively. Notably, when the current density returned to 0.1 A∙g−1, the discharge capacity of the Ba2FeMoO6-based electrode increased from 90 to 208 mAh∙g−1 after 100 cycles. Furthermore, the long-term cycling performance of the Ba2FeMoO6 electrode was also tested at a high current density (Fig. 8(d)) and exhibited a high retention ratio for 10,000 cycles at 10 A∙g−1. The exceptional long-term cycling stability could be attributed mainly to the stable structure of Ba2FeMoO6. Additionally, the Coulombic efficiency of the Ba2FeMoO6 electrode remains at approximately 100% throughout the entire cycling process.

To gain deeper insight into the superior electrochemical performance of the Ba2FeMoO6-based electrode, CV measurements were performed at various scanning rates ranging from 0.2 to 1.0 mV∙s−1 (Fig. 8(e)). The charge storage mechanism was analyzed by examining the relationship between the peak current (i*) and the scanning rate (*v*), following Eq. (11):

(11) i(v)=a×vb

where the *b* value, obtained by fitting log(*i*) versus log(*v*), provides insight into the charge storage mechanism. A *b* value close to 0.5 suggested a diffusion-controlled process, whereas a *b* value near 1 indicated a capacitive-dominated charge storage mechanism. As shown in Fig. S16(a) in the ESM, the calculated *b* values for the anodic and cathodic peaks were 0.82 and 0.96, respectively, indicating that the Li+ storage behavior involved both capacitive-controlled and diffusion-controlled processes.

Furthermore, the relative contributions of capacitive and diffusion-controlled charge storage were quantified via Eq. (12):

(12) i(v)=k1×v+k2×v1/2

where  k1×v represents the capacitive contribution;  k2×v1/2 represents the diffusion-controlled contribution. As illustrated in Fig. 8(f), the capacitive contribution to the total capacity of the Ba2FeMoO6-based electrode progressively increased with increasing scanning rates (Figs. S16(b)–S16(f) in the ESM). At a scanning rate of 1.0 mV∙s−1, the capacitive contribution reached 66.7%, demonstrating a synergistic combination of capacitive-controlled and diffusion-controlled processes in terms of the electrochemical performance. This characteristic enhanced the rapid transport of Li+ during the lithiation and delithiation processes.

The good performance of the Ba2FeMoO6 electrode can be attributed to three key factors. First, its robust crystal structure maintained stability during repeated lithium-ion intercalation/deintercalation cycles. Second, the interconnected [FeO6]/[MoO6] octahedral framework provided stable three-dimensional diffusion pathways for efficient Li+ transport. Third, Ba2FeMoO6 exhibited a relatively low work function, which enhanced the lithium-ion affinity, accelerated the surface reduction kinetics, and facilitated Li+ migration and intercalation. These combined effects contributed to high charge–discharge efficiency, excellent cycling stability, and overall superior electrochemical performance, highlighting the potential of Ba2FeMoO6 as a promising candidate for high-performance lithium-ion battery electrodes.

## 5 Conclusions

We developed an innovative computational design strategy that integrates ML with DFT calculations to efficiently identify perovskite oxides with low work functions. Unlike conventional high-throughput screening methods based solely on brute-force DFT computations, our ML-assisted approach leverages advanced feature engineering and predictive modeling to reduce the number of required DFT calculations by more than 99%. This significantly lowered the computational cost while enabling the effective exploration of a vast perovskite chemical space. Using this approach, we successfully identified 27 stable perovskite oxides with AO-terminated (001) surface exhibiting work functions below 2.5 eV from a dataset of 23,822 candidates, providing meaningful guidance for experimental synthesis.

To validate these predictions, we synthesized two representative compounds, Ba2TiWO8 (a newly discovered material) and Ba2FeMoO6. Comprehensive characterizations, including XRD, SEM, EDS, and work function measurements, confirmed their structural integrity and electronic properties. Notably, 2 wt% Ru-loaded Ba2TiWO8 exhibited excellent catalytic activity for both NH3 synthesis and decomposition, whereas Ba2FeMoO6 demonstrated outstanding long-term electrochemical performance as a Li-ion battery electrode (10,000 cycles at 10 A∙g−1). These results demonstrated a strong link between low work function and superior catalytic/electrochemical performance, underscoring the practical value of our design strategy. More broadly, the ML-DFT framework presented here offers a robust and generalizable platform for accelerating the discovery of novel functional materials across a wide range of applications.
