## 1 Introduction

Owing to the need for environmental protection and high-temperature service environments in the aerospace field, the development of lead-free piezoelectric ceramics with a high Curie temperature (*T*C) has become a significant focus of current research [1−3]. Bismuth layered-structure ferroelectrics (BLSFs) are promising piezoelectric ceramic materials used in high-temperature piezoelectric vibration sensors. The chemical formula of BLSFs is (Bi2O2)2+(A*m*−1B*m*O3*m*+1)2−, and their structure consists of a regular arrangement of perovskite layers (A*m*−1B*m*O3*m*+1)2− and bismuth oxide layers (Bi2O2)2+ [4]. The *T*C of BLSFs varies depending on the number of Bi2O2 layers [5,6].

Bi4Ti3O12 (BIT), a significant type of BLSF, holds great application prospects in high-temperature environments because of its excellent *T*C (675 °C), large spontaneous polarization, and large coercive field [7]. However, the *P*s of BIT is primarily oriented along the *a*–*b* plane, resulting in low piezoelectric activity (*d*33 = 6–8 pC·N−1) [8−10]. Additionally, Bi volatilization during the sintering process of BIT-based ceramics leads to the generation of oxygen vacancy defects (* VO⋅⋅), which deteriorates the electrical properties. To mitigate the anisotropic electrical properties and oxygen vacancy defects in BIT ceramics, researchers have explored modifications through compositional design, such as doping substitution at the A or B site [11,12]. These studies aim to reduce the leakage current and oxygen vacancies, thereby enhancing the piezoelectric and ferroelectric properties [13−15].

A-site doping typically causes significant lattice distortion, which improves the piezoelectric performance but usually reduces the T*C. Pineda-Flores *et al.* [16] replaced Bi3+ ions at the A-site with Pr3+, Nd3+, Gd3+, and Dy3+ ions, resulting in decreased conductivity. However, this substitution significantly impacted the *T*C, which decreased to approximately 400 °C when *x* = 0.8. Conversely, B-site modification of BIT-based ceramics can decrease the electrical conductivity and enhance the piezoelectric coefficient, yet it may also lead to a decrease in the *T*C [17−19]. A/B-site co-doping is one of the most effective modification methods. Introducing lanthanide ions (La3+, Sm3+, Nd3+, Ce4+, Ce3+, etc.) at the A-site and substituting high-valence ions (Nb5+, Ta5+, W6+, etc.) at the B-site can effectively cause lattice distortion and improve piezoelectric activity. However, achieving both a high piezoelectric coefficient and a high *T*C remains a significant challenge. Xu *et al.* [20] reported that Bi3.96Ce0.04Ti2.97W0.015Nb0.015O12 piezoceramics had a high *d*33 (34 pC·N−1) together with a *T*C of 643 °C.

In our previous research, we employed a Cr/Ta non-equivalent co-doping strategy, wherein low-valence ions were substituted with high-valence ions to increase the free electron concentration and reduce the hole concentration. This approach led to both enhanced piezoelectric performance and increased resistivity, a phenomenon also observed in the BaTiO3 system [21,22]. The (Cr1/3/Ta2/3) non-equivalent co-doped BIT ceramics effectively addressed the challenges of simultaneously achieving good piezoelectric performance, high Curie temperature (*T*C), and high-temperature resistivity in BIT-based ceramics. However, there remains significant potential for further improvement in piezoelectric performance.

To further enhance the electrical properties, this study introduces a novel A/B-site multi-co-doping strategy. Specifically, Ce ions, with an ionic radius similar to that of Bi ions, were incorporated at the A-site to reduce the oxygen vacancy concentration and enhance the electrical properties due to their unique electron configuration [23], along with non-equivalent co-doping at the B-site. This strategy aims to improve the piezoelectric coefficient while preserving the high Curie temperature characteristics of BIT-based ceramics. Ce3+/Cr3+/Ta5+ multidoped BIT-based ceramics (Bi4-*x*Ce*x*Ti2.97(Cr1/3Ta2/3)0.03O12, *x* = 0, 0.02, 0.04, 0.06, and 0.08, abbreviated as BiCTCT-100*x*) were synthesized using the solid-state reaction method. The structural, piezoelectric, dielectric, ferroelectric, and temperature stability properties of these ceramics were systematically investigated. Notably, the composition with *x* = 0.04 exhibited a high piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33) of 37 pC·N−1, a high Curie temperature of 681 °C, and excellent high-temperature resistance. Furthermore, the internal conduction and piezoelectric enhancement mechanisms were comprehensively characterized, providing valuable insights into the design and optimization of BIT-based high-temperature piezoceramics.

### 2.1 Sample synthesis

Bi4−*x*Ce*x*Ti2.97(Cr1/3Ta2/3)0.03O12 (*x* = 0, 0.02, 0.04, 0.06, and 0.08, abbreviated as BiCTCT-100*x*) piezoelectric ceramics were prepared via the solid-state reaction technique. Bi2O3 (99.99%), CeO2 (99.9%), TiO2 (99.8%), Cr2O3 (99%), and Ta2O5 (99.99%) were used as raw materials. The metal oxides were weighed according to their stoichiometric compositions and ball milled with ethanol for 4 h. Then, the dried mixtures were calcined at 850 °C for 2 h in air. The calcined powders were remilled under the same conditions, and then dried and ground. The powders were pressed into pellets using polyvinyl butyral (PVB, 6 wt%) as the binder. The pellets were slowly heated to 500 °C to burn out PVB and then sintered at 1120 °C for 2 h.

### 2.2 Electrical properties

To characterize the electrical performance, the top and bottom surfaces of the samples were coated with silver paint and then heated at 720 °C for 15 min. The dielectric properties were measured by an LCR analyzer (E4990 A, Keysight) with frequencies of 1k, 10k, 100k, and 1M Hz at temperatures ranging from 30 to 700 °C. The impendence behaviors were characterized by an LCR analyzer (E4990 A, Keysight) over 400–650 °C, with further detailed analysis by Zview software. The direct current (DC) resistivity (*ρ*) as a function of temperature was tested by a high-resistance meter (6517B, Keyley). The activity energy (*E*a) was determined by the Arrhenius equation as Eq. (1):

(1) σ=σ0exp(−EakBT)

where *σ* is the conductivity; *σ*0 is the exponential factor; *T* is the measured temperature; *k*B denotes the Boltzmann constant. The piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33) was measured by a quasistatic *d*33 meter (ZJ-6BN, Institute of Acoustics, Chinese Academy of Sciences) with a force of 0.5 N after the samples were poled at 7–8 kV·mm−1 in a silicone oil bath at 190 °C for 30 min. Polarization hysteresis (*P*–*E*) loops were carried out by a ferroelectric analyzer (Analyzer 2000E, Aix-ACCT) at a frequency of 1 Hz at room temperature.

### 2.3 Structural characterization

The crystalline phase of ceramics was analyzed by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Empyrean, PANalytical), which employs Cu-K*α* radiation in the 2*θ* range of 10°–90°. The XRD data were further refined by the Rietveld method utilizing commercial GSAS-Ⅱ software, with Crystallographic Information File (CIF) card #ICSD-159929. The natural microstructures were observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; TM4000plus, Hitachi) at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. The valence of the main elements was analyzed by an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB 250XI, Thermo) after weak Ar ion etching for 30 s. Local piezomechanical response, polarization direction, and lithography were assessed by a piezoresponse force microscope (PFM; Cypher S, Asylum Research).

### 3.1 Structural and chemical environment characteristics

The XRD patterns of BiCTCT-100*x* ceramics within the 2*θ* range of 10°–60° are displayed in Fig. 1(a). All the samples exhibit phase alignment consistent with the primary phase of Bi4Ti3O12 (JCPDS No. 72-1019), indicating that no secondary phases were formed during the sintering process and that Ce ions were successfully incorporated into the A-site of the BIT lattice. The most prominent diffraction peak corresponds to the (117) plane, which aligns with the strongest peak of layered perovskites of the (112*m*+1) type [24]. As the doping content increases, the (117) peak shifts to a higher angle, suggesting a reduction in the lattice constant and thus a lattice contraction [25].

The crystal structure of BiCTCT-4 is illustrated in Fig. 1(b), which was derived from refined XRD data, although the doping elements are not displayed due to their low concentration. The microstructures of BiCTCT-100*x* ceramics are shown in Fig. 2, revealing typical plate-like grains with a random orientation. This microstructure is characteristic of BLSFs due to the anisotropic grain growth, where the growth rate of crystals along the *a*–*b* plane exceeds that along the *c*-axis. As the Ce doping content increases, the average grain size gradually decreases. Specifically, the grain size of BiCTCT-4 (11.9 μm) is approximately half that of BiCTCT-0 (21.1 μm), confirming that Ce additives suppress grain growth. The observed reduction in the grain size of BiCTCT-100*x* ceramics may be associated with a decrease in the number of oxygen vacancies. During the sintering process, fewer oxygen vacancies slow the transport of oxygen ions, inhibit diffusion within the grains and at the grain boundaries, and consequently hinder grain growth [29]. Therefore, the introduction of Ce ions likely reduces the number of oxygen vacancies, thereby enhancing the piezoelectric coefficient.

The XPS spectra of BiCTCT-100*x* (*x* = 0, 0.02, 0.04, 0.08) ceramics are shown in Fig. 3. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the core-level photo-emission of O 1s splits into two peaks at ~530.1 and 531.4 eV, attributed to lattice oxygen and oxygen vacancy defects, respectively [30]. Calculating the area ratio of oxygen vacancies to lattice oxygen for BiCTCT-0 and BiCTCT-4 reveals that the ratio decreases from 15/85 to 8/92 as the doping level increases, indicating that Ce ions effectively reduce the oxygen vacancy concentration by replacing Bi ions and thereby mitigating the volatilization of bismuth oxide.

Two dominant XPS peaks at 159.4 and 164.6 eV are shown in Fig. 3(b), corresponding to Bi 4f7/2 and Bi 4f5/2, respectively. The splitting energy of ΔE* = 5.2 eV is consistent with the spin–orbit splitting reported in Bi2O3 for the doublet (5.3±0.1) eV. As the Ce ion content increases, the two peaks remain unchanged, indicating that the valence state of Bi ions in the samples has not altered. Additionally, the peaks at 162.6 and 157.3 eV can be assigned to metallic Bi [31]. As shown in Fig. 3(c), the binding energy locations of Ti 2p3/2 and Ti 2p1/2 peaks for fitting analysis are 458.4±0.2 and 464.4±0.2 eV, respectively. These values match the Ti–O bond energy, indicating that the valence of Ti in the samples is approximately +4 [32]. Therefore, the doping concentration of Ce/Cr/Ta complex ions has no obvious effect on the valence states of Bi3+ and Ti4+ ions. With increasing Ce ions, the peak of Bi 4d3/2 (465.7 eV) gradually decreases, suggesting that Ce ions successfully replace the Bi ions and diffuse into the A-site of the crystal lattice.

The spin–orbit splitting is approximately 18.6 eV, which is consistent with Ref. [33]. According to the fitting results, there are no obvious peaks at 917 eV, and only four peaks are present in this spectrum, indicating that the valence of Ce ions doped into BiCTCT-100*x* is +3. When Ce3+ ions (1.01 Å) with a smaller ionic radius substitute for Bi3+ (1.03 Å) ions [28], lattice contraction occurs, which aligns with the XRD results. In addition, the Cr 2p3/2 spectra are shown in Fig. 3(e). Owing to the low Cr concentration, which results in weak XPS spectral signals, the valence state of Cr could only be determined through analysis of the 2p3/2 peak position. The 2p3/2 peak position essentially remains unchanged before and after doping and is consistently located at 577.1 eV, which corresponds to the characteristic peak of Cr3+.

### 3.2 Electrical performance and conduction mechanism

The relative dielectric constant (*ε*r) and dielectric loss (tan*δ*) of the BiCTCT-100*x* ceramics as a function of temperature measured at a frequency of 100 kHz are displayed in Figs. 4**(**a) and 4(b). The *T*C values of the samples range from 687 to 670 °C, as shown in Fig. 4(c). The *T*C value of BLSFs correlates with the tolerance factor (*t*) [34], which is calculated using Eq. (4):

(4) t=rA+rO2（rB+rO）

where *r*A, *r*B, and *r*O are the ionic radii of A-site cation, B-site cation, and oxygen ion, respectively. Generally, a lower tolerance factor (*t*) typically corresponds to a higher Curie temperature (*T*C) in BLSFs. Given the similar ionic radii of Bi3+ (1.03 Å) and Ce3+ (1.01 Å), the *t* value remains relatively unchanged after substitution. However, according to the refined XRD results, as the Ce content increases, the orthorhombicity (*δ*) decreases from 0.0072 to 0.0066, resulting in a slight decrease in *T*C values. Consequently, introducing Ce ions at the A-site, with a similar radius to that of Bi ions, effectively minimizes the impact on *T*C, maintaining a relatively high *T*C value. Compared with pure BIT (*T*C = 675 °C), the BiCTCT-4 ceramics exhibit a slightly higher *T*C value (*T*C = 681 °C), indicating potential for high-temperature applications.

Additionally, the tan*δ* values at 500 °C as a function of the doping amount are presented in Fig. 4(c). With Ce doping, the tan*δ* values of BiCTCT-100*x* ceramics decrease significantly. Notably, the tan*δ* value of BiCTCT-4 is approximately one order of magnitude lower than that of BiCTCT-0. This decrease in tan*δ* can be attributed to the decrease in oxygen vacancies induced by Ce doping, which enhances the activation energy of the oxygen vacancies.

We also investigated the piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33) of the BiCTCT ceramics at room temperature, as shown in Fig. 4(d). The *d*33 initially increases and then decreases with increasing Ce doping content, suggesting that the dopants significantly alter the piezoelectric properties. Importantly, a high *d*33 of 37 pC·N−1 is achieved in the BiCTCT-4 ceramics, which is more than 5 times that (*d*33 = 6–8 pC·N−1) of pure BIT ceramics and 40% greater than that (*d*33 = 26 pC·N−1) of BiCTCT-0.

To assess the thermal stability of the piezoelectric properties, the *d*33 values of BiCTCT ceramics were measured at various annealing temperatures ranging from room temperature to 700 °C (Fig. 4(e)). The *d*33 of BiCTCT-100*x* ceramics slightly decreases before the annealing temperature reaches the *T*C, after which it drops rapidly. At 500 °C, the *d*33 value of BiCTCT-4 decreases by no more than 14% of the room temperature value, indicating very good thermal stability, which is beneficial for high-temperature applications. The relationship between the *d*33 values and *T*C of BIT-based ceramics, including BiCTCT-4 ceramics and those reported in Refs. [17−20,35−42], is shown in Fig. 4(f). Clearly, the BiCTCT-4 ceramics exhibit a superior piezoelectric coefficient *d*33 of 37 pC·N−1 among BIT-based ceramics, along with a high Curie temperature of 681 °C, compared with other A/B sites co-doped BIT-based ceramics.

The ferroelectricity of BiCTCT-100*x* ceramics is displayed in Fig. 5. The *P*–*E* loops at a frequency of 1 Hz for BiCTCT-100*x* are shown in Figs. 5(a)–5(e), and coercive field (*E*c) and 2*P*r as a function of Ce doping content are summarized in Fig. 5(f). As the electric field increases, the *P–E* loops and the remnant polarization (*P*r) values of the samples increase. For BiCTCT-0, the *P*–*E* loop clearly shows a ferroelectric loop and a low coercive field (*E*c = (|*E*+|+|*E*−|)/2, *E*+ and *E*− are positive and negative electric fields corresponding to the polarization of zero respectively). When Ce ions are introduced, the shape of the hysteresis loop becomes more irregular, and the coercive field increases. Compared with BiCTCT-0 (2*P*r = 14.5 μC·cm−2, *E*c = 25 kV·cm−1), BiCTCT-4 has a higher 2*P*r of 15.3 μC·cm−2 and a high *E*c of 44 kV·cm−1. The increase in *E*c is closely related to the decrease in grain size [43,44]. In addition, the introduction of a suitable amount of Ce ions in BIT-based ceramics can effectively improve the ferroelectricity of the sample. Compared with the sample with only B-site non-equivalent co-doping, the polarization difficulty of the A/B-site co-doped sample increases. Furthermore, all the samples exhibit an internal bias field (*E*i). The asymmetric *P*–*E* loops may be due to the existence of defects (A-site vacancies, oxygen vacancies, nonequivalent-doped ions, etc.), which typically lead to *E*i [45].

To further investigate the effects of Ce ions at the A site on the conduction mechanism of ceramics at high temperatures, variable temperature impedance and DC resistivity tests were conducted. The impedance and DC resistivity measurements were performed over temperature ranges of 400–650 and 100–600 °C, respectively, at intervals of 50 °C. The Nyquist plots of BiCTCT-100*x* ceramics within the measurement frequency range of 100 Hz–10 MHz are displayed in Figs. 6(a)–6(e). As the frequency increases, the impedance is generally attributed to contributions from the ceramic–electrode surface, grain boundaries, and grain bulk [40,46,47]. The impedance spectra of the same component at different temperatures clearly exhibit a similar shape, characterized by a single semicircle. This indicates that the material is internally uniform and the ceramic grains play a major role in the electrical conduction process. The impedance data of the samples were fitted to an equivalent circuit model consisting of a series of combinations of (R//C//CPE), where R represents the resistor, C represents the capacitance, and CPE represents the constant phase element (inset of Fig. 6(a)). With increasing temperature, the radius of the semicircle in the frequency region decreases, indicating a reduction in the internal resistance of the sample. When the temperature exceeds 500 °C, a Warburg diffusion line with a 45° inclination angle appears in the low-frequency region, suggesting the presence of weak ion conductance related to oxygen vacancy migration [48]. The solid lines in Figs. 6(a)–6(e) represent the fitted data, which are in good agreement with the experimental results.

The logarithmic conductivity of BiCTCT-100*x* (*x* = 0, 0.02, 0.04, 0.06, 0.08) obtained from the fitted *Z** (*Z** = *Z**'* + j*Z''*) data as a function of temperature is displayed in Fig. 6(f). By fitting the *Z** data using the Arrhenius equation, the activation energy (*E*a,g) of the grains for *x* = 0 is determined to be 1.38 eV. The values of *E*a,g first increase and then decrease with the introduction of Ce ions; when *x* increases to 0.04, the *E*a,g is 1.51 eV. These results confirm that the combination of A-site and B-site non-equivalent co-doping effectively reduces the oxygen vacancy concentration and significantly enhances the high-temperature performance.

To investigate the temperature stability and conduction mechanism, the DC resistivity of the ceramics was measured from room temperature to 600 °C, as shown in Fig. 6(g). As the temperature increases, the DC resistivity gradually decreases, indicating that high temperatures activate charge carriers [23]. At 500 °C, the resistivity of BiCTCT-4 is 6.6×106 Ω·cm, which is three times greater than that of BiCTCT-0 and at least two orders of magnitude greater than that of pure BIT ceramics (*ρ* ≈ 104 Ω·cm). The introduction of Ce ions at the A-site effectively improves the high-temperature resistivity of ceramics and reduces the transport of charge carriers caused by piezoelectric-induced charges [49], thereby enhancing the stability of piezoelectric ceramics at high temperatures. This suggests significant potential for applications in high-temperature environments.

The high-temperature DC activation energy (*E*a,dc) of the BiCTCT-100*x* ceramics was calculated using the Arrhenius equation from the resistivity data in the 400–600 °C range, with the fitted results shown in Figs. 6(h) and 6(i). The results indicate that as the Ce content (*x*) increases, the *E*a,dc values initially increase and then decrease. Compared with BiCTCT-0, which has an *E*a,dc of 1.35 eV, BiCTCT-4 exhibits an *E*a,dc of 1.42 eV, nearly half of the BIT band gap, indicating that intrinsic conduction dominates at high temperatures. In general, the electrical conduction behavior of BIT materials at high temperatures is dominated by ion conduction due to oxygen vacancies [50]. Oxygen vacancies typically arise from the volatilization of bismuth during high-temperature sintering. These oxygen vacancies can be thermally activated, acting as charge carriers. A reduction in the number of oxygen vacancies results in an increase in the activation energy. Owing to the non-equivalent B-site doping and the formation of oxygen vacancies within the crystal structure, the introduction of CeO2 leads to the predominant reduction of Ce4+ to Ce3+ at elevated temperatures, as evidenced by the XPS analysis. These Ce3+ ions then replace Bi3+ ions in BIT, creating * CeBi× defects and inhibiting the volatilization of Bi3+ ions to a certain extent. This process reduces the concentration of oxygen vacancies. Therefore, donor doping with Ce ions effectively reduces the number of oxygen vacancies, which is a primary reason for the increase in resistivity of BiCTCT-100x* ceramics.

### 3.3 Domain structures and dielectric breakdown behaviors

To further investigate the mechanism underlying the improved piezoelectric performance, vertical piezoresponse force microscopy (VPFM) was employed to analyze the piezoelectric effect associated with *d*33. The VPFM amplitude images of BiCTCT-100*x* ceramics are shown in Figs. 7(a1), 7(b1), and 7(c1), revealing striped domain structures that are consistent with previously reported results [29]. With increasing Ce content, the domain size initially increases and then decreases, indicating that an optimal Ce doping content promotes larger domain sizes and reduces the domain wall density.

The statistical distributions of VPFM amplitudes for BiCTCT-100*x* ceramics are shown in Figs. 7(a3), 7(b3), and 7(c3). For the BiCTCT-0 sample, the VPFM amplitude is predominantly distributed within the range of 110–200 pm, with a peak count of approximately 1200. In contrast, the BiCTCT-4 sample exhibits a broader amplitude distribution, primarily within the range of 120–330 pm, with a maximum count of approximately 1800. The BiCTCT-8 sample, however, shows a significantly reduced amplitude, mostly in the range of 10–60 pm, with a peak count of approximately 2300. A higher VPFM amplitude corresponds to a stronger piezoelectric response and a higher *d*33 value. Moreover, the polarization dynamics of BiCTCT-4 ceramics were investigated under DC voltages of ±70 V using box-in-box writing processes. The litho-PFM images of the samples before and after the box-in-box writing processes are displayed in Figs. 7(d1) and 7(d2). The phase change along the dotted line in the phase image (Fig. 7(d2)) is shown in Fig. 7(d3), clearly demonstrating 180° polarization switching in the BiCTCT-4 sample. These findings demonstrate efficient polarization dynamics and enhanced local piezoelectric responses. From a microscopic perspective, the superior piezoelectric performance of the BiCTCT-4 sample can be attributed to the increase in domain size and the increase in the piezoelectric response due to the introduction of Ce. This observation aligns well with the measured *d*33 values (Fig. 4(d)). Additionally, the phase analysis of VPFM data (Figs. 7(a2), 7(b2), and 7(c2)) indicates that appropriate Ce incorporation reduces the phase difference, facilitating domain switching. This further substantiates the role of Ce ions in optimizing the domain structure and enhancing the piezoelectric properties of the ceramics.

Synchronous phase field simulations were performed to compare the breakdown resistance of BiCTCT-0 and BiCTCT-4 ceramics, as shown in Fig. 8. The initial simulation region, covering an area of 50 μm × 50 μm, was extracted from SEM images of the samples after hot etching, as illustrated in Figs. 8(a) and 8(b). All the simulations were conducted using two-dimensional (2D) models in COMSOL software. The breakdown behavior in ceramics was simulated as the propagation of an electric tree initiated at points of high electric field concentration. Grain boundaries typically exhibit strong resistance to electric fields, which slows the expansion of the electric tree and dissipates energy during its progression [51].

*
Fig. 8Phase–field simulations of BiCTCT-0 and BiCTCT-4 ceramics: (a, b) initial simulation regions extracted from SEM images of BiCTCT-0 and BiCTCT-4 ceramics, respectively; (c, e) time-dependent evolution of local electric tree in BiCTCT-0 and BiCTCT-4 ceramics, respectively. The evolution of the electric tree in BiCTCT-0 and BiCTCT-4 ceramics under a 10 kV·mm−1 electric field is illustrated in Figs. 8(c) and 8(e). As the electric tree propagates from the high-potential boundary through the grain, its expansion is impeded by large-scale grain boundaries. This not only slows the tree’s growth but also induces energy dissipation, reducing the potential difference at its leading edge. Over time, the electric tree ultimately penetrates the ceramics, resulting in breakdown. Notably, in BiCTCT-4 ceramics, the electric tree exhibits slower expansion and greater suppression, with its head potential difference eventually dissipating completely, preventing breakdown. The simulation results reveal that BiCTCT-4 ceramics have a breakdown time of 56.60 ns at 10 kV·mm−1, nearly double that of BiCTCT-0 ceramics (30.44 ns).

Moreover, high electric field concentrations tend to localize at grain boundaries rather than within grains [52]. The local electric potential distribution is shown in Figs. 8(d) and 8(f), showing that BiCTCT-4 ceramics, with their higher grain boundary density and incorporation of Ce, exhibit a more uniform potential distribution than BiCTCT-0 ceramics. This uniformity reduces the likelihood of localized breakdown. Therefore, the combination of enhanced dielectric properties and microstructural features in BiCTCT-4 ceramics contributed to their higher breakdown strength and improved piezoelectric response.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, we successfully synthesized high-performance BIT piezoelectric ceramics with Ce/Cr/Ta co-doping via the solid-state reaction method. The results demonstrate that A/B co-doping effectively reduces the concentration of oxygen vacancies in BIT-based ceramics, leading to enhanced piezoelectric and dielectric properties, improved high-temperature resistivity, and excellent thermal stability. Notably, at *x* = 0.04, the ceramics exhibit a high piezoelectric coefficient (37 pC·N−1), excellent DC resistivity (6.6×106 Ω·cm), a high Curie temperature (681 °C), good thermal stability, and substantial remnant polarization (2*P*r = 15.3 μC·cm−2), indicating their strong potential for high-temperature piezoelectric applications. Moreover, BiCTCT-4 displays larger domain sizes and improved domain switching under an external electric field, which contributes to its enhanced piezoelectric performance. Phase-field simulations of dielectric breakdown behavior further confirm that the incorporation of Ce ions increases the uniformity of the local potential distribution and enhances the breakdown field strength, both of which are critical for improving electrical performance. This study confirms that optimizing the A-site ion concentration in combination with B-site non-equivalent co-doping is an effective strategy for improving the electrical properties of BIT-based ceramics while maintaining high Curie temperatures.
