## 1 Introduction

It is known that entropy (*S*) is a thermodynamic state function whose value is related to the degree of disorder in a substance or system, and the entropy of mixing (Δ*S*mix) for mixtures with equiatomic compositions can be simply calculated with Eq. (1):

(1) ΔSmix=RlnN

where *R* is the universal gas constant and *N* denotes the total number of elements [1]. In general, when the Δ*S*mix value is larger than 1.5*R* (*N* ≥ 5), the mixed-composition with a single phase is defined as a high-entropy (HE) material. If the Δ*S*mix value is between 1.0*R* and 1.5*R* (*N* = 3–4) or lower than 1.0*R* (*N* = 1–2), the substance is categorized as a medium-entropy or low-entropy material, respectively [2].

For the past decade, HE, as an emerging concept or idea for material design, has been extended from HE alloys [3,4] to HE ceramic materials [5−8]. The construction of HE ceramics is different from that of traditional ceramics, which refers to the formation of a single-phase solid solution by five or more cations with equal or near molar atomic ratios [9]. For HE ceramic materials, the chaotic distribution of five or more cations in an equivalent lattice position is liable to result in more flexible composition design and performance modulation than traditional ceramics [10]. Recent studies have demonstrated that a series of HE ceramic materials with excellent mechanical [11−13], thermal [14−16], electrical [17−19], and magnetic [20−22] performance have been successfully constructed. However, the optical properties, particularly the luminescence performance, of HE ceramic materials have rarely been reported [23,24].

CeO2, which is popularly called ceria, is a rare earth oxide with a fluorite structure (face-centered cubic unit cell). In 2012, we prepared a rare earth Eu3+-activated CeO2 red phosphor through solution combustion synthesis (SCS) and observed the dominant excitation wavelength at a blue-light of 466 nm when the Eu3+ doping concentration was greater than 8 mol% [25]. SCS allows for the achievement of a variety of nanoscale oxide materials, which evolves propagation of self-sustained exothermic reactions in aqueous media [26]. Since then, a series of nano/submicroscale Eu3+-activated ceria-based rare earth composite oxide phosphors, including Y2Ce2O7 [27] and Ce2LnO5.5 (Ln = La, Lu) [28,29], have been prepared by SCS. All the above rare earth composite oxides possess a cubic fluorite structure, which is the same as that of pure CeO2; even 1/2 Ce lattice has been replaced with other rare earth elements. Moreover, the luminescence intensity of Eu3+ in these composite oxides was greater than that in CeO2. In 2020, La2Ce2O7:Eu3+ phosphors were synthesized via a self-rising reaction under hydrothermal conditions [30]. By combining a commercial blue-light-emitting diode (blue LED) chip (InGaN, 465 nm), efficient pure red emission was achieved. The white LEDs were fabricated by coating blue LED chips with blends of YAG:Ce3+ and La2Ce2O7:Eu3+ phosphors, yielding prototypes with an enhanced color rendering index, demonstrating that this strategy may be used to complement the red component in white LEDs and has great application potential for solid-state lighting.

In this work, inspired by HE engineering in ceramic materials, novel Eu3+-activated HE Ce0.2La0.2Gd0.2Y0.2Lu0.2O1.6 (*N* = 5), as well as medium-entropy Ce1/3Y1/3Lu1/3O5/3 (*N* = 3) and Ce0.25Gd0.25Y0.25Lu0.25O1.625 (*N* = 4) rare earth oxide ceramic materials, was successfully achieved through the SCS. The multi-component rare earth oxides retain the distinctive cubic fluorite structure of CeO2 despite the tiny molar fraction of ceria in the samples. The photoluminescence measurements indicate that the HE rare earth oxide phosphor has dramatic advantages over the medium-entropy and low-entropy phosphors. The structural variation, surface morphology, and luminescent thermal stability of the samples were also analyzed in detail. Moreover, a bright red LED was fabricated by coating a commercial blue light-emitting chip with the optimal HE oxide phosphor.

### 2.1 Preparation of rare earth composite oxide phosphors

The Eu3+-activated HE rare earth oxide phosphors Ce0.2La0.2Gd0.2Y0.2Lu0.2O1.6:xEu3+ (*x* = 4–16 mol%) were prepared by the urea-nitrate SCS, in which urea was used as fuel. The raw materials included La2O3 (99.99%), Gd2O3 (99.99%), Y2O3 (99.99%), Lu2O3 (99.99%), Eu2O3 (99.99%), Ce(NO3)3·6H2O (Aladdin, 99.5%), urea (AR, ≥ 99.0%), and HNO3 (AR). The rare earth oxides were all purchased from the General Institute of Nonferrous Metals, China. First, the rare earth oxides were dissolved with dilute HNO3 to prepare the corresponding nitrate aqueous solutions (0.2 mol·L−1). Then, the rare earth (Ce4+, Y3+, Gd3+, Lu3+, and La3+) nitrate solutions were mixed in accordance with the equiatomic ratio, in which Eu3+ as an activator was doped in the range of 4–16 mol% and urea as fuel was approximately 5 times the total rare earth content, and the mixture was stirred on a magnetic stirrer for 30 min. After the mixture was completely dissolved, the mixed solution was put into a crucible and transferred to a preheated 500 °C furnace. A few minutes later, the combustion reaction started, and a bright flame was clearly observed from the top vent of the furnace. The combustion reaction lasted for only a few seconds, and the furnace temperature was rapidly increased to 510 °C. After the combustion reaction, the swelling product was taken out from the crucible and ground in a mortar to obtain the ceramic powder.

For the comparison of convenience, Eu3+-activated medium-entropy Ce1/3Y1/3Lu1/3O5/3 (*N* = 3) and Ce0.25Gd0.25Y0.25Lu0.25O1.625 (*N* = 4) as well as low-entropy CeO2 and Y2Ce2O7 (*N* = 1–2) phosphors were also achieved with the above SCS method, in which the Eu3+ doping concentration was kept constant at 12 mol% of the total rare earth content. The HE phosphor after SCS was subsequently heat-treated at 1200–1500 °C for another 2 h for further optimization. Furthermore, multi-component rare earth oxides (*N* = 3–5) were also prepared via direct high-temperature solid-state reactions at 1400 °C without the initial SCS process.

### 2.2 Fabrication of LED devices

First, the optimal HE oxide phosphor and epoxy resin were fully mixed in equal volumes and coated on a 466 nm LED chip. Then, the LED assembly was placed in an oven at 60 °C for 2 h to induce gelation, which is similar to what was recently reported in Ref. [31].

### 2.3 Characterization

The crystal structure of the samples was studied via X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) patterns, which were measured with an X-ray diffractometer (Smart Lab) with Cu Kα (*λ* = 1.5406 Å) radiation at 40 kV and 40 mA. Rietveld refinements of the HE phosphor were performed to identify the detailed structural parameters by using the software package Fullprof. The Raman spectra were measured by an XploRA PLUS Raman spectrometer with a laser wavelength of 532 nm. The morphology and elemental mapping of the samples were obtained using an S-4800 field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM). To define the chemical composition and valence state of the phosphor, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted by using an ESCALAB QXi unit. The photoluminescence spectra and fluorescence decay curves of the samples were obtained with an Edinburgh FS5-TCSPC fluorescence spectrometer equipped with a xenon lamp as the excitation source. The temperature-dependent photoluminescence emission spectra and internal quantum yields (QYs) of the samples were measured by an Edinburgh FLS-980 fluorescence spectrometer. All the measurements were accomplished at ambient temperature, except for temperature-dependent emission spectra.

### 3.1 Structural analysis

The XRD patterns were measured to investigate the crystal structure of rare earth composite oxides achieved through SCS. Figure 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of Eu3+-activated (the doping concentration of Eu3+ is constant at 12 mol%) HE oxide Ce0.2La0.2Gd0.2Y0.2Lu0.2O1.6 (*N* = 5), medium-entropy oxide Ce0.25Gd0.25Y0.25Lu0.25O1.625 (*N* = 4), Ce1/3Y1/3Lu1/3O5/3 (*N* = 3), and low-entropy oxide CeO2 and Y2Ce2O7 (*N* = 1–2) phosphors. The positions and shapes of the diffraction peaks for all the samples are basically the same and are well-matched with the CeO2 standard card (JCPDS No. 43-1002). No other diffraction peaks appear, indicating that all rare earth ions have successfully entered the host lattice and formed a solid solution. Although the rare earth oxides La2O3, Gd2O3, Y2O3, and Lu2O3 and the activator Eu2O3 possess diverse structural species, the composite oxides still maintain a single-phase cubic fluorite structure with *Fm*3*m* space group, which is quite similar to that of CeO2. Owing to the difference in the ionic radius of the rare earth elements, a peak shift is observed from 28.6° to 29.1° for the dominant (111) plane in the rare earth composite oxides. The four main diffraction peak positions for HE oxide (blue curve) and CeO2 (yellow curve) are almost the same and are located at 28.6°, 33.2°, 47.6°, and 56.5° (2*θ*) corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes, respectively. More interestingly, the diffraction peak intensity of the HE oxide phosphor is obviously higher than those of the other medium-entropy and low-entropy oxide samples, implying that HE oxide has superior crystallization in comparison with those of medium-entropy and low-entropy oxides.

The XRD patterns of HE oxide ceramic samples with different Eu3+ doping concentrations (*x* = 4–16 mol%) are shown in Fig. 1(b). There is a negligible shift in the main diffraction peaks, implying that Eu3+ doping has little influence on the crystal structure of HE oxide phosphors. Moreover, the XRD pattern of the HE oxide prepared with SCS and subsequent heat treatment at 1400 °C is also added to Fig. 1(b). The heat treatment after SCS can effectively enhance the intensity of main diffraction peaks and therefore promote the crystallization of HE phosphors.

We also tried to prepare multi-component rare earth oxides (*N* = 3–5) through traditional high-temperature solid-state reactions, in which the raw materials are directly mixed and calcined at 1400 °C for 2 h. Unfortunately, a few diffraction peaks are observed to gather together in the main peak positions in the XRD patterns (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)), indicating that the direct calcination process can only achieve a mixed phase without obtaining a single-phase solid solution. In other words, quite a few rare earth cations are not able to occupy Ce4+ sites in the fluorite structure. It can be concluded from the XRD results that the SCS process is a prerequisite to achieve the HE rare earth oxide phosphor.

To clarify the crystal structure of HE oxide phosphor accurately, the sample calcined at 1400 °C after SCS was refined by XRD Rietveld, as shown in Fig. 1(c). The Rietveld refinement results reveal that only a cubic fluorite phase with a space group of *Fm*3*m* is present, and the *R*exp and *R*p factors are both very small. The refined reliability parameters are *a* = *b* = *c* = 5.41325 Å and *α* = *β* = *γ* = 90.00°, with *V* = 158.626 Å3. Furthermore, a structural schematic diagram of the HE oxide is depicted in Fig. 1(d). A Ce4+ cation is surrounded by 8 equivalents of O2−, and La3+, Gd3+, Y3+, Lu3+, and Eu3+ (all denoted as Ln3+ in Fig. 1(d)) cations randomly occupy the Ce4+ site. Owing to the different charges of cations, oxygen vacancies are generated to compensate for the charge difference, and the difference in the ionic radius also leads to lattice distortion. The existence of large amount of oxygen vacancies and lattice distortion should be the main factors influencing the luminescence properties of rare earth composite oxides [32].

The existence of oxygen vacancies and their dependence on the number *N* of atoms in HE and other multi-component rare earth oxides can be discussed in detail through Raman spectra, as shown in Fig. 2. The wide band at approximately 550–800 cm−1 is attributed to the Raman peak of oxygen vacancies [33]. To compensate for the reduced positive charge due to the substitution of Ce4+ with other Ln3+ ions (La3+, Gd3+, Y3+, Lu3+, or Eu3+), the defects of oxygen vacancies certainly occur in the host. With the increase of cation number *N*, the Raman peak position is gradually right-shifted from 650 to 707 cm−1, and the Raman band area is subsequently enlarged, indicating that HE oxide has more oxygen vacancies. To further define the chemical composition and valence state of Ce, the XPS spectra of HE oxide as a phosphor were measured and are shown in Fig. 4. The signals of Ce, La, Gd, Y, Lu, Eu, and O elements are clearly observed in Fig. 4(a), proving that the actual elements are consistent with the theoretical components of the sample. The emergence of large amounts of carbon (C) is due primarily to the adsorption of CO2 in the atmosphere [34]. Figure 4(b) presents the XPS data of Ce 3d core levels for the HE oxide phosphor. With increasing *N*, the peak intensity at 531.5 eV gradually increases, and the peak intensity at approximately 528 eV accordingly decreases. These results imply that the concentration of oxygen vacancies increases with increasing *N*, which is quite consistent with that of Raman spectra in Fig. 2.

### 3.2 Photoluminescence properties

The ambient temperature photoluminescence excitation and emission spectra of HE oxide Ce0.2La0.2Gd0.2Y0.2Lu0.2O1.6 with different Eu3+ doping concentrations (*x* = 4–16 mol%) are presented in Fig. 5. The excitation spectra (*λ*em = 613 nm) display three sharp peaks at 394, 466, and 532 nm, which are attributed to the 7F0→5L6, 7F0→5D2, and 7F0→5D1 f–f transitions of the activator Eu3+ [37]. Additionally, a very weak band from 250 to 275 nm belongs to the charge transfer from Eu3+ to O2− (Fig. 5(a)). It is obvious that the peak at 466 nm around the blue-light region is the dominant peak; therefore, the subsequent studies on the emission spectra all included excitation at 466 nm. In the emission spectra (Fig. 5(b), *λ*ex = 466 nm) of the HE oxides, all the emission peaks of the samples are almost the same except for their intensity difference. The emission peaks mainly include 594 nm (5D0→7F1 transition), and 613 and 630 nm (5D0→7F2 transition) peaks, and the emission peak at 613 nm is the strongest, indicating that the HE phosphor may be effectively excited with blue light at 466 nm and emit the characteristic red light of Eu3+. With the increase of Eu3+ doping concentration, the luminescence intensity is also gradually increased until *x* reaches 12 mol%. Further increase in the Eu3+ concentration would result in concentration quenching. Thus, the optimal Eu3+ doping concentration is determined to be 12 mol% in the HE oxide phosphor.

To verify the advantages of HE oxide phosphor, the emission spectra of Eu3+-activated multi-component rare earth oxide phosphors (*N* = 1–5) are compared (Fig. 6(a)), in which the Eu3+ doping concentration is kept at 12 mol%. The emission peaks are essentially the same as those in Fig. 5(b), which all correspond to the characteristic Eu3+ emission transitions. However, their emission intensities are quite different, which depend on the number *N*, as can be clearly seen from Fig. 6(b). Suppose the relative emission intensity is 1.0 when *N* = 1, the relative emission intensity is further increased to 2.9 and 7.2 when *N* = 3 and 5, respectively. Moreover, the dependence of the internal QY on *N* also displays a similar phenomenon (Fig. 6(c)). With the increase of the number *N*, the internal QY of the multi-component rare earth oxide gradually increases from 10.5% (*N* = 1) and 18.2% (*N* = 2) with low entropy to 29.6% (*N* = 3) and 38.1% (*N* = 4) with medium entropy and further to 49.7% (*N* = 5) with HE. For the HE oxide after heat treatment at 1400 °C, the internal QY can greatly increase to 67.8%. Compared with those of medium-entropy and low-entropy oxide phosphors, the tremendous luminescence enhancement for HE oxides should be closely related to the modulation of the activator Eu3+ surroundings, which involves the symmetry of Eu3+ in the host lattice. The 5D0→7F2 electric dipole transition at 613 nm is known to be hypersensitive to the surrounding crystal field. On the other hand, the 5D0→7F1 magnetic dipole transition at 594 nm is not sensitive to the local surroundings. Therefore, the relative emission intensity ratio of 613 to 594 nm (*I*(5D0→7F2)/*I*(5D0→7F1)) can be used to probe the internal crystal field surroundings of Eu3+-activated phosphor [25]. Figure 6(d) shows the dependence of the emission intensity ratio *I*(5D0→7F2)/*I*(5D0→7F1) on *N* under excitation at 466 nm. As shown in Fig. 6(d), the *I*(5D0→7F2)/*I*(5D0→7F1) is 2.8 when *N* = 1. With increasing *N*, the *I*(5D0→7F2)/*I*(5D0→7F1) gradually increases from 7.3 (*N* = 2) to 9.4 (*N* = 3) and reaches 11 until *N* = 5. These results indicate that the crystal field surroundings of activator Eu3+ are seriously influenced by the degree of disorder (Δ*S*mix) in the host. The largest Δ*S*mix in the HE oxide would lead to almost all the Eu3+ occupying sites without inversion symmetry. Consequently, the characteristic red emission of Eu3+ at 613 nm is incredibly enhanced in HE oxide phosphors.

The heat-treatment temperature of the HE oxide phosphor is crucial to the luminescence performance, and the emission spectra (*λ*ex = 466 nm) of the HE oxide phosphor prepared by SCS and subsequent heat treatment at 1200–1500 °C for 2 h are given in Fig. 7(a). After the calcination process, the position of the emission peaks remains unchanged, but the emission intensity changes with the increase of calcination temperature. The dependence of the relative emission intensity at 613 nm on the calcination temperature is shown in Fig. 7(b). At the beginning, the emission intensity increases with the increase of calcination temperature. Until the temperature increases to 1400 °C, the emission intensity reaches the summit. Thus, the suitable heat-treatment temperature is concluded to be 1400 °C, and the luminescence internal QY of the optimal HE oxide phosphor can further increase to 67.8%, as shown in Fig. 6(c).

In general, the phosphor materials used to manufacture LED devices should have good luminescence thermal stability to ensure that the optical properties are less affected by the surrounding temperature [38]. Therefore, thermal stability is a key parameter to determine the performance of phosphors. In the following, the temperature-dependent emission spectra (*λ*ex = 466 nm) of Eu3+-activated HE oxide (*N* = 5), medium-entropy oxide (*N* = 3), and low-entropy oxide (*N* = 1) were studied from 303 K (room temperature) to 543 K. Figure 8(a) gives the temperature-dependent emission spectra of the optimal HE phosphor (achieved with SCS and subsequent heat treatment at 1400 °C). The main emission peaks at 613 and 594 nm are not influenced by the surrounding temperature, but the emission intensity gradually decreases with the increase of surrounding temperature due to the thermal quenching effect. The other multi-component rare earth oxide samples have similar temperature-dependent emission peaks as those in Fig. 8(a), as shown in Figs. S2(a)–S2(c) in the ESM. To compare their luminescent thermal stability, the temperature-dependent normalized integrated intensities of all the multi-component rare earth oxide samples are shown in Fig. 8(b). At 373 K, the emission intensity remains 81%–95% of that at room temperature, implying that the luminescence of all the samples is relatively stable from room temperature to 373 K. As the surrounding temperature increases, the emission intensity clearly decreases. Owing to the operation temperature of phosphor-converted LEDs being able to reach 423 K, the ratio of the integrated intensity at 423 K to the initial intensity at room temperature should be considered. At 423 K, the ratio of the integrated intensity for the phosphors is 35% (*N* = 1, SCS), 44% (*N* = 3, SCS), 59% (*N* = 5, SCS), and 73% (*N* = 5, SCS + 1400 °C) of the initial intensity, respectively. Additionally, the quenching temperature (*T*1/2), which represents half of the initial intensity, is increased from 405 K (*N* = 1, SCS) and 413 K (*N* = 3, SCS) to 438 K (*N* = 5, SCS) and 460 K (*N* = 5, SCS + 1400 °C). It can be inferred from the ratio of the integrated intensity and *T*1/2 data that the luminescence thermal stability of multi-component rare earth oxide phosphor samples also closely depends on the number *N*. The larger the *N* value, the more stable the luminescence. The HE rare earth oxide phosphors with high luminescence internal QYs and thermal stability are beneficial for promoting their application in the field of solid-state lighting.

In high-entropy ceramic materials, the high-entropy effect not only promotes thermodynamic stability due to the decrease of Gibbs free energy but also leads to lattice distortion and sluggish diffusion effects [39]. In this work, with the increase of number *N*, the lattice distortion effect becomes severer, which can be verified from the Raman spectra (Fig. 2) and XPS data of O 1s (Fig. 4(c)). The defect reaction equation (Reaction (2)) further illustrates the reason for the increase in the number of oxygen vacancies in the high-entropy system.

According to the optical characterization analysis, the luminescence intensity, internal QY, and thermal stability are all enhanced with the increase of number *N*. Especially, the relative emission intensity ratio of 613 to 594 nm (*I*(5D0→7F2)/*I*(5D0→7F1)) is a popular measure for probing the internal crystal field surroundings of Eu3+-activated phosphor. With the increase of number *N*, the relative emission intensity ratio of 613 to 594 nm increases accordingly. These results fully manifest that the optical properties of rare earth Eu3+ are closely related to the local structure of the crystal field [40]. High entropy means that materials have greater structural complexity and stability, resulting in high-entropy phosphors with unique optical performance. As a result, the luminescence internal QY and thermal stability are much better than those of low-entropy samples.

Furthermore, the decay lifetime of Eu3+ is less affected by heat treatment, although after heat treatment at 1400 °C, the characteristic red luminescence of Eu3+ is clearly promoted.

### 3.3 Application in phosphor-converted LEDs

To demonstrate the application potential of HE rare earth oxide phosphors in solid-state lighting, an LED device was fabricated by coating the optimal HE oxide red phosphor on a 466 nm blue-light LED chip.

Figure 10(a) displays the electroluminescence emission spectra driven by diverse currents (20–100 mA) and the image of a fabricated LED device (60 mA). The electroluminescence emission spectrum from 550 to 750 nm is practically the same as the photoluminescence result, in which a red emission peak at 613 nm is predominant and the relative emission intensity ratio of 613 to 594 nm is greater than 10. In addition, the emission peak at approximately 466 nm is attributed to the emission of the LED chip. With the increase of driving current, the emission intensity also increases. In accordance with the electroluminescence emission spectra, the CIE chromaticity coordinates of the HE phosphor-fabricated LED device and the commercial LED are calculated to be (0.3119, 0.1605) and (0.1473, 0.0365), respectively, as plotted in Fig. 10(b). Therefore, the HE rare earth oxide phosphor is expected to be used in the field of solid-state lighting.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, Eu3+-activated HE rare earth oxide Ce0.2La0.2Gd0.2Y0.2Lu0.2O1.6 phosphors were successfully achieved through SCS, which has a very high luminescence internal QY and superior thermal stability under blue-light excitation at 466 nm. The HE rare earth oxide phosphors possess a single-phase cubic fluorite structure despite the tiny ceria present in the host. After heat treatment at 1400 °C for 2 h, the ceramic particles are uniform with nearly spherical shapes, and the size is around 400–600 nm. The specific cationic component (*N* = 5) and difference in charge and radius result in an enormous increase in the degree of disorder or Δ*S*mix of the material system in comparison with those of the medium-entropy and low-entropy oxides. The crystal field surrounding activator Eu3+ is greatly changed, and the symmetry around Eu3+ is modulated. The luminescent internal QY and thermal stability are both increased with the increase of number *N*. The luminescent internal QY is incredibly increased from 10.5% (*N* = 1, low-entropy) and 29.6% (*N* = 3, medium-entropy) to 49.7% (*N* = 5, HE) and even 67.8% after a subsequent heat-treatment process. Similarly, the ratio of the integrated intensity at 423 K is increased from 35% (*N* = 1, low-entropy), 44% (*N* = 3, medium-entropy) to 59% (*N* = 5, HE) and 73% after heat treatment. The optimal Eu3+ doping concentration and heat-treatment temperature are 12 mol% and 1400 °C, respectively. As a result, the excellent optical properties of HE rare earth phosphors have great potential for application in solid-state lighting. This work is of great significance not only in academia but also in industry.
