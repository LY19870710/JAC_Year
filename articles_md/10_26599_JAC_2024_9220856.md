## 1 Introduction

Ultra-high-temperature ceramics (UHTCs), especially UHTC carbides, are considered the most promising thermal protection materials due to their extremely high melting points, considerable hardness, and chemical stability [1−5]. However, the poor oxidation resistance of conventional carbides, such as HfC and ZrC, restrict their high-temperature applications [6−12].

Recently, the emergence of novel multicomponent UHTC carbides (including high-entropy carbides, HECs) has offered new approaches for improving their oxidation resistance [13−18]. In particular, (Hf,Zr,Ti)C medium-entropy carbides have attracted considerable attention because of their excellent ablation resistance. Li *et al*. [19] showed that an amorphous Zr–Hf–Ti–C–O layer, reinforced by m-(Hf,Zr,Ti)O2 nanoparticles, formed on the surface of (Hf,Zr,Ti)C after ablation at 2100 ℃, thereby enhancing its oxidation resistance. Ye *et al*. [20] observed an intermediate Hf–Zr–Ti–O–C layer and precipitated carbon between the oxide layer and the (Hf,Zr,Ti)C matrix after ablation at 2500 ℃, which hindered rapid oxygen diffusion and contributed to good ablation resistance. However, the (Zr,Hf,Ti)O2 layer tends to crack during the oxidation of (Hf,Zr,Ti)C, thereby reducing the protective effect of the oxide layer [21]. This suggests that the oxidation resistance can be further improved by optimising the composition of (Hf,Zr,Ti)C.

The incorporation of Nb and Ta has been recognised as an effective method for improving the oxidation resistance of UHTCs. Wang and Reece [22] demonstrated that (Hf,Ta,Zr,Nb)C incorporated with Nb and Ta exhibits better oxidation resistance compared to binary carbides. Opila *et al*. [23] discovered that adding 20 vol% TaSi2 in ZrB2–SiC significantly enhanced its oxidation resistance at 1627 ℃, and the enhancement was more likely related to Ta than Si. Tan *et al*. [24] found that (Ti,Nb)C exhibited optimal oxidation resistance in a steam environment between 1000 and 1400 ℃ owing to the dense oxide layer formed by Nb-doped TiO2, which inhibited outward diffusion of Ti and inward diffusion of oxygen. These studies suggest that incorporating Nb and Ta can potentially enhance the oxidation resistance of (Hf,Zr,Ti)C.

However, few studies have focused on the influence of Nb and Ta doping on the oxidation resistance of (Hf,Zr,Ti)C. Therefore, the role of Nb and Ta doping in the phase transformation and microstructural evolution during the oxidation of (Hf,Zr,Ti)C remains unclear. Moreover, the oxidation behaviour of the materials is greatly affected by the oxidation temperature. Present research on the oxidation behaviour of multicomponent UHTC carbides has concentrated on temperatures below 1600 ℃, thereby limiting insights into the intrinsic oxidation mechanisms at potential operating temperatures above 2000 ℃.

To address these gaps, this study investigated the oxidation behaviours of (Hf,Zr,Ti)C, (Hf,Zr,Ti,Nb)C, and (Hf,Zr,Ti,Ta)C at 2500 ℃. The influences of Nb and Ta doping on the oxidation behaviours were evaluated. Furthermore, the compositional changes and microstructural evolution during the oxidation process were comprehensively investigated. Therefore, this study offers valuable theoretical guidance for the compositional design and optimisation of multicomponent UHTC carbides.

### 2.1 Material preparation

Commercial powders of HfC, ZrC, TiC, TaC, and NbC (Warwick Technology, 99%, ≤ 0.8 μm) were utilised as raw materials. The proportions of HfC and ZrC were fixed at 0.5 and 0.3 because their high melting points ensured the overall high-temperature resistance of the oxide layer. The other three powders were added in the following ratios: (a) HfC : ZrC : TiC = 5 : 3 : 2 (HZT), (b) HfC : ZrC : TiC : NbC = 5 : 3 : 1 : 1 (HZTN), and (c) HfC : ZrC : TiC : TaC = 5 : 3 : 1 : 1 (HZTT). The powders were mixed using a planetary ball mill with ethanol and WC balls as the milling media. The ball-to-powder ratio was set to 5 : 1. After mixing for 3 h at a speed of 200 r/min, the powder mixtures were dried at 40 ℃ for 48 h. Subsequently, the dried powders were sintered using a hot-pressing furnace (ZT-40-21Y, Shanghai Chenhua Technology Co., Ltd., China) under a vertical pressure of 30 MPa at 1900 ℃ in vacuum for 1 h. The vacuum level was kept below 10 Pa during the sintering process.

### 2.2 Oxidation test

The sintered samples were cut into small discs of 15 mm in diameter and 2 mm in thickness. These discs were then polished using a 1 μm diamond paste to make their surfaces smooth and even. Subsequently, a customised high-temperature oxidation setup was employed to examine the oxidation behaviours of HZT, HZTT, and HZTN at 2500 ℃ (Fig. 1). The temperature of the sample surface was monitored using a two-colour infrared pyrometer (Marathon MR1SCSF, Raytek, accuracy: ±(0.5%*T*meas (the measured temperature) + 2 ℃)). After the sample was heated to 2500 ℃ in vacuum at an average heating rate of 15 ℃/s, air was introduced into a test chamber to maintain a pressure of 20,000 Pa. The pressure was regulated by a pressure control unit (ZCMD-I-10-LED, Chengdu Zhenghua Electronic Instruments, China, applicable range: 1×102–2×104 Pa). The oxidation times were 300, 600, 900, and 1500 s. The obtained samples were labelled according to the duration of oxidation. For example, after 300 s of oxidation, the HZT sample was designated as HZT-300 s. After oxidation, the samples were rapidly cooled to room temperature for approximately 10 min in vacuum.

### 2.3 Characterisations

The composition of the samples, both pre- and post-oxidation, was analysed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/max-2550VB, Rigaku Co., Japan) equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source at a scan rate of 2 (°)/min. Rietveld refinements of the XRD patterns were conducted using the General Structure Analysis System software (GSAS-Ⅱ). The microstructures and elemental compositions of the samples were examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; MIRA4 LMH, TESCAN, Czech Republic) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS; Ultim Max 40, Oxford Instruments, UK). The fine microstructures of the oxidised samples were further examined using a scanning and transmission electron microscope (S/TEM; Talos F200x, FEI, the Netherlands) combined with an EDS system. The TEM samples were prepared using a focused ion beam (FIB) milling machine (Helios 5UC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Volatility diagrams were constructed based on the FactPS and FToxid databases in FactSage. The sizes of phases and pores within the oxide layer were measured using the Nano Measurer software. The relative densities of the sintered samples, measured using Archimedes’ method, were > 95%.

### 3.1 Initial sample characterisation

As shown in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b), apart from trace amounts of (Hf,Zr)O2 impurities, only one set of diffraction peaks was observed, confirming the formation of multicomponent carbide solid solution with a face-centred cubic (FCC) structure. (Hf,Zr)O2 is the typical impurity found during the fabrication of multicomponent carbides, yet its exceptionally low content has a negligible effect on the tests [27]. The lattice constants of HZT, HZTN, and HZTT were determined using Rietveld refinement of the XRD patterns (Table 1). Compared to HZT, the main peaks of HZTN and HZTT shifted slightly towards lower angles because substituting smaller Ti atoms with Nb and Ta atoms increases the lattice constant, which is consistent with the XRD refinement results. As illustrated in Table 1, HZT, HZTN, and HZTT exhibited similar relative densities, showing dense microstructures (porosity Figs. 2(c)–2(e)). The average grain size of the sintered samples, measured using fracture morphologies, ranged from 0.82 to 1.22 μm. The average grain size of HZTT is slightly smaller than that of HZT and HZTN owing to the slower diffusion rate of Ta [27], but the effect on the oxidation resistance is insignificant. Figure 2(f) shows the elemental mapping of HZTT, wherein all elements are uniformly distributed without any segregation, confirming that the as-synthesised sample is homogeneous in composition.

### 3.2 Oxidation kinetics

The thickness of the oxide layer, as shown in Fig. 3, was determined through SEM analysis of the cross-sectional microstructure and the corresponding oxygen distribution in the central region of the oxidised samples. The average thicknesses of the oxide layers in HZT, HZTN, and HZTT increase with oxidation time (Fig. 3(a)). Figure 3(b) demonstrates a good linear relationship between the square of the oxide layer thickness (*d*2) and the oxidation time, indicating that the oxidation behaviours follow a parabolic law. In other words, the oxidation of HZT, HZTN, and HZTT is controlled by the diffusion of oxygen in the oxide layer and can be described as Eq. (1):

where d*0 (μm) is the initial thickness of the oxide layer, *t* (s) is the oxidation time, *d* (μm) is the thickness of the oxide layer after oxidation, and *k* (μm2·s−1) is the oxidation rate constant.

The oxidation rate constants (*k*) of HZT, HZTN, and HZTT were calculated as 85.29, 71.08, and 63.82 μm2·s−1, respectively. The addition of Nb and Ta reduced the oxidation rate of HZT by 16.67% and 25.17%, respectively, indicating that doping with Nb and Ta effectively enhances HZT’s oxidation resistance. Similar performance improvements were also reported in ablation studies of Nb- or Ta-doped multicomponent carbides in comparable temperature ranges, although the ablation and oxidation test conditions are different [28].

#### 3.3.1 Surface microstructure of oxide layer

The macroscopic morphologies before and after oxidation at 2500 ℃ are illustrated in Fig. 4(a). Initially, HZT, HZTN, and HZTT possess mirror-like surfaces. After oxidation, a rough oxide layer formed on the HZT surface, along with randomly distributed bubbles due to the release of gaseous oxidation products. The addition of Nb and Ta effectively inhibited bubble formation, which is evident by the significantly reduced number and size of bubbles on the surfaces. The bubbles only occupy a minor fraction of the oxidised surface area.

Figures 4(b)–4(d) show the XRD patterns of HZT, HZTN, and HZTT after oxidation at 2500 ℃ for different durations. For HZT, only (Hf,Zr)O2 solid solution was detected on the oxidised surface (Fig. 4(b)). The absence of Ti-containing oxides may be attributed to their melting and subsequent flow into the inner layer or evaporation at the test temperature (e.g., TiO2 with a melting point of 1912 ℃ [29]). Compared with HZT, the main peaks of oxides on the HZTN surface shifted slightly to higher angles (Fig. 4(c)). Incorporating the Nb atom with a smaller atomic radius into the (Hf,Zr)O2 lattice results in the formation of (Hf,Zr,Nb)O2 solid solution and a decrease in the lattice parameter, which are confirmed by the XRD refinement results of HZT-1500 s and HZTT-1500 s. For HZTT, (Hf,Zr)O2 and Ta2O5 peaks emerged after oxidation for 300 s (Fig. 4(d)). However, the peak of Ta2O5 disappeared as the oxidation time increased, possibly because the molten Ta2O5 with a melting point of 1872 ℃ [29] filled the pores of the inner oxide layer or evaporated at 2500 ℃.

The surface microstructures of HZT, HZTN, and HZTT after oxidation at 2500 ℃ are depicted in Fig. 5. The oxidised surface of HZT consists of (Hf,Zr)O2 grains, which remain compact as the oxidation time increases (Figs. 5(a)–5(c)). In contrast, the oxidised surface of HZTN contains (Hf,Zr,Nb)O2 grains with numerous micropores (Figs. 5(d)–5(f)). During the oxidation process, Nb atoms dissolve into the (Hf,Zr)O2 lattice to form the (Hf,Zr,Nb)O2 solid solution, as confirmed by the homogeneous elemental distribution illustrated in Fig. 6. As the melting point of the Nb-containing oxide (Nb2O5, 1512 ℃) is lower than those of HfO2 (2900 ℃) and ZrO2 (2700 ℃) [30,31], the incorporation of Nb decreases the overall melting point of the solid solution. Consequently, the evaporation of (Hf,Zr,Nb)O2 from the surface of HZTN is promoted at high temperatures and low pressures, resulting in the formation of pores. Although the surface morphology of the oxidised HZTT is similar to that of HZT, it consists of (Hf,Zr)O2 grains doped with trace Ta atoms (Figs. 5(g)–5(i)). Residual Ta2O5 nanoparticles are visible near the bubbles on the surface of HZTT-300 s, which completely evaporate with increasing the oxidation time, consistent with the XRD results. Remarkably, the surface of HZT exhibits a denser microstructure than that of HZTN, making it favourable for hindering inward oxygen diffusion. However, the oxidation rate of HZTN is lower than that of HZT, implying that the oxidation resistance is not solely determined by the surface microstructure of the oxide layer.

#### 3.3.2 Cross-sectional microstructure of oxide layer

To further elucidate the impact of Nb and Ta on the oxidation behaviour of HZT, a detailed analysis of the cross section of the oxide layer was conducted to explore the microstructural evolution. The cross-sectional microstructures of HZT, HZTN, and HZTT after oxidation at 2500 ℃ for 300 s are presented in Fig. 7. For all samples, the oxide layers are well bonded to the matrix and exhibit a uniform thickness. A typical laminated structure is observed in the oxide layers of HZT and HZTN, comprising a porous outer oxide layer and a dense inner oxide layer. Cracks can be observed in the outer oxide layer of HZTN-300 s, which were induced by the thermal mismatch between the inner and outer oxide layers (Fig. 7(e)). In contrast, the HZTT oxide layer exhibits a more homogeneous microstructure.

### 4.1 Oxidation mechanism

The intricate structure of the oxide layer suggests that the oxidation of HZT, HZTN, and HZTT is a multi-step process. The oxygen partial pressure progressively decreases from the surface towards the interior, due to the shielding effect of the oxide layer. As a result, the lowest oxygen partial pressure occurs at the interface between the oxide layer and the matrix. At extremely low oxygen partial pressures, there is insufficient oxygen to react with all constituent elements, leading to the preferential oxidation of more reactive elements and the formation of multiphase oxide layers. The Gibbs free energy and volatility phase diagrams of the oxidation products of binary carbides are depicted in Fig. 12. As shown in Fig. 12(a), the negative Gibbs free energy values indicate that the oxidation reactions are thermodynamically favourable. Furthermore, the Gibbs free energy for the oxidation of HfC and ZrC is more negative compared to those of TiC, TaC, and NbC, indicating a higher tendency for oxidation.

Volatility phase diagrams offer insights into the oxidation behaviour at a specific temperature [32−34]. As illustrated in Figs. 12(b)–12(f), HfC and ZrC exhibit significantly lower oxygen partial pressures required for initial oxidation compared to TiC, TaC, and NbC. Consequently, Hf and Zr are preferentially oxidised, whereas the oxidation of Ti, Ta, and Nb is delayed, which is consistent with the preferential oxidation law reported in previous studies [28,35,36]. The formation sequences of two oxycarbides are determined using the preferential oxidation law. M-C-O-1 is generated first because of the elevated contents of the highly reactive Hf and Zr. Subsequently, M-C-O-2 forms with greater retention of low-activity elements (e.g., Ti, Nb, and Ta) during further oxidation.

In the oxidation process of carbides, oxycarbides and carbon phases initially form, followed by the emergence of oxides [8,10,37]. Despite their distinct compositions, HZT, HZTT, and HZTN, collectively called carbide matrix (CM), have similar phase structures in the oxide layers, indicating identical oxidation behaviours, as described by Reactions (2)–(6):

(2) CM+O2(g)→M−C−O−1+C

(3) M−C−O−1+O2(g)→M−C−O−2+(Hf,Zr)O2+C

(4) M−C−O−2+O2(g)→(Hf,Zr)O2+MxOy(g)+CO(g)

(5) C+O2(g)→CO(g)

(6) CO(g)+O2(g)→CO2(g)

where M*x*O*y* denotes low-melting-point oxides (e.g., TiO2, Nb2O5, and Ta2O5).

Figure 13 shows a schematic of the oxidation processes of HZT, HZTN, and HZTT. In the initial stage of oxidation, a small number of oxygen atoms dissolve in the carbide lattice to form an M-C-O-1 phase (Fig. 13(a)), accompanied by the release of carbon (Fig. 13(b)). The M-C-O-1 phase maintains its original FCC structure until the oxygen content of the carbide lattice reaches its maximum solubility. As oxygen continues to diffuse inward and reaches the interface between the oxide layer and the matrix, the oxidation of carbon occurs after that of metallic elements [20,38,39]. Consequently, the Hf and Zr atoms within the M-C-O-1 phase preferentially react with oxygen to form (Hf,Zr)O2, thereby generating an M-C-O-2 phase with a higher Ti/Nb/Ta content (Fig. 13(c)). At this stage, the structure of the inner oxide layer comprising (Hf,Zr)O2 + M-C-O-1 + M-C-O-2 + carbon is formed. As shown in Fig. 13(d), the remaining M-C-O-1 decomposes to generate both M-C-O-2 and (Hf,Zr)O2 during continued oxidation. Simultaneously, all precipitated carbon is oxidised to CO/CO2, resulting in an outer oxide layer mainly composed of (Hf,Zr)O2 + M-C-O-2. Finally, M-C-O-2 is completely oxidised, producing (Hf,Zr)O2, CO/CO2, and low-melting-point oxides. Among these products, gaseous species are released, leaving a porous (Hf,Zr)O2 layer on the surface (Fig. 13(e)).

### 4.2 Effect of Nb and Ta addition on oxidation resistance

From the microstructural characterisation of the oxide layer, it is evident that the inclusion of Nb and Ta substantially improves the oxidation resistance of HZT. This enhancement is attributed to the optimisation of the distribution and composition of oxycarbides (M-C-O-1 and M-C-O-2).

As shown in Figs. 10 and 11, the doped Nb or Ta is retained in the M-C-O-1 and M-C-O-2 oxide layers after oxidation, forming single-phase solid solution with complex elemental compositions. The retention of Nb or Ta within oxycarbides improves oxidation resistance. Firstly, as depicted in Fig. 12, Nb and Ta exhibit the highest initial oxygen partial pressures, thereby acting as components that possess the lowest oxidation reactivity. Consequently, doped Nb and Ta improve the initial oxidation resistance of oxycarbides. Secondly, the entropy effect becomes more pronounced with the increase in constituent elements in the single-phase solid solution, leading to greater lattice distortion and more sluggish diffusion, as reported in prior research on HECs [40−43]. Hence, oxygen diffusion into the M-C-O-1 and M-C-O-2 lattice becomes more difficult, enhancing the oxidation resistance of HZT.

More importantly, the incorporation of Nb and Ta affects the size and distribution of oxycarbides, resulting in a more compact oxide layer. As depicted in Fig. 8, the addition of Nb and Ta significantly reduces the size of oxycarbides (M-C-O-2) within the outer oxide layer as well as the number and size of micropores. This can be attributed to the effect of composition change on the diffusion coefficient (*D*) of carbon within the multicomponent carbide lattice [44,45]. Based on the reports of Wallace and Butt [46] and Meshcheryakov [47], the diffusion coefficients of carbon were calculated in binary carbides (TiC, NbC, and TaC), as shown in Fig. 14. The *D* values in NbC and TaC at 2500 ℃ were significantly lower than that of TiC. Thus, substituting Ti with Nb or Ta decreases the diffusion rate of carbon in the carbide and oxycarbide lattices (also known as O-doped carbides). This result is similar to that described by Lun *et al*. [39] and Wallace and Butt [46]. In solid solution, the precipitated phase particles grow spontaneously to minimise the overall free energy of the system; this phenomenon is known as Ostwald Ripening [48]. In this study, oxycarbides, particularly M-C-O-2, demonstrated a similar trend of spontaneous growth. The evolution mechanism of oxycarbides (Figs. 13(c) and 13(d)) reveals that a slow carbon diffusion rate decelerates the growth rate of M-C-O-2, consequently influencing the size of M-C-O-2.

The mechanism underlying the influence of Nb and Ta on the distribution of M-C-O-2 is shown in Fig. 15. The Ti–Hf–Zr–C–O grains in the HZT assemble and grow as the oxidation time increases (Figs. 15(a) and 15(b)). However, the growth of Ti–Nb–Hf–Zr–C–O in HZTN and Ta–Ti–Hf–Zr–C–O in HZTT is impeded by the slow carbon diffusion (Figs. 15(d) and 15(e)). Thus, Ti–Nb–Hf–Zr–C–O and Ta–Ti–Hf–Zr–C–O remain dispersed in the oxide layer at small sizes. Further oxidation of these M-C-O-2 leads to the formation of low-melting-point oxides, which evaporate intensely at 2500 ℃ and leave pores. Consequently, the oxide layer in HZT exhibits larger elongated pores (Fig. 15(c)), whereas considerably smaller micropores are observed in HZTN and HZTT (Fig. 15(f)). This observation is consistent with the denser microstructure of the oxide layers in HZTN and HZTT depicted in Fig. 8. Evidently, a denser oxide layer is favourable for impeding the inward diffusion of oxygen. Therefore, HZTN and HZTT exhibit enhanced oxidation resistance.

Notably, Ta improves the oxidation resistance of HZT more than Nb, as evidenced by the denser oxide layer observed in HZTT. Unlike HZTN, the surface microstructure of HZTT after oxidation is more compact (as shown in Fig. 5), which effectively hinders the inward diffusion of oxygen. Furthermore, the carbon diffusion coefficient within the TaC lattice is significantly 1–2 orders of magnitude lower than that of TiC and NbC at 2500 ℃, as illustrated in Fig. 14. This reduced carbon diffusion leads to a lower content of precipitated carbon and smaller sizes of oxycarbides within the oxide layer of HZTT compared to HZT and HZTN. Consequently, this results in a more homogeneous microstructure. The layered structure within the oxide layer of HZT and HZTN can result in stress concentration and crack formation (Fig. 7(e)), thereby providing a rapid pathway for oxygen diffusion. However, the oxide layer of HZTT exhibits a more homogeneous microstructure with fewer cracks, enabling more effective protection.

From the aforementioned discussion, the following theoretical guidance can be provided: First, prioritising elements that form high-melting-point oxidation products (M*H, e.g., Hf/Zr) as principal constituents. An excess of elements producing low-melting-point oxidation products (*M*L) might degrade the overall temperature resistance of the oxide layer, potentially damaging its structural integrity during oxidation at ultra-high temperatures. Second, introducing a minor quantity of *M*L (e.g., Ti/Nb/Ta) can effectively decelerate ultra-high-temperature oxidation rates. These elements facilitate the optimisation of phase composition and distribution within the oxide layer, enhancing its protective capabilities. Lastly, variations in the contributions of different ML elements to enhancing ultra-high-temperature oxidation resistance were observed, with Ta showing a more pronounced improvement.

## 5 Conclusions

In this study, the oxidation behaviours of (Hf,Zr,Ti)C (HZT), (Hf,Zr,Ti,Nb)C (HZTN), and (Hf,Zr,Ti,Ta)C (HZTT) were investigated at 2500 ℃ for the first time. The effects of doping Nb and Ta on their oxidation resistance were comprehensively investigated. The oxidation processes of HZT, HZTN, and HZTT follow a parabolic law. During the initial stage of oxidation, oxygen dissolves into the carbide lattice, leading to the formation of M-C-O-1 (Hf–Zr–Ti–C–O, Hf–Zr–Ti–Nb–C–O, and Hf–Zr–Ti–Ta–C–O). Subsequently, Hf and Zr within M-C-O-1 are preferentially oxidised to (Hf,Zr)O2 as skeletons of the oxide layer. Ti, Nb, and Ta are retained in M-C-O-1 and gradually transform into M-C-O-2 (Ti–Hf–Zr–C–O, Ta–Ti–Hf–Zr–C–O, and Ti–Nb–Hf–Zr–C–O), which are dispersed within the (Hf,Zr)O2. Compared to HZT, the addition of Nb or Ta optimised the composition of oxycarbides (M-C-O-1 and M-C-O-2). Consequently, the oxidation rates of HZTN and HZTT were significantly lowered by 16.67% and 25.17%, respectively. Furthermore, the retention of doped Nb and Ta within oxycarbides hinders the diffusion of oxygen into the oxycarbide lattice. The inclusion of Nb and Ta also effectively reduces the size of oxycarbides in the oxide layer, minimising pores and creating a denser oxide layer. As a result, the addition of Nb and Ta significantly enhances the oxidation resistance of HZT, with Ta showing a more substantial improvement.
