## 1 Introduction

Ceramic coatings have been widely applied in aerospace, military, industrial, and other fields [1−6]. Al2O3-based ceramic coatings are increasingly important for wear, thermal, and corrosion protection because of their superior hardness, high melting point, chemical inertness, and cost-effectiveness [2,7−10]. However, owing to the inherent low fracture toughness of Al2O3, single-component Al2O3 coatings are prone to spalling failure caused by crack propagation, which significantly limits their application in wear protection [10−15]. Introducing a second phase (such as TiO2, ZrO2, or Y2O3) is an effective approach to improve the toughness of Al2O3 coatings [11−13,16]. Among these materials, ZrO2 has been proven to promote crack closure through volume expansion induced by the t‒m phase transformation during crack propagation, thus enhancing both the fracture toughness and wear resistance of the coating [4,12−14,17,18]. Dejang *et al*. [13] reported that adding 20 wt% ZrO2 to an Al2O3 coating increased the toughness from 2.97±0.24 to 3.68±0.23 MPa·m1/2 and decreased the wear rate from 4.44 to 0.18×10−4 mm3·N·m−1. Abedel-Samad *et al*. [18] reported that adding 40 wt% ZrO2 to an Al2O3 coating reduced the microhardness by 12% but improved the wear resistance by 56% due to increased toughness and densification.

Atmospheric plasma spraying (APS) is an ideal thermal spraying technique for preparing ceramic coatings [8,19]. Owing to the nonuniform field of the jet, the feedstock particles experience varying thermal histories during the APS process, resulting in different degrees of melting [7,9]. In APS coatings, fully melted (FM) particles (i.e., molten droplets) stack to form the main framework after flattening and solidification, while some unmelted or partially melted (PM) particles are incorporated [5,20,21]. The microstructure of coatings is fundamentally determined by the characteristics of the feedstock. Conventional APS coatings are prepared using powders with micron-scale structures (e.g., blended powders and fused-crushed-clad powders) as feedstocks [4,5,7,9,22,23]. Owing to their coarser microstructure (micron or submicron scale) and poor compositional uniformity, the mechanical performance and wear resistance of conventional coatings are usually unsatisfactory. In recent years, nanostructured coatings have attracted considerable attention [2,5,20,22]. Nanostructured coatings exhibit a bimodal microstructure, consisting of FM regions with a homogeneous microstructure and PM regions with a nanostructure retained from feedstocks [4,5,12,20,24]. Many studies have confirmed that, compared with conventional coatings, nanostructured coatings significantly increase the crack resistance, adhesion strength, and spall resistance [9,17,20,23]. The PM region in these coatings plays a crucial role in improving the mechanical properties and wear resistance [4,5,9,10,25−27]. Wang *et al*. [9,23] reported that, compared with conventional coatings, Al2O3-based nanostructured coatings presented greater crack growth and wear resistance, benefiting from their finer grain size, more uniform compositional distribution, and toughening effect in the PM region. Zhao *et al*. [25] reported that in APS Al2O3–40 wt% ZrO2 coatings, a reduction of approximately 18%–46% in the wear rate was achieved in nanostructured coatings. Lima *et al*. [5] reported that the excellent toughness of nanostructured coatings results from enhanced interlamellar bonding and crack arrest by the PM region, where frequent crack deflection and stress redistribution occur. Nevertheless, to date, the feedstocks for APS nanostructured coatings are limited primarily to reconstituted powders, which are micron-sized agglomerates derived from nanoparticles through reconstitution processes involving slurrying, spraying, and drying [3,5,9,20−22]. Owing to the high demand for nanoparticles, issues such as high cost, agglomeration, and potential harm to human health are difficult to avoid. Furthermore, reconstituted powders usually require posttreatment to reduce porosity and remove organic additives, which adds extra steps to the complex process [5,20,22]. More importantly, the microstructure of these coatings is only limitedly adjustable, as it is strongly dependent on the size and distribution of the nanoparticles in the feedstock. These disadvantages hinder the development and application of nanostructured coatings. However, current research on nanostructured coatings focuses on compositional design, spray parameter optimization, and coating post-treatment [2,5,14,20,28]. The exploration of the preparation techniques and microstructure design of feedstock powders remains limited.

In previous work, we developed a technique that combines combustion synthesis-air atomization (CS-AA) to prepare oxide composite powders (e.g., Al2O3–ZrO2 and Al2O3–TiO2) [29,30]. Compared with the reconstitution process, the CS-AA technique offers significant advantages, including high efficiency, low cost, and environmental friendliness, as the process is simple and does not require nanoparticles or an external heat source. Owing to the rapid solidification of the melt (with cooling rates exceeding 103 K·s−1), the resulting air-atomized powders exhibit well-defined spherical shapes and fine microstructures. Specifically, the Al2O3–47.6 wt% ZrO2 composite powders feature a self-generated nanoeutectic structure composed of fibrous or lamellar grains [29], which can be considered a promising feedstock for constructing nanostructured coatings.

In the present work, nanostructured Al2O3–ZrO2 composite coatings are prepared via APS using the nanoeutectic powders obtained from CS-AA as the feedstock. The microstructure and phase composition are characterized and analyzed in detail. To explore the wear protection potential, the mechanical properties and dry sliding friction performance of the coatings were evaluated. The microhardness and crack propagation behavior of the FM and PM regions in the bimodal coatings are investigated, and the impact mechanism of the PM region on the mechanical properties of the nanostructured coating is discussed. To optimize the performance, the coatings sprayed with three plasma powers were compared. Furthermore, the structure‒property relationships of the coatings are systematically discussed.

### 2.1 Preparation of the Al2O3‒ZrO2 composite powders

The Al2O3–ZrO2 composite powders were prepared via a previously developed CS-AA technique [29], and a schematic diagram is presented in Fig. 1. Specifically, the raw materials, including Al powders (3–5 μm, > 99.9% purity, Northeast Light Alloy Co., Ltd., China), Al2O3 powders (5–7 μm, > 99% purity, Zhengzhou Nonferrous Metals Research Institute Co., Ltd., China), and ZrO2 powders (5–7 μm, > 99% purity, Jiaozuo Lifeng Industrial Co., Ltd., China), were mixed and ball milled at 80 r·min−1 for 6 h. Among them, Al2O3 and ZrO2 are the trigonal (α) and monoclinic (m) phases, respectively (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). The mixture was transferred into a specially designed high-pressure reactor. After being sealed and vacuumed, the reactor was filled with high-purity O2 gas (> 99.99% purity, supplied by Harbin Tianli Industrial Gas Co., Ltd., China) to a pressure of 15 MPa. The combustion reaction between Al and O2 was initiated by an electrical spark, as described in Eq. (1). The combustion wave subsequently propagated throughout the reactor. Under the high-temperature environment provided by the exothermic reaction, the raw materials were fully melted and uniformly mixed at the atomic scale, forming a homogeneous melt. After several seconds, a specially designed nozzle at the bottom of the reactor was opened. Then, the high-temperature melt was ejected into a container filled with room-temperature air under a high pressure of 20 MPa generated by the heated O2 gas. The droplets produced by the initial atomization through the nozzle were further fragmented by air convection and then rapidly cooled in the air at a cooling rate of 103–104 K·s−1. Finally, the solidified composite powders were collected from the container.

### 2.2 Preparation of the Al2O3–ZrO2 composite coatings

### 2.3 Material characterization

The particle size distribution of the CS-AA powders was measured via a laser particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 2000, Malvern Panalytical Ltd., UK). The microstructure and elemental distribution of the powders and APS coatings were characterized via a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Gemini 560, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDXS; Ultim Max 100, Oxford Instruments, UK). The samples for cross-sectional characterization were obtained by grinding and polishing the powders and coatings, in which the powder particles were first embedded in a resin matrix. The porosity of the coating and the proportion of the PM region were determined through an image analysis method [12,24,32] on the basis of statistical analysis of at least 5 SEM images per sample via ImageJ software (v. 1.54d, developed by the National Institutes of Health, USA). The phase composition of the samples was identified through an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Empyrean Alpha-1, Malvern Panalytical Ltd., UK) with Cu-Kα radiation (*λ* = 0.15406 nm) at 40 kV and 2*θ* = 10°–90°. The detailed microstructure, phase composition, and elemental analysis of the coatings were further characterized via a transmission electron microscope (TEM) equipped with an EDS system (Talos F200X G2 with ChemiSTEM system, FEI, USA) at an operating voltage of 200 kV. The TEM samples were prepared by grinding and polishing the composite coating to a thickness within 80 μm, followed by argon ion beam thinning via the PIPS II 695 system (Gatan, Inc., USA) for 12–24 h. The surface roughness and wear track of the samples were measured via a 3D optical surface profilometer (Newview 8200, Zygo Co., USA, and SuperView W1, Chotest Technology Inc., China).

### 2.4 Mechanical property testing

The microhardness, elastic modulus, fracture toughness, and tribological properties of the coatings were tested. Prior to the tests, the surfaces or cross-sections of the coatings were ground with grit SiC papers and subsequently polished with diamond slurries to achieve a mean surface roughness (*S*a) of less than 0.5 µm. The microhardness of the coatings was measured in cross-sections via the Vickers indentation method via a microhardness tester (HV-1000STA, Laizhou Veiyee Experimental Machinery Manufacturing Co., Ltd., China) with a diamond indenter. A normal load of 0.2 kgf (1.96 N) with a dwell time of 15 s was applied. For individual samples, at least 25 indentations were performed at different positions, and more than 5 measurements were obtained from each characteristic structural region. The microhardness (HV0.2) was determined by the average value of the calculated results via Eq. (5):

(5) HV=1.8544×Fd2

where *F* is the load (kgf), and *d* is the diagonal length of the indentation (mm).

The Young’s modulus of the coating was obtained via the quasistatic depth-sensing indentation technique based on the Oliver–Pharr method via a nanoindentation instrument (G200, Agilent Technologies Inc., USA) [33−35]. A Berkovich-type diamond indenter was used to apply a maximum load of 20 *g* with a holding time of 10 s on the cross-section of the coating at a loading/unloading rate of 13 mN·s−1. The stress‒displacement (*P*‒*h*) curve is shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. For the calculation, the contact stiffness (*S, *N·m−1) was determined via Eq. (6) on the basis of the upper segment of the unloading curve from the initial unloading point (corresponding to *P*max and *h*max):

(6) S=dPdh

where *P* is the load (N), and *h* is the indentation depth. Thus, the reduced modulus *E*r was obtained via Eq. (7):

(7) S=β2πErA

where *β* is the geometric correction factor of the indenter, with a value of 1.034; *A* is the contact area (m2), which depends on the indentation depth and the geometry of the indenter. The Young’s modulus *E* of the coating was calculated from Eq. (8):

(8) 1Er=1−ν2E+1−νi2Ei

where *ν* and *ν*i represent the Poisson’s ratios of the sample and the indenter, with values of 0.181 (cited from the report on the APS coating of ZrO2 stabilized with 8 wt% Y2O3 by Rybicki *et al*. [36]) and 0.07 [37], respectively; *E*i is the Young’s modulus of the diamond indenter, with a value of 1,140 GPa. Each sample was measured at more than 5 distinct locations, and the average value was calculated. The measurement results revealed that the AZC-1, AZC-2, and AZC-3 samples had similar Young’s moduli, with values of 152.95±13.82, 153.92±16.74, and 153.57±10.22 GPa, respectively. To simplify the subsequent calculations, the average value (*E*avg) of 153.48 GPa was used as the representative Young’s modulus for the Al2O3–ZrO2 coatings in this work.

The fracture toughness and in-plane hardness of the coating were measured via the Vickers indentation technique. To obtain valid cracks and avoid the influence of anisotropy in the mechanical properties of the coating, an indentation test was performed on the surface [14,37−40]. The test parameters were set as a load of 1 kgf (9.8 N) and a dwell time of 15 s. The fracture toughness (*K*IC, MPa·m1/2) was calculated according to Eq. (9) [13,34,41]:

(9) KIC=δ(EH)1/2Pc3/2

where *δ* is a material-independent constant for Vickers-produced radial cracks, with a value of 0.016; *E* is the Young’s modulus of the coating (GPa); *H* is the hardness (MPa); *P* is the pressure (N); and *c* is the length of the crack (mm). *c* was measured from the center of the indentation to the crack tip via SEM. For each sample, the hardness and fracture toughness were determined as the average of the calculated values, where the toughness was derived from no less than 20 valid cracks.

### 2.5 Tribological testing

The samples were subjected to dry sliding friction and wear tests via a tribometer (Bruker UMT-2, Bruker Co., USA). The test was based on a ball-on-disc configuration, in which the coating samples were used as the disc component, and a Si3N4 ball (hardness: 16±0.6 GPa, bending fracture strength *σ*f: 775±25 MPa, *K*IC: 6±0.5 MPa·m1/2, *E*: 310±10 GPa) with a diameter of 4 mm was used as the counterbody. All tests were conducted at room temperature with a relative humidity of 45%±5%. A circular sliding trajectory with a diameter of 4 mm was employed for 5000 cycles, corresponding to a total wear distance of 62.8 m. For each disc test, the normal loads applied were 2, 4, and 6 N, and the rotation speeds were 250 and 500 r·min−1 (corresponding to sliding velocities of 0.05 and 0.1 m·s−1), respectively. During the test, the coefficient of friction was continuously recorded by the instrument, which was based on the measured frictional force divided by the applied load. The wear volume (Δ*V*) was determined from the wear track data measured via a profilometer. For each sample, the average was calculated on the basis of at least four measurements. The wear volume rate (*W*rv) was then calculated via Eq. (10) [42]:

(10) WrV=ΔVFN×l

where *F*N is the normal load, and *l* is the length of the wear distance. The wear mechanism of the samples was analyzed through microstructural characterization via SEM.

### 3.1 Microstructural characteristics of the Al2O3–ZrO2 composite powder

In this work, Al2O3–47.6 wt% ZrO2 composite powders were prepared via the CS-AA technique. The microstructure characteristics of the Al2O3–ZrO2 composite powders are shown in Fig. 2. The particle size distribution of the powders ranged from 7 to 100 μm, with an average size of 32.97 μm and a median size *D*50 of 27.81 μm (Fig. 2(a)). As displayed in Fig. 2(b), the particles have a well-defined spherical morphology and smooth surfaces. These features are ideally suited for thermal spraying technology. As shown in Figs. 2(c) and 2(d) and Fig. S3 in the ESM, the microstructural and compositional characterization confirms that the air-atomized powders are characterized primarily by micron-sized cellular eutectic colonies, which comprise fibrous or lamellar Al2O3 and ZrO2 phases with an average eutectic spacing of 65 nm. This self-assembled nanoeutectic structure results from nonequilibrium solidification of the Al2O3–47.6 wt% ZrO2 melt (~5 wt% deviation from the equilibrium eutectic composition) at high cooling rates (103–104 K·s−1) [29]. Benefiting from the high undercooling supported by the CS-AA process, the solid‒liquid interfaces of the Al2O3 and ZrO2 phases advance at a similar rate during solidification, forming a coupled eutectic structure. Moreover, the suppression of lateral solute diffusion promotes the refinement of the eutectic structure.

Nanostructured powders hold significant importance in thermal spraying applications because of their potential to induce nanostructures in coatings [4,5,24,28]. Compared with common reconstitution methods for preparing nanostructured powders, the CS-AA technique offers notable advantages in terms of cost-effectiveness and environmental sustainability by eliminating the need for expensive nanosized powders and simplifying processing steps. Crucially, the fine microstructure forms spontaneously in the powders through the coupled growth of eutectic phases from the melt during rapid cooling.

The microstructural characteristics of the spray-coated samples are detailed in Figs. 3 and 4. As shown in Fig. 3(a), cross-sectional image reveals that the sample (from bottom to top) consists of a Ti alloy substrate, a NiCrAlY bond coating, and an Al2O3–ZrO2 ceramic top coating. EDS analysis (Figs. 3(b)–3(f)) reveals the different elemental compositions in the top coating, bond coating, and substrate, where Zr and Al are uniformly distributed within the ceramic coating.

The detailed microstructural characterization of the Al2O3–ZrO2 ceramic coating is presented in Fig. 4. The morphology of the coating surface is characterized by the coexistence of relatively flat regions formed by FM particles (molten droplets) and near-spherical protrusions caused by PM particles (Fig. 4(a)). The cross-sectional back-scattered electron (BSE) images (Figs. 4(b) and 4(c)) indicate that defects such as pores and cracks exist within the coating, which are typical features of thermally sprayed coatings [2,4]. During APS, feedstock particles melt within the plasma jet and form molten droplets, which are subsequently sprayed onto the substrate (or deposited coating). Upon impact, the droplets flatten and solidify rapidly due to the efficient heat dissipation of the solid substrate, thereby stacking to form the coating. The formation of defects in coatings is associated with entrapment of gases within the droplets during rapid cooling; poor interfacial bonding resulting from geometrically irregular deposited surfaces involving particles with varying melting or solidification states; and nonuniform shrinkage caused by variations in deposition thickness or compositional distribution, among other factors [2−5,8].

Notably, two distinct microstructural regions exist in the plasma-sprayed Al2O3–ZrO2 coating derived from the air-atomized powder: the FM region and the PM region, as shown in Fig. 4(d). This phenomenon is consistent with previously reported nanostructured ceramic coatings and is also known as the bimodal microstructure [5,12,21,24]. The high-magnification (HM) BSE images (Figs. 4(e)–4(h)) for each of the two distinct regions demonstrate their microstructural details. As displayed in Figs. 4(e) and 4(f), the FM region is characterized by the stacking of lamellar splats formed from fully melted and highly spread droplets. Although slight elemental enrichment is present in some local zones, the FM region generally has a homogeneous microstructure. In contrast, the PM region has a spherical (or near-spherical) outline and exhibits significant phase separation (Figs. 4(g) and 4(h)). The characteristic microstructure of this region is a multicolony structure composed of nanosized eutectic phases, similar to that of air-atomized powder. During the plasma spraying process, particles flow along different trajectories in the plasma jet. Owing to the nonuniform temperature field of the jet, particles passing through the center tend to be fully melted (with higher temperatures), whereas those farther from the center are more likely to be only partially melted or even remain unmelted (with lower temperatures) [4,5,8]. After rapid solidification, the FM particles transform into FM regions with a homogeneous microstructure in the coating. Moreover, the PM particles are embedded in fully melted droplets, forming PM regions and highly retaining the microstructural characteristics of the feedstock powder. In addition, the grains in a few PM regions tend toward spheroidization and slight coarsening, although the eutectic characteristics are retained to some extent (Fig. S4 in the ESM). This observation aligns with previous findings on Al2O3–ZrO2 powders with nanoeutectic structures after heat treatment [29], indicating that the feedstock particles undergo different thermal histories during APS and that microstructural evolution occurs in some PM particles at intermediate temperatures. Moreover, owing to the complex and variable local solidification conditions during the deposition process, phenomena resulting from the partial crystallization of the melt are also observed in the coating, including the precipitation of fine ZrO2 spherical grains within the FM region and the formation of transitional zones characterized by equiaxed grains of two phases near the FM/PM interface. This will be further discussed in Section 3.2.3. In summary, powder with a nanoeutectic structure prepared by CS-AA can be directly used in plasma spraying to fabricate composite ceramic coatings with bimodal microstructural characteristics.

#### 3.2.2 Phase composition of the plasma-sprayed Al2O3–ZrO2 coatings

The phase compositions of the samples were characterized and analyzed. Figure 5 presents the XRD patterns of the air-atomized Al2O3–ZrO2 powders and the plasma-sprayed coatings. Clearly, the three coatings exhibit similar phase compositions, which are distinct from those of the air-atomized powder. The broad peaks observed between 25° and 40° in the XRD patterns of the coatings confirm the emergence of amorphous phases. For the crystalline phases, ZrO2 exists as the tetragonal (t) phase in both the powders and the coatings. However, the transformation in the crystal structure of Al2O3 occurs after plasma spraying: shifting from the coexistence of α-Al2O3 and δ-Al2O3 phases (in the powder) to the coexistence of δ-Al2O3 and γ-Al2O3 (in the coatings). The formation and coexistence of metastable phases, including γ-Al2O3, t-ZrO2, and the amorphous phase, in plasma-sprayed Al2O3–ZrO2 coatings have been widely reported [11,14,43−45]. This is attributed to the nonequilibrium solidification behavior of the molten droplets under extremely high cooling rates (exceeding 106 K·s−1) during the deposition process. The formation of the amorphous phase implies that crystal nucleation is significantly suppressed because of the highly undercooled solidification interface. This phenomenon is more common in Al2O3–ZrO2 composites with eutectic or near-eutectic compositions because the glass-forming ability increases as the composition approaches the eutectic point (~42.6 wt% ZrO2) [46,47]. Furthermore, the appearance of the γ-Al2O3 phase indicates that partial crystallization occurred during the solidification of the molten droplets. The nucleation of the metastable γ-Al2O3 and t-ZrO2 phases from the melt or amorphous phase is more favorable than that of the low-temperature stable α-Al2O3 and m-ZrO2 phases [14,48−53]. Under the rapid cooling conditions provided by APS, atomic diffusion and grain growth are significantly suppressed, and the time available for γ-α and t-m phase transitions is greatly shortened, resulting in substantial retention of these metastable phases until room temperature [14,44]. Moreover, the retention of t-ZrO2 in rapidly solidified Al2O3–ZrO2 composites without other stabilizers is also believed to be related to the constraint of the Al2O3 matrix on the expansion effect of the t–m transformation and the lattice stabilization caused by the solid solution of Al3+ in the ZrO2 [46,52,54,55]. In fact, since the CS-AA process also involves rapid melt solidification (cooling rate of 103–104 K·s−1), metastable δ-Al2O3 (a transitional phase between the γ- and α-phases) and t-ZrO2 phases have already formed in the air-atomized powders before APS deposition, as discussed in previous work [29]. However, the presence of the γ-Al2O3 and amorphous phases in the coatings indicates that the solidification conditions in the APS process are more extreme (further deviating from equilibrium solidification) than those in the CS-AA process. Therefore, the phase composition of the Al2O3–ZrO2 coatings is determined by the characteristics of the air-atomized powders retained in the PM region and the features of the rapid solidification products of the FM particles during APS. Combined with the phase composition of the raw materials (Fig. S1 in the ESM), the evolution of the phase composition involved in this work can be summarized as follows: α-Al2O3 + m-ZrO2 (in the raw materials) → δ-Al2O3 + α-Al2O3 + t-ZrO2 (in the air-atomized powder) → γ-Al2O3 + δ-Al2O3 + t-ZrO2 + amorphous phase (in the APS coatings).

In addition, a transitional zone ranging in thickness from 300 nanometers to several micrometers is present near the interface between the FM and PM regions (Figs. 6(a), 7(a), and 7(i)). The typical feature of this zone is a polycrystalline structure consisting of equiaxed grains with sizes ranging from the nanoscale to the submicron scale, as shown in Figs. 7(a), 7(i), and Fig. S5 in the ESM. The FFT calibration results for the lattice fringes of different grains are displayed in Figs. 7(j)–7(l), revealing that the main crystalline phases are t-ZrO2 and γ-Al2O3. The formation of the transitional zone results from the partial crystallization of the liquid phase (i.e., the FM droplets or the melted portions of the PM particles) during rapid solidification. Owing to variable local solidification conditions during the APS deposition process, slight differences are observed in the thickness and microstructure of the transition zones. Nevertheless, the overall trend shows that the crystalline grains closer to the PM region are more densely distributed and larger in size. This can be attributed to the unmelted structure in the PM region providing a substrate for heterogeneous nucleation, which effectively lowers the nucleation energy barrier during solidification. As it approaches the FM region, nucleation is significantly suppressed owing to the extremely high undercooling caused by the rapid cooling process. In this case, only a portion of the leading phase (such as ZrO2 in the Al2O3–47 wt% ZrO2 system [29]) nucleates, whereas the remaining liquid phase is rapidly frozen to form the amorphous phase. As shown in Fig. 6(a) and S6 in the ESM, the precipitation of fine ZrO2 spherical grains sparsely embedded in the continuous amorphous phase is observed in the transitional zone (particularly near the edge of the FM region), which is also found in a few locations within the FM region (Figs. 4(e) and 4(f)). Furthermore, the FM region is predominantly composed of an amorphous phase due to insufficient time for nucleation.

In brief, the FM region and PM region of the Al2O3–ZrO2 coating are characterized mainly by the amorphous phase and nanoeutectic structure, respectively, whereas equiaxed grains resulting from the partial crystallization of the melt exist in some zones.

#### 3.2.4 Effect of the plasma spray power on the coating microstructure

The influence of the plasma spray power on the microstructure of the coatings was explored. Three Al2O3–ZrO2 composite coatings were fabricated under CPSPs of 653, 713, and 772 (corresponding to spray powers of 52.25, 57, and 61.75 kW, respectively). The surfaces of the three coatings clearly exhibit similar morphologies, characterized by the stacking of flattened splats with embedded protruding PM particles, as shown in Figs. 8(a)–8(c). *S*a of the coatings is used to preliminarily evaluate the deposition quality [4,8,37,56,57]. As the CPSP increases from 653 to 772 (Figs. 8(d)–8(f)), the *S*a of the coatings gradually decreases from 11.55±1.65 to 8.01±0.14 μm (Table 2), indicating more complete melting and spreading of the particles. Moreover, the average coating thickness increases from 364.2±15.9 to 807.8±20.7 μm under the same powder feeding rate and number of torch passes (Figs. 8(g)–8(i) and Table 2), further confirming the gradual improvement in deposition efficiency. In addition, the cross-sectional BSE images confirmed the similar bimodal microstructures of the three coatings, as shown in Figs. 8(j)–8(r). All the coatings consisted of an FM region with a uniform structure and a PM region with a nanoeutectic structure. However, variations in the defect density and the proportion of the PM region can be observed. Based on image analysis, with increasing CPSP, the porosity and proportion of the PM region in the Al2O3–ZrO2 composite coatings decreased from 12.6%±1.7% to 7.8%±1.6% and from 34.7±4.4 to 23.2±3.8 vol%, respectively (Table 2).

It is well known that variations in plasma spray parameters, such as spray power, primary/secondary gas flow rates, and powder feeding rates, significantly affect the microstructure and properties of coatings due to changes in the heating and velocity of the particles [8,20,31,37]. An increase in spray power increases the central temperature of the plasma flame and influences the temperature field, resulting in more thorough and widespread heating of particles within the jet [4,8,31]. During plasma spraying, unavoidable material loss occurs due to inelastic collisions between particles (or droplets) and the substrate. FM particles are more likely to adhere to and spread on the substrate. Therefore, as observed in this work, increasing the spray power contributes to improving the deposition efficiency. Moreover, sufficient melting of particles facilitates the migration of pores and promotes effective bonding between splats, thereby improving the densification of the coatings [4,8]. This is beneficial for enhancing the mechanical properties by alleviating potential stress concentrations and lowering the risk of crack propagation. However, the proportion of the PM region in the coatings decreases as the CPSP increases (Table 2 and Fig. S7 in the ESM). This trend is as expected because the splats lose the original microstructural characteristics of the feedstock when the particles are fully melted. Considering that PM regions with nanostructures play a crucial role in enhancing the mechanical properties of ceramic coatings [4,5,9,25], a decrease in the PM region fraction inevitably restricts the potential benefits. Therefore, in the design and optimization of spray power for ceramic coatings with nanoeutectic structures, a reasonable balance must be achieved between the proportion of the PM region and both the deposition efficiency and the relative density of the coating.

The shape parameters *λ*1 and *λ*2 of each curve represent the fitted characteristic values for the low-hardness and high-hardness subpopulations, respectively. The corresponding values are 801.04 and 1436.07 for AZC-1, 863.79 and 1458.97 for AZC-2, and 897.54 and 1442.97 for AZC-3. In addition, compared with the scale parameter *k*2, the relatively lower *k*1 of each sample indicates a more dispersed microhardness distribution in the low-hardness subpopulation. Furthermore, the *λ* and *k* values of the low-hardness subpopulation are more sensitive to changes in spraying power.

Furthermore, microstructural identification near each indentation via SEM imaging confirmed that the bimodal distribution resulted from the significant hardness difference between the two characteristic regions. Taking the AZC-2 sample as an example, Fig. 10 presents the typical morphology of two indentations with the same load located in the FM (Fig. 10(a)) and PM (Fig. 10(b)) regions, wherein the relatively shorter diagonal length of the indentation in the PM region suggests relatively higher hardness. The hardness difference between the two characteristic regions can be attributed to their distinct phase compositions and microstructural features. As discussed earlier, the phase composition of the samples changed after plasma spraying. The amorphous phase is generated in the coatings, and the crystalline Al2O3 phase exists as the δ and γ phases, which are different from the original powders characterized by α-Al2O3 and δ-Al2O3. It has been reported that the hardness of amorphous phases and metastable γ-Al2O3 phases formed in thermally sprayed Al2O3-based coatings through rapid solidification is lower than that of the α-Al2O3 phase, in which the amorphous phases exhibit the lowest hardness [11,14,43,48]. On the other hand, the FM region has relatively high porosity due to gas entrapment within the molten droplets [56]. Moreover, compared with the FM region, which has a uniform microstructure, the PM region tends to exhibit enhanced deformation resistance due to its nanoeutectic structure, where refined grains increase the proportion of grain boundaries that hinder dislocation movement. Therefore, the PM region in the coating has a relatively high hardness.

In addition, the influence of spray power on the hardness of the two characteristic regions differs: As CPSP increases, the hardness of the FM region increases due to reduced porosity and enhanced cohesion strength [4,8,57,62,63], whereas the PM region shows no significant change in hardness since its microstructure is largely retained from the raw particles. Overall, the evolution of coating hardness with increasing spray power (i.e., CPSP from 653–772) can be considered a comprehensive response to the hardness increase in the FM region and the decrease in the proportion of the relatively harder PM region. The cross-sectional microhardness of the overall coating is optimized at a CPSP of 713, achieving a value of 1008.39±308.54 HV0.2.

As shown in Fig. 11, compared with the thermally sprayed coatings of similar systems that have been reported, the coating with a nanoeutectic structure derived from the CS-AA powders results in a high level of microhardness [17,18,25,64−70]. Interestingly, in contrast to the relatively lower hardness of the PM region commonly observed in nanostructured coatings derived from reconstituted powders [21,58,59,70], the PM region presents a significant hardness advantage in coatings derived from air-atomized powder. This can be attributed to the fact that, unlike reconstituted powders, which possess a looser structure and whose internal nanoparticles are typically bound by van der Waals forces [5,59], the refined nanoeutectic structure in the air-atomized powders is self-generated from the melt, and semicoherent or coherent lattice relationships exist at the interface between the Al2O3 and ZrO2 grains [29], thereby enhancing interphase bonding and improving the mechanical properties of the coating.

#### 3.3.2 Modulus and fracture toughness

The elastic modulus of the coatings was measured via the quasistatic depth-sensing indentation technique, which is based on the Oliver‒Pharr method [33−35]. As listed in Table 3, the three coatings exhibit similar Young’s moduli: 152.95±13.82, 153.92±16.74, and 153.57±10.22 GPa for AZC-1, AZC-2, and AZC-3, respectively. These values are comparable to the results reported for APS Al2O3–ZrO2 coatings and FM regions by Suffner *et al*. [71] and Bhadauria *et al*. [70] but are higher than those reported for most similar coatings in other studies [57,72,73], as shown in Table S1 in the ESM.

The fracture toughness of the Al2O3–ZrO2 composite coatings with nanoeutectic structures was evaluated through Vickers indentation measurements with a 1 kgf load on the polished surface, and the results are summarized in Table 3. Notably, the in-plane hardness values of the coatings are relatively lower than the cross-sectional results, which is consistent with previous reports and has been attributed to the anisotropic deformation behavior of thermally sprayed coatings with a stacked splat structure [37,57]. The fracture toughness values of the AZC-1, AZC-2, and AZC-3 samples were 4.07±0.50, 4.22±0.58, and 3.70±0.61 MPa·m1/2, respectively. Interestingly, the AZC-3 sample fabricated at the highest spray power (i.e., CPSP 772) exhibited the lowest toughness among the samples, which was significantly different from the hardness results. Regarding the relative density, the AZC-3 coating with the lowest porosity is expected to exhibit the highest fracture toughness due to the increased structural strength provided by more closely packed splats with stronger intersplat bonding. However, the fracture toughness of ceramics is significantly influenced by their microstructure. Some previous studies on thermally sprayed ceramic coatings with bimodal microstructures have confirmed that PM regions play an important role in increasing coating toughness [5,9,20,26,74]. Compared with FM regions, PM regions demonstrate increased crack propagation resistance owing to their refined microstructure and the contribution of additional toughening mechanisms. In this work, the potential contribution of the PM region with a nanoeutectic structure to fracture toughness was qualitatively analyzed through microscopic observations and comparisons of crack paths. Figures 12(a) and 12(b) illustrate the distinct crack paths in the FM and PM regions. Clearly, when cracks propagate in the FM region with a homogeneous microstructure, the paths remain relatively straight, suggesting the absence of additional toughening mechanisms (Fig. 12(c)). In contrast, when cracks propagate in the PM region, the paths become significantly more tortuous, with crack deflection occurring frequently (Figs. 12(d)–12(f)). During the fracture of nanoeutectic ceramics, cracks tend to develop along or deflect toward the interface between the eutectic phases due to the high strength of fine eutectic grains and the lattice mismatch of the phase interface [75,76]. In the plasma-sprayed coatings obtained from air-atomized powder, the nanosized eutectic grains and the variable orientation relationship between eutectic colonies are notable features of the PM region. During crack propagation in the PM region, the path frequently deflects due to microstructural constraints and stress fluctuations, leading to stress redistribution at the crack tip and increased energy required for crack growth. Moreover, the pulling-out and bridging mechanisms of nanosized grains with fibrous or lamellar morphologies contribute to energy dissipation and promote crack closure, thereby further enhancing resistance to crack propagation (Figs. 12(d)–12(f)). In addition, with increasing CPSP, a greater proportion of the FM region corresponds to a greater content of the amorphous phase in the coating. As observed by Xu *et al*. [77,78] in their studies on Al2O3–ZrO2 composite ceramics with controlled crystallization, an increase in the amorphous phase content leads to a decrease in both hardness and fracture toughness because the amorphous phase has a lower true density and looser ionic packing (weaker atomic bonding) than the crystalline phase does. The above analysis indicates that the PM region with a nanoeutectic structure has an important beneficial effect on the fracture toughness of the plasma-sprayed coatings. Compared with those of the other two samples, the significant decrease in the proportion of the PM region can be considered the main reason for the reduced fracture toughness of the AZC-3 coating. However, when CPSP decreases from 713 to 653, the fracture toughness decreases because of the significant increase in porosity and weakness in intersplat bonding, which underscores the negative effects of excessively low spray power. Consequently, AZC-2 with CPSP 713 has an optimized fracture toughness of 4.22±0.58 MPa·m1/2, which exceeds that of most APS coatings of similar systems reported previously [11,13,39,72,79] (Table S1 in the ESM).

In brief, the increase in spray power produces two opposite effects for the Al2O3–ZrO2 coating with a nanoeutectic structure: One is the improvement in the densification effect and cohesive strength, and the other is the reduction in the proportion of the PM region with the potential for toughness enhancement. This opposition needs to be rationally balanced in the regulation of spray power to optimize the mechanical properties of nanostructured coatings.

### 3.4 Wear behavior of the Al2O3–ZrO2 coatings with a nanoeutectic structure

To investigate the potential of Al2O3–ZrO2 composite coatings with nanoeutectic structures for wear protection applications, the samples were subjected to dry sliding wear tests via the ball-on-disk method with a Si3N4 ball as the counterbody. The coefficients of friction for the three samples under different loads and rotational speeds during the testing process are presented in Fig. 13. Under relatively mild wear conditions (lower load and rotational rates), the differences in the running-in stage are likely caused by variations in the porosity and hardness of the coatings, as the wear behavior of the coatings under these conditions is significantly influenced by contact surface conditions (including porosity and roughness), plastic deformation, and other factors [13,80−82]. When the wear conditions are increased to a load of 4 N and a rotational speed of 500 r·min−1, the observed trend in the change in the running-in stage duration may be attributed to a shift in the dominant wear mechanism. As previously reported, when the wear conditions for ceramics exceed a certain threshold, the wear mechanism transitions to being predominantly governed by fatigue fracture caused by the development of microcracks [17,80,81]. After the running-in stage, the three coatings exhibit similar coefficients of friction under the same wear conditions (Table 4). As expected, an increase in the normal load or rotational speed led to an increase in the friction coefficient. At a rotational speed of 250 r·min−1, the coefficients of friction for the Al2O3–ZrO2 coatings with nanoeutectic structures at loads of 2, 4, and 6 N are 0.063–0.086, 0.295–0.312, and 0.665–0.708, respectively. When the rotational speed is increased to 500 r·min−1, the coefficients of friction for the coatings at 2, 4, and 6 N loads are 0.167–0.215, 0.358–0.504, and 0.744–0.758, respectively.

As shown in Fig. 16, the microstructural characterization confirms that the wear mechanisms for all three coatings are similar under each testing condition and that the transition of the wear mechanism is influenced primarily by the load. At a load of 2 N, the wear mechanism of the coatings is a mixed mode involving adhesive wear, plastic deformation, and material detachment caused by intersplat fracture. As displayed in Figs. 16(a) and 16(b), plow grooves are observed on the worn surface, indicating that the coatings undergo plastic deformation due to sliding contact. Furthermore, distinct adhesive wear is evident. This can be attributed to the Si3N4 ball being cut by the hard protrusions on the coating surface during grinding. The generated debris then adheres to the worn surface, leading to material transfer. The EDS semiquantitative analysis (Fig. S8 in the ESM) confirmed that the adhered materials were composed of wear debris from both the ceramic coating and the Si3N4 balls. Owing to repeated shear and compaction, the wear debris undergoes plastic deformation and forms a thin-layered structure with a smooth surface. Moreover, as depicted in Figs. 16(c) and 16(d), spalling pits featuring smooth walls (ranging in size from several to tens of micrometers) are sparsely distributed across the worn surface. These pits predominantly emerge at the boundaries of the splats. The interfaces between stacked splats are the weakest bonding areas in the coating and the areas with high preexisting defect concentrations [5,9,84]. During the wear process, microfractures are more likely to initiate in these areas because of localized stress concentrations [17]. Under lower contact stresses, cracks tend to propagate along or toward splat boundaries, causing interface separation between splats with weaker bonding [9,17,83]. This results in the detachment of a few splats (or unmelted particles) and a slight brittle fracture near the splat boundaries. Moreover, the detached abrasive debris further plows the worn surface under cyclic loading (third-body wear), accelerating coating failure [85]. As previously reported in numerous studies [9,13,17,86], brittle and porous APS ceramic coatings generally present complex wear behavior with multiple coexisting mechanisms, including plastic deformation, splat fracture, and fatigue fracture originating from intrasplat crack propagation. The wear resistance of coatings is collectively influenced by the microhardness, toughness, porosity, and interplastic bonding. As shown in Figs. 16(a)–16(d), under a relatively low load of 2 N, crack growth within the splats is not obvious. The differences in the wear resistance of the coatings with different CPSP values are more likely due to the influences of microhardness, porosity, and intersplat bonding. In this work, the AZC-2 and AZC-3 coatings have relatively high microhardnesses, which provide them with a better foundation to resist plastic deformation. Moreover, increasing the CPSP (i.e., spray power) promoted the densification of the coatings (reduced porosity) and enhanced intersplat bonding. This is beneficial for alleviating the material detachment caused by the fracture between splats, thereby improving the wear resistance. Under these influences, the AZC-3 sample with CPSP 772 exhibited the lowest wear rates at 2 N.

However, as the load increases, the phenomenon of fatigue fracture in the wear tracks becomes obvious. This can be attributed to the propagation and network-like crosslinking of fatigue cracks in the coating surface and subsurface under cyclic loading [11,28]. As shown in Figs. 16(e)–16(h), when the load increases to 4 N, in addition to adhesive wear, the Al2O3–ZrO2 composite coatings with nanoeutectic structures exhibit a mixed wear mode consisting of intersplat delamination and intrasplat microfractures (fatigue fractures). This means that as the contact stress increases, cracks not only propagate along the weak interfaces between splats but also extend within individual splats. In this case, the advantage of the PM region in resisting crack propagation becomes more evident. As shown in Figs. 16(g) and 16(h), the intrasplat fracture surfaces display two distinct morphological features attributable to their formation in regions with different microstructures: a relatively smooth fracture surface, marked by overall flatness or interspersed with occasional irregular steps (in the FM region), and a significantly rougher surface, featuring dense fine protrusions (in the PM region). As discussed previously, compared with the FM region of a homogeneous structure, cracks passing through the PM region with a nanoeutectic structure may frequently undergo deflection, and toughening mechanisms, including the pulling-out and bridging of nanosized grains with fibrous or lamellar morphologies, contribute to additional energy dissipation. Therefore, the presence of the PM region enhances the wear resistance of the coating by increasing the resistance to intrasplat crack propagation. As listed in Table 4, when the load is increased from 2 to 4 N, the AZC-3 sample with the lowest PM content has a decreasing trend in terms of wear resistance. Even so, the AZC-1 coating still has the worst wear resistance due to its excessively high porosity, poor intersplat bonding, and low microhardness.

When the applied load is further increased to 6 N, not only is the adhesive wear phenomenon observed, but a significant increase in granular wear debris is also found in the pits of the worn surface due to intensified material damage (Figs. 16(i)–16(p) and Fig. S8 in the ESM). EDS analysis revealed the presence of Si in both types of wear debris (with relatively lower Si contents in the granular debris), and its content significantly differed from that in the microfracture surface of the coating (Fig. S8 in the ESM). More importantly, the significantly rougher worn surface indicates that intrasplat fracture plays a dominant role in the mixed wear mechanism. The contribution of the PM region to the wear resistance of the coatings is further emphasized. Consequently, the AZC-3 sample, which contains the lowest proportion of the PM region (and has the lowest toughness), exhibited the greatest wear loss among the three samples under the 6 N load. In contrast, optimized wear resistance is achieved in the AZC-2 sample with CPSP 713 owing to the proper balance of the PM region content and intersplat bonding (as well as porosity). In summary, an adequately high spray power to ensure sufficient cohesive strength is fundamental to the abrasion resistance of the coating.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, plasma-sprayed Al2O3–ZrO2 composite coatings were fabricated using nanoeutectic powders synthesized via CS-AA as the feedstock. The coating has a bimodal microstructure featuring the coexistence of the FM region and the PM region. The FM region has a homogeneous microstructure, primarily consisting of an amorphous phase, whereas the PM region typically features cellular eutectic colonies composed of fibrous or lamellar t-ZrO2 and δ-Al2O3 eutectic phases, with eutectic spacings of 30–75 nm. Moreover, partial crystallization of the melt was also observed in local zones of the coating, including the formation of transition zones and the precipitation of fine t-ZrO2 grains. The presence of the PM region has an important effect on the mechanical properties of the coating. Compared with the FM region (microhardness from 756.72±157.45 to 877.36±125.83 HV0.2), the PM region (microhardness from 1415.83±119.61 to 1439.60±134.69 HV0.2) has a significantly greater microhardness, which is attributed to the refined microstructure and lower amorphous content. Moreover, the PM region contributes to the fracture toughness of the coating because of the frequent crack deflection and the toughening mechanisms of the pulling-out and bridging of eutectic grains. Increasing the plasma spray power improved the deposition efficiency, densification, and intersplat bonding of the coatings but reduced the proportion of the PM region. By balancing the opposing effects, the composite coating with CPSP 713 (corresponding to a spray power of 57 kW) exhibited an optimized hardness (1008.39±308.54 HV0.2) and fracture toughness (4.22±0.58 MPa·m1/2). During the dry sliding friction process, the coatings demonstrate mixed wear mechanisms, including plastic deformation, adhesive wear, intersplat fracture, and fatigue fracture. At applied loads of 2–4 N, the coating with CPSP 772 has the highest wear resistance, which is attributed primarily to its advantages in hardness, relative density, and intersplat bonding. When the load increases to 6 N, the intrasplat fracture induced by fatigue crack propagation becomes the predominant wear mechanism. The coating with CPSP 713 achieves a wear rate of 5×10−5 mm3·N−1·m−1, benefiting from its enhanced fracture toughness. Combustion synthesis-air atomization offers a promising approach for producing nanostructured feedstocks for thermal spraying. The findings of this work may offer valuable references for the development and optimization of nanostructured ceramic coatings for wear protection applications.
