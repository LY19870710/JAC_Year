## 1 Introduction

In the face of the challenges brought about by the resource crisis and climate change, secondary batteries with high specific energy have become a research hotspot in the field of global clean energy and are highly important for reducing carbon emissions [1−3]. The most widely used secondary electrochemical systems include rechargeable lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) and sodium-ion batteries (SIBs), which are mostly composed of essential components, including electrolytes, separators, and cathode and anode materials [4−6]. Cathode materials account for approximately 40% of the material cost (Fig. 1(a)), which directly affects the battery’s energy density and safety and, in turn, its overall performance [7−9]. Therefore, the development of cathode materials with advantages such as safety, low cost, good stability, high performance, and high capacity is crucial. This will effectively promote the further development and widespread application of power batteries.

*
Fig. 1(a) Cathode material accounts for total battery material cost. (b) Characteristics of ideal cathode material.

Typically, well-performing cathode materials need to possess the following characteristics: (1) high redox voltage, (2) high safety, (3) stable discharge voltage, (4) small structural changes during charge and discharge processes, (5) high electronic and ionic conductivity, and (6) abundant resources and simple preparation (Fig. 1(b)).

According to the cathode material system [10,11], commercial LIBs are currently classified into four categories: lithium cobalt oxide (LCO), lithium manganese oxide (LMO), lithium iron phosphate (LFP), and ternary materials, which are represented by nickel cobalt manganese (NCM) oxide. LCO has a high working voltage, large volumetric energy density, and good electrochemical performance. It is mainly used in the 3C field. However, the global reserves of cobalt (Co) are very limited, leading to high battery costs [12−14]. LMO has a low energy density and short cycle life, and it is mainly used in small power and new energy specialty vehicle applications [15,16]. LFP and ternary materials are the mainstream cathode materials used in current power batteries [17,18]. Ternary batteries have a greater capacity than LFP batteries, but their safety and stability are not as good as those of LFP batteries [19]. Research on cathode materials for SIBs has focused mainly on transition metal (TM) oxides [20−23], polyanionic compounds [24,25], Prussian blue (PB) and its analogs (PBAs) [26−29]. The most promising cathode materials at present are layered oxides. However, because single metal layered oxides have structural instability and low cycle efficiency, research has concentrated on ternary or multiple oxides [30,31]. Among polyanionic compounds, the NASICON-type structure Na3M2(PO4)3 exhibits excellent ionic conductivity, with a focus on the study of phosphate fluoride (Na3V2O2x*(PO4)2F3−2*x*) with a high working voltage [32,33]. PBAs exhibit good structural stability and a high theoretical capacity, but the presence of crystal vacancies and crystalline water seriously affect both the capacity and long cycling performance of batteries, thereby constraining their further development in the energy storage field [34,35].

In this context, this paper provides a comprehensive overview of current cathode materials for power batteries. This paper first outlines the development history, practical prospects, and challenges of cathode materials for LIBs and SIBs. On this basis, we systematically elucidate various material modification strategies, including process optimization, element doping, surface coating, and structural architecture. Furthermore, we will review the design concept of high-entropy battery materials (HEBMs) and summarize the existing research results and problems, emphasizing the important role of high-entropy effects on electrochemical performance. Finally, we predict the future development direction of cathode materials for rechargeable power batteries and look forward to the prospect of HEBMs to promote their functional applications in the field of energy.

### 2.1 Development process of cathodes for LIBs

In 1976, Whittingham [36] used titanium disulfide as a cathode material and lithium metal as an anode material to study the mechanism of lithium extraction and insertion. However, due to the low operating voltage of sulfide, the energy density of the battery is low. According to the redox mechanism, the lower the oxidation energy of the cathode material is, the better, and the lower the reduction energy of the anode material is, the better. Research shows that the orbital energy of O2− is lower than that of S2−, and when it matches that of TMs, the redox potential of TM oxides can increase. Inspired by this, Professor Goodenough and others discovered the layered oxide LiCoO2 in 1980, the spinel-type LiMn2O4 in 1983, and the polyanionic material LiFePO4 in 1997 for LIB cathode materials [37−39]. In 1999, Liu *et al*. [40] prepared a Li[Ni1−*x*−*y*Co*x*Mn*y*]O2 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.5, 0 ≤ *y* ≤ 0.3) ternary layered cathode material. This ternary system material cangreatly improve the battery’s specific capacity and cycle stability while reducing the cost, and it has very broad development prospects. In 2001, Ohzuku and Makimura [41] prepared a Li[Ni1/3Co1/3Mn1/3]O2 ternary layer cathode material (NCM333) by a hydroxide coprecipitation method, which showed a reversible specific capacity of 160 mAh·g−1, good cycling performance, and good safety performance. Reports of NCM333 ternary materials immediately attracted the attention of researchers, and NCM ternary cathode materials entered a period of rapid development and gradual commercialization. In 2004, Oh *et al*. [42] prepared a Li[Ni0.4Mn0.4Co0.2]O2 (NCM424) ternary material, which can provide a specific capacity of 175 mAh·g−1 at a voltage of 2.8–4.4 V.The study revealed that the higher the nickel content in the NCM ternary material was, the greater the specific capacity of the material. In 2005, Liao *et al*. [43] further increased the proportion of nickel in ternary materials and prepared a series of materials, Li[Ni0.6Co*x*Mn0.4−*x*]O2 (*x* = 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, and 0.2), among which the initial discharge capacity of the NCM622 ternary material reached 178.4 mAh·g−1. The results showed that an increase in the nickel content is beneficial for increasing the specific capacity, while the cycling performance and thermal stability of the material deteriorate with decreasing manganese content. In 2006, Kim *et al*. [44] synthesized Li[Ni0.8Co0.1Mn0.1]O2 (NCM811) layered material, whose initial discharge specific capacity reached 198 mAh·g−1. NCM811 has excellent overall electrochemical performance and has become the main research direction of high-capacity cathode materials for new energy vehicles in recent years. In the future, the development of ternary materials in the direction of high nickel and low cobalt or no cobalt will improve battery performance and reduce costs. ### 2.2 Practical prospects and challenges

As mentioned above, the cathode material is a critical core component in power batteries, determining the energy density and safety performance, thus influencing the overall performance of the battery. Four main types of LIB cathode materials have been successfully developed and widely commercialized: LCO, LMO, LFP, and ternary materials such as lithium NCM and lithium nickel cobalt aluminum oxide (NCA). The actual reversible capacity of LiCoO2 is only approximately 150 mAh·g−1, leading to low utilization of Co. Additionally, Co is highly toxic, scarce in resources, and expensive. Therefore, the current primary research focus is to find substitute elements for Co to decrease the production cost of cathode materials [45,46]. Spinel-type LiMn2O4 cathode materials exhibit good structural stability, excellent low-temperature performance, and low cost. However, the Jahn–Teller effect, dissolution of Mn3+, and electrolyte decomposition result in severe capacity decay and deterioration of cycle performance for LiMn2O4 cathode materials [47]. Olivine-type LiFePO4 is a cost-effective and safe option that meets the safety requirements of electric vehicles. However, it has a low Li+ diffusion coefficient in the bulk phase, poor low-temperature performance, and a relatively low theoretical capacity. These factors make it challenging to meet the demands of current high energy density and high power density requirements [48]. The ternary materials NCM and NCA exhibit high specific capacity and excellent cycle life, but they suffer from a low voltage platform and difficulties in controlling the element content [49−51]. With increasing market demand for capacity, the development direction of ternary materials is gradually shifting toward high-nickel (Ni ≥ 80%) and low-cobalt cathode materials, simultaneously improving energy density and reducing costs [52]. Furthermore, monocrystalline materials at high voltages have emerged as a new technical approach for enhancing energy density and improving the safety performance and cycling performance of ternary materials [53−55]. In terms of energy density, LFP has a lower theoretical specific capacity than ternary materials. However, the energy density difference between these two materials is somewhat reduced when they are made into battery modules due to the complex battery management system required for ternary material batteries. Regarding safety, ternary materials typically experience self-heating above 180 °C, decompose and release oxygen at approximately 200 °C, and experience electrolyte combustion at high temperatures, which can lead to intensified chain reactions. LFP demonstrates relatively good safety performance, with thermal phenomena occurring only above 250 °C. It decomposes at 700–800 °C without releasing oxygen, resulting in less intense combustion than ternary materials. Therefore, LFP cathode materials are safer.

In terms of manufacturing cost, cobalt salts, nickel salts, and lithium salts are the main raw materials used to prepare ternary materials. Among them, cobalt and nickel salts have relatively small extractable reserves in China, leading to a tight supply and high production costs for ternary materials. For LFP, the main raw materials are lithium sources, iron sources, and phosphorus sources. Due to the relatively abundant availability of iron and phosphorus, LFP has a cost advantage. Based on these characteristics, LFP cathode materials were initially used mainly in new energy commercial vehicles. With relatively high energy density, ternary materials are primarily used in new energy passenger vehicles. Given the recent development of LIB technology and its global application, the future development of LIB cathode materials will mainly focus on ternary materials taking the mainstream position, high-nickelization, and the coexistence of LFP and ternary materials.

### 3.1 Development process of cathodes for SIBs

As shown in Fig. 4, SIBs were in the development stage from 1970 to 1980, with the emergence of high-temperature sodium-sulfur batteries and ZEBRA batteries [56,57]. Between 1980 and 1990, sodium was applied in power and energy storage, leading to the invention of high-temperature SIBs [58,59]. However, during this period, a stable anode electrode was lacking. In 1981, Delmas *et al*. [60] first reported the electrochemical performance of Na*x*CoO2 layered oxide cathode materials and proposed a classification method for layered oxide structures. In 1991, Sony successfully commercialized LIBs using a soft carbon and LiCoO2 system, marking a decade of stagnation for SIBs. It was not until the year 2000 that Stevens and Dahn [61] discovered that hard carbon materials exhibited excellent sodium storage performance, which led to the first turning point in the development of SIBs. The second crucial discovery occurred in 2006 when Okada *et al*. [62] reported the reversible transition of Fe4+/Fe3+ redox couples in NaFeO2, while these redox couples showed no electrochemical activity in LiFeO2. In addition to layered oxides, Ellis *et al*. [63] reported Na2FePO4F polyanion cathode materials in 2007, which demonstrated good structural stability during sodium ion extraction/insertion processes.

Fig. 4Development history of sodium ion cathode materials.

Apart from investigating new material systems, the development of SIBs has also focused on achieving low-cost and practical applications. In 2011, Komaba et al*. [64] reported the performance of a full cell made of hard carbon||NaNi0.5Mn0.5O2. In the same year, Faradion, the world’s first SIB company, was established in the UK. In 2012, Lee *et al*. [65] and Lu *et al*. [66] proposed sodium cathode materials based on PBAs that exhibit high voltage and excellent rate capability. In 2014, Xu *et al*. [67] discovered Cu3+/Cu2+ redox couples in layered oxides for the first time. In 2017, the HaiNa Battery, China’s first SIB research and development company, was established. In 2018 and 2019, the company launched the world’s first SIB low-speed electric vehicle powered by an SIB and the first 100 kWh sodium-ion energy storage station, respectively. In 2021, Contemporary Amperex Technology Co., Ltd. (CATL) released the first generation of SIBs. Since then, SIBs have entered a period of vigorous development [68].

### 3.2 Practical prospects and challenges

After 2010, the demand for power batteries increased, leading to a shortage of materials for LIBs. As a result, unprecedented progress has been made in the research of SIB cathode materials [69,70]. Currently, the three main cathode materials for SIBs are layered TM oxides, polyanion-type compounds, and PBAs. The aim is to produce affordable SIBs with performance characteristics similar to LIBs. This emphasizes the selection of abundant elements on earth, such as iron and manganese, for the composition of cathode materials [71−73].

Layered TM oxides have a layered structure formed by alternating layers of TM and alkali metal, with the structural formula Na*x*TMO2 (TM represents a TM element) [20,74,75]. The TM layer consists of repeating TMO6 octahedra connected by edges, and Na+ is positioned between the TM layers, forming an alkali metal layer. Based on the coordination configuration of Na+ in the TMO6 octahedra and the stacking mode of O2−, Delmas *et al*. [60] classified layered TM oxides into four different structures: O3, O2, P3, and P2, as shown in Fig. 5. The most common structures are O3 and P2, where O and P represent octahedral and trigonal prism coordination configurations of Na+, respectively, and 2 and 3 represent the minimum number of stacking layers for O2−. These two structures have advantages when used as cathode materials for SIBs. O3-phase cathode materials have a higher initial Na content, enabling the extraction of more sodium ions and providing a higher capacity. P2-phase cathode materials have a larger Na layer spacing, enhancing the sodium ion transport rate and maintaining the integrity of the layered structure, resulting in excellent rate and cycling performance [23,76,77]. Due to their convenient synthesis, simple structure, and wide availability of source materials, layered TM oxides are widely considered the most promising materials for commercialization, with representative companies including the HaiNa Battery, Natrium, and Faradion. In addition, some famous universities and research institutes have also studied layered oxide SIB routes and have made remarkable achievements. For instance, Wang *et al*. [78] took the lead in large-scale synthesis and scale-up experiments of NaNi1/3Fe1/3Mn1/3O2 ternary cathode materials, systematically revealing the structural phase transition law of the sodium ion insertion/extraction process [79] and providing a theoretical basis for the design and management of SIBs. Zuo *et al*. [80] systematically investigated the constitutive relationship of layered oxide cathode materials for SIBs and provided unique insights into overcoming such problems as their air stability [81].

Polyanion-type compounds have the general formula Nax*M*y*(X*a*O*b*)zZ*w* (M represents a TM atom; X represents P, S, etc.; Z represents F, OH, etc.) [82,83]. They are formed by connecting polyanion polyhedra and TM ion polyhedra through strong covalent bonds, resulting in a robust and open three-dimensional network structure. Polyanion-type compounds exhibit the highest thermal and electrochemical stability, making them optimal for cycling. However, their drawbacks include high cost, low energy density, and poor rate performance. Polyanion-type compounds can be categorized into phosphate, sulfate, and mixed anion compound systems (Fig. 6). Among them, phosphate compounds have been extensively studied, including olivine-structured NaMPO4, NASICON-structured Na3V2(PO4)3, and pyrophosphate-structured Na2MP2O7 (M = Fe, Mn, and Co), all of which have potential application prospects [84−87]. Like LiFePO4, NaFePO4 exhibits excellent electrochemical performance but has a lower energy density. NASICON-structured materials, represented by Na3V2(PO4)3, feature a highly open framework structure, providing three-dimensional diffusion channels and large migration gaps for sodium ions, resulting in outstanding rate performance [88,89]. Sodium vanadium phosphate is currently in small-scale production, but its cost remains high. Representative enterprises in the polyanion pathway include Zoolnasm, China; Great Power, China; Tiamat, France; and Naiades, France. Zoolnasm uses sodium iron sulfate as the cathode, with lower raw material costs. At the same time, Great Power employs sodium vanadium phosphate and sodium fluorophosphate as the cathode, achieving a noticeable increase in energy density, albeit with higher costs due to the addition of vanadium.

The chemical formula of the PB/PBAs is Nax*M1[M2(CN)6] (M represents a TM atom, 0 90,91]. According to the sodium ion content, PBAs are divided into Prussian white (high) and PB (low) materials. PBAs have a typical cubic crystal structure, where all metal ions are located at the corners of the cube, forming a large tunnel structure that allows for the free insertion and de-insertion of sodium ions, resulting in excellent theoretical rate performance and cycling stability [92,93]. Additionally, they have high energy density, and their core materials are Fe and Mn, making them relatively inexpensive. The phase of PB and PBAs can change based on the content of gap water, vacancies, and sodium ions, including monoclinic, rhombic, cubic, and tetragonal structures (Fig. 7). During the charging process, as the alkali content increases, the PB phase changes from cubic to rhombic [91,94]. However, due to the difficulty in completely removing coordinated water from the lattice, various side reactions can occur during practical use, significantly affecting the battery’s capacity and cycling performance. Furthermore, it is worth noting that PBAs exhibit poor thermal stability and can generate toxic HCN when reacting with water, which presents significant safety and environmental challenges [26,35,95]. Currently, enterprises adopting the PB/white analog technology pathway include CATL, Star Sodium, Natron Energy (USA), and Altris (Sweden).

At present, SIBs are transitioning from laboratory to practical applications. Many companies, such as FARADION in the UK, Natron Energy in the USA, Tiamat in France, and Toyota, Panasonic, and Mitsubishi Chemical in Japan, are involved in this field.* In addition to China’s HaiNa Battery, Natrium, and CATL are carrying out the relevant layout of SIB industrialization and have made important progress (Fig. 8). Current SIB manufacturers are predominantly focusing on the cathode material layout in layered oxide structures. The British Faradion Company used Mn–Ni–Ti–Mg quaternary layered oxide as the cathode material, the battery energy density exceeded 140 Wh·kg−1, and the cycle life exceeded 3000 cycles. The HaiNa Battery employs a Cu–Fe–Mn ternary layered oxide cathode material, and the battery energy density reaches 145 Wh·kg−1. Natrium utilises a Fe–Ni–Mn ternary layered oxide with a specific capacity exceeding 130 mAh·g−1 and an energy density of approximately 130–160 Wh·kg−1.

Moreover, the PB (white) and polyanion routes are continuously being developed among major manufacturers. The SIB released by CATL in 2021 has a cell energy density of 160 Wh·kg−1, emerging at the highest level in the world at present and exhibiting good fast-charge performance. Zoolnasm, Naiades, and Tiamat use vanadium-based polyanion-type compounds as cathode materials, where the energy density of the Zoolnasm cell is 120–160 Wh·kg−1, the cycle performance can reach 2000–10,000 cycles, and the cell can work stably at −20 °C, while the energy density of Naiades and Tiamat is only 90 Wh·kg−1, and the cycle life is 4000 cycles at 1 C.

In general, China’s sodium battery industry is in the early stage of small-scale industrialization with better battery performance, but it will take some time for the market to mature compared with that of foreign enterprises. Referring to the technological iteration process of LIBs, different cathode technology routes are expected to coexist in the long term and compete with each other. Due to differences in energy density and cost, various technological routes are likely to find extensive application in different usage scenarios.

## 4 Modification strategies

Overall, LFP and ternary materials have gradually become mainstream materials in the commercialization of LIBs. However, it is crucial to address key issues such as improving electronic and ionic conductivity, enhancing cycling stability, and meeting high energy density requirements. Similarly, in the commercialization of SIBs, layered TM oxides face challenges such as complex irreversible phase transitions and high air sensitivity [21,31]. Polyanion compounds have limited commercial applications due to their lower electronic conductivity. PBAs lose competitiveness due to excess crystalline water. This section summarizes and analyzes the research results related to component regulation, providing a basis for subsequent comparative analysis with high-entropy component design and regulation. Typically, doping can achieve the following functions: (1) Increasing the material’s electronic conductivity and ion migration rate. The implementation involves replacing a portion of the elements with one or more other ions. Commonly used doping elements include K, Ca, Al, Ti, Co, Cu, Zn, and others. Each element has unique properties, and the electrochemical performance can be enhanced through customized doping strategies.

### 4.1 Enhancing electronic/ionic conductivity

Element doping is a crucial method for improving the internal electronic conductivity and ion diffusion rate of LiFePO4 and NASICON materials [149−151]. Current research is mainly divided into cation doping and anion doping. Cation doping involves the substitution of alkali metal ions, polyanion positions, and TM positions, enlarging the ion diffusion channels, generating holes, and tuning the band gap. Anion doping aims to enhance the induction effect. The selection of doping elements varies with different doping sites. Generally, the closer the radii of two ions are, the easier their mutual substitution. Moreover, higher valence states of doped ions are more favorable for forming more defects in the lattice after doping, which plays a crucial role in promoting material conductivity and the diffusion rate of alkali metal ions in the material.

For alkali metal site doping, smaller metal ions are usually employed. Researchers have made great efforts in this area. Chung *et al*. [152] doped high valence ions into the LiFePO4 lattice, forming Li1−*x*M*x*FePO4 (M = Mg2+, Al3+, Ti4+, Zr4+, Nb5+, W6+, etc*.*), which introduces cationic defects. This process significantly increases the conductivity by approximately eight orders of magnitude to 10−2 S·cm−1, greatly enhancing the electrochemical performance of LiFePO4. First-principles studies indicate that small-radius high-valence metal ions doped into the Li site can increase the hole mobility in LiFePO4, facilitating the transition from an intrinsic semiconductor to an extrinsic P-type semiconductor, thereby increasing the conductivity and further improving the electrochemical performance of the material [153]. The doping elements in transition metal Fe or V sites are usually equivalent or higher valence state ions with smaller radii, such as Mg, Al, Ti, Cr, Mn, Cu, Nb, and Mo [154−160]. TM site doping weakens the TM–O bond, increases the lattice volume, and results in higher ion migration rates and diffusion coefficients.

Despite the effective improvement in conductivity and promotion of lithium/sodium ion diffusion kinetics by doping small-radius high-valence metal ions, excessive doping can negatively affect the material. For example, excessive Mn2+ doping can lead to severe Jahn–Teller distortion, and the dissolution of Mn2+ can alter the structure of the cathode material [161,162]. Therefore, choosing suitable doping ions and determining the optimal doping amount is crucial. Current research suggests that the optimal doping amount is within the range of 10 wt%.

### 4.2 Enhancing specific capacity

The charge compensation of traditional battery cathode materials is typically based on the redox pairs of TM ions, including Ni3+/Ni2+, Ni4+/Ni3+, Co4+/Co3+, Fe4+/Fe3+, Mn4+/Mn3+, Cu3+/Cu2+, and others. The energy density of oxide cathode materials mainly depends on the charge transfer number achievable by the redox pairs and their operating voltage [163,164]. Therefore, high-nickel and manganese-rich oxide materials can be designed to increase the achievable charge transfer number of TMs and enhance the specific capacity [54,165,166]. For PBA cathode materials, doping with active elements such as Fe, Co, and Mn can activate two redox sites, Fe2+/Fe3+ and M2+/M3+, thereby achieving a high reversible capacity [167−169].

In addition to TM cation redox pairs, anion redox reactions have attracted global attention in recent years due to their potential to increase the working voltage and contribute to high-capacity cathodes [170−172]. In particular, TMs located on the right side of the periodic table exhibit a tendency to induce oxygen redox reactions. This is due to their ability to form strong covalent bonds with oxygen, including the overlap of TMs and oxygen orbitals. Notably, when only 3d TMs are present without 4d or 5d TMs, the TM–O covalent bond is weaker, leading to the release of O2 under high pressure. In general, the main substitute TMs that can induce anionic redox activity include Al, Li, Mg, Zn, Cu, Ti, and Sn [173−176]. Furthermore, in addition to inducing lattice oxygen redox reactions to increase the specific capacity, doping elements with high electronegativity, such as F and Cl, can also induce anion redox reactions to improve the electrochemical performance of cathode materials [177−181].

Although anionic redox reactions can significantly increase the specific capacity, these reactions still suffer from unavoidable oxygen release and structural damage caused by phase transitions. Therefore, improving the structural stability is crucial for enhancing the electrochemical performance of oxide-positive electrode materials.

### 4.3 Enhancing structural stability

Lithium-ion manganese-based oxides and sodium-ion layered oxide cathode materials face challenges such as the Jahn–Teller effect, the migration and dissolution of TM ions, and irreversible phase transitions, which significantly affect the cycling stability of batteries. The introduction of a small number of columnar cations, such as K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Ti4+, Zn2+, etc., can effectively reduce the extent of volume changes during phase transitions, thereby maintaining the stability of the crystal structure [182−188]. Doping with a relatively large radius K can increase the interlayer spacing of Li ions, weaken the formation of three vacancies in the Li layer, and prevent the migration of Mn toward the Li layer [98,189−191]. In PBA battery materials, K and Ba doping can effectively prevent the entry of gap water and stabilize the material structure [192,193].

Ca and Mg ions typically exhibit a columnar effect in Li/Na layers, suppressing phase transitions and improving cyclic stability. Shen *et al*. [194] designed a P2-Na0.76Ca0.05[Ni0.23□0.08Mn0.69]O2 cathode material by introducing vacancies (□) in the TM layer and doping calcium ions in the sodium layer to regulate the structure. The intrinsic vacancies in the TM layer can form “Na–O–□” and “□–O–□” configurations, generating nonbonding O 2p orbitals and consequently causing a change in oxygen valence. Ca2+ doped into the sodium layer not only directly disturbs the Na+/vacancy ordered rearrangement during charge and discharge processes but also acts as a pillar during charging to mitigate O–O repulsion, suppress P2–O2 phase transitions and significantly improve the structural stability of the material. Moreover, the stronger Ca–O bond compared to the Na–O bond effectively mitigates the irreversible release of lattice oxygen at a high degree of sodiation. Xu *et al*. [195] pioneered the design of a Cr and Mg codoped P2-Na2/3Ni1/3Mn2/3O2 cathode material, which exhibited up to 82% capacity retention after 100 cycles at 1 C. *In-situ* X-ray diffraction analysis indicated that the “pillar effect” of Mg weakened the electrostatic shielding and effectively suppressed the generation of P2–O2 phase transitions during charge and discharge processes, preventing severe volume expansion caused by phase transitions and enhancing Na+ migration. At the same time, *in situ* Raman spectroscopy and *in situ* X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy tests demonstrated that the strong oxygen affinity of Cr formed robust TM–O bonds, effectively suppressing lattice oxygen precipitation during deep charging processes.

Due to the stable oxidation state of Ti4+ and strong Ti–O bonding, Ti4+-based doping is widely employed to enhance the electrochemical performance of high-nickel cathode materials [196,197]. This not only induces cation disorder to form a disordered layered phase structure but also significantly increases the surface binding energy of O in the disordered layered phase, effectively preventing reactions with H2O, CO2, and electrolytes. Zn2+, as a nonactive element, mainly serves to enhance structural stability. For example, Cheng *et al*. [198] designed a P2-Na0.67Cu0.05Zn0.07Ni0.21Mn0.67O2 (NNMCZO) cathode material through dual-ion codoping with Cu and Zn. Zn2+, with a similar ionic radius to that of Ni2+ but different Fermi level characteristics, successfully inhibited P2–O2 phase transitions and the ordered rearrangement of sodium ions/vacancies. Moreover, Cu2+, through enhanced TM–O hybridized orbitals, not only increased the oxidation-reduction potential of Ni but also suppressed irreversible oxygen oxidation.

## 5 High-entropy strategy

HEMs represent a new class of materials composed of multiple elements [199−201]. Combining multiple primary elements can achieve the maximum entropy configuration, resulting in improved performance. Inspired by the concept of entropy stabilization in metal alloys, this strategy has rapidly expanded into the field of secondary battery systems for energy storage [202−206].

High-entropy oxides (HEOs) exhibit high ionic conductivity, and most TM HEOs have high theoretical specific capacities. In addition, an almost equimolar ratio of elemental composition addresses the issues of material cost reduction and sustainability. Furthermore, the high-entropy characteristics effectively enhance the cycling stability of electrode materials [207−210]. Given these advantages, research on HEBMs has garnered considerable attention, and some excellent reviews have been published on this topic [211−214]. Figure 10 summarizes the distribution map of research on high-entropy cathode materials according to the data from the Web of Science. Bérardan *et al.* [215] reported a series of rock-salt structure HEOs, (MgCoNiCuZn)1−*x*−*y*Ga*y*A*x*O (with A = Li, Na, and K), which have unexpectedly superior ionic conductivity. In particular, (MgCoNiCuZn)0.67Li0.33O exhibits excellent Li+ ionic conductivity (> 10−3 S·cm−1) at room temperature, making it possible for application as a solid-state electrolyte in all-solid-state LIBs. In addition, (MgCoNiCuZn)O was found to have excellent reversible lithium storage properties in 2018 by Sarkar *et al*. [216], offering long-term cycling stability with an amazing specific capacity. This pioneering work opens up new possibilities for the development of high-energy-density anode materials for LIBs.

### 6.1 Definition of high-entropy and high-entropy effects

Clausius first introduced the concept of entropy in 1865 to measure disorder in a system. The term “high entropy” refers to a highly disordered state of a system, encompassing configurational entropy, vibrational entropy, magnetic dipole entropy, and electronic random entropy. Configurational entropy predominates and is easily quantifiable, making it the representative measure of system entropy in most studies.

The concept of HEMs originates from high-entropy alloys (HEAs), first proposed in 2004 by Cantor *et al*. [219] and Yeh *et al*. [220]. High-entropy alloys were initially defined as those composed of five or more elements in approximately equimolar ratios, where the atomic percentage of each element falls between 5% and 35% [221,222]. According to the definition based on configurational entropy, alloys with *S*mix ≥ 1.5*R* are considered high-entropy alloys. In contrast, alloys with 1.5*R* > *S*mix ≥ 1*R* and *S*mix Fig. 11(a)). The concept of HEOs was introduced by Rost *et al*. [223] in 2015, who demonstrated that entropy dominates thermodynamic processes and drives reversible transitions between multiple phases and a single phase.

Compared to conventional materials, HEMs exhibit a range of superior properties, such as high fracture toughness and strength in terms of mechanical performance and excellent energy storage capabilities in terms of thermodynamics [200,224−228]. ### 6.2 Research progress on high-entropy strategies in LIB cathode materials

During commercializing cathode materials for LIBs, Co plays a crucial role in stabilizing the cathode. However, due to its lower abundance and geopolitical concerns, it poses certain economic and security issues. By applying the high-entropy concept to lithium-ion cathode materials, the performance of electrode materials can be tuned by changing the elemental composition and the proportion of each metal [231−233]. This can reduce the use of cobalt, making it more cost-competitive with other materials. For example, adjusting the amounts of nickel, cobalt, and manganese can alter the battery’s initial capacity and capacity retention by using ternary materials with medium configurational entropy. The fluorine doping strategy can improve the cycling stability of ternary materials. Furthermore, incorporating TM elements with atomic radii close to those of cobalt, such as Mg, Ti, and Al, enhances battery performance with the aim of partially or completely replacing cobalt. In addition, layered HE materials with uniformly distributed elements have more oxygen vacancies, which promotes lithium-ion transport and increases ion conductivity.

The most widely studied HEOs in LIB applications are primarily rock salts [223,234] and layered oxides [235,236−238]. The rock salt type (Co0.2Cu0.2Mg0.2Ni0.2Zn0.2)O was first reported by Rost and colleagues in groundbreaking work [223]. However, the stable *S*config effect only comes from the cations present in the crystal structure, with no contribution from anion sites. Therefore, current rock salt-type lithium-ion high-entropy electrodes are based on fluorides. According to the concept of the crystal field, metal fluorides generally exhibit higher electrochemical potentials than oxides due to the strong ionic nature of the M–F bonds. Wang *et al*. [234] first prepared the multianion and multication rock salt compound Li(HEO)F for lithium-ion cathode materials using a simple mechanochemical method (Fig. 12(a)). The results indicate that the introduction of fluorine increases the working potential to 3.4 V compared to that of Li+/Li. In comparison, the working potential of various TM-based HEOs is approximately 1.0 V (Figs. 12(b) and 12(c)). In addition, the contribution of the anion lattice contributes to the configurational entropy and produces an additional structural stability effect, resulting in good capacity retention and Li+ deintercalation ability of the material (Fig. 12(d)). Subsequently, they investigated various layered HEOs, including LiNa(NiCoMnAlFe)1O2 [239]. The results show that incorporating a small amount of Na ions can widen the ion diffusion channel and improve battery cycling performance [80]. However, these materials exhibit low capacity (~80 mAh·g−1) and high capacity degradation. Similarly, Sturman *et al*. [235] reported the electrochemical performance of Li(NiCoMnTiFe)1O2 layered HEO using the sol−gel method, observing an initial capacity of 160 mAh·g−1 for a high-entropy sample with a stable capacity of 85 mAh·g−1.

### 6.3 Research progress on high-entropy strategies in SIB cathode materials

The high-entropy strategy has been successfully applied to enhance the performance of various sodium-ion cathodes, including layered TM oxides [243−245], NASICON [246−248], and PBA cathodes [249−251], with a predominant focus on layered oxides. In 2019, Zhao *et al*. [252] pioneered applying the high-entropy strategy in sodium-layered oxide cathode materials. They designed an O3-type HEO cathode material, NaNi0.12Cu0.12Mg0.12Fe0.15Co0.15Mn0.1Ti0.1Sn0.1Sb0.04O2, consisting of nine TM ions. Using this material as a model HEO, the study revealed that the high-entropy configuration on the oxide matrix effectively stabilized the layered O3-type structure, delaying the transition from O3 to P3 (Fig. 15(a)). The multielement composition further balances the interactions between the TMO2 layer and NaO2 layer, resulting in improved rate performance and long cycling stability (Figs. 15(b) and 15(c)). The capacity retention at a 5 C charge/discharge rate is 80% of the capacity at 0.1 C and 83% at a 3 C rate after 500 cycles (Fig. 15(d)). Subsequently, high-entropy layered oxide sodium-ion cathode materials with different types and contents of TMs were designed and developed, such as O3-type Na0.94Ni0.29Cu0.1Fe0.16Mn0.3Ti0.15O2 [253], P2-type Na0.6(Ti0.2Mn0.2Co0.2Ni0.2Ru0.2)O2 [254], O3-type NaMn0.2Fe0.2Co0.2Ni0.2Ti0.2O2 [244], Na(Fe0.2Co0.2Ni0.2Ti0.2Sn0.1Li0.1)O2  [255], NaCu0.1Ni0.3Fe0.2Mn0.2Ti0.2O2 [256], and NaNi0.25Mg0.05Cu0.1Fe0.2Mn0.2Ti0.1Sn0.1O2 [257]. The active elements Ni2+, Fe3+, and Co3+ mainly provide capacity to the materials; Mn4+, Mg2+, and Ti4+ contribute to stabilizing the structure and consequently inhibiting phase transitions; Cu2+ and Sn4+ help increase the average voltage; Li+ triggers the redox reactions of anions, leading to an expansion of the voltage range and capacity of the cathode material [214,258]. High-entropy doping widens the transport channels of the TM layer, resulting in faster Na+ transport kinetics [257]. It also significantly reduces the Jahn–Teller distortion of Ni3+ octahedra, Na+/vacancy-ordered–disordered transitions, and lattice parameter changes [236]. Furthermore, the high-entropy effect suppresses the dissolution of TM ions and intergranular cracks, enhancing thermal stability [210].

Due to irreversible phase transitions and severe structural distortion, NASICON-type Na3VM(PO4)3 (M: TM) cathodes generally exhibit poor cycling stability in the voltage range above 4 V compared to that of Na+/Na. Li et al*. [259] extended the concept of high entropy to NASICONs and obtained high-purity Na3VAl0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2In0.2Ga0.2(PO4)3 (NVMP, Fig. 16(a)). The NVMP material exhibited a high reversible specific capacity of 102 mAh·g−1 in the voltage range of 2.5–4.4 V with a minimal volume change (1.1%) and maintained a capacity retention rate of 86.8% after 5000 cycles at 20 C (Figs. 16(b)–16(d)). Moreover, at an extreme temperature of −20 °C, the NVMP cathode still exhibited excellent cycling performance (94.2% capacity retention after 1000 cycles at 5 C) and rate capability. Non-*in situ* XRD analysis revealed a solid-solution-type sodium-ion storage mechanism in the voltage range of 2.5–3.8 V and a biphasic mechanism in the range of 3.8–4.4 V. Similarly, Gu *et al*. [260] synthesized a high-entropy Na3V1.9(Ca, Mg, Al, Cr, Mn)0.1(PO4)2F3 (HE-NVPF, Fig. 16(e)) cathode material via a sol-gel method. The results demonstrate that HE-NVPF exhibits superior high-entropy effects, which significantly enhance the average voltage platform (increasing from 3.67 to 3.81 V), substantially improve the Na storage capacity and energy density (calculated based on the mass of the electrode material, increasing from 395.78 to 445.42 Wh·kg−1), and exhibit excellent cycling stability (Figs. 16(f) and 16(g)). Furthermore, the high-entropy effect can suppress undesirable phase transition behaviors during the charge and discharge processes. DFT calculations indicate that the introduced high-entropy doping can alter the electronic states near the Fermi level, enhancing the conductivity of HE-NVPF and accelerating the migration rate of Na+ due to the decreased energy barriers (Figs. 17(h) and 17(i)). In addition, in their subsequent work, they modeled Na3.5Fe0.5V0.5Cr0.5Ti0.5(PO4)3 and simulated it via DFT to demonstrate that multiple redox pairs can efficiently overcome slow reactivity by modulating reaction pathways [261]. In summary, the high-entropy doping strategy fills the gap in the voltage tunability of active TM centers in a fixed crystal lattice, thereby enhancing the competitiveness of NASICON or phosphate-type SIB cathodes in commercial applications.

The manganese-based hexacyanoferrate (Mn-HCF) SIB cathode exhibits poor reversibility and capacity decay during cycling due to unfavorable phase transitions and structural degradation. To address this issue, Ma et al*. [262] prepared a high-entropy Na*x*Mn0.4Fe0.15Ni0.15Cu0.15Co0.15[Fe(CN)6] (HEM-HCF, 40% Mn, Fig. 17(a)) material by incorporating four metal ions (Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu). The research results indicate that introducing high configurational entropy (composition disorder) in Mn-HCFs can significantly enhance the reversible sodium storage performance. At 0.01 A·g−1, the first discharge-specific capacity is approximately 120 mAh·g−1, with a high Coulombic efficiency of 94.9% and a capacity retention rate of 90% after 200 cycles (Fig. 17(b)). The changes in these configurations are crucial for inhibiting the Na+ migration barrier (Figs. 17(e) and 17(f)). This work provides guidance for the subsequent rational design of HEMs (especially low-nickel or low-cobalt materials) for various applications beyond rechargeable battery systems.

### 6.4 Mechanisms of high-entropy battery cathode materials

Table 4 summarizes and analyzes the battery cathode materials mentioned above and reveals the strengthening mechanism of HEMs by comparing the performances of traditional materials and HEMs [234−270]. Table 4 shows that the alkali metal sites of the cathode materials for high-entropy batteries are usually Li ions and Na ions, and the TM sites are usually composed of at least five or more metal elements according to a certain molar ratio. Considering the performance of the cathode materials (Table 4) and the effects of the doping elements (Table 3), we can infer that Ni2+, Cu2+, Fe3+, and Co3+ are electrochemically active and provide capacity for the material. Zn2+, Zr4+, Mn4+, Mg2+, and Ti4+ stabilize the structure and inhibit the phase transition. Cu2+, Fe3+, Sn4+, and Sb5+ help to increase the average voltage. Li+, Mg2+, and Zn2+ can trigger the redox of anions, leading to an increase in the voltage range and capacity of the cathode material. These TM elements synergistically improve the overall electrochemical performance of battery cathode materials. The primary benefit of high-entropy battery cathode materials over conventional materials is their improved long-term performance. This is due to the high-entropy core effects [271−273] as follows: (1) High-entropy effect: According to the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation, Δ*G*mix = Δ*H*mix − *T*Δ*S*mix, where Δ*G*mix, Δ*H*mix, and Δ*S*mix represent the free energy, enthalpy, and entropy, respectively, and *T* is the reaction temperature. The high-entropy strategy can increase the configurational entropy in the system, thereby increasing the value of Δ*S*mix and promoting the formation of single-phase solid solutions. This provides the possibility for battery materials to form a single phase at higher temperatures, overcoming the energy barriers of enthalpy. (2) Sluggish diffusion effect: Compared to traditional materials, HEMs exhibit slow diffusion of multiple ions and consequently inhibit related phase transitions. The differences in the arrangement of adjacent atoms between crystal sites may increase the migration barriers for diffusion between these sites, thereby reducing unnecessary migration. This can result in the formation of a more stable crystal structure and the suppression of intergranular cracking. (3) Lattice distortion effect: The asymmetry in the spatial arrangement of elements with different ion sizes and radii may lead to lattice distortion, which affects the behavior of charged ions (such as Li+ and Na+) in battery materials. This, in turn, influences the properties of high-entropy battery cathode materials, including electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, and mechanical strength. Additionally, highly distorted structures can generate defects in the lattice, promoting the migration of electrons, Li+, and Na+. (4) Cocktail effect: In HEBMs, the electrochemical performance depends on the average characteristics of the individual doping elements and the interactions between the elements. Therefore, adjusting the types and contents of different components can influence the overall performance of high-entropy battery cathode materials. This will reduce the cost of battery materials, increase the phase and structural stability, inhibit unnecessary migration of ions, and adjust the electrical, thermal, and mechanical properties, resulting in high-performance cathode materials.

### 6.5 Synthesis process of high-entropy cathode materials

The laboratory button cell creation process involves several steps, including synthesizing the electrode material, mixing, coating, tailoring the battery pole pieces, and assembling the cell (Fig. 18). The electrode material plays a crucial role in determining the upper limit of battery performance. In contrast, the synthesis process determines the lower limit of battery performance. Therefore, refining the synthesis process to approach the upper limit of performance is essential.

The traditional synthesis methods of cathode materials for batteries include high-temperature solid-phase reaction, coprecipitation, hydrothermal/solvothermal, sol−gel, and spray pyrolysis methods. Similarly, HEBMs also use the above preparation methods, in which a high-temperature solid-phase reaction is often used to prepare HEO bulk materials [274,275]. This method involves mixing metal oxides (hydroxides or carbonates) with Li/Na hydroxides (carbonates or nitrates) in a certain stoichiometric ratio, uniformly blending them, and then calcining them at high temperatures to form solid solutions or new compounds through ion diffusion. The speed and uniformity of ion diffusion in this method significantly impact the quality of the resulting product. Therefore, improving the performance of material ion diffusion through suitable methods can facilitate the processing of multicomponent powder systems into phases. For instance, reducing the powder particle size, improving the powder uniformity, and appropriately increasing the calcination temperature can enhance the product purity and shorten the reaction time. The synthesis conditions of the solid-state method, including precursor selection, calcining procedures, atmosphere, and synthesis temperature, can influence the final product, with synthesis temperature having the most significant impact. As the temperature increases, the diffusion coefficient of the materials also increases. This promotes processes such as ion and vacancy diffusion, as well as particle rearrangement. However, excessively high temperatures can lead to the formation of oxygen-deficient compounds, secondary recrystallization, and an increase in grain size, reducing the surface area, which is unfavorable for the extraction and insertion of lithium/sodium ions in the material. In summary, the solid-state reaction method has advantages such as simplicity, ease of control, short processing time, and ease of industrial production, making it convenient for researchers to screen and develop high-performance electrode materials quickly. However, samples obtained through this method may not achieve complete atomic-level uniformity, have larger and irregularly shaped particles, and have certain limitations.

Coprecipitation [251,276] is a method that involves the presence of two or more cations in a solution in a homogeneous state. Various uniformly composed precipitates can be obtained by adding a precipitating agent and undergoing a precipitation reaction. This is an important method for preparing high-entropy nanopowder materials. The coprecipitation method can generally be divided into two methods. The first is a one-step coprecipitation method, which typically involves adding an appropriate coprecipitator to the raw material solution so that the ions mixed in the solution are coprecipitated according to the stoichiometric ratio, followed by pumping and filtration drying. The second is the combination of coprecipitation and a high-temperature solid phase method to obtain the target product, where the precursor is obtained by precipitation. Then, the final product is obtained by high-temperature calcination decomposition and crystallization. Coprecipitation reactions result from the simultaneous effects of crystal nucleus formation (nucleation) and crystal growth. The reaction commences with nucleation, which is accompanied by growth. Therefore, process parameters such as concentration, synthesis temperature, reaction time, and pH significantly impact precursor particle size and distribution, morphology, crystallinity, specific surface area, and tap density. In summary, the coprecipitation method allows for controlled particle size and morphology of the precursor, ensuring uniformity in the target product’s particles. The effective components in the product can achieve atomic or molecular-level mixing, overcoming the drawbacks of long reaction cycles and high energy consumption associated with solid-state methods.

The hydrothermal/solvothermal method is a wet chemical synthesis technique in which reactants are sealed in a reaction vessel [277]. Under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions, the target product is synthesized through chemical reactions in solution. The synthesis temperature typically ranges from 100 to 350 °C, and the pressure is between 0.3 and 4 MPa. This method is suitable for synthesizing composite oxides, substances that are difficult to dissolve, and unstable phases at high temperatures. Due to its uniform particle size distribution, the hydrothermal/solvothermal method is particularly effective in preventing impurities and defects introduced by ball milling and high-temperature sintering. Moreover, the hydrothermal/solvothermal method is an effective and straightforward technique for growing single crystals. This method is particularly useful for synthesizing nanocrystals with unique morphologies and exceptional performance, making it an effective approach for the experimental synthesis of high-entropy single-crystal battery materials.

The sol−gel method prepares metal alkoxide or inorganic salt solutions into sol or gel, followed by low-temperature heat treatment to convert the gel into oxides [278−280]. The preparation process includes the preparation of sol, sol−gel transformation, and gel drying, with gel preparation and drying being crucial steps. The sol−gel method can mix components at the colloidal level to obtain highly homogeneous and high-surface-area gels, resulting in high-purity, extremely high-entropy nanomaterials. However, the method has a relatively low yield, and the related raw materials are generally expensive.

Spray drying, also known as spray drying granulation, is a physical method that involves dispersing a solution, sol, or suspension with flowability into droplets under high pressure [281,282]. This increases the surface area of the material to accelerate the evaporation of moisture. These droplets are sprayed into a drying chamber with flowing hot air. Through extensive contact and heat exchange with the hot air, the droplets can rapidly lose moisture, resulting in dried powder materials. The typical form of the product is small particle aggregates that form spherical secondary particles. By adjusting the experimental conditions, electrode materials with nanostructures can be obtained, combining the advantages of short ion diffusion paths in nanosized particles and high bulk density in micrometer-sized particles. Spray drying is a simple, cost-effective method suitable for large-scale production.

Table 5 summarizes the advantages and disadvantages of the above synthesis methods (✓ and × represent clear strengths and weaknesses, respectively). In general, there is no essential difference between the preparation methods of high-entropy cathode materials and those of traditional battery materials. However, due to the complexity of the composition of HEMs, it is necessary to consider the balance of their crystal structure, electronegativity, and coordination number in the design. When the same crystallographic site contains multiple elements, they must have a similar ionic radius and maintain electrical neutrality. In addition, charge transfer between coexisting elements should be avoided, such as the formation of reduced Mn2+ and oxidized V5+ between Mn3+ and V3+ at the same crystallographic site, so redox incompatible cation pairs should be excluded during the selection process. In addition, when considering the stoichiometry of components, there is a trade-off between high entropy and high capacity. Adding additional cationic components may dilute the concentration of the redox active ingredient, resulting in a reduction in capacity.

## 7 Summary and outlook

As an energy storage device, the ultimate function of a battery is to provide electrical energy to relevant devices. Therefore, cost, energy density, and safety are the three most important factors. Prior to 2014, LCO dominated the cathode materials industry for LIBs. With the growth of the new energy vehicle industry, LFP was first applied to electric vehicles due to its technological maturity and lower cost. Subsequently, driven by the demand for long-range electric vehicles, ternary materials have taken the lead. LFP and ternary materials are now the main cathode materials used in energy batteries. Ternary batteries have a higher specific capacity than LFPs, but their safety and stability are not as good. Cobalt materials account for a significant proportion of the total cost of ternary materials.

Cathode materials are evolving toward high-nickel, low-cobalt, or cobalt-free directions to reduce battery costs. In terms of nickel content, the most common ternary cathode materials in the industry are classified as low nickel (mainly NCM333, etc., from the three series), medium nickel (mainly NCM523, etc., from the five series), medium-high nickel (mainly NCM613, NCM622, etc., from the six series), and high nickel (mainly NCM811, etc., from the eight series). The energy density increases with increasing nickel content but also introduces several problems, such as cation mixing, structural degradation, and surface-side reactions.

Due to the increasing prices of the core raw material lithium carbonate in LIBs, its high cost limits its continued development. Compared with its widespread availability and low cost, sodium exhibits excellent performance in SIBs, showing strong potential in the transportation and large-scale energy storage sectors. With technological advancements, SIBs have gradually become high-quality alternatives and potential competitors to LIBs. Research on SIB cathode materials includes the use of TM oxides, polyanionic-type compounds, and PBAs. Layered TM oxides are mainstream materials with high theoretical energy density and voltage platforms but poor cycling performance and stability. Polyanionic-type compounds have good thermal stability and a high-voltage platform but low theoretical energy density and poor conductivity. PBAs have lower costs and good material stability, but controlling the coordination of water during the preparation process is difficult, resulting in poor cycling performance and stability.

To address the challenges of accessing cathode materials for power batteries, optimization of synthesis processes, element doping, surface coatings, and structural architecture strategies are commonly employed in commercial progress to enhance the overall electrochemical performance.

Recent development trends indicate that the main theme of cathode material development research is still focused on the theoretical study, design, and synthesis of cathode materials with high energy density and good safety performance. HEBMs have promising applications in terms of cost, safety, and cycle life, but they are currently in the early stages. In addition, the introduction of multiple TMs increases the complexity of the materials, and there is currently no systematic theory to explain the storage mechanism of HEBMs. Moreover, the electrochemical performance of many HEMs still has significant room for improvement at the current stage. Therefore, battery cathode materials designed based on a high-entropy strategy present both opportunities and challenges (Fig. 19).

The future development of HEBMs can be considered from the following aspects:

(1) Computational guide design. Entropy is a measure of the degree of disorder in a system. The higher the entropy is, the greater the degree of disorder in the system. In other words, the chemical disorder of atoms or topological disorder in HEMs is more complex, which can lead to uncertain and diverse underlying mechanisms. For example, when studying the effect of defect behavior on the properties of materials, the disorder of the atomic arrangement in the lattice will pose a great challenge to constructing its lattice structure, while building multiple structures for simulation simultaneously will increase the computational difficulty and cost. Furthermore, the four high-entropy core effects exhibit inconsistency, and an increase in entropy does not necessarily lead to optimal material performance. Therefore, when designing materials, it is necessary to comprehensively consider the integrated influence of each high-entropy core on the material performance. Design guidelines for HEMs can be developed by combining methods such as DFT, ab initio* molecular dynamics, and phase diagram calculations.

(2) Specific new synthesis methods. The future development of power batteries is trending toward high energy density, high cycling performance, fast charging, long life, stability, environmental friendliness, and sustainable development. Therefore, finding a balance point in the material design and synthesis process is necessary. A higher energy density and better cycle performance are expected to be achieved by designing multiple materials with specific elements and synthesizing new nanostructures. It is essential to break through the conventional trial-and-error method, develop morphology modulation techniques suitable for high-entropy components, and establish a mapping relationship between the material influencing factors (e.g., composition and process) and the target parameters (e.g., performance, phase composition, morphology, and structure) to realize the quick discovery and rapid screening of new materials and reduce the development cycle time and cost.

(3) High-entropy electrochemistry. HEMs have significant potential in the field of electrochemical energy conversion technology. However, as the number of elements increases, fundamental scientific problems such as structure evolution, charge compensation, and transport in complex systems at multiple scales become more complicated. Currently, reported HEMs lack advanced characterization tools for analyzing the intrinsic mechanisms of high-entropy cathode materials. Therefore, it is necessary to combine existing *in situ* characterization techniques, such as *in situ* microscopy, *in situ* X-ray techniques, and *in situ* neutron techniques, to gain a deeper understanding of the evolution of the morphology, structure, composition, and chemical state of batteries during charging and discharging, both at the atomic and macroscopic scales. This will help to reveal the mechanism of the electrochemical reaction under real conditions and elucidate the structural composition of electrode materials, the microstructure of the electrolyte, and the interfacial parts, as well as their dynamic evolution laws.

(4) High-throughput databases. High-entropy components are complex, and data are essential for modeling and simulating high-entropy mechanisms, from understanding the microstructure to optimizing the preparation process. Therefore, it is highly important to establish an experimental database of the components, synthesis processes, and properties of HEBMs and to minimize repetitive work by using advanced means, such as machine learning, to accelerate the design and development of new materials for high-entropy batteries.
