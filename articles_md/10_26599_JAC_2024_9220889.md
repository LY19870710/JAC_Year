## 1 Introduction

In recent years, high-entropy carbide ceramics, which are also known as multi-component carbide ceramics, have received extensive attention and have become another research focus in the field of high-entropy materials [1,2]. Multi-component carbide ceramics not only inherit the special properties of high-entropy alloys resulting from complex compositions [3−6] but also retain the advantages of transition metal carbide ceramics as a kind of ultra-high temperature ceramics (UHTCs), such as high melting points, high-temperature stability, high Young’s moduli, and high hardnesses [7,8]. Hence, multi-component carbide ceramics have potential applications in the aerospace industry, nuclear industry, machinery, metallurgy field, etc. According to the research results on UHTCs, multi-phase ceramics usually possess better mechanical properties than single-phase ceramics because of their high relative densities, fine grain sizes, and the occurrence of the second phase. After the first report of multi-component (ZrHfNbTa)C carbide ceramics by Castle *et* *al*. [9], research on multi-component carbide ceramics has made great progress in recent years. Nevertheless, most previous studies of multi-component carbide ceramics have focused on equimolar single-phase ceramics.

Compared with those of single-component carbide ceramics, the comprehensive properties of single-phase multi-component ceramics have been improved according to the experimental results in Refs. [1,10−13], including mechanical properties, oxidation resistance in air or water vapor, and creep resistance, which can be attributed to their complex composition, electronic structure, and lattice distortion. A number of multi-component carbide ceramics have been studied, including quaternary, quinary, senary, and even nonary system carbides [14−16]. Thus, whether multi-component ceramics can improve comprehensive properties has become a critical topic in the field of multi-component ceramics.

For a variety of multi-component carbide ceramics with nearly infinite combinations of metal elements, many systems can hardly form single-phase multi-component carbide ceramics. In fact, the single-phase formation ability of multi-component carbide ceramics has been predicted via first-principle density functional theory (DFT) and machine learning [17,18]. Meng *et* *al*. [19] selected 8 parameters with the best prediction of single-phase formation ability out of 22 parameters of formation ability and successfully predicted the single-phase formation ability of equimolar multi-component ceramics through machine learning and genetic algorithms. The results above reveal that the addition or removal of a certain metal may affect single-phase formation and cause a change from a single phase to multiple phases or vice versa. Thus, the phase of multi-component carbide ceramics transforms in response to the continuous variation in certain metal elements, which can significantly affect the mechanical properties. Currently, a few studies on the variation of metal elements have been carried out in single-phase multi-component carbide ceramics, but the transition between single-phase and multi-phase ceramics has not been studied [20−22]. In a study on the phase decomposition of (Ti,Zr)C carbides, Li *et* *al*. [23,24] reported the significant influence of the molar ratio of Ti and Zr on whether phase decomposition occurred and the annealing temperature of the phase decomposition. This indicates that the variation in the number of metal atoms can indeed affect the phase composition of carbides. The formation of lamellar nanostructures derived from phase decomposition can effectively improve the hardness and strength [25].

Based on the above results, to achieve a phase transition from a single phase to multiple phases and improve comprehensive mechanical properties, a series of (TiZrV*x*Nb)C0.8 ceramics with different V contents were fabricated by spark plasma sintering (SPS) at 2200 °C for 10 min. The phase transition, microstructure evolution, and mechanical properties derived from the variation in V content were investigated in detail.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

Commercially available TiC (particle size of 1.5–2 μm), ZrC (particle size of 1.0–1.5 μm), VC (particle size of 1.0–1.5 μm), and NbC (particle size of 1.0–1.5 μm) with purities above 98.0% were obtained from Zhuzhou Haokun Hard Materials Co., Ltd., China. These materials were used as the starting raw materials, along with TiH2 and ZrH2 (particle size of 1.0–1.5 μm, purity ≥ 99.8%, Jinzhou Haixin Metal Materials Co., Ltd., China). Therefore, titanium hydride and zirconium hydride were introduced to adjust the carbon content. To avoid the influence of carbon content on (TiZrV*x*Nb)C0.8, the carbon content was kept constant at 0.8. The codes and compositions of different metal atoms for different samples are listed in Table 1. The contents of V are 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 mol%, namely, V0, V5, V10, V15, V20, and V25, respectively. Therein, molimolar titanium hydride and zirconium hydride were introduced to adjust the carbon content. In addition, the molar ratio of hydride and carbide raw powders was set at 0.25 to maintain the carbon content at a constant value of 0.8. The carbon content significantly affects the sintering activity, mechanical properties, and thermal properties of high-entropy carbide ceramics [26,27]. Hence, the influence of the carbon content is considered in this work to determine the effect of the V content on the (TiZrV*x*Nb)C0.8 carbides. The carbon and oxygen contents of all the samples are listed in Table 2. The experimental carbon contents of the samples are controlled in the range of 72.28–75.92 at% with nearly no free carbon.

After weighing the corresponding qualities of the carbide and hydride powders, the powder mixtures were ball milled homogeneously by a high-energy planetary mill (Model P4, Fritsch, Germany) in a cemented carbide jar at 600 r/min for 20 h with cemented carbide balls as the medium. The ball-to-powder ratio was selected to be 20 : 1 in weight. After milling, the powder mixtures were passed through an 80-mesh sieve in an argon atmosphere. Then, the monolithic samples were fabricated by spark plasma sintering (model HPD25 SI, FCT, Germany) in graphite dies lined with graphite foil (~0.4 mm in thickness). All samples were heated to 2200 °C at a speed of 50 °C/min for a holding time of 10 min under argon atmosphere protection. Afterward, the compacts were cooled to room temperature at 100 °C/min. In addition, heat treatment was conducted at 2100 °C for 10 h at a heating rate of 10 °C/min in a pressureless sintering furnace (WS0404, Ningxia Sincere Co., Ltd., China).

### 2.2 Characterizations

The phase composition of the different samples was analyzed by X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Panaytical Corp., Empyrean, the Netherlands). The diffraction data were collected using Cu Kα radiation at 40 kV and 100 mA with a 2*θ* range of 20°–90° for phase composition analysis and Le-Bail refinement. The FullProf suite software package was used to calculate the lattice parameters of all the solid solution phases. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; SU5000, Hitachi, Japan) with an X-ray spectrometer (AZTEC, Oxford Instrument, UK) was used to observe the microstructure and measure the elemental content of the polished surface and fracture surface of the samples. Wavelength dispersive spectroscopy (WDS) was performed by a microanalyzer (JXA-8230, JEOL, Japan) to test the element distribution utilizing electron probe microanalysis (EPMA). The grain orientation, kernel average misorientation (KAM) map, grain misorientation angle distribution, and grain size were measured via electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD), and the EBSD data were analyzed via AZtecCrystal software (AZTEC, Oxford Instrument, UK). A transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200X G2, FEI, USA) equipped with a Super-X energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) detector was also used for high-resolution TEM (HRTEM), selected area electron diffraction (SAED), and other analyses. The lattice strain was analyzed by fast Fourier transformation (FFT) and inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT). In addition, the relative densities of the samples were measured by image analysis software according to the area fraction of the pores on the polished surface by counting several positions. Carbon/sulfur analyzer (CS744, LECO, USA) and oxygen/nitrogen analyzer (ON736, LECO, USA) were used to determine the carbon and oxygen contents of the ceramics, respectively. The specific mass of the sample was weighed and then dissolved in hydrofluoric acid and nitric acid using a water bath heated at 90 °C for 30 min. The dissolved sample was filtered through filter paper and then washed with distilled water to neutralize it. After burning the filter paper and filter residue at 450 °C for 30 min, the free carbon content could be determined according to the mass of the ash and the mass of the filter paper and filter residue.

The hardness was measured by a Vickers hardness tester (HVS-30, SCTMC, China) under a load of 9.8 N with a dwell time of 15 s. The bars with the sizes of 3 mm × 4 mm × 20 mm were used to perform three-point flexural strength (*σ*) tests on a mechanical testing system (Instron-5569, Instron, USA) with a 16 mm span and a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min. According to Niihara’s equation [28], the indentation fracture toughness, *K*IC, was calculated by the crack lengths of the indents. The elastic modulus was estimated by the impulse excitation method using a Young’s modulus and damping analyzer (RFDA-HTVP1750 C, IMCE, Belgium) with a sample size of 3 mm × 4 mm × 20 mm.

### 2.3 Computational methods

The Vienna *ab* *initio* simulation package (VASP) [29,30] was used to carry out the first-principles DFT calculations. Structural optimizations were performed by the projector-augmented wave (PAW) [31,32] coupled with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional [33]. Since transition metal carbides possess a typical rock-salt structure, the metal atoms and carbon atoms occupy one face-centered cubic (fcc) sublattice. The metal atoms were assumed to be randomly arranged in the cation sublattice, and the carbon atoms and carbon vacancies were assumed to be arranged in the anion sublattice. Here, the “MCSQS” code of the alloy theoretic automated toolkit (ATAT) [34] was adopted to generate special quasirandom structures (SQSs) [35] with 40 atoms. To calculate the lattice constants and total energies of the SQS supercells, a *Γ*-centered 6 × 6 × 6 *k*-point grid and a kinetic energy cutoff of 500 eV were selected. In addition, to ensure full relaxation for all atoms, the geometric optimization process was terminated when the force components were smaller than 0.02 eV/Å.

*Ω*A–B can be described as a function of the A–A, B–B, and A–B interaction energies in terms of nearest neighbor interactions [36]:

(1) ΩA−B=zL[uA−B−12(uA−A+uB−B)]

where *z* is the coordination number, *L* is Avogadro’s number, and *u*A–A, *u*B–B, and *u*A–B are the energies of the A–A, B–B, and A–B interactions, respectively. Afterward, the mixed enthalpy for multi-component carbide solution with n elements can be calculated by the value of the interaction coefficient *Ω*A–B:

(2) ΔHmixΩA−B=∑i=1n∑j=i+1nΩi−jxixj

where *Ω**i*–*j* is the interaction coefficient between elements *i* and *j*; and *x**i* and *x**j* are the atomic percentages of components *i* and *j*, respectively.

### 4.1 Effect of V addition on phase composition

With increasing V content, the diffraction intensities of the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase increase, corresponding to the increase in the phase volume fraction (Fig. 1). Moreover, the phase volume fraction of the homogenetic phase decreases with increasing V content, and the diffraction intensity of the homogenetic phase even disappears in the V25 sample. This phenomenon indicates that the addition of V promotes the phase transition from a single phase to multiple phases.

To reveal the reason for the phase transition from single phase to multiple phases, the phase diagram was calculated by FactSage 7.1 software (Fig. 7), and the settings of each element’s content are consistent with the initial component design, including the carbon content. With increasing V content, the transition behavior can be clearly observed at 2200 °C in this diagram, and the critical content is 0.17. Notably, the microstructure evolution of phase decomposition is first observed in the V15 sample with a V content of 0.15, which is in agreement with the calculated phase diagram. In addition, the lattice distortion (*δ*) and mixed enthalpy (* ΔHmixΩA—B) are widely recognized as important parameters for the criterion of single-phase formation for high-entropy alloys and ceramics [18,37,38]. The variation tendencies of the lattice distortion and mixed enthalpy with increasing V content are calculated and depicted in Fig. 8. The lattice distortion and mixed enthalpy of the whole system increase with increasing V content, illustrating that increasing the V content has a negative effect on the formation of a single-phase solid solution for the (TiZrVx*Nb)C system. These results can also provide an explanation for the phase transition in addition to the phase diagram.

Furthermore, the lattice parameter of the homogenetic phase decreases with increasing V content according to the Le Bail refinement. In general, the variation in the lattice parameters for the same crystal structure can be attributed to the difference in the element content, especially the metal content. Since the radius of the V atom is the smallest among these metal elements, the increase in V leads to shrinkage of the crystal lattice, corresponding to a decrease in the lattice parameter. Similar experimental results have also been found for (TiZrHfVNbTa)C ceramics [22]. Moreover, the variation in the lattice parameters for the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase can also be attributed to the change in metal content, as shown in Table 3. The large radius of Zr atoms (1.597 Å) and the small radius of V atoms (1.338 Å) [39] result in an increase in the lattice parameter for the Zr-rich phase and a decrease in the Zr-poor phase, corresponding to the shift of diffraction peaks to low- and high-angle ranges (Fig. 1). However, the V content in the Zr-rich phase almost never changes for the V15, V20, and V25 samples as the V content increases throughout the whole system. However, the Zr content in the Zr-rich phase increases at the same time, and the addition of V enriches the Zr-poor phase, which indicates that Zr and V tend to separate in different carbide phases. Moreover, the V content remains relatively high in the Zr-poor phase of the V25 sample as the V content increases instead of diffusing into the Zr-rich phase. This result provides effective evidence indicating that the formation of two carbide phases is led by the incompatibility between Zr and V.

The interaction coefficients among all the metal elements in the transition metal carbides were calculated via DFT calculations to reveal the reason for the distribution of the metal elements. The values of the interaction coefficients *Ω*Ti–Zr, *Ω*Ti–V, *Ω*Ti–Nb, *Ω*Zr–V, *Ω*Zr–Nb, and *Ω*V–Nb are 55.178, 1.566, −9.708, 124.301, 3.927, and 38.134 kJ/mol, respectively. This value represents the formation ability of the solid solution. As a consequence, it is difficult for Zr to form a solid solution with Ti and V because of its high interaction coefficient, which leads to the formation of a Zr-rich phase and a Zr-poor phase. Moreover, the high value of *Ω*Ti–Zr and the low value of *Ω*Ti–V make Ti rich in the V-rich phase. For the distribution of Nb, Nb is slightly enriched in the Zr-rich phase, even though the value of *Ω*Zr–Nb is not the lowest among those of Ti, Zr, and Nb. However, the value of *Ω*V–Nb is relatively high, leading to Nb enrichment in the Zr-rich phase. Nevertheless, Nb is more uniform than other metal elements due to the low formation energy of Nb vacancies, as reported in previous studies [40,41]. It is worth noting that the distribution of carbon atoms is also different in the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase. The content of carbon in the Zr-poor phase is slightly greater than that in the Zr-rich phase.

### 4.2 Effect of V addition on microstructural evolution and mechanical properties

The nanometer lamellar structures in the V15 sample show the same orientation (Fig. 4). Considering the complete grain boundaries of lamellar structures, special structures that originate from phase decomposition and inherit the grain orientation of the parent phase are formed. The same grain orientations of the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase are consequently obtained. To confirm the phase decomposition behavior of the V15 sample, the V15 sample was heat-treated at 2100 °C for 10 h, as shown in Fig. 9. According to the comparison of the SEM micrographs, apparent phase decomposition occurs in the V15 sample after heat treatment, which effectively supports the occurrence of phase decomposition. The strain in this phase decomposition area is much greater than that in other single-phase areas, according to the KAM map, as shown in Fig. 4(a). With increasing V content, this system tends to form two carbide phases. There are some lamellar structures with the same orientation in the V20 sample, as marked in Fig. 4(q). However, when the V content increases to 25 mol%, the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase have nearly no orientation relationship and form two phases completely. Moreover, the strain in the lattice also disappears.

The strain of the phase decomposition area can be attributed to edge dislocations at the semi-coherent interface, as shown in Figs. 5(e) and 5(f), which can adjust the lattice mismatch between the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase to maintain the semi-coherent interface. The strain concentration caused by those edge dislocations at the interface is in the form of a discrete dark contrast. In addition to the interface of the two phases, dislocations can be observed in the interior of the grains (Fig. 5(a)). After annealing, lamellar structures with a width of several hundred nanometers and a coherent or semi-coherent orientation relationship between two phases are similar to those of phase decomposition in (Ti,Zr)C ceramics [24,25]. These lamellar structures can also be observed in the phase decomposition structure of high-entropy alloys [42,43]. Li et* *al*. [23] reported that a strain field with a periodic contrast pattern could be found at the interface of the phase decomposition in (Ti, Zr)C, which could be attributed to the lattice mismatch between TiC-rich and ZrC-rich phases. This discovery can explain the strain in the area of the phase decomposition structure in the V15 and V20 samples (Figs. 4(o) and 4(s)). Coherent or semi-coherent interfaces are recognized as effective evidence of spinodal decomposition in the micromorphology of alloys [44]. As a consequence, the phase decomposition in the V15 sample is similar to the spinodal decomposition in micromorphology, which can be considered spinodal-like decomposition. The phase decomposition may originate from the local composition fluctuation and then bloom into lamellar structures. The formation of alternate lamellar structures can be attributed to the effect of coherency stresses. As reported for the decomposition of (Ti,Zr)C [25], the lamellar structure of phase decomposition can effectively reduce the effect of coherency stresses.

Since the relative densities of all the samples are above 99% and the carbon content is approximately equal, the variation in the mechanical properties mainly depends on the microstructure and element composition. For the V0, V5, and V10 samples, the increase in hardness can be attributed to lattice distortion and solid solution hardening. However, the increase in V content enhances the effect of sluggish diffusion on inhibiting grain growth, which improves the mechanical properties [22]. For the V15 sample, in addition to solid solution and grain refinement hardening, the nanoscale lamellar structures resulting from phase decomposition have a positive effect on the increase in hardness, especially due to the high dislocation density and high strain concentration at the intragranular region and at the interface. A high dislocation density and strain concentration can restrain the growth and sliding of dislocations and improve the hardness effectively. These hardening mechanisms also apply to the V20 sample with fine grain sizes and the existence of semi-coherent interfaces. Since partial grains with semi-coherent interfaces are identified as the same grain by EBSD, the average grain size of the V25 sample is smaller than that of the V20 sample. In fact, the average grain size of the V25 sample is slightly high. With increasing the V content to 25 mol%, the hardness decreases due to the disappearance of the semi-coherent interface, but the hardness is still higher than that of other samples due to grain refinement. For multiple phases, the existence of two phases will restrain the grain growth of each phase according to coupled grain growth behavior [45].

For flexural strength, the flexural strength of the single-phase samples reached 386±11 MPa in the V10 sample due to the strengthening of the solid solution and grain refinement. However, for the V20 sample, the strength is still relatively high, together with a partial loss of the advantages of solid solution strengthening. This can be attributed to grain refinement and the presence of semi-coherent interfaces. The semi-coherent interfaces with high dislocation density and stress concentration can prevent the sliding of dislocations as a barrier to dislocation migration [46,47]. The dislocation pileup can be clearly observed at the interface of the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase, as shown in Fig. 5(a), which verifies the hardening and strengthening effect of the semi-coherent interface with a high dislocation density and strain concentration. Above, the optimal mechanical properties of hardness (26.3 GPa), strength (369 MPa), and indentation fracture toughness (3.1 MPa·m1/2) are achieved in the V20 sample. The enhancement of the mechanical properties could be attributed to grain refinement and the presence of semi-coherent interfaces with high dislocation density and strain concentration.

## 5 Conclusions

In this work, a series of (TiZrV*x*Nb)C0.8 ceramics with different V contents were fabricated by SPS at 2200 °C. The phase composition, microstructure evolution, and mechanical properties caused by the variation in the V content were investigated in detail. The following conclusions can be drawn:

1) The addition of V enlarges the lattice distortion and mixed enthalpy in this system, which is consistent with the calculated phase diagram, resulting in the transition from a single phase to multiple phases.

2) Phase decomposition was discovered in the V15 sample, which formed nanometer lamellar structures composed of a Zr-rich phase and a Zr-poor phase with semi-coherent interfaces.

3) Grain refinement, the formation of multiple phases, and the presence of semi-coherent interfaces with high dislocation density and strain concentration positively influence the mechanical properties. The optimal comprehensive mechanical properties of Vickers hardness (26.3 GPa), flexural strength (369 MPa), and fracture toughness (3.1 MPa·m1/2) were obtained for the V20 sample.

This work offers a potential approach for adjusting the microstructure from a single phase to multiple phases in multi-component carbide ceramics to achieve novel mechanical properties.
