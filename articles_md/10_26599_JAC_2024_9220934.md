## 1 Introduction

Gas turbine engines will remain irreplaceable as energy sources for aircraft in the foreseeable future. The thermodynamic evidence supports the view that increasing the engine operating temperature can enhance the thrust-to-weight ratio and efficiency of the engine [1,2]. Thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) are high-temperature protective coatings applied on the hot section components of gas turbine engines and have become a key technology for advanced gas turbines [3]. However, increasing the gas temperature causes the ingested environmental particles (such as sand, dust, and ash) to melt and adhere to the TBCs, which are highly corrosive [4]. The deposits are primarily composed of CaO, MgO, Al2O3, and SiO2 and are collectively abbreviated as CMAS [5]. The most commonly used TBC material is yttria partially stabilized zirconia (YSZ), but it is susceptible to CMAS attack [6]. CMAS corrosion occurs through a coupled thermochemical and thermomechanical effect, leading to premature failure of the YSZ TBC [7,8]. Molten CMAS destroys the microstructure and chemical composition of the coatings and fills the pores in the coating, thereby reducing the strain tolerance and thermal insulation, as well as decreasing the service life [9].

To improve the CMAS resistance of TBCs, several methods have been proposed by scholars. Currently, there are many reports about applying a protective layer on YSZ TBC surfaces. Bakkar *et* *al*. [10] used a silicon nitride layer to seal the surface of a YSZ coating, and CMAS tests showed that silicon nitride could significantly prevent CMAS infiltration. Guo *et* *al*. [6] designed new Ti2AlC/YSZ TBCs that exhibit high microstructural stability when exposed to CMAS and demonstrate more efficient resistance against CMAS. The function of the protective layer is to prevent CMAS from infiltrating the pores of the TBC. Structural modification of the YSZ coating surface can also achieve this purpose. Laser modification can tailor the surface microstructure of the coating, creating a laser-glazed layer, which has high microstructural stability in molten CMAS and can inhibit CMAS penetration [11,12]. These CMAS protection methods primarily focused on preventing CMAS infiltration after CMAS has adhered to TBC surfaces. However, they did not consider the approach to mitigate CMAS corrosion from the source, which is mainly related to the wetting behavior of molten CMAS when in contact with the coating surface.

To alleviate or even prevent CMAS corrosion at the source, it is necessary to develop CMAS-phobic TBCs that prevent CMAS from adhering to the TBC surface [13,14]. Some researchers have conducted investigations on this topic. Guo *et* *al*. [13] fabricated TBCs with a lotus-leaf-like dual-scale microstructure using plasma spray-physical vapor deposition (PS-PVD), imparting coatings with large silicate phobicity. Furthermore, they used ultrafast laser direct writing technology to reconstruct this coating and obtain a triple-scale micro/nanostructure, which possesses long-term high-temperature CAMS-phobicity [15]. Wang *et* *al*. [16] prepared TBCs with a micro/nano double-scale structure using solution precursor plasma spray (SPPS), effectively mitigating the wettability and adhesion of molten CMAS. These methods for reducing CMAS wettability are based on TBC surface design and modification.

The characteristics of the TBC material also affect the CMAS wettability and adhesion on the TBC surface. Hence, selecting a more promising CMAS-phobic TBC material may be more efficient and economical. Currently, many novel candidates have been proposed for applications in TBCs. Gd2Zr2O7 has attracted much attention due to its low thermal conductivity, high thermal expansion coefficient, and good CMAS resistance [14,17]. Moreover, the doping of Sc or Yb into Gd2Zr2O7 further enhances its toughness and CMAS resistance [15,18]. GdPO4 has been reported to have low thermal conductivity, a high thermal expansion coefficient, and exceptional high-temperature phase stability [19]. Guo *et* *al*. [20,21] developed a GdPO4/YSZ double ceramic-layer coating with an extended thermal cycling life and excellent resistance to CMAS. Both Gd2Zr2O7 and GdPO4 show promise as next-generation TBCs, and they can be utilized together with YSZ to prepare double ceramic-layer TBCs, which not only shield YSZ from CMAS corrosion but also enhance its thermal insulation performance. However, the wettability of GdPO4 and Gd2Zr2O7 has been inadequately investigated until recently.

First-principles calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) have been widely employed to investigate various interfacial interactions, such as Al/VN [22] and Fe/TiC [23]. This approach is an important complementary method for studying the wettability of solids. Passerone *et* *al*. [24] studied the wetting and spreading behavior of the (Cu,Ag,Au)/ZrB2 system and demonstrated that adhesion work can be used to interpret the wetting behavior of transition metal boride-molten metal systems. Zhang *et* *al*. [25] reported that incorporating reactive elements (Ti, Zr, and Hf) on Ag/ZrB2 interfaces can greatly increase their adhesion, which improves the wettability between Ag and ZrB2. Liu and Ning [26] used first-principles calculations to show that surface reconstruction can improve the adhesion of Al/α-Al2O3 (0001) interfacial systems, providing a theoretical foundation for improving the wettability of Al/α-Al2O3 systems with surface reconstruction. In related reports on CMAS wetting, Li *et* *al*. [27] calculated the surface energy of different lattice planes in YSZ and demonstrated that a higher surface energy means easier wetting by CMAS. Zhou *et* *al*. [28] investigated the wetting mechanism of CMAS on YSZ columnar grains by calculating the surface energy and adhesion work using first-principles calculations. Therefore, combining experimental and first-principles calculations is promising for studying the wetting mechanism of molten CMAS on TBC surfaces.

In this study, we discovered that GdPO4 exhibits CMAS-phobic and infiltration-inhibiting properties and is, therefore, emerging as a promising protective layer material for TBCs against CMAS attack. The evolution of the wetting behavior and contact angle of molten CMAS on GdPO4 pellets was investigated. The surface properties of the GdPO4 pellet and its interface reaction layer with CMAS are investigated through first-principles calculations. Considering the microstructure of the interface reaction layer and the surface properties of GdPO4 and the reaction layer, the mechanisms for the CMAS-phobicity of GdPO4 are clarified. The wetting behavior of CMAS on Gd2Zr2O7 and YSZ, as well as relevant theoretical calculations, was also investigated for comparison.

### 2.1 Experimental procedures

GdPO4, Gd2Zr2O7, and YSZ powders were synthesized using a chemical coprecipitation method. A Gd(NO3)3 solution was prepared by dissolving pure Gd2O3 (99.9%, Jiangtian Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) in nitric acid (65%, Jiangtian Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China). (NH4)2HPO4 (99.9%, Jiangtian Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) was dissolved in deionized water and then added dropwise to the Gd(NO3)3 solution to obtain a white precipitate. The precipitate was washed several times with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol until a pH of 7 was reached, and then it was subsequently dried to obtain the GdPO4 precursor. ZrOCl2·8H2O (99.9%, Jiangtian Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) was dissolved in deionized water and mixed with Gd(NO3)3 solution. The resulting mixture was dripped into excess ammonia water to obtain a white precipitate. The precipitate was thoroughly washed and dried, and the Gd2Zr2O7 precursor was obtained. Similarly, pure Y2O3 (99.9%, Jiangtian Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) was dissolved in nitric acid and mixed with a ZrOCl2 solution. The mixture was then added dropwise to ammonia water to form a precipitate, which was subsequently washed and dried, and the YSZ precursor was obtained. The obtained precursors were calcined at 900 °C for 4 h to obtain the desired powders. These powders were then ground and sieved through a 200-mesh sieve to obtain fine particles. Then, they were cold-pressed at 300 MPa and sintered in a muffle furnace (SK-G08163, Tianjin Zhong-Huan Experimental Electric Furnace Co., Ltd., China). GdPO4 and Gd2Zr2O7 were sintered at 1650 °C for 10 h, while YSZ was sintered at 1400 °C for 2 h to achieve densification.

CMAS, with a composition of 33CaO–9MgO–13AlO1.5–45SiO2 was selected for this study [29]. High-purity CaO, MgO, Al2O3, and SiO2 powders (99.9%, Jiangtian Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) were mixed at the desired molar ratios, and then an appropriate quantity of deionized water was added. The mixture was subjected to ball milling using a planetary ball mill (QM-3SP4, Nanjing Nan-Da Instrument Co., Ltd., China) at 400 r/min for 6 h. The resultant suspension was dried at 120 °C for 8 h to obtain dry powders. Subsequently, powders were heat-treated in a muffle furnace (SK-G08163, Tianjin Zhong-Huan Experimental Electric Furnace Co., Ltd., China) at 1550 °C for 1 h to ensure complete melting of the homogeneously mixed powders. Finally, the melt was poured into deionized water, resulting in the formation of CMAS glass. The glass was subsequently ground into powders and sieved through a 200-mesh sieve for further experimentation.

In the experimental investigation of CMAS wetting behavior, the surfaces of the GdPO4, Gd2Zr2O7, and YSZ pellets were evenly coated with CMAS powders at a concentration of 10 mg/cm2. To ensure that the powders on different pellets were in the same initial state, a 200-mesh sieve was used to evenly sieve the powders into the pellets, and the powders were weighed multiple times to guarantee a consistent concentration. Then, the samples were subjected to heat treatment in a muffle furnace (SX-G05165, Tianjin Zhong-Huan Experimental Electric Furnace Co., Ltd., China) at 1250 °C for 2, 5, 10, 20, 30, or 60 min. The samples were retrieved from the furnace at the corresponding moment to observe the morphology and then returned to the furnace for further heating. The contact angles of CMAS melts on different pellets were measured using the sessile drop method for 5, 30, and 60 min. A total of 50 mg of CMAS powders was compacted into a small cylindrical pellet and placed at the center of the three different pellets. Subsequently, the sample was maintained at 1250 °C for the specified duration in a muffle furnace (SX-G05165, Tianjin Zhong-Huan Experimental Electric Furnace Co., Ltd., China), followed by prompt removal. To minimize the influence of surface roughness on the experimental results, all the pellets in this study were manually sanded with 400#, 800#, and 1200# silicon carbide sandpaper.

A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Nanosem 430, FEI, USA) equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS; IE 350, Penta FET X-3, UK) was used to analyze the microstructure and composition. Back-scattered (BSE) mode was used to observe the cross-sectional microstructures. An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Bruker D8 Advanced, Germany) using Cu Kα radiation was used to characterize the phase structure of the synthesized powders and pellets. The scanning range was from 20° to 80° with a rate of 0.2 (°)/s, and the current and voltage were 40 mA and 40 kV, respectively. The surface roughness of the pellets was measured using a laser confocal microscope (LSM; LEXT OLS5100, Japan).

### 2.2 Theoretical calculation methods

In this study, first-principles calculations were used to study the surface energy * (Esurf) of the three materials and their reaction products with CMAS. Additionally, the adhesion work  (Wad) between CMAS and its reaction products was also calculated. DFT was utilized for all calculations, which were performed using the Vienna ab* *initio* simulation package (VASP 5.4.4) software [30]. The projector augmented wave (PAW) method was used to express the electron–ion interactions, and the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was used to express the electron exchange correlation potentials [31,32].

Models of GdPO4, Gd2Zr2O7, YSZ, and their corrosion products were established based on the experimental results and previous research. To reduce computational complexity, the CMAS model was simplified to Ca3MgAl2Si6O19, which closely matches the atomic ratios of the CMAS composition used in the experiments. The plane-wave cutoff energy for the calculations was set to 500 eV in all calculations. The convergence criterion of structural optimization and the self-consistent field was set at 1×10−6, and the stress of each atom was less than 0.01 eV/atom. The Gamma *k*-points were set to 5 × 5 × 5 for the bulk models and 5 × 5 × 1 for the slab models and interface models. The thickness of the vacuum layer was set to 15 Å in all the slab and interface models to minimize the influence of the periodic structure. The equations for computing the surface energy (* Esurf) and adhesion work ( Wad) are as Eqs. (1) and (2), respectively [25]:

(1) Esurf=Eslab−NEbulk2S

(2) Wad=EA+EB−EA/BtotS

where S* refers to the cross-sectional area of the slab models and interface models, *E*slab is the total energy of the relaxed slab models, and *E*bulk is the total energy of the bulk unit cell after complete relaxation, *N* is the number of bulk units in the corresponding slab, and 2 in the denominator means that there are two surfaces in the slab unit cell. *E*A and *E*B refer to the energies of the A and B surface models after complete relaxation, respectively, and * EA/Btot refers to the energy of the A/B interface model after complete relaxation.

The wetting process of CMAS on the GdPO4 surface was simulated using a large-scale atomic/molecular massively parallel simulator (LAMMPS) [33]. The CMAS droplet was obtained by expanding the Ca3MgAl2Si6O19 model to 12,400 atoms and fully melting it after relaxation at 4000 K for 1000 ps. The conjugate gradient algorithm was used for the energy minimization of the initial model, and the particle‒particle particle‒mesh (PPPM) algorithm was adopted to calculate the Coulomb interaction with a cutoff radius of 10 Å [34]. The simulation process was performed in the canonical ensemble (NVT), and the Nosè–Hoover thermostat was used to control the temperature balance of the system [35], with a temperature of 1573 K and a temperature damping constant of 0.1. A trajectory file was output every 0.1 ps, and the total simulation time lasted for 2000 ps. In this study, the Buckingham potential is the main potential function, which is expressed as

As shown in Fig. 3(a), CMAS powders melt at approximately 2 min and form tiny droplets on the pellet surface. The molten CMAS flows across the surface and tends to gather in several regions with time. The convergence phenomenon is obvious at 10 min, and it becomes more pronounced with further increases in the exposure time. After 60 min, the molten CMAS is gathered only in certain regions, and most regions have almost no melt covered. By comparison, Gd2Zr2O7 and YSZ do not exhibit molten CMAS-phobicity. At 2 min, the CMAS powders also turn into tiny droplets on the Gd2Zr2O7 pellet surface. However, these droplets tend to uniformly flow across the surface with time (Fig. 3(b)). After 60 min of exposure, followed by cooling, a thin glassy layer is clearly observed on the Gd2Zr2O7 pellet surface. In Fig. 3(c), tiny droplets are also observed on the YSZ pellet surface at 2 min, and they uniformly flow across the surface within 10 min. However, with further increases in the exposure time, the amount of molten CMAS on the surface decreases as a result of melt penetration. After 60 min, no CMAS is observed on the YSZ pellet surface, as shown in Fig. 3(c). This is consistent with the results reported by other researchers, who revealed that the YSZ is vulnerable to molten CMAS infiltration [8].

The wetting behavior of a liquid on a solid surface is primarily influenced by two factors: surface chemistry and surface microstructure [21]. The CMAS wetting process is characterized by reactive wetting behavior that occurs at high temperatures. According to existing research, both GdPO4 and Gd2Zr2O7 can react with molten CMAS quickly, forming continuous crystalline reaction layers [38,39]. Therefore, the wetting behavior of CMAS on GdPO4 and Gd2Zr2O7 is related to these interfacial reaction layers, including their material characteristics and microstructure. Section 3.2 mainly focuses on analyzing the interface reaction layer.

#### 3.2.1 Microstructure of CMAS/GdPO4 interfacial reaction layer

Figure 4(a) shows the macroscopic appearance of the GdPO4 pellet after 60 min of exposure to CMAS at 1250 °C, and cross-sectional images of regions 2 and 1 are shown in Figs. 4(b) and 4(c), respectively. A continuous interface reaction layer is found between the CMAS and the pellet, beneath which no CMAS trace can be found. This suggested that the GdPO4 pellet is highly resistant to CMAS penetration. Figures 4(d)–4(f) show magnified images of regions 3–5 in Figs. 4(b) and 4(c), respectively. The microstructures of the interface reaction layers in the three regions are different. In Fig. 4(d), there is a dark gray contrasted interface reaction layer with a thickness of ~3.2 μm. In Fig. 4(e), the interface reaction layer reveals a double-layer microstructure. The upper sublayer is composed of many acicular crystals with a white contrast, while the lower sublayer is composed of compactly dark gray contrasted crystals. The thicknesses of the above sublayers are ~10.5 and ~3.2 μm, respectively. In Fig. 4(f), the interface reaction layer also reveals a double-layer microstructure. With the substitution of Ca2+ for Gd3+ and SiO44− for PO43−, the formation enthalpy decreases [41]. Therefore, the formation enthalpy of dark gray contrasted Gd-lean apatite is lower than that of white contrasted acicular Gd-rich apatite, indicating that Gd-lean apatite more easily forms via thermodynamics. Additionally, the influence of the residual amount of CMAS on the reaction layer can be observed in Fig. 4. The compact lower sublayer consisting of Gd-lean apatite is formed by diffusion-recrystallization theory at the interface between GdPO4 and CMAS [42]. Since Gd3+ has a high solubility in molten CMAS [5], abundant Gd3+ migrates into the melt, leading to many vacancies in the GdPO4 lattice. At the same time, a large amount of Ca2+ is transported from CMAS to occupy these vacancies and reconstruct the lattice structure of monazite to apatite. Moreover, some  PO43− is substituted by  SiO44− due to their similar tetrahedral structures. As a result, the thermodynamically stable Gd-lean apatite lower sublayer is formed. In Fig. 4(d), there is a substantial quantity of residual CMAS above the reaction layer. The presence of sufficient Ca2+ promotes the formation of a compact Gd-lean apatite layer, thereby inhibiting the formation of an acicular upper sublayer.

The formation of the upper sublayer consisting of Gd-rich apatite can be understood based on a dissolution-reprecipitation mechanism [40]. During the formation process of the compact Gd-lean apatite layer, if the amount of molten CMAS is insufficient, its formation is suppressed, while the formation of acicular Gd-rich apatite is favorable. As shown in Fig. 4(e), the residual CMAS melt on the compact sublayer is not sufficient, where Ca2+ is continuously consumed, resulting in the relative enrichment of Gd3+ and  SiO44−, leading to a gradual increase in the tendency for reprecipitation of acicular Gd-rich apatite by a spiral growth mechanism [43]. If the amount of molten CMAS on the compact Gd-lean apatite layer is rather limited, as shown in Fig. 4(f), the elements required for forming the acicular Gd-rich apatite may be lacking, resulting in the upper sublayer not being as crowded as that in Fig. 4(e).

#### 3.3.1 Surface energy of GdPO4, Gd2Zr2O7, and YSZ and their reaction products with CMAS

Molten CMAS initially comes into contact with the pellet and remains for a certain period, during which the surface energy of the pellet plays a crucial role in CMAS wetting. Surface energy can reflect the chemical activity of a surface and is a crucial factor influencing solid wetting properties. However, it is challenging to measure the surface energy experimentally [45]. In DFT calculations, the surface energy can be determined by computing the energy difference between the slab model and the equivalent bulk model [25].

The surface energies of multiple lattice planes in the GdPO4 and Gd2Zr2O7 lattices were calculated. The selected lattice planes correspond to the strong diffraction peaks observed in the XRD pattern [18,20]. GdPO4 presents a typical *P*21/*n* space group, and our calculated lattice constant is 6.717 Å, which is close to the experimental value of 6.651 Å [40]. Gd2Zr2O7 is a typical *Fd** 3¯m* space group, and the calculated lattice constant is 10.601 Å, which is close to the reported value (10.682 Å) [18]. After performing convergence tests, it was determined that the optimal slab model for GdPO4 consists of 6 layers, while for Gd2Zr2O7, 4 layers are sufficient. During the calculation, the atoms in the lower half of the model were kept fixed to represent the interior atoms that are far from the surface. The representative slab models are shown in Fig. 8.

#### 3.4.1 Influence of interface reaction layer microstructure on CMAS wetting process

The formation of the acicular upper sublayer is one of the primary factors affecting the wetting process. It has been reported that a surface microstructure with dual-scale or triple-scale characteristics greatly contributes to CMAS-phobicity [13,15]. These multiscale microstructures construct a complex three-dimensional structure that can trap air and reduce the contact between CMAS and the coatings, resulting in a CMAS-phobic attribute [16]. The acicular Gd-rich apatite exhibits conical micropillars, which is consistent with the above characteristics. Additionally, the acicular upper sublayer on the CMAS-attacked GdPO4 surface lacks continuity and appears as several “steps” protruding from the lower sublayer, which hinders the spreading of molten CMAS by pinning the three-phase contact line [48,49]. It is difficult for the molten CMAS on the lower sublayer to cross the steps. A microstructure that could prevent the spreading of CMAS was not observed in the interface reaction layer of Gd2Zr2O7 and YSZ.

The CMAS wetting behavior on the interface reaction layer of CMAS-attacked Gd2Zr2O7 is completely different. This reaction layer can obviously be considered a rough surface without multiscale microstructures. A rough surface can enhance the effective contact area between the melt and the solid, leading to good wettability [21,50]. As a result, the contact area of the CMAS-attacked Gd2Zr2O7 reaction layer cannot be reduced by trapping air, which does not improve the CMAS-phobicity. Improving the ability of YSZ to prevent CMAS infiltration is more urgent. The reaction layer formed by its reaction with CMAS is porous and unable to prevent the infiltration of CMAS into the interior. Therefore, the infiltration behavior of molten CMAS on the YSZ surface is the primary mechanism, while wetting plays a secondary role. In conclusion, considering the structure of the reaction layer, GdPO4 exhibits better CMAS-phobicity.

#### 3.4.2 Influence of material characteristics on CMAS wetting process

Wenzel improved Young’s equation by introducing the roughness factor, denoted as “*r*”, and proposed a relationship between the apparent contact angle and intrinsic contact angle [51]. The driving force (*f*d) of the three-phase contact line is given as Eqs. (4) and (5), respectively [52]:

(4) cosθW=rcosθY

(5) fd=σlv(cosθY−cosθW)

where *σ*lv represents the surface tension of the CMAS melt; *r* represents the ratio between the actual contact area of the solid‒liquid interaction and the macroscopic apparent contact area on a rough surface, which is always greater than 1; *θ*Y represents the equilibrium contact angle, which is considered the intrinsic contact angle obtained under ideal conditions; *θ*W refers to the Wenzel contact angle, which represents the apparent contact angle formed on a rough surface. According to Eqs. (4) and (5), *f*d can be written as Eq. (6):

(6) fd=σlvcosθY(1−r)

The surface energy exhibits a negative correlation with the intrinsic contact angle [53,54]. In other words, as the surface energy increases, *θ**Y* decreases. On surfaces with high surface energy, *θ*Y decreases while cos*θ*Y increases. A higher surface energy corresponds to a greater driving force. Note that the spreading velocity of the three-phase contact line is directly proportional to *f*d. Thus, the pellet with the highest surface energy has the smallest equilibrium contact angle and the fastest spreading velocity of the triple line in theory. Therefore, prior to the formation of the reaction layer, the molten CMAS exhibits the smallest equilibrium contact angle and the fastest spreading rate of the contact line on the Gd2Zr2O7 pellet. GdPO4 exhibits completely opposite characteristics, which favor CMAS-phobic behavior. After the reaction layer is formed, the reaction layer of GdPO4 exhibits a lower surface energy, thereby enhancing its CMAS-phobic properties.

The relationship between the adhesion work and the contact angle can be described by the Young–Dupre equation (Eq. (7)) [55]:

(7) Wad=σlv(1+cosθY)

A larger *W*ad is inversely correlated with the contact angle, which means a greater driving force and faster-spreading velocity of the contact line. The adhesion between the Gd2Zr2O7 interfacial reaction layer and CMAS is significantly high. Disregarding the influence of the surface microstructure, a higher adhesion work indicates superior wettability. As a result, CMAS faces reduced resistance on the reaction layer of Gd2Zr2O7, facilitating its spreading behavior. Additionally, a higher adhesion means stronger adsorption between the molten CMAS and pellet surfaces [28]. The adhesion of CMAS to the interfacial reaction layer of Gd2Zr2O7 is significantly greater than that of the CMAS/Gd-lean lower sublayer and CMAS/Gd-rich upper sublayer, which indicates that it is difficult to separate the molten material from the Gd2Zr2O7 pellet.

A material must be resistant to CMAS infiltration, and its wettability must be accurately evaluated. Taking GdPO4 and YSZ as examples, the surface energy of YSZ is only slightly greater than that of GdPO4. The surface energy of m-ZrO2 is also close to that of the GdPO4 products. Although YSZ theoretically has similar wettability to GdPO4, it is unable to form an effective interface reactive layer to prevent CMAS penetration, rendering its efforts futile. Hence, based on the surface energy and adhesion work, GdPO4 and its double-layer microstructure reaction layer exhibit superior CMAS-phobicity.

#### 3.4.3 Influence of interface reaction-induced viscosity change on CMAS wetting process

Existing research suggests that energy dissipation resulting from viscosity serves as a primary factor influencing the spreading dynamics of molten CMAS [5]. The spreading ability of the melt decreases as the viscosity increases. It has been reported that a large Si : Ca ratio in CMAS leads to a higher viscosity of the melt [40]. The formation of apatite significantly affects the SiO2/CaO ratio in CMAS during the interfacial reaction process.

The Si : Ca ratios are 0.14 and 1.67 in Gd-lean apatite and Gd-rich apatite, respectively. In contrast, this ratio in Ca2Gd8(SiO4)6O2 is 3 for Gd-lean apatite and Gd-rich apatite. This results in the enrichment of SiO2 in CMAS after the GdPO4/CMAS interface reaction, which increases the viscosity of the residual CMAS. As a result, the CMAS-phobicity of GdPO4 is enhanced. However, for the Gd2Zr2O7/CMAS system, CaO is enriched in the residual CMAS, which reduces the melt viscosity and increases the spreading ability of the melt. The chemical compositions of the residual CMAS on GdPO4 and Gd2Zr2O7 are presented in Table 5. The Si : Ca ratio of residual CMAS on GdPO4 is ~1.8, which is higher than that on Gd2Zr2O7 (~1.3). These results are consistent with the above analysis.

### 3.5 Mechanisms for CMAS-phobicity of GdPO4

Schematic diagrams depicting the CMAS wetting evolution on GdPO4 and Gd2Zr2O7 pellets are shown in Fig. 12 and are obtained based on the above experimental observations and theoretical calculation results. For the CMAS wetting experiments on the GdPO4 pellet, in the initial stage, CMAS powders melt and shrink into droplets with high viscosity, as shown in Figs. 12(a) and 12(b). Because both GdPO4 and the initially formed Gd-lean lower sublayer have low surface energies, the spreading velocity of the three-phase contact line is slow, resulting in a long time for the dispersed CMAS droplets to contact each other. This means that the gaps between these small CMAS droplets can remain for a long period. As a result, acicular apatite products could form in these gaps in addition to the compact lower sublayer due to the absence of sufficient CMAS. Hence, a discontinuous upper sublayer is created at the interface, which acts as a “step” to pin the molten material when the three-phase contact line encounters these protruding acicular apatite products.

The complex three-dimensional structure of the acicular upper sublayer and its low adhesion to CMAS means that molten CMAS has less resistance to flow on its surface. Furthermore, the interfacial reaction between GdPO4 and CMAS enhances the viscosity of the residual CMAS, consequently increasing the surface tension of the melt. As a result, CMAS on the acicular apatite layer flows toward the compact layer due to the surface tension, as shown in Fig. 12(c). The agglomeration of molten CMAS in the compact apatite layer further inhibits the formation of the acicular upper Gd-rich apatite sublayer, resulting in the continuous gathering of the molten CMAS to the area where only the compact apatite layer is present, as shown in Fig. 12(d). Therefore, the unique structure of the interface reaction layer on the GdPO4 pellet and its special material characteristics contribute to the distinctive wetting behavior of CMAS on the GdPO4 pellet, minimizing the effective area of attack by CMAS and imparting CMAS-phobicity.

By comparing the wetting behavior of molten CMAS on the Gd2Zr2O7 pellet with that on the GdPO4 pellet, their initial behavior is similar, as shown in Figs. 12(e) and 12(f). However, as wetting progresses, distinct differences emerge because molten CMAS wetting Gd2Zr2O7 faces more challenges and obstacles. First, the surface energy of Gd2Zr2O7 is significantly greater than that of GdPO4. The CMAS droplets on the Gd2Zr2O7 surface rapidly contact each other. The subsequent formation of a reaction layer does not hinder this process, as the interface reaction layer of Gd2Zr2O7 is composed primarily of Ca2Gd8(SiO4)6O2 and c-ZrO2, which exhibits a higher surface energy and adhesion work with CMAS. Furthermore, the alternating distribution of reaction products results in an increased surface roughness of the reaction layer, which also enhances CMAS wetting. Finally, with the formation of the reaction layer, the viscosity of the residual CMAS melt is lower than that of the residual CMAS of GdPO4. The combination of these factors ultimately results in the molten CMAS wetting Gd2Zr2O7 more easily, which causes rapid connection of the molten CMAS droplets and covering the entire surface of Gd2Zr2O7, as shown in Figs. 12(f) and 12(g). Figure 3(b) shows that the entire process takes no longer than 10 min. Therefore, Gd2Zr2O7 does not exhibit CMAS-phobic properties.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, the wetting behavior of molten CMAS on GdPO4, Gd2Zr2O7, and YSZ pellets was investigated. GdPO4 exhibits CMAS-phobic and infiltration-inhibiting properties, making it difficult for CMAS to wet and penetrate it, thereby significantly mitigating the harm caused by CMAS attack. The related mechanisms are clarified, and the conclusions are as follows:

1) The interface reaction layer between GdPO4 and CMAS exhibits a double-layer microstructure composed of an acicular upper sublayer and a compact lower sublayer. The upper layer is composed of Gd-rich apatite, and the lower layer is composed of Gd-lean apatite. The lower sublayer effectively inhibits CMAS infiltration, and the discontinuous acicular upper sublayer acts as a “step” to restrict the spreading of CMAS.

2) First-principles calculations reveal that GdPO4 and its double-layer structured reaction layer have lower surface energies than Gd2Zr2O7 and YSZ and their respective reaction layers. Additionally, the adhesion between CMAS and the double-layer reaction layer of GdPO4 is also lower than that between CMAS and the reaction layer of Gd2Zr2O7. Compared to those of Gd2Zr2O7 and YSZ, the material characteristics of GdPO4 and its reaction layer are favorable for the multiphobic properties of CMAS.

3) As the interfacial reaction proceeds, the formation of Gd-rich and Gd-lean apatites on GdPO4 elevates the Si : Ca ratio of the residual CMAS melt. An increase in the Si : Ca ratio leads to an increase in the viscosity of the residual melt, which subsequently inhibits the wetting and permeation of the melt. However, the viscosity of the residual melt decreases as the interfacial reaction of Gd2Zr2O7 proceeds.

4) The results of molecular dynamics simulations indicate that molten CMAS can still maintain a relatively high contact angle (~41°) on the ( 1¯11) lattice plane of GdPO4, which has the largest surface energy among the calculated lattice planes. Experiments show that the equilibrium contact angle of CMAS on the surface of GdPO4 is approximately three times greater than that on the Gd2Zr2O7 surface. The higher contact angle demonstrates the CMAS-phobicity of GdPO4.

5) GdPO4 exhibits both CMAS-phobicity and infiltration-inhibiting ability, which can minimize damage caused by CMAS. Therefore, GdPO4 is a promising material for protecting TBCs by mitigating CMAS adhesion and attack.
