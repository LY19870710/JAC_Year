## 1 Introduction

Lead-free bismuth sodium titanate-based (Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3, BNT) ceramics with significant strain responses are strong competitors to Pb(Zr1−*x*Ti*x*)O3 (PZT)-based ferroelectrics [1,2]. Unfortunately, the negative strain (*S*neg) and low positive strain in BNT systems are not favorable for the application of precise actuators [3−7]. To solve these problems, many strategies have been proposed to improve strain performance, including the use of core–shell structures, grain size engineering, defect modulation, and texturing techniques [8−19]. For instance, Steiner *et al*. [13] constructed core–shell structures in SrTiO3-modified BNT ceramics and found that oxygen vacancies could enhance the diffusion of Sr2+ ions into the core region and transform the ferroelectric behavior to ergodic relaxor (ER) states, which effectively promoted the strain response of *d*33*. By introducing plate-like NaNbO3 templates into the BNT–ST system, grain orientation growth was successfully induced, relaxor ergodicity was enhanced, and a high strain of ~0.39% was achieved in -oriented ceramics at an electric field of 60 kV/cm [14]. However, the strategies of core–shell structure regulation and texturing techniques are quite inconvenient, making it difficult to easily and accurately modulate its strain properties [20,21]. Therefore, exploring simple and effective methodologies to improve strain performance has become increasingly urgent [22,23].

The BNT system is similar to the PZT piezoelectric system because the electrical and mechanical properties are enhanced near the phase boundary (PB) region [24−26]. With 6 mol% BaTiO3, PB consisting of rhombohedral (R) and tetragonal (T) phases existed in the BNT–0.06BT system [27,28]. Owing to the low Gibbs free energy flattening Landau energy density and making the phase structure and ferroelectric domains susceptible to change by external stresses or electric fields, a sizable strain was achieved in this PB composition [29]. However, the original nonergodic relaxor (NR) state in this PB region is irreversibly shifted to a ferroelectric state under the application of electric fields, which leads to the achieved electrostrain values often accompanied by large negative strains (negative strain greater than 0.2%) [30,31]. Furthermore, by adding orthorhombic K0.5Na0.5NbO3 (KNN) to the BNT–0.06BT system, the canonical NR state was disrupted and changed to an ER state [32]. As a result, a highly replicable strain was attained, which was related to the reversible phase transition from the ER state to the electric field-induced metastable ferroelectric phase [33,34]. The key differences between the NR and ER phases are the states of their ferroelectric domains [35,36]. Decreases in domain size and changes in domain morphology are considered to be the main factors affecting its activity, leading to reversible phase transitions and large nonnegative strains [37,38]. Therefore, combining ferroelectric domains and phase boundaries to engineer electromechanical activity provides a feasible way to improve the strain performance of BNT-based materials.

In this work, (1−*x*)Bi0.50Na0.41K0.09TiO3–*x*NaNbO3 (BNKT–*x*NN) ceramic systems were prepared. The phase boundary and domain structures of BNKT–*x*NN ceramics were systematically investigated with respect to their component and strain behavior, as shown in Fig. 1. By lowering the ferroelectric-relaxor phase transition temperature (*T*F–R), the lamellar domains in the ferroelectric state were transformed to nanodomains in the ER state. Understanding the domain states via piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM) and first-order reversal curves (FORCs), a large strain response (0.41%) was obtained from the reversible inter-transformation of sub-stable macro-domains and polar nano-regions (PNRs) in the phase boundary. This work provides a deep understanding of the source of the large strains in BNT-based ceramics in terms of ferroelectric domains and relaxor phase boundaries and clarifies the relationship between these factors.

## 2 Experimental

(1−*x*)Bi0.50Na0.41K0.09TiO3–*x*NaNbO3 (BNKT–*x*NN, *x =* 0–0.1) ceramics were prepared by a conventional solid phase reaction method. The raw materials were carbonates and oxides of Bi2O3 (99.975%), Na2CO3 (99.5%), K2CO3 (99.5%), Nb2O5 (99.5%), and TiO2 (99.5%) (all of them were procured from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China), and the synthesis process can be found in our previous work [39]. In this work, the sintering conditions were 1145 °C for 2 h in an atmosphere. Prior to the sintering process, degumming was carried out at 500 °C for 30 min to eliminate the effect of PVA. The phase structure of the BNKT–*x*NN ceramics was characterized by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Mini-Flex600, Rigaku, Japan). Densification tests were performed by the Archimedes’ method. The surface microstructure and elemental distributions were examined by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; Regulus 8100, Hitachi, Japan). The grain size distribution was measured and estimated from the corresponding SEM images, and no less than 100 grains were collected. Before electrical performance testing, the samples were polarized under 40 kV/cm in silicone oil for 30 min. The dielectric responses were tested using a precision impedance analyzer (HP4294A, Agilent, USA) over a temperature range of 25–500 °C. The ferroelectric hysteresis (*P–E*) loops and FORCs of the samples were examined by using ferroelectric equipment (TF-2000, Aix ACCT, Germany). Before performing the *P–E* tests, the sintered pellets were polished to a final thickness of 0.2*–*0.8 mm, and silver electrodes were coated on both polished surfaces with a diameter of 8 mm. PFM was performed using an atomic force microscope (MFP-3D, Asylum Research, USA).

### 3.1 Strain responses

Room-temperature *P–E* loops for the BNKT*–x*NN ceramics are displayed in Fig. 2(a). A typical *P–E* loop is observed in the BNKT ceramics (*x =* 0), featuring a normal ferroelectric (FE) behavior (large coercive field, *E*c and high remnant polarization, *P*r). Notably, after modifications through the action of sodium niobate, the *P–E* loops shift from square to pinched and then to slim with dramatically decreased *P*r and *E*c. The *P–E* loops and *S‒E* loops of BNKT*–x*NN under different electric fields are displayed in Figs. S1 and S2 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The maximum polarization (*P*max) and strain values increase significantly when the electric field reaches or exceeds 40 kV/cm for the BNKT*–*0.04NN sample. In addition, the corresponding *S‒E* and *J–E* loops are also significantly influenced in the BNKT–*x*NN ceramics (Fig. 2(b) and Fig. S3 in the ESM). The BNKT sample has a butterfly shaped strain loop and an apparent negative strain value (*S*neg = −0.14%) (Fig. 2(b)). Interestingly, the strain loop is shifted to a sprout shape as *S*neg almost disappears, and the strain increases significantly to 0.41% (*x* = 0.04). It is well known that the negative strain is closely related to the remnant polarization and the switching of ferroelectric domains [40]. To explore the changes in the strain behavior more intuitively, the *S–P*2 curves, *P*r, and *S*max/*E*max values with respect to the NN content are shown in Figs. 2(c) and 2(d). The linearity of the *S–P*2 curves increases with the increase of NN content (Fig. 2(c)), which implies that the ferroelectricity is weakened and the relaxor behavior is enhanced, and the macrodomains are transformed to nanodomains. *P*r decreases consistently with increasing NN content (Fig. 2(d)), and the *S*max/*E*max reaches a maximum value of 742 pm/V (*S*max = 0.41%) for the *x* = 0.04 sample (the unipolar *S‒E* curves of BNKT–*x*NN are shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM). By comparing the maximum strain values of several BNT-based ceramics at various driving electric fields, it can be seen that the present work has good strain performance at relatively low electric fields (Fig. 2(e)) [41−48].

To explore the evolution of strain characteristics, the temperature-dependent *P‒E* loops, *J‒E* loops, *S‒E* loops, thermally stimulated depolarization currents (TSDCs), and *S*max/*E*max values of *x* = 0 and *x* = 0.04 samples were investigated and are displayed in Fig. 3, and Figs. S5 and S6 in the ESM. With increasing temperature, the *P‒E* loops of the BNKT sample exhibit a sharp decrease in *E*c, while *P*max is still maintained (Fig. 3(a)). The corresponding *S‒E* loops also change significantly with increasing temperature. The negative strain disappears, and the strain loops transform from butterfly shaped to sprout-shaped, and accordingly, the strain and *S*max/*E*max increase significantly to 0.44% and 820 pm/V at 120 °C, respectively. Additionally, the TSDC curves are associated with the phase transitions and a sharp anomaly appears at 110 °C for *x* = 0 (Fig. 3(c)), implying that the long-range ferroelectric state induced by the polarized electric field transforms into an ER state upon heating. Therefore, it is concluded that the BNKT is predominantly in an NR state in its original state. These phenomena can also be verified from results reported in Ref. [24] for other BNT-based ceramics. For the *x* = 0.04 sample, the NR and ER states are already present at room temperature, which is confirmed by the pinched *P–E* loops and *S‒E* loops in Figs. 3(d) and 3(e). The TSDC is close to zero and remains constant, and the *S*max/*E*max values gradually decrease with increasing temperature (Fig. 3(f)). It can be concluded that the dynamic domain in this state of BNKT–0.04NN becomes weak, and the lack of phase transition results in a low strain value with increasing temperature.

### 3.2 Crystal structure and phase structure evolution

Generally, the strain response is directly affected by the micromorphology and phase structure of ceramics [49,50]. Figure 4(a) displays the XRD pattern of the BNKT–*x*NN ceramics. Except for the *x* = 0.1 sample, all samples show a single-phase perovskite structure without redundant peaks, indicating that NaNbO3 is firmly dissolved into the crystal structure of BNKT to form a homogeneous solid solution [51]. The appearance of the second phase (Bi1.74Ti2O6.624) in the *x* = 0.1 sample is attributed to the excess Nb breaking through the limits of the perovskite structure. To further understand the evolution of the phase structure of BNKT–*x*NN, Rietveld refinements were performed using the GSAS software [52], and the Rietveld refinement results and conditions are shown in Fig. 4(b), and Fig. S7 and Table S1 in the ESM. The reliability coefficients (*R*wp and *R*p) are less than 10%, which indicates that the refinement results are reliable. With increasing amounts of the dopant NN, the relative content of the rhombohedral phase (*R*3*c*) decreases. In contrast, the content of the tetragonal phase (*P*4*bm*) increases (Fig. 4(c)). The coexistence of strongly and weakly coupled polar regions (*R*3*c* and *P*4*bm*) results in a relatively high *P*max and a low *P*r [53]. As a result, an insignificant decrease in *P*max is observed in Fig. 2(a). Additionally, SEM images and the elemental distribution of the BNKT–*x*NN (*x* = 0 and 0.04) ceramics are shown in Figs. 4(d) and 4(f) (the remaining samples and grain size distributions can be found in Fig. S8 in the ESM). The samples are well sintered and present dense morphologies with relative densities (Fig. 4(e)) in excess of 90%. By using a linear intercept method [54], the average grain size is 0.65 μm for the *x* = 0.04 sample. As the NN doping amount increases, the grain size expands gradually to 0.94 μm at *x* = 0.1. This result indicates that moderate NN doping may provide the driving force for grain growth.

The temperature- and frequency-dependent dielectric responses of the polarized BNKT–*x*NN ceramics are shown in Figs. 5(a)–5(d). Several dielectric anomalies are observed throughout the temperature range of 25–500 °C. *T*m is defined as the dielectric maximum temperature, which varies between 220 and 290 °C. *T*m broadens with increasing the NN content, indicating that NN doping favors the enhancement of the relaxor characteristics of BNKT ceramics. This may be due to the random distribution of Na+ and Nb5+ ions resulting in the occurrence of a local random field that favors the generation of randomly oriented PNRs [55]. Specifically, there is a shoulder-shaped dielectric anomaly below *T*m (i.e., *T*S) and a strong dispersion near *T*S, which originates from the thermal evolution of discrete PNRs. With increasing NN doping, the maximum value of the dielectric spectra becomes wider for *x* ≥ 0.04, leading to the distinction between *T*S and *T*m not being easily defined. This phenomenon is associated with the gradual switching of the relaxor process of the rhombohedral-phase PNRs by the tetragonal-phase PNRs [56,57]. Interestingly, a sharp anomaly in the dielectric spectra at *T*F–R occurs when *x* F–R, the phase structure transforms again to the ER state, which can be confirmed by the variable temperature *P‒E* loops in Fig. 3(a). In addition, the *T*F–R peak disappears at *x* ≥ 0.04, while at the same time, the strain curves (Fig. 2(b)) show the features of the ER state (sprout-shaped *S‒E* loops). It is concluded that the *x* > 0.04 samples are in an ER state at room temperature, and a phase boundary with NR and ER states is achieved in the *x* = 0.04 sample (Fig. 5(f)). Therefore, the reversible phase transition between the ER and metastable FE phases can occur near *T*F–R, which shows the unique property of the largest strain response in the BNKT–0.04NN ceramics (~0.41% at 55 kV/cm).

### 3.3 Domain structure evolution

In addition to the phase structure, the ferroelectric domain is also one of the most important parameters influencing the strain properties [58,59]. To further explore the relationship between the ER and FE states, the domain morphologies were investigated, and the distribution of the dynamic evolution of the domain structure under an applied electric field was elucidated. The amplitude and phase images of the BNKT–*x*NN (*x* = 0, 0.04, and 0.08) ceramics are shown in Figs. 6(b)–6(d). Many large macrodomains are observed in the *x* = 0 sample, which is consistent with its hysteresis loop characteristics (Fig. 2(a)). In contrast, scattered nanodomains and a few macrodomains appear in the *x* = 0.04 sample, demonstrating that the doped NN can break up the macrodomains. With the NN content up to *x* = 0.08, the size of the domains is further reduced to the nanoscale. Additionally, the ferroelectric domains can be switched under an electric field of 30 V, with a clear contrast between the left and right sides. The morphologies of the domains after 10 min of depressurization are shown in Fig. S9 in the ESM. The macrodomains in the *x* = 0 sample do not undergo a switching, suggesting that locally induced irreversible ferroelectric phases occur at the microscopic scale, which is the reason for its large *P*r and *S*neg (0.14% in Fig. 2(b)). In contrast, a large number of nanodomains rapidly switch back at *x* = 0.04 (Fig. S9(b) in the ESM), reflecting that the ER state begins to dominate at room temperature and that excellent strain properties can be obtained.

To further elucidate the domain evolution under applied electric fields, the FORC was investigated [60,61]. The FORC approach is built on the Preisach model, which assumes that a hysteresis loop consists of a series of *P–E* loops characterized by a positively switched electric field (*α*) and a negatively switched electric field (*β*, *β* ≤ *α*) [62]. The coercivity field (*E*c = (*E *− *E*r)/2) and the internal bias field (*E*bias = (*E *+ *E*r)/2) are further defined on this basis. The measured FORC loop data are subjected to a quadratic polynomial combination, which in turn utilizes Eq. (1):

(1) p(αi,βi)=12∂2p(αi,βi)∂αi∂βi

The statistical distribution function of the switch field (*E*) and the slew field (*E*r) of each domain, also known as the FORC density, is displayed in Fig. 6(a) [63]. This figure shows that a region of high-intensity distribution is obtained in the *x* = 0 sample (Fig. 6(e1)), which indicates the shifting of ferroelectric domains under the electric field and is responsible for the sizable maximum polarization. Other areas of *p*(*α*, *β*) intensity are significantly suppressed by domain switching due to saturation. The typical strongly polarized nonlinear ferroelectric properties are reflected in this inhomogeneous distribution [62]. When NN doping reaches *x* = 0.04 (Fig. 6(e2)), the high-intensity region shrinks significantly and splits away from the origin, indicating that *E*c decreases and that nonlinearity decreases. This suggests that the *x* = 0.04 sample transfers to the boundary of the NR and ER states, in which the nanodomains have smaller switching energy barriers and ultralow coercivity. When *x* = 0.08, the FORC density becomes more homogeneous across the electric field range, indicating further weakening of the ferroelectric nonlinearity. The nanodomains and more dispersed polarization contribute to this relaxor state. To shed light on the relationship between strain and the electric domains, the strain behaviors of BNKT–*x*NN ceramics at room temperature under the FORC cyclic electric field are shown in Figs. 7(a)–7(c). The negative strain phenomenon gradually disappears with increasing NN component, which is consistent with the results in Fig. 2(b) and Fig. S2 in the ESM. Owing to the irreversible electric field-induced phase transition and the irreversible non-180° domains in the *x* = 0 sample, there is a significant difference in the strain characteristics (∆*S* = 0.043%) between the positive and negative maximum electric fields (Fig. 7(a)). ∆*S* decreases gradually with increasing NN doping content, indicating an increase in the activity of the domains. The fast response of the domains under the action of an electric field reflects the replacement of ferroelectric macrodomains by PNRs. Therefore, based on the results of the FORC and dielectric spectra (Fig. 5), it can be concluded that the transition from the NR state to the ER state with increasing NN content and the reversible transition between ferroelectric macrodomains and PNRs at the phase boundary (*x* = 0.04) as well as at the applied electric field can induce a great strain response.

*
Fig. 7Variation in strain with electric field for BNKT–x*NN: (a) *x* = 0, (b) *x* = 0.04, and (c) *x* = 0.08. FORC distributions at 80 and 120 °C for (d, e) *x* = 0, (f, g) *x* = 0.04, and (h, i) *x* = 0.08.

The influence of temperature on the *P–E* loops and domain evolution was further investigated, as shown in Figs. 7(d)–7(i). For the *x* = 0 sample, the high-intensity distribution region weakens and splits as the temperature increases, suggesting that the ferroelectric long-range order is gradually replaced by nanodomains, reflecting the transition from the ferroelectric to the relaxor state (Fig. 5(f)). This temperature-induced phase transition of the *x* = 0 sample is consistent with the behavior of the component induced in BNKT–*x*NN (Fig. 6(e)). Because the phase boundary exists in this temperature range (~120 °C), a large strain response (0.44% in Fig. 3(b)) resulted from the reversible transformation between the ferroelectric domains and the PNRs. For the *x* = 0.04 and 0.08 samples (Figs. 7(f)–7(i)), the FORC densities are relatively uniformly distributed over the entire range of the electric field since they are already in the ER state at room temperature. At high temperatures (≥ 80 °C), the domains in this ER state become finer and are unable to induce phase transitions under the corresponding electric field. Therefore, the strain responses are weakened under elevated temperature conditions (Fig. 3(e)).

### 3.4 Mechanism responsible for strain behavior

Based on the above results, the relationship between the reversible phase transition induced by the applied electric fields and temperature as well as the evolution of the domains in this process and the strain responses for BNKT–*x*NN ceramics can be obtained, as shown in Fig. 8. The ceramics with *x* Fig. 2), featuring typical FE behavior, which is corroborated by the irreversible non-180° macrodomains shown in Figs. 6(b) and 6(e1). The ferroelectric phase induced by electric field polarization is stable until *T*F–R and transforms to the ER state upon further heating (Fig. 5(f)). A large strain value (0.44%) is obtained near the temperature-induced ferroelectric to relaxor ferroelectric (FE-RFE) phase boundary (Fig. 8(b)).

For the *x* = 0.04 sample, a relaxor state (NR/ER) that coexists with the rhombohedral phase (*R*3*c*) and tetragonal phase (*P*4*bm*) can be achieved. At this phase boundary, the NR/ER state facilitates the transition to the ferroelectric phase once an electric field is applied (> 40 kV/cm in Fig. S1(c) in the ESM), which corresponds to the sub-stable long-range ordered domains transformed by the PNRs (Fig. 6(c)). However, this sub-stable FE state transitions back to the RFE state with the removal of the electric field and is accompanied by a depolarization process (Fig. S9(b) in the ESM). This electric field-induced phase transition and the corresponding polarization rearrangements result in a significant strain in this composition (0.41% for *x* = 0.04). In addition, the PNRs become more dynamic, and their size and number decreases with increasing temperature. Then, a linear reduction in *S*max/*E*max is achieved (Fig. 3(f)). With further increasing NN, the *R*3*c* phase structure gradually transforms into *P*4*bm* with small and weakly coupled PNRs (Fig. 4(b)). However, the local random field increases as a result of charge and lattice misalignment, which can stabilize the ER state at room temperature for *x* > 0.04 samples. The PNRs in this ER state remain stable under an applied electric field and cannot be transformed into long-range ordered FE states, resulting in a weak strain response (Fig. 8(a)). Therefore, by doping BNKT with an appropriate NN, the FE/NR/ER phase boundaries and domain structures can be effectively engineered to induce reversible phase transitions, resulting in large electric field-induced strains.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, BNKT–*x*NN (*x* = 0–0.1) ceramics were prepared by the conventional solid phase reaction method. The effect of the phase and domain structures of the ceramics on the component- and temperature-dependent strain behavior was systematically investigated. The substitution of NN led to a decrease in the content of the rhombohedral phase and an increase in the content of the tetragonal phase. The temperature-induced phase transition was consistent with the behavior of the component-induced phase transition in the BNKT–*x*NN ceramics. The ferroelectric and relaxor domain structures coexisting in the BNKT–0.04NN ceramics were revealed with the aid of the PFM technique, and the enhancement of the strain response was associated with the reversible electric field-induced phase transition and dynamic domain shifting in the mixed nonergodic and ergodic relaxor states. This work provides a good example of designing high-performance actuators by engineering phase and domain structures in BNT-based relaxor ferroelectrics.
