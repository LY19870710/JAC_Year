## 1 Introduction

The rapid development of aerospace technology has posed challenges for materials used at the leading edges and nose tips of hypersonic vehicles and the thrust components of scramjet engines, which puts forward stringent requirements for the properties of materials [1,2]. Transition metal diborides (TMB2s), as a type of ultrahigh-temperature ceramics (UHTCs), are materials of choice for applications in hypersonic vehicles and scramjet engines because of their unique combination of fascinating properties, such as high melting points, high elastic moduli, high hardness, high thermal conductivity, good abrasion resistance, excellent thermal, and chemical stability, etc. [3−6]. Nevertheless, the intrinsic brittleness, poor thermal shock and oxidation resistance of TMB2s are the main obstacles hindering their practical applications [7]. The macroscopic properties of UHTCs are underpinned by their crystal structure, chemical bonding, and microstructure features. Therefore, understanding microscopic information, such as the electronic structure and chemical bonding of TMB2s, is essential not only for establishing structure‒property relationships but also for addressing the issue of how to tailor their properties from a crystal structure and chemical bonding point of view, which is instructive for promoting their practical application in extreme aerospace environments.

It is generally accepted that TMB2s crystallize in an AlB2-type structure with a space group of *P*6/*mmm* on the basis of X-ray diffraction experiments, i.e., from information in reciprocal space. However, direct observation of the atom arrangement in TMB2 has rarely been conducted because of the low resolution of transmission electron microscopy. Recent advances in transmission electron microscopy have made it possible to directly observe the arrangement of atom columns in TMB2, even for light elements such as B. The confirmation of the crystal structure of TMB2 through direct observation is appealing since it provides direct evidence of atom arrangement in real space. In addition to their crystal structure, the chemical bonding in TMB2 also influences their properties. To establish the structure‒property relationship, a number of theoretical works were conducted via first-principles calculations based on density functional theory (DFT). For example, as early as 1994, Wang *et al*. [8] performed theoretical calculations on the electronic structure and chemical stability of a series of TMB2 via self-consistent linear muffin-tin orbitals (LMTOs) with the atomic sphere approximation (ASA). The results revealed that a pseudogap existed in the density of state (DOS) of TMB2 and that the energy band structure was determined by the *sp*2 hybridized state and the *p**z* state of B, as well as the *s* and *d* states of transition metals (TMs), indicating that the sublattice of B had a planar graphite-like structure. In 2001, Vajeeston *et al*. [9] used self-consistent LMTO to perform theoretical calculations on a series of TMB2. The results revealed that there were mixed bonding properties in TMB2 in the presence of covalent bonds as well as ionic bonds between TM and B. In addition, the covalent nature of the bonding increased with the increasing number of valence electrons per nearby atom. In 2015, Zhou *et al*. [7] systematically investigated the electronic structure and chemical bonding properties of TMB2 through first-principles calculations based on DFT, emphasizing the effects of the valence electron concentration on the chemical bonding and mechanical properties. This work revealed the coexistence of covalent, ionic, and metallic bonds in TMB2, and that as the excess valence electrons occupied the antibonding state, the TM–B interaction was enhanced with increasing valence electron concentration, whereas the TM *dd* interaction was weakened. Although few data on electronic structure and mechanical properties have been reported for TMB2, a comprehensive experimental study on the crystal structure and chemical bonding of TMB2 would be helpful for understanding the structure‒property relationship in detail. Therefore, it is crucial to verify the crystal structure, chemical bonding, and other structural information of TMB2 through direct experiments.

Concurrently, as a special member of TMB2, CrB2 has a unique property of antiferromagnetism [10], which has attracted the attention of material scientists, chemists, and physicists despite its relatively low melting point (approximately 2200 °C) compared with other TMB2. In particular, much attention has been given to high-entropy transition metal diboride ceramic in recent years, in which CrB2 has been frequently utilized as a key component to improve the electromagnetic wave absorption performance of high-entropy TMB2. For example, Zhang *et al*. [11] synthesized several high-entropy TMB2 powder on the basis of compositional design, and the results showed that the incorporation of CrB2 could significantly improve impedance matching and endow high-entropy TMB2 with excellent electromagnetic wave absorption properties. In addition, the incorporation of CrB2 into high-entropy TMB2 has been proven to improve the oxidation resistance of high-entropy TMB2 [12]. However, until recently, theoretical calculations on the antiferromagnetic properties of CrB2 have been scarce, and few experiments have attempted to directly confirm the antiferromagnetic properties of CrB2, which is of paramount importance for understanding its macroscopic properties.

To fill the aforementioned research gap, in this work, a comprehensive study on the crystal structure and chemical bonding properties of CrB2 was conducted via a combination of theoretical calculations and experimental validation. Theoretical calculations were mainly performed via first-principles calculations based on DFT, and the detailed electronic structure and chemical bonding of CrB2 were systematically analyzed, whereas the experiments were mainly performed via a spherical aberration-corrected transmission electron microscopy to observe the structure of CrB2 at the atomic scale via electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS), which provides direct evidence of the crystal structure and chemical bonding information. Finally, the hysteresis loop of CrB2, which has not been reported to the best of the authors’ knowledge, was measured, and its magnetic properties were analyzed in conjunction with theoretical calculations. In summary, the present work provides a detailed analysis and demonstration of the structure and bonding of CrB2 by combining theoretical calculations and experimental validations, which is important for understanding the structure‒property relationships of CrB2 and other TMB2s.

### 2.1 Theoretical methods

First-principles calculations based on DFT were performed on CrB2 via the Cambridge sequential total energy package (CASTEP) [13]. Vanderbilt-type ultrasoft pseudopotentials [14] were employed to describe the ion‒electron interactions (Cr 3*p*63*d*44*s*2 and B 2*s*22*p*1), and the electronic exchange-correlation energy was employed under the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) scheme [15]. The plane-wave basis set cutoff was 600 eV for all calculations. Special *k*-point sampling integration over the Brillouin zone was employed via the Monkhorst–Pack method with a 12 × 12 × 10 special *k*-point mesh [16]. The crystal structure was fully optimized by modifying the lattice parameters to minimize the enthalpy and interatomic forces under the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) minimization scheme [17]. The tolerances for geometry optimization were as follows: differences in total energy within 5×10−6 eV/atom, maximum ionic Hellmann‒Feynman forces within 0.01 eV/Å, maximum stress within 0.02 GPa, and maximum ionic displacements within 5×10−6 Å.

### 2.2 Experimental

The CrB2 powder used in this work was purchased from Qinhuangdao Eno High Tech Material Development Co., Ltd., China (99.5%). The powder was ground and sieved and then hot pressed in a furnace (ZT-50-22Y, Shanghai Chenhua Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China) at 1600 °C under 35 MPa for 60 min to obtain a bulk CrB2 sample. The heating rate was 10 °C/min, and the cooling rate was set at 20 °C/min. After cooling to room temperature, the surface contaminants were removed via a grinding machine.

To obtain the atomic-scale microstructure, bulk CrB2 was cut via the focused ion beam (FIB) technique to obtain a sufficiently thin sample. Atomic-resolution high annular dark field (HADDF), annular bright field (ABF), integrated differential phase contrast (iDPC), and scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) were conducted with an aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope (Spectra Ultra S/TEM, ThermoFisher, USA). In parallel, the electron-loss near-edge structure (ELNES) of CrB2 was acquired via the electron energy loss spectroscopy accessory of STEM to obtain the bonding information of CrB2, and atomic-resolution energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping was performed via the Gatan Digiscan imaging mode, which is better able to characterize light elements such as B. In addition, to verify the spin-polarized electronic structure of CrB2, magnetization measurements of CrB2 powder were carried out via a vibrating sample magnetometer (Lake Shore 8604, VSM, USA).

#### 3.1.1 Equilibrium crystal structure of CrB<sub>2</sub>

CrB2 crystallizes in an AlB2-type structure with a space group of *P*6*/mmm* (No. 191), in which graphitic-like B layers alternatively stack with closely packed Cr layers, as shown in Fig. 1. Cr and B are located at the (0, 0, 0) and (1/3, 2/3, 1/2) Wyckoff positions, respectively. Owing to the presence of Cr atoms, spontaneous magnetization may influence the calculated results. Hence, to explore this effect, the present work was carried out separately for systems with and without spin polarization. The lattice constants after geometry optimization of CrB2 are listed in Table 1, along with the experimental values and the calculated values from previous works [7,9]. Our calculated lattice constants are very close to the experimental data as well as the calculated values from previous works [7,9], which demonstrates the reliability of the present calculations. In addition, a comparison of the effects of spin polarization on the lattice parameters after geometry optimization reveals that the effect is mainly on the value of *c*, whereas the effect on the value of *a* is marginal, in which the lattice parameters considering spin polarization are closer to the experimental values. The reason may be that CrB2 is an antiferromagnetic material, and considering spin polarization would more correctly delineate its electronic distribution as well as its magnetic moments, thus significantly affecting the results of the calculated lattice constants. As shown in Fig. 2, the calculated DOS considering the spin polarization of CrB2 indicates that the majority and minority spin channels lack full symmetry, indicating the presence of magnetism, which may affect the geometry-optimized lattice constants of CrB2. The phenomenon where spin polarization has a greater effect on the value of *c* also needs to be considered in the characterization of magnetic properties. The structure of magnetic ordering in CrB2 was studied by Kaya *et al*. [18], who confirmed that the Cr moment forms a simple helical structure that rotates in the plane formed by the *c* axis and [110] (propagation direction). This may account for the greater effect of spin polarization on the value of *c* in the calculations. More investigations on the magnetic properties of CrB2 will be described in Section 3.3.

#### 3.1.2 Electronic structure and chemical bonding

To minutely understand the bonding properties of CrB2, the energy band structure and DOS of CrB2 were calculated, as shown in Fig. 3. A closer scrutiny of the electronic structure reveals that the Fermi level crosses the energy band structure and that the DOS diagram has nonvanishing values at the Fermi energy levels, indicating that CrB2 exhibits metallic conductivity, which is mainly contributed by the 3*d* electrons of Cr atoms. In addition, a clear pseudopotential appears near the Fermi energy level in the DOS. According to the literature, the presence of this pseudopotential can categorize the DOS into bonding and antibonding states, located below and above the pseudopotential, respectively [8]. Compared with those of TiB2 and VB2, which have lower valence electron concentrations than CrB2 does, the Fermi energy level of CrB2 is located above the antibonding state, implying that the bonding state is filled and that the antibonding state is partially filled, which leads to relatively lower chemical stability [8,9]. Chemical stability is strongly correlated with the melting point; as such, the lower chemical stability results in a relatively lower melting point of CrB2 among transition metal diborides (approximately 2200 °C).

To investigate the chemical bonding nature of CrB2 more visually and in detail, the total density of states (TDOSs) and partial density of states (PDOSs) as well as the electron density difference maps of B and Cr were calculated and investigated in conjunction with each other, as displayed in Fig. 4. As shown in Fig. 4(a), the finite states at the Fermi energy level are mainly contributed by the Cr 3d* state, with some contribution from the B 2*p* state. A phenomenon where the states of B 2*s* orbitals are found to be higher than those of B 2*p* in the range of −15 to −10 eV in the PDOS of B implies that the *s* and *p* orbitals are hybridized. Figure 4(a) shows that the PDOS of B is higher than that of Cr in this energy range, suggesting that the states of B dominate in TDOS. Figures 4(b)–4(d) show the electron density difference maps in the range of −14.7 to −9.2 eV (green dashed region in Fig. 4(a)). The electron density difference map is a visualization of the changes in the electron distribution due to the formation of chemical bonds in the system. As illustrated in Fig. 4(c), the electron density difference map on the (002) plane shows strong localization between neighboring B atoms, with six B atoms forming a graphite-like six-membered ring, which is a sign of overlapping hybridized *sp*2–*sp*2 orbitals. The mechanism of *sp*2 hybridization of B was explained previously [7]. For CrB2 in this work, B receives an electron from Cr, thus obtaining a valence electron structure similar to that of the C atom, and subsequently, one 2*s* electron is excited to a 2*p* orbital, after which one 2*s* orbital and two 2*p* orbitals (2*p**x* and 2*p**y*) hybridize to form three B *sp*2 orbitals. The remaining electron bonds with another B atom in the *c*-direction, i.e., the B 2*p**z*–B 2*p**z* orbitals, overlap to form a π-like bond, as shown in Fig. 4(d). The charge transfer phenomenon described above and the appearance of *sp*2 hybridized orbitals indicate the presence of ionic and covalent bonding in CrB2, whereas the triangular aggregation of electrons between the three Cr atoms in Fig. 4(b) suggests the presence of metallic bonding between the Cr atoms. Therefore, the simultaneous existence of three bonding modes, metallic, covalent, and ionic, in CrB2 is consistent with reports in Ref. [7], further indicating the reliability of the theoretical calculations in this work.

For comparison, theoretical calculations of the electron localization function (ELF) [19] of CrB2 have been performed, the analysis of which allows for a better understanding of the nature of the chemical bonding interactions, which have been demonstrated in previous studies [20−23]. The 3D representation of the ELF and 2D cross section through different planes of CrB2 are illustrated in Fig. 7. Owing to the coexistence of three types of chemical bonding, an iso-surface level with ELF = 0.5 (white line in Fig. 7), which corresponds to the electron‒gas-like pair probability [19], was chosen to facilitate the differentiation of the types of chemical bonds. The region of lower localization, similar to the electron gas between Cr in Fig. 7(b), is characteristic of metal bonding [21,24], whereas the high localization region around Cr originates from the covalent bond between Cr and B in the c* direction, as shown in Fig. 7(d). One can see from Fig. 7(c) that strong covalent bonding, which is characterized by the presence of a nonnuclear maximum ELF in the interaction region [24], is present between B in the shape of a graphite-like six-membered ring. In addition, although the ionic bonding between Cr and B is more difficult to observe because of the presence of covalent bonding, a narrow region of minimal ELF can still be observed in Fig. 7(d), which suggests that ionic bonding exists between Cr and B.

The above theoretical investigations highlight the presence of three bonding modes, covalent, metallic, and ionic, in CrB2. Cr bonds to each other in the (001) plane via metallic bonding. B is bonded in the form of a graphite-like six-membered ring in the (002) plane due to the presence of sp*2 hybridization, whereas ionic bonding, as well as covalent bonding, is formed between B and Cr in the (110) plane. This is consistent with the generally accepted bonding mode of TMB2 [7−9].

#### 3.2.1 Crystal structure of CrB<sub>2</sub>

Since experimental verification of the crystal structure and chemical bonding of CrB2 has been overlooked, in this work, spherical aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-STEM) combined with EELS was used to characterize the structure and bonding of CrB2 to fill this gap.

Figures 8(a) and 9(a) show high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) images of CrB2 in the [001] and [110] zone axes, respectively, where the atomistic structures are overlaid in the HAADF images, and the insets show the fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns on the corresponding zone axes. The brightness of the atomic columns in the HAADF image is approximately proportional to the square of the atomic number, so the brighter columns in Figs. 8(a) and 9(a) are considered to be Cr columns, which is consistent with the arrangements of Cr in the crystal structure of CrB2 (Fig. 1(a)). From the selected area electron diffraction pattern (SAED) patterns (Figs. 8(b) and 9(b)), lattice constants of *a* = *b* = 2.967 Å and *c* = 3.036 Å are calculated for CrB2, which are in agreement with the data determined by X-ray diffraction and the theoretical values considering spin polarization in Table 1. Figures 8(c)–8(e) and 9(c)–9(e) show the atomic resolution EDS maps of Cr and B of CrB2 in the [001] and [100] directions, respectively. The distribution of Cr is consistent with the HAADF image, whereas B lies between the Cr elements. Close scrutiny of the combined EDS map reveals that the B atomic columns are located at the center of the triangle built through the three Cr atomic columns, as shown by the blue circles in Fig. 8(e). In Fig. 9(e), two adjunct B atomic columns are located in the center formed by the Cr column rectangle. The above results directly corroborate the crystal structure of CrB2, confirming that it is an AlB2-type structure of transition metal diborides. One of the first striking advantages of the iDPC technique is that it is able to image light and heavier elements together in one image, whereas a standard HAADF-STEM image shows only heavier atoms [25]. To observe the arrangement of Cr and B simultaneously, iDPC images of CrB2 in the [001] and [100] zone axes were obtained and are shown in Figs. 8(f) and 9(f) together with the overlaid atomistic structure, from which the atom arrangement in the AlB2-type structure of CrB2 can be visualized more clearly.

#### 3.2.2 Chemical bonding in CrB<sub>2</sub>

STEM combined with EELS can provide information about chemical bonding. Information about the electronic structure can be obtained from the fine structure of the EELS, mainly by analyzing the ELNES of a given EELS edge [26,27]. For CrB2 in this work, the focus is on the graphite-like six-membered ring structure formed by the *sp*2 hybridization of the B atom and the ionic‒covalent bonding between B and Cr. Thus, a high-resolution EELS of B was obtained via a spherical aberration electron microscope equipped with an EELS accessory. The thickness of the sample is thin enough because of the use of an FIB to prepare the STEM sample. The value of the mean number of scattering events per incident electron (*t*/*λ)* is essential for EELS analysis, so it is calculated to be approximately 0.72. According to the inelastic mean free path formula (Eq. (1)) [28], the *λ* value of a sample is approximately 172 nm.

(1) λ≈106×F×E0Em×ln(2×β×E0Em)

where *E*0 is the incident electron energy (keV), *E*m is the average energy loss (eV), *β* is the spectrum collection semiangle (mrad), and *F* is the relativistic factor. Therefore, the sample thickness is predicted to be 124 nm, which is more desirable.

Figure 10 shows the *K*-edge spectrum of B obtained from the CrB2 sample compared with the results of the theoretical calculations, where the abscissa of the theoretical ELNES should be Δ*E* (counting from the *K*-edge onset energy of B), which actually corresponds to the experimental value. For easy comparison, the energy range of the abscissa of the theoretical spectra was modified. The theoretical calculations of EELS were performed by calculating core-level spectroscopy [29] via the CASTEP code, and the parameters were set up in the same way as described in Section 2.1, except that the on-the-fly generated (OTFG) ultrasoft pseudopotential [30] was employed to describe the ion‒electron interactions. The experimental spectra are ELNES after energy drift correction and subtraction of the background using a power law function via a digital micrograph [31]. Figure 10(a) shows that the spectrum of B in CrB2 is similar to those of previously reported AlB2-type transition-metal diborides, such as TiB2, ZrB2, and TaB2. The spectrum can be divided into four peaks, namely, *A*, *B*, *C*, and *D*, as shown in Fig. 10(a). ELNES spectra are related mainly to the energy loss of incident electrons from inelastic scattering, where the inner electrons are excited to antibands. For the *K*-edge of B in CrB2, the *A* and *C* peaks arise from the excitation of electrons from the 1*s* orbital to the antibonding orbital, where excitation to π* antibonding requires lower energy; thus, the inelastic scattering loss is lower, corresponding to the *A* peak (*p**z*), whereas the *C* peak corresponds to excitation to σ* antibonding (*sp*2 hybridization) [32,33]. The formation of the *B* and *D* peaks is explained below. Figure 10(b) shows the calculated ELNES considering different polarizations in different directions. The change in the polarization direction affects the spectra of the energy ranges corresponding to the *A* and *B* peaks in the experimental results, and with increasing polarization in the *c* direction, peak *A* and peak *B* gradually overlap, which may be attributed to covalent bonds in the *c* direction (Cr 3*d*–B 2*p**z*). Compared with the experimental spectra collected in various regions of the sample, as shown in Fig. 10(d), the ELNES of different regions of the sample (as shown in Fig. 10(c)) varies and is similar to the calculated results of different polarization directions, indicating that the direction of polarization varies in different regions of the sample.

*
Fig. 10(a) K*-edge spectra of B in CrB2 obtained from experiments (for comparison, *K*-edge spectrum of B in MgB2 is also included). (b) Calculated ELNES considering different polarizations in different directions, and (d) experimental ELNES collected in various regions in (c).

However, in comparison with the ELNES of MgB2, which is also an AlB2-type diboride, the σ* bands of CrB2 in the present work, as well as transition metal diborides in other works, are much broader (i.e., the B and D peaks are not obvious in MgB2, as shown in Fig. 10(a)) [32–34]. Notably, the energy range of MgB2 in the literature is generally greater than that of CrB2 in the present work. To elucidate this difference, theoretical calculations of the electronic structure and chemical bonding of MgB2 were also carried out in this work.

Figure 11 shows TDOS, PDOS, and electron density difference maps for different crystalline planes of MgB2. The bonding between Mg atoms is predominantly metallic (Fig. 11(e)), whereas the bonding between the B atoms is similar to that of CrB2, i.e., B *sp*2–B *sp*2 (as shown in the PDOS of B in Fig. 11(a)) as well as B 2*p**z*–B 2*p**z* (as shown in Figs. 11(b)–11(d)). Compared with the theoretical calculations of the electronic structure and chemical bonding of CrB2 described above, the major difference is that fewer covalent bonding components exist between Mg and B in MgB2, but rather, it consists mainly of ionic bonds (see the explanation of the formation of the *sp*2 hybridized orbitals above), probably related to the lower valence electrons and the absence of 3*d* electrons in Mg.

Hence, it is reasonable to conclude that the broader σ* band in the ELNES of CrB2 and other transition metal diborides is attributed to the contribution of the covalent bonding component between B and the TM, i.e., resonances arising from the hybridization of B 2p**z*–TM 3*d*(*t*2g), B 2*p**z*–TM 3*d*(*e*g), B *sp*2–TM 3*d*(*t*2g), B *sp*2–TM 3*d*(*e*g), etc., resulting in a change in the energy of the antibonding orbitals. In more detail, on the basis of the comparison of the theoretical calculations and experimental results of ELNES above, the B peak originates mainly from covalent bonds in the *c* direction (TM 3*d*–B 2*p**z*), whereas the *D* peak may originate from the hybridization of B *sp*2 with TM 3*d*. In addition, more fine structures in the theoretical ELNES spectra were not shown in the experimental spectra because of factors such as the thickness of the sample and the resolution of the experimental instruments, and it was worthwhile to analyze these fine structures. As explained above, the *B* and *D* peaks originate from the B–Cr covalent bond, while according to the crystal field theory and DOS as well as electron density difference maps, the 3*d* orbitals of Cr are split into 3*d*(*t*2g) orbitals with lower energy and 3*d*(*e*g) orbitals with higher energy, leading to more fine structures between the three peaks of *B*, *C*, and *D* in the theoretical ELNES spectra.

### 3.3 Magnetism

One of the important findings to emerge from recent studies is that CrB2 is an antiferromagnetic material, which is typical of magnetic materials in diborides [10,35,36]. Owing to its special magnetic properties, CrB2 is frequently used to improve the electromagnetic wave (EMW) absorption properties of ultrahigh-temperature ceramics such as high-entropy diborides. Prior efforts have focused primarily on theoretical calculations as well as experimental tests on the magnetic properties of CrB2. For example, as early as 1977, Funahashi *et al*. [10] determined the magnetic structure of CrB2 via neutron diffraction experiments. Experimentally, most of the previous works were performed via the Faraday balance method [37]. Nevertheless, it is well known that the hysteresis loop contains a variety of magnetic parameters and is a significant characteristic curve of magnetic materials. Unfortunately, to the best of our knowledge, no CrB2 hysteresis loops have been reported before. In this work, therefore, the magnetic characteristics of CrB2 are illustrated via theoretical calculations, and the hysteresis loop of CrB2 at ambient temperature is tested via a VSM to fill this gap.

Figures 13(a)–13(c) show the density of spin-projected states of CrB2 obtained from theoretical calculations. One can see that the majority and minority spin channels lack full symmetry between them and have a net magnetic moment, which is mainly provided by Cr 3*d* (Fig. 13(b)). This is consistent with previously reported antiferromagnetism with the incommensurate magnetic ordering of CrB2 [10,35]. Figure 13(d) shows the dependence of the magnetization strength (*M*) on the magnetic field strength (*H*) for CrB2 obtained from the VSM experiment up to 20 kOe. Above 5 kOe, the curve is almost linear, the magnetization is not saturated, and evident hysteresis is present. The calculated slope of the *M* vs. *H* curve for 5–10 kOe gives a molar susceptibility of approximately 5.77×10−4 emu/mol for CrB2, which is in agreement with the results of previous studies [37,38]. We can conclude that the magnetic properties of CrB2 contribute to the superior EMW absorption performance of Cr-containing diboride solid solutions and high-entropy diborides. The above results not only help explain why Cr-containing diboride solid solutions and high-entropy diborides show superior EMW absorption properties but also open a new avenue for the design of new EMW absorption materials.

## 4 Conclusions

In the present work, direct experimental evidence is reported for the first time to corroborate the crystal structure and chemical bonding in CrB2. Theoretically, three types of chemical bonds, covalent, metallic, and ionic, are predicted to exist in CrB2. Specifically, Cr is bonded to each other in the (001) plane with metallic bonds, whereas B is bonded in the (002) plane with a graphite-like six-membered ring due to *sp*2 hybridization, which originates from the ionic bonding formed by the electron exchange between Cr and B and forms covalent bonds in the (110) plane by hybridization with Cr 3*d* (*t*2g and *e*g). Experimentally, atomic-resolution HADDF, ABF, and iDPC STEM images were obtained via aberration-corrected transmission electron microscopy. The atomic-resolution HADDF images and the atomic-resolution EDS maps, as well as the iDPC images of CrB2, were analyzed, and it was demonstrated that CrB2 possesses an AlB2-type structure in agreement with that determined from X-ray diffraction (XRD) (reciprocal lattice). The chemical bonding information of CrB2 was obtained via the EELS accessory of STEM, and the peaks in the ELNES of B originated mainly from *p**z* and *sp*2 hybridization. In addition, the ELNES and theoretically calculated chemical bonding information of CrB2 are compared with those of MgB2, which possesses fewer valence electrons. The appearance of broader peaks in the ELNES of CrB2 and other TMB2 can be attributed to the covalent bonding between B and the transition metal, i.e., the resonance arising from the hybridization of B *sp*2 and *p**z* with Cr 3*d*(*t*2g) and 3*d*(*e*g), which is absent in MgB2 owing to the lack of d orbitals. Finally, theoretical calculations revealed that CrB2 has a net magnetic moment, and the hysteresis loop of CrB2 was measured via VSM. The results show that the molar magnetization of CrB2 obtained from the hysteresis loop generally agrees with that measured by Faraday’s balance method in previous studies. In summary, all the experimental results in the present work corroborate the theoretical calculations and are highly important for investigating the crystal structure and chemical bonding features of CrB2, which is helpful for understanding the structure‒property relationships and promoting practical applications of TMB2 in extreme aerospace environments. In addition, the hysteresis loop further confirms the magnetic properties of CrB2, providing a theoretical basis for its application in electromagnetic fields such as high-entropy EMW absorption materials.
