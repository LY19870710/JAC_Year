## 1 Introduction

Thermal barrier coating (TBC) materials are essential for developing advanced gas turbines to protect substrates from harsh environments [1–3]. 7–8 wt %Y2O3 partially stabilized ZrO2 (YSZ) is a state-of-the-art material owing to its excellent thermal and mechanical properties [4,5]. Unfortunately, YSZ coatings undergo adverse phase transitions with volumetric changes after long-term annealing at temperatures higher than 1200 °C. The volumetric change induces stress in the YSZ coating and causes severe delamination of the coating. In addition, YSZ is powerless under silicate melt (CaO–MgO–Al2O3–SiO2, CMAS) attack. At high temperatures, CMAS melts can infiltrate coatings and reduce the overall porosity of YSZ, resulting in a low strain tolerance and a decrease in lifetime of nearly two orders of magnitude [6−8]. Thus, there is an urgent need for new TBC materials that not only possess excellent thermal and mechanical properties but also show high resistance to CMAS corrosion.

In recent years, rare earth zirconates (RE2Zr2O7) have been explored as potential TBC candidates. These materials exhibit low thermal conductivity (1.5**–**2 W·m−1·K−1), high chemical stability, and a coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of approximately 10×10−6 K−1, which is comparable to that of YSZ (11.2×10−6 K−1) [9−13]. Moreover, studies have shown that rare earth zirconates have high resistance to CMAS corrosion. The rare earth elements dissolve into the CMAS melt and quickly form a stable reaction layer with a composition of Ca2RE8(SiO4)6O, thereby impeding the further infiltration of CMAS [14−17]. Despite these advantages, the practical application of rare earth zirconates is seriously restricted by their low fracture toughness (0.9–1.3 MPa·m1/2), which is much lower than that of YSZ (3.5 MPa·m1/2) [18]. The inadequate resistance to crack propagation usually results in a relatively short thermal cycling life [5]. Therefore, considerable efforts have been devoted to enhancing the toughness of rare earth zirconates, such as introducing a second phase as a toughening agent, which is often combined with RE2Zr2O7 to form composite materials used as TBCs [19−22]. Recently, perovskite-type rare earth aluminates (REAlO3) have been studied as toughening agents for RE2Zr2O7. In addition to having a high melting point and low oxygen diffusivity, REAlO3 has good thermochemical compatibility with RE2Zr2O7 above 1400 °C and has a high toughness due to the ferroelastic domain switching toughening mechanism [23−25]. Schmitt *et al*. [26] reported that the GdAlO3/Gd2Zr2O7 composite exhibited better mechanical properties and CaO–MgO–SiO2–AlO1.5 resistance than Gd2Zr2O7. Tu *et al*. [27] fabricated a series of high-entropy REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites, and their research showed that the composites exhibited extremely low thermal conductivity and high fracture toughness. Although these REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites demonstrate attraction for improved mechanical properties, the toughening mechanisms have not been extensively studied. Moreover, as the temperature increases, the corrosion of CMASs becomes more severe. The influence of adding REAlO3 on the CMAS corrosion resistance of composites remains unclear.

In this work, we designed a series of REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 (RE = La, Nd, Sm, Eu, Gd, and Dy) composites with a eutectic composition, wherein the molar ratio of REAlO3 to RE2Zr2O7 is 72 : 28 [28]. The eutectic composition has a small grain size due to coupled two-phase nucleation. The corresponding volume ratio of RE2Zr2O7 to REAlO3 is 46 : 54, resulting in high-volume heterogeneous interfaces and alternating stress fields, which contribute to reducing the thermal conductivity and enhancing the fracture toughness. These REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites demonstrate thermophysical properties comparable to those of YSZ and exhibit a fracture toughness increase exceeding 40% relative to that of RE2Zr2O7. The toughening mechanisms include ferroelastic toughening, fine-grain strengthening, and residual stress toughening. Multiple mechanisms trigger more crack defects and energy consumption. Furthermore, the CMAS penetration depth of the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites is approximately 36% lower than that of RE2Zr2O7. The Al–O constituents in the composite play a beneficial role in inhibiting CMAS corrosion, which captures CaO, SiO2, and MgO in the CMAS melt and increases the CMAS viscosity. From the above perspectives, the viability of REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites with eutectic composition for advanced thermal barrier coatings has been elucidated.

### 2.1 Preparation of REAlO<sub>3</sub>/RE<sub>2</sub>Zr<sub>2</sub>O<sub>7</sub> composites with eutectic composition

The REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites were prepared via a two-step solid-state reaction process. The raw powders, including RE2O3 (99.99%, RE = La, Nd, Sm, Eu, Gd, Dy, and Ho, Macklin, China), Al2O3 (99.99%, Sinopharm, China), and ZrO2 (99.99%, Mreda, China) were heat treated at 1000 °C for 2 h before weighing to remove the absorbed water and hydrocarbons. Subsequently, the eutectic composition of REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 was weighed and included 36 mol% ZrO2, 41 mol% RE2O3, and 23 mol% Al2O3. The stoichiometric mixed powders were mixed in ethanol and ball-milled for 4 h at a rotation speed of 250 r·min−1 in a planetary mill (Miqi Co., Ltd., Changsha, China). After that, the slurry was rotating-evaporated under vacuum at 50 °C and heat treated at 100 °C for 4 h. The dried powders were then calcined at 1100 °C for 5 h and subjected to a further 10 h of ball milling. In the following step, the resulting powders were sieved through a 200-mesh sifter and uniaxially pressed under a pressure of 20 MPa to form green bodies. The green bodies were further densified via cold isostatic pressing at 220 MPa for 90 s. Finally, dense bulk samples were obtained after sintering at 1600 °C for 10 h. For microstructure analysis, the sample surface was polished and then thermally etched at 1550 °C for 30 min.

### 2.2 Characterization

The density of the bulk samples was evaluated by the Archimede method, with all the samples showing a relative density exceeding 99%. An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/max-2500/PC, Rigaku, Japan) was employed to analyze the phase composition utilizing Cu-Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5417 Å) across a 2*θ* range of 20°–80° at a scanning speed of 10 (°)·min−1. An scanning electron microscope (SEM; MERLIN Compact, ZEISS, Germany) equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) was used to study the microstructure of the prepared bulk samples and CMAS corrosion surface. The contact angle was observed by an optical contact angle instrument (OCA25, Dataphysics, Germany).

### 2.5 CMAS corrosion resistance

The resistance of the dense bulk samples to 33CaO–9MgO–13AlO1.5–45SiO2 (CMAS) was evaluated. CMAS powders were synthesized by the solid-state reaction method and subsequently made into a cubic ingot with a size of 2 mm × 2 mm × 2 mm through sintering at 1000 °C for 5 h. The CMAS ingot was placed onto the polished surface of the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples for corrosion testing, and the same process was conducted for the Gd2Zr2O7 and YSZ samples for comparison. The testing temperature and time were 1300 °C and 20 h, respectively.

### 3.1 Phase composition of sintered REAlO<sub>3</sub>/RE<sub>2</sub>Zr<sub>2</sub>O<sub>7</sub> composites

Figure 1(a) displays the XRD patterns of the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 (RE = La, Nd, Sm, Eu, Gd, Dy, and Ho) bulk samples. The diffraction peaks are assigned to the perovskite phase for REAlO3 and the pyrochlore phase for RE2Zr2O7. Clearly, the diffraction peaks gradually shift to higher angles with increasing rare earth atomic number due to the reduction in ionic size [29]. Among these materials, REAlO3 and RE2Zr2O7 (RE = La, Nd, Sm, Eu, and Gd) exhibit excellent chemical compatibility up to 1600 °C. For the HoAlO3/Ho2Zr2O7 system, chemical reactions occur during high-temperature exposure, leading to the formation of a more stable garnet phase (Ho3Al5O12). By correlating these results with the CTE results, we constructed a phase diagram of REAlO3 in REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites, covering a temperature range from 20 to 1300 °C. As shown in Fig. 1(d), LaAlO3 and NdAlO3 exhibit a rhombohedral structure in space group *R*3*c*, while other rare earth aluminates display an orthorhombic structure in space group *Imma*. A phase transition from a rhombohedral to a cubic structure is found in the LaAlO3/La2Zr2O7 system, which is characterized in Ref. [25] as a second-order phase transition without any volumetric change. Conversely, there is a sudden decrease in the CTE of the SmAlO3/Sm2Zr2O7 composite due to the phase transition of SmAlO3 from orthorhombic to rhombohedral structure (Figs. 1(b) and 1(c)). According to t Vasylechko *et al*. [29], this transition involves a lattice volume reduction for SmAlO3 from 53.29 to 53.17 Å3, corresponding to a volumetric change of approximately 0.22%. Fortunately, this change (less than 1 vol%) is significantly less than that of monoclinic ZrO2. Moreover, the high-temperature phase structure of SmAlO3/Sm2Zr2O7 is similar to that of NdAlO3/Nd2Zr2O7, suggesting that SmAlO3/Sm2Zr2O7 possesses mechanical properties similar to those of the NdAlO3/Nd2Zr2O7 system. Therefore, the phase transition is thought to have a minimal impact on the thermal cycling performance of the corresponding TBCs [30]. The CTE values of the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites, which are critical for TBC performance, range from 10.3×10−6 to 11.3×10−6 K−1 (Fig. 1(c)). These values are relatively high in comparison with those of other Al2O3-containing compounds and are comparable to that of YSZ (11.2×10−6 K−1).

Reducing the thermal conductivity can be achieved by introducing point defects into the material to decrease the phonon relaxation time through phonon–defect scattering. This strategy has been widely applied to ZrO2-based oxides. As reported in our previous work [39], pyrochlore-type RE2Zr2O7 possesses low thermal conductivity, which approaches its limitations at high temperatures. This low thermal conductivity can be attributed to the strong phonon–phonon and phonon–defect (oxygen vacancy) scattering processes [40]. In contrast, rare earth aluminates exhibit higher thermal conductivity due to a defect-lacking lattice structure and stiff Al–O bonds, leading to prolonged relaxation time and a considerable average acoustic velocity [41]. It has been reported that oxide ceramics containing a large mole fraction of Al–O constituents and a small coordination number (four O2− ions) possess high thermal conductivity. For example, the thermal conductivity of RAl11O18 (R = Ca, Sr, Ba) lies between 2.6 and 3.5 W·m−1·K−1 at 1000 °C [42].

The average grain size of the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites is much smaller than that of each individual component. Therefore, the volume fraction of the grain boundaries (especially the heterogeneous interface between the REAlO3 and RE2Zr2O7 grains) in the composite is much greater. These boundaries are expected to reduce the thermal conductivity according to the thermal transport theory depicted by Eq. (13). The Maxwelle thermal tranmodel is widely used to predict the thermal conductivity of composites via Eq. (14) [43]:

(14) κ−κ1κ+2κ2=fκ2−κ1κ2+2κ1

where *κ*, *κ*1, and *κ*2 are the thermal conductivities of the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites (RE = La, Nd, Sm, and Gd), REAlO3, and RE2Zr2O7, respectively. The calculated results are shown in Fig. 4(c), where the predicted values are significantly greater than the measured values. This indicates that the Kaptize thermal conductance of the grain boundaries in the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites is low enough to reduce the overall thermal conductivity. The Kaptize thermal conductance is calculated using Eqs. (15) and (16) proposed by Benvensite [44] and Hasselman and Johnson [45]:

(15) κ=κ1κ2(1+2κ1α)+2κ1+2f[κ2(1−κ1α)−κ1]κ2(1+2κ1α)+2κ1−f[κ2(1−κ1α)−κ1]

(16) α=αKr=κ1λK⋅r

where parameter * α is related to the grain boundary resistance; r* is the average particle radius; *λ*K is the Kaptize thermal conductance. The thermal conductivities of REAlO3 and RE2Zr2O7 used in this calculation are listed in Table 2. As shown in Fig. 4(d), the thermal conductance of heterogeneous grain boundaries in the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composite is approximately 4×105 W·m−2·K−1, which is much lower than the conductance of YSZ grain boundaries [46], indicating that heterogeneous grain boundaries can effectively suppress heat transfer between REAlO3 and RE2Zr2O7 grains.

### 3.4 CMAS corrosion resistance

Figures 5(a)–5(f) show cross-sectional SEM images of REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 after CMAS corrosion at 1300 °C for 20 h. The majority of CMAS melt (glass at room temperature) is consumed by REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7, resulting in the formation of a thick reaction layer. The average thicknesses of the reaction layers are 103.3, 67.2, 73.4, 94.7, 96.8, and 97.8 μm for the La, Nd, Sm, Eu, Gd, and Dy composites, respectively. The reaction products of CMAS and the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites were analyzed by XRD (Fig. 5(g)). The main reaction products are indexed as rare earth apatite (RE-apatite; Ca2RE8(SiO4)6O), monoclinic zirconia, cubic zirconia, mullite, anorthite, CaAl2SiO7, and spinel. Moreover, the reaction products vary with the ionic radius of the rare earth elements. Monoclinic zirconia is observed in the La-, Nd-, and Sm-composites but is not detected in the Gd- and Dy-composites. The analysis above suggests that the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites strongly react with the CMAS melt, resulting in the formation of stable rare earth apatite and Al2O3-based compounds with high melting points that impede further penetration of the CMAS melt. For comparison, the CMAS corrosion behavior of pure pyrochlore rare earth zirconate, specifically Gd2Zr2O7, was also investigated. The cross-sectional SEM image and elemental distribution of CMAS-corroded Gd2Zr2O7 are shown in Fig. 6(a), with a penetration depth of 155.7 μm. It is ~50% thicker than that of the GdAlO3/Gd2Zr2O7 sample, indicating a remarkable improvement in the CMAS resistance of the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites. Gd2Zr2O7 reacts with CMAS, and Gd-apatite (Ca2Gd8(SiO4)6O) is the main reaction product, accompanied by a small amount of spinel (MgAl2O4) [47]. It can be deduced that rare earth elements can effectively suppress the penetration by consuming CMAS melts [14]. As a result, both the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites and Gd2Zr2O7 show better resistance to CMAS corrosion than YSZ. A dense YSZ bulk sample was prepared and attacked under a CMAS melt, and the infiltration depth of the CMAS melt is greater than 400 μm, which is much greater than those of REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 and Gd2Zr2O7.

The cross-sectional element distribution of the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 bulk sample after CMAS corrosion is displayed in Figs. 6(b)–6(g). In the reaction layer, the Ca and RE are captured by the bright phase in Fig. 6(b), which is determined to be the RE-apatite phase. The dark phase is composed of Al- and Mg-rich phases that can be labeled as spinel and mullite (MgAl2O4, 2200 °C; CaAl2O4, 1300 °C; Si2Al6O13, 1934 °C). The distribution of the Mg-rich phase and ZrO2 varies with the change in the amount of rare earth elements in the composite. As displayed in Fig. 6(d), the MgAl2O4 phase is located near the interface between the interaction layer and the SmAlO3/Sm2Zr2O7 substrate. However, the MgAl2O4 phase is homogenously distributed in the Dy-composite, which is different from that in the La-, Nd-, and Sm-composites. Furthermore, the size of the round ZrO2-rich particles is smaller in the Dy-composite than in the Sm- and La-composites. Larger rare earth elements can act as stabilizers by dissolving into the ZrO2 lattice. This process accelerates the nucleation of small ZrO2 particles [48]. The reaction process can be explained as Eq. (17):

(17) (1−x)Zr4++(2−3x2)O2−+xRE3+→PrecipitateRExZr1−xO2−3x2

The solution limit of rare earth elements in ZrO2 increases with decreasing atomic size [49]. This conclusion is also supported by the XRD patterns shown in Fig. 6, where cubic ZrO2 is detected in the DyAlO3/Dy2Zr2O7 sample. The reaction process between the CMAS melt and the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 sample can be expressed as Eqs. (18) and (19) [14,50]:

(18) RE2Zr2O7+CMAS→Ca2RE8(SiO4)6O+(REZr)O2

(19) REAlO3+CMAS→Ca2RE8(SiO4)6O+MgAl2O4+CaSi2Al2O12

The consumption of CMAS melts occurs at the cost of rare earth oxides in composites, and a higher utilization rate results in better CMAS corrosion resistance [50,51]. However, not all rare earth oxides can be utilized to crystallize CMAS melts because of the precipitation of ZrO2, and RE2O3 can act as a stabilizer by dissolving into ZrO2 [52]. In other words, the CMAS resistance decreases with increasing ZrO2 concentration in the RE2O3–ZrO2 solid solution. For the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites, the utilization rate of rare earth elements increases by ~11% (per mole cations) compared with that of rare earth zirconates owing to the reduction in the ZrO2 concentration. In addition, the introduction of Al–O constituents in REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 not only narrows the liquid phase region of CaO–Al2O3–SiO2 (CAS) but also captures MgO to further reduce the liquid phase region. More importantly, the addition of Al–O constituents increases the viscosity of CMAS melt to reduce the fatality of CMAS [47]. The contact angles of the CMAS melts on the surfaces of YSZ, Gd2Zr2O7, and SmAlO3/Sm2Zr2O7 are shown in Fig. 7. Notably, the contact angles are as follows: YSZ > SmAlO3/Sm2Zr2O7 > Gd2Zr2O7. A higher contact angle indicates a slower spreading speed over the surface. The low spreading speed of CMAS on the YSZ surface can be attributed to the dissolution of Zr4+, which increases the viscosity of the CMAS melt. For the SmAlO3/Sm2Zr2O7 composite, the dissolution of Al–O constituents in CMAS increases its viscosity so that the spreading of CMAS is suppressed compared to that of RE2Zr2O7. As a result, the aggressiveness of CMAS corrosion is reduced for the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, the mechanical properties, thermophysical properties, and CMAS corrosion resistance of REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites with eutectic composition were studied. The fracture toughness and hardness of the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites are greater than 2.7 MPa·m1/2 and 10 GPa, respectively, exhibiting notable superiority over rare earth zirconates. The CTEs of the composites range from 10.3×10−6 to 11.3×10−6 K−1, comparable to that of YSZ. Moreover, the high-temperature thermal conductivity ranges from 2.0 to 3.0 W·m−1·K−1, which is lower than the value predicted by the MG model. This indicates that the heterogeneous interface between REAlO3 and RE2Zr2O7 can effectively suppress heat transfer. The REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites show excellent CMAS corrosion resistance, and a thick and stable reaction layer impedes further infiltration of the CMAS melt. The thickness of the reaction layer ranges from 67.2 to 103.3 μm, which is significantly thinner than those observed in YSZ and Gd2Zr2O7. The utilization rate of rare earth oxides increases by ~11% (per mole cations) compared to that of rare earth zirconates due to the reduction in the ZrO2 concentration. Additionally, the presence of Al in the composites results in the capture of MgO, CaO, and SiO2 to form reaction products with high melting points. Moreover, the dissolution of Al−O constituents increases the viscosity of the CMAS melt so that the spreading speed of CMAS decreases. As a result, the REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites can act as a robust resistance layer for CMAS corrosion.
