## 1 Introduction

Low-temperature co-fired ceramic (LTCC) technology has played a key role in three-dimensional packaging and multifunctional modules with high robustness and high integration density for millimeter/terahertz communication, electric vehicles, and aerospace applications [1−4]. Since the dielectric constant is inversely proportional to the signal propagation speed, microwave dielectric ceramics with low dielectric constants (*ε*r 5−7]. In most current commercial LTCC materials, the glass phase is employed to reduce the sintering temperature of LTCC materials and obtain a low *ε*r to reduce the high-frequency signal delay. Nevertheless, the inherently disordered structure of glass phases also results in the low thermal conductivity of 2–5 W/(m·K) and low bending strength (150–300 MPa) of LTCC materials [8−11], which fails to meet the stringent requirements of high-power and high-density electronic packaging and other advanced fields for high reliability, efficient heat dissipation, and resistance to mechanical shock [12−16]. Therefore, interest in developing novel LTCC material systems with high bending strength and high thermal conductivity has increased.

Some efforts have been made to improve the bending strength or thermal conductivity of glass-based LTCC materials through glass phase adjustment and ceramic filler optimization. For example, Lee *et al*. [17] added small amounts of graphene oxide (GO) and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (CNTs) into CaO–B2O3–SiO2 (CBS) glass/40 wt% Al2O3 composites and achieved a high bending strength of 420 MPa and a thermal conductivity of 5.51 W/(m·K). In addition, replacing conventional alumina fillers with high-thermal-conductivity ceramic fillers, such as aluminum nitride (AlN) and silicon nitride (Si3N4), can result in a high thermal conductivity of 18.8 W/(m·K) along with a bending strength of 226 MPa [18]. However, it is worth mentioning that previous reports on glass-based LTCC materials have shown that they can only improve either bending strength or thermal conductivity. The simultaneous improvement of both the bending strength and thermal conductivity within the same glass-based LTCC material system remains a major challenge. Therefore, exploring glass-free LTCC materials is considered an effective method to improve the bending strength and thermal conductivity of LTCC materials [19]. The CuO–TiO2–Nb2O5 (CTN) composite sintering aid was reported to reduce the sintering temperature of Al2O3 from above 1500 °C to below 1000 °C while maintaining the intrinsic high thermal conductivity (18.4 W/(m·K)) and bending strength (320 MPa) of alumina, and the low-temperature sintering mechanism revealed that the CTN could form short-circuit diffusion pathways in Al2O3 via Cu2+/Al3+ substitution [20−22]. These reports prove that CTN nonglass phase additives can overcome the traditional trade-off between low-temperature sintering and excellent comprehensive performance. The CTN composite oxide additive is therefore considered a very promising sintering aid for a much higher sintering temperature ceramics matrix.

As electronic substrate materials, zirconia-toughened alumina (ZTA) ceramics have demonstrated superiority over conventional ceramic substrate materials by combining the good thermal properties and dielectric performance of alumina [23,24] with the high mechanical strength of zirconia [25,26]. However, the inherently high sintering temperature (> 1550 °C) of ZTA ceramics hinders their low-temperature co-firing compatibility with commonly used high-conductivity metals such as Cu and Ag [27]. Therefore, ZTA ceramics can only be fabricated with a surface electrode by direct bonded copper (DBC) or active metal brazing (AMB), whose metal electrodes are limited in achieving internal three-dimensional integrated structures. In addition, the microwave dielectric properties of ZTA ceramics and their co-firing compatibility with copper electrodes have not been fully explored.

In this work, we propose a novel LTCC system with the addition of CTN composite oxide sintering aids in ZTA ceramics, which successfully strikes a balance between high performance and lower sintering temperatures. Previous studies on ZTA ceramics have focused mainly on their mechanical properties as structural ceramics. In contrast, this work reported their performance as microwave dielectric ceramics, including millimeter-wave dielectric properties, the temperature coefficient of the resonant frequency, and co-firing compatibility with copper, for the first time. Unlike the sintering mechanism in the Al2O3 + CTN system, CTN in ZTA exhibited a differentiated dual-phase synergistic effect that significantly promoted mass transport and densification, and the sintering activation energy was markedly reduced from 854 to 410.6 kJ/mol. The sintering temperature of ZTA ceramics doped with 5 wt% CTN can be significantly reduced to 1050 °C, resulting in high thermal conductivity (18.7 W/(m·K)), high bending strength (405 MPa), and low dielectric loss (9.97×10−4@11.97 GHz). The low-temperature sintering mechanism of the ZTA + CTN systems and their co-firing compatibility with multilayer copper inner electrodes were also investigated.

## 2 Experimental

The ZTA + CTN ceramics were prepared via a conventional solid-state sintering process. First, CuO (0.04–0.05 μm, purity ≥ 99.90%), TiO2 (0.05–0.06 μm, purity ≥ 99.80%), and Nb2O5 (0.05–0.1 μm, purity ≥ 99.00%) powders (Xuancheng Jingrui New Materials Co., Ltd., China) were weighed at a molar ratio of 4 : 1 : 4 and composited with the ZTA raw powder through direct mixing. ZTA powder (ZTA12, average particle size of 0.15 μm, supplied by Nanoe, France) was subsequently mechanically mixed with 2.5–10 wt% of the above CTN powders, with ethanol added as a solvent, followed by ball milling for 6 h. The ball milling conditions were as follows: ball‒powder ratio of 4 : 1; rotation speed of 400 r/min, and a mixture of grinding balls with diameters of 3, 5, and 7 mm at a ratio of 1 : 2 : 1. After the ball-milled slurry was dried, 5 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) aqueous solution was added as a binder and thoroughly mixed to ensure homogeneity. The prepared powder was pressed into cylindrical, disc, and bar-shaped samples via a flat-plate vulcanizing press to meet the requirements for dielectric, thermal, and mechanical property testing. The ceramic green bodies were debindered in air by heating in a muffle furnace (KSL-1400X-A3, Hefei Kejing, China) at a rate of 2 °C/min to 600 °C and holding for 2 h, followed by sintering at 925–1075 °C for 2–16 h at a heating rate of 5 °C/min.

The *ε*r and dielectric loss (tan*δ*) were measured via the Hakki‒Coleman dielectric resonator method at approximately 11.97 GHz with a network analyzer (E8363A, AGLENT, USA). The density of the ceramic samples was measured via the Archimedes’ drainage method. The specific heat capacity was determined via a high-temperature specific heat tester (MHTC96, SETARAM, France), and the thermal diffusivity was measured via a laser flash thermal analyzer (LFA467, NETZSCH, Germany). The thermal conductivity is calculated by multiplying the thermal diffusivity by the density and specific heat. The coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) was measured in the range of 25–400 °C via a dilatometer (L75, LINSEIS, Germany). A thermomechanical analyzer (402F3, NETZSCH, Germany) was used to measure the dimensional changes in the samples from 30 to 1400 °C at heating rates of 5, 10, 15, and 20 °C/min. The bending strength, elastic modulus, and fracture toughness were determined via a universal testing machine (5566, Instron Co., Ltd., China). Phase identification was performed via a high-resolution powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 ADVANCE, Bruker Corporation, Germany). The fracture surface morphology of the samples was observed via a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Verios G4, FEI, USA). The valence states of the elements were analyzed via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB 250, Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK). The grain boundary microstructure and elemental distribution were examined via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; EM2100 F, JEOL, Japan) in combination with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS).

## 3 Results and discussion

According to the test results of the sintering shrinkage, *ε*r, and dielectric loss of the ZTA ceramics with the addition of 2.5 wt%, 5 wt%, 7.5 wt%, and 10 wt% CTN at different holding times, as shown in Figs. S1 and S2 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), the results show that, for all four holding times, the sintering shrinkage of the samples increases and the dielectric loss decreases significantly with the addition of sintering aids; however, when the addition exceeds 5 wt%, the improvement tends to level off. At the same sintering temperature, extending the holding time also promotes shrinkage and reduces dielectric loss, with the effect being particularly pronounced for reducing dielectric loss. The properties of the samples sintered for 8 h are clearly superior to those of the samples sintered for 2 h, whereas those of the samples held for 4 h are inferior to those of the 8 h samples. Although a 16 h holding time also led to a significant improvement compared with 2 h, the enhancement relative to 8 h was only marginal. On the basis of the above preliminary results, considering both the sintering shrinkage behavior and dielectric properties, the optimal composition of ZTA + 5 wt% CTN with a holding time of 8 h was selected for subsequent investigations. Figure 1 shows the density of the ZTA + 5 wt% CTN ceramics as a function of the sintering temperature. As the sintering temperature increased, the density of the samples increased and gradually leveled off, reaching 4.13 g/cm3 at 1050 °C, which was very close to the theoretical density of the composition (4.14 g/cm3). Notably, the density of the samples suddenly increased when the samples were sintered at 950 °C. The reason is that at 925 °C, the samples were still in the initial sintering stage, where only particle necks were formed and pores had not been effectively eliminated, resulting in relatively low density. When the temperature increased to approximately 950 °C, the subsequent XRD results indicated that CTN reacted with ZrO2 to form the CuZrNb2O8 phase, which was close to its eutectic point (~920 °C) [42], producing a small amount of liquid phase. This liquid phase promoted particle rearrangement and pore filling through capillary action. Moreover, subsequent TEM results revealed that CTN segregated at the Al2O3 grain boundaries to form amorphous films that acted as fast diffusion channels. Combined with the enhanced atomic diffusion at elevated temperatures, these factors drove the material into a stage of rapid densification, leading to the observed sharp increase in density. Figure 2 shows scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the fracture surfaces of samples sintered at different temperatures. With increasing sintering temperature, the number of pores inside the material gradually decreases, and the grain morphology becomes more regular. As shown in Fig. 2, the average grain size tends to increase with increasing temperature, increasing from 363.05 nm at 950 °C to 583.84 nm at 1050 °C. However, when the temperature reached 1075 °C, abnormal grain growth occurred, leading to a slight decrease in the grain size. These results indicate that grain growth is the key factor influencing the densification process of ZTA + CTN ceramics.

Owing to the poor quality of some samples sintered at low temperatures, the dielectric properties could not be measured via the Hakki‒Coleman method. The ZTA + 2.5 wt% CTN sample exhibited relatively poor sintering behavior and high dielectric loss. The other three compositions follow a similar trend: with increasing sintering temperature, *ε*r increases, whereas the dielectric loss initially decreases but then increases. All three compositions reach their lowest dielectric loss when sintered at 1050 °C. When the sintering temperature exceeds 1050 °C, the dielectric loss increases, which is likely attributed to defect formation in the ceramics at excessively high temperatures [29,30]. XPS analysis indicates that, under high-temperature conditions, part of the Cu2+ is reduced to Cu+, accompanied by the generation of oxygen vacancies for charge compensation, which can be expressed as Eq. (3):

(3) Cu2++OO×⇒Cu++12O2+VO⋅⋅+e′

When the temperature exceeds 1050 °C, oxygen vacancies accumulate further. These oxygen vacancies act as mobile defects and defect dipoles, not only participating in the dielectric relaxation process but also enhancing electronic and ionic conduction, thereby leading to an increase in dielectric loss [31]. In addition, the SEM results revealed abnormal grain growth at 1075 °C. The resulting inhomogeneous grain structure causes concentration of the electric field and charge at the interfaces between large and small grains (field inhomogeneity), which promotes space-charge accumulation and local conduction behavior, enhances polarization effects, and results in increased dielectric loss. The ZTA ceramic sample with 5 wt% CTN sintered at 1050 °C exhibited the lowest dielectric loss of 9.97×10−4@11.97 GHz. According to the porosity-corrected dielectric constant results, at lower sintering temperatures, the actual dielectric constant of the samples is strongly influenced by porosity [32]; however, when the temperature is increased to 1025 °C, the corrected dielectric constant clearly increases. As a composite ceramic material, the dielectric behavior of the ZTA + CTN system can be better described by the Lichtenecker logarithmic mixture rule of effective medium theory [33], which can be expressed as Eq. (4), where *ε*eff represents the dielectric constant of the composite material, and *εn* represents that of its *n-*th component. *V**n*/*V* represents the volume ratio of the *n*th component to the whole.

(4) ln⁡εeff=∑n=1NVnVln⁡εn

Rietveld refinement of the XRD data revealed that at 1025 °C, a phase transformation from m-ZrO2 to t-ZrO2 occurred, with the t-ZrO2 phase content exceeding that of m-ZrO2, and the contents of the other components basically remained unchanged. Since the theoretical dielectric constant of t-ZrO2 is greater than that of m-ZrO2 [34], the increased proportion of t-ZrO2 enhances its contribution to the dielectric constant of the composite, whereas the contribution from m-ZrO2 decreases accordingly, leading to a significant increase in the overall dielectric constant at this temperature. We also measured the dielectric properties of the samples in the frequency range of 20–40 GHz. As shown in Fig. 3(d), the *ε*r remains stable at approximately 12 with minimal fluctuations across the tested millimeter-wave band, demonstrating good frequency stability and indicating that this material holds great potential for millimeter-wave communication applications.

The bending strengths of the samples with different compositions at various temperatures were measured, as shown in Fig. 4(a). All four compositions exhibit a similar trend: the bending strength of the ceramic increases with increasing sintering temperature and then decreases at higher temperatures. The ZTA sample containing 5 wt% CTN sintered at 1050 °C achieves the highest bending strength of 405 MPa. As the CTN content increased, the bending strength of the ZTA ceramic deteriorated. This is mainly because, as the CTN composite sintering aid content increases, the proportion of the high-strength ZTA matrix decreases, while significant grain coarsening occurs. At the same sintering temperature, the grain size increases from 583.84 nm to 1.84 μm, as shown in the inset of Figs. 4(b)–4(d), and such excessive grain growth leads to deterioration of the ceramic flexural strength [35,36]. Additional properties of the sintered ceramics, including the thermal conductivity, coefficient of thermal expansion, Young’s modulus, fracture toughness, quality factor (*Q*×*f* value), and temperature coefficient of resonant frequency (*τ*f), were also measured. The ZTA ceramic containing 5 wt% CTN sintered at 1050 °C exhibited excellent comprehensive properties, including a high thermal conductivity of 18.7 W/(m·K), a high bending strength of 405 MPa, and a low dielectric loss of 9.97×10−4@11.97 GHz, as shown in Table 1. Compared with the previously reported LTCC materials, which have both measured bending strength and thermal conductivity, the ZTA + CTN ceramics exhibit the best comprehensive performance, combining high bending strength with high thermal conductivity, as shown in Fig. 5. Compared with Al2O3 + CTN (18.4 W/(m·K), 320 MPa), the ZTA + CTN system not only maintains a similarly high thermal conductivity but also achieves a significantly greater bending strength (405 MPa). This improvement primarily originates from the toughening of retained tetragonal ZrO2, which is stabilized by the rigid Al2O3 matrix and further promoted by the CTN-assisted densification process. The measured fracture toughness (5.67 MPa·m1/2) provides quantitative evidence for this mechanism. Thus, the innovative value of this work lies in combining the low-temperature sintering ability of CTN with the transformation toughening effect of ZrO2, simultaneously realizing high thermal conductivity and high mechanical strength in a glass-free LTCC system, which is difficult to achieve in Al2O3-based LTCC.

The densification behavior was evaluated on the basis of the measured shrinkage values. Accordingly, the variation in relative density with temperature is calculated via Eq. (5) [37]:

(5) ρ=ρ0(1−ΔL)3

where ρ* represents the relative density at a given temperature, *ρ*0 denotes the relative density of the green body, and Δ*L* refers to the thermal shrinkage of the sample at that temperature. The value of *ρ*0 was obtained through a two-step calculation: the density was first calculated by dividing the mass by the volume of the green body; then, the relative density was determined by taking the ratio of the calculated density to the theoretical density. For both the ZTA and ZTA + CTN green bodies, *ρ*0 was 55.8%. On the basis of Eq. (5) and the shrinkage data of the two samples, the relative density–temperature curves under different heating rates are shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b). The first derivatives of the relative density–temperature relationships under different heating rates are calculated as shown in Eq. (6):

*
Fig. 6(a) Thermomechanical curves of ZTA green bodies at different heating rates. (b) Thermomechanical curves of ZTA + CTN green bodies at different heating rates. (c) Linear fitting of ln(T*d*ρ*/d*t*) vs. 104/*T* for ZTA samples, and (d) linear fitting of ln(*T*d*ρ*/d*t*) vs. 104/*T* for ZTA + CTN samples.

(6) dρdt=dρdT×dTdt

where d*ρ*/d*t* represents the densification rate at a given temperature, d*ρ*/d*T* represents the first derivative of the relative density with respect to temperature, and d*T*/d*t* represents the heating rate of the sample. On the basis of this relationship, the densification rate–temperature data for the two samples were obtained. As a representative of the full-stage sintering model, the densification behavior of ceramics follows Herring’s law [38], as expressed in Eq. (7):

(7) dρdt=Af(ρ)exp⁡(−QdRT)TGn

where *A* is a material-specific constant independent of temperature, sintering density, and grain size; *f*(*ρ*) is a function of density, *Q*d represents the sintering activation energy, *R* is the ideal gas constant; *T* is the absolute temperature, *G* denotes the grain size of the ceramic, and *n* is the grain growth exponent, which depends on the specific grain growth mechanism.

By taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq. (7), the Arrhenius-type Eq. (8) was derived, which allows the calculation of the sintering activation energy of ceramics at a given relative density:

(8) ln⁡(Tdρdt)=(−QdRT)+ln⁡Af−nln⁡G

When the sintered densities of samples with the same composition remain nearly constant under different heating rates, ln*Af* − *n*ln*G* can be regarded as a constant [39]. ln(*T*d*ρ*/d*t*) is linearly dependent on 1/*T*, and the sintering activation energy can be determined from the slope of the fitted line. Therefore, linear fitting of ln(*T*d*ρ*/d*t*) vs. 104/*T* was performed for the ZTA and ZTA + CTN ceramics at relative densities of 60%, 65%, 70%, 75%, 80%, and 85%, respectively. The average sintering activation energies are calculated from the slopes and found to be 854 kJ/mol for ZTA and 410.6 kJ/mol for ZTA + CTN, as shown in Figs. 6(c) and 6(d). These results demonstrate that the introduction of the glass-free composite oxide sintering aid CTN significantly enhances the sintering activity and driving force, thereby reducing the sintering activation energy of the ZTA ceramic.

To further clarify the role of CTN in promoting the sintering process, detailed phase and microstructural analyses were carried out. Figure 7(a) shows the XRD patterns of the ZTA + 5 wt% CTN ceramics sintered at different temperatures, and Figs. 7(b) and 7(c) present the Rietveld refinement results for the ZTA + CTN ceramics sintered at 1000, 1025, and 1050 °C. In all ceramics, the primary phases of α-Al2O3 and ZrO2 are observed, along with several peaks corresponding to the CuZrNb2O8 phase. Figure 8 displays the relative mass fractions of various phase compositions at different temperatures calculated through Rietveld refinement. The quantitative refinement results indicate that at 1000 °C, ZrO2 exists entirely in the monoclinic form (m-ZrO2; ~11%), with no tetragonal phase observed. At 1025 °C, the amount of m-ZrO2 decreases to ~5.4%, whereas a fraction of t-ZrO2 (~6.1%) appears, suggesting the onset of the m–t transformation. At 1050 °C, the m-ZrO2 content further decreases to ~3.6%, whereas t-ZrO2 remains at ~6.2%, indicating the coexistence of the two phases at elevated temperatures. These findings are consistent with the reported transformation range of 930–1170 °C [40]. The stabilization of t-ZrO2 at high temperatures can be attributed to its higher crystallographic symmetry and entropy contribution, which lowers the Gibbs free energy relative to that of m-ZrO2. Upon cooling, a large amount of metastable t-ZrO2 is still retained at room temperature, which is attributed to the mechanical constraint imposed by the dense and rigid Al2O3 matrix that suppresses the volume expansion associated with the t‒m phase transformation. This suppression serves as the energy source for transformation toughening. However, grains of t-ZrO2 with sizes exceeding the critical diameter (*d*m) transform back to m-ZrO2, accompanied by volume expansion. This transformation induces microcracking at the grain boundaries, which contributes to the increase in fracture toughness (5.67 MPa·m1/2) observed in the ZTA ceramics [41]. In addition, during the sintering process, CTN reacts with ZrO2 to form the CuZrNb2O8 phase. Since, compared with ZTA ceramics, CuZrNb2O8 has a lower sintering temperature (920 °C) and a relatively high *ε*r (28.7) [42], the formation of this compound may facilitate the low-temperature sintering of ZTA.

XPS measurements were performed on the surface of the ZTA + 5 wt% CTN sample sintered at 1050 °C for 8 h and on the sample after 10 s of etching. From the full-spectrum scan of the sample after 10 s of etching shown in Fig. 9(a), peaks corresponding to Al 2p, Nb 3d, Ti 2p, O 1s, Cu 2p, and Zr 3d are observed. Fine scans are conducted for each peak to analyze the valence states of the elements, as shown in Figs. 9(b)–9(e). In Fig. 9(b), the peaks at binding energies of 933.1 and 952.8 eV correspond to the Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 peaks of Cu+, respectively [43,44]. In Fig. 9(c), the peaks at 458.8 and 464.5 eV are assigned to the Ti 2p3/2 and Ti 2p1/2 peaks of Ti4+, respectively [45,46]. Figure 9(d) shows peaks at 207.5 and 210.3 eV, corresponding to the Nb 3d5/2 and Nb 3d3/2 of Nb5+ [47]. In Fig. 9(e), the peaks at 182.9 and 185.2 eV are attributed to the Zr 3d5/2 and Zr 3d3/2 peaks of Zr4+, respectively [48]. Compared with the sample etched for 10 s, only the valence state of Cu significantly changed on the original surface. In Fig. 9(f), the peaks at 933.5 and 953.2 eV correspond to the Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 peaks of Cu2 +, respectively [49]. These results are consistent with the valence states of the CuZrNb2O8 phase identified by XRD and further indicate the coexistence of Cu2+ and Cu+ ions in the ZTA ceramic doped with 5 wt% CTN. Cu2+ ions are known to be highly sensitive to the oxygen partial pressure at elevated temperatures. During the high-temperature sintering process, Cu2+ ions in the interior of a sample may be partially reduced to Cu+ under oxygen-deficient conditions via the following reaction [50]. The changes in the valence state of copper (between Cu2+ and Cu+) in the CTN sintering aid may play an important role in the densification of ZTA ceramics.

To further understand the low-temperature sintering mechanism of the ZTA + CTN system, the microstructure and elemental distribution of the sample sintered at 1050 °C for 8 h were analyzed via TEM. Figures 10(a)–10(d) present high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) images of the ceramic sample, revealing significant contrast differences: The dark regions exhibit typical α-Al2O3 grain morphologies, which are confirmed by selected area electron diffraction and EDS analysis results for region 2, as shown in Figs. 10(e)–10(j) and Table 2. These findings verify that these regions are primarily composed of Al and O. The bright regions display liquid-phase-like features, with higher atomic number contrast indicating the enrichment of heavy elements such as Cu, Ti, Nb, and Zr, as shown in Figs. 10(e)–10(j) and Table 2. Combined with XRD phase analysis, these regions are identified as the coexistence of ZrO2 and CuZrNb2O8 phases. Given that the eutectic temperature of CuZrNb2O8 (920 °C) is significantly lower than the sintering temperature, it is likely that this phase exists in a liquid state and contributes to the densification process. The microstructure reveals two key characteristic regions: In the Al2O3–ZrO2 multiphase region, the CTN additive selectively segregates to the ZrO2 phase boundaries and reacts to form a liquid CuZrNb2O8 phase; continuous bright thin layers are present along all the grain boundary regions between the Al2O3 grains, including the triple junctions and their extended boundaries, as shown in Figs. 10(a)–10(c) and Fig. S3 in the ESM. Owing to the poor quality of the area scan, it is not possible to accurately determine the elemental distribution of the intergranular nanoscale film (Fig. S4 in the ESM) on the basis of the atomic number contrast principle of HAADF imaging, where brighter contrast indicates enrichment of high-atomic-number elements [51], and supported by EDS analyses at point 3 near the triple junction (Table 2), enrichment of Cu, Ti, and Nb elements is observed. Moreover, the bright layer is continuously distributed strictly along the grain boundaries, ruling out the presence of isolated impurity phases. These observations collectively suggest that the interfacial layer is a CTN additive-enriched phase.

During high-temperature sintering, the densification of ceramic materials primarily depends on the migration and elimination of pores [52−54]. Triple junctions, high-energy defect regions where multiple grains converge (Fig. 10(d)), exhibit highly disordered atomic arrangements and significantly higher local free energies than do grain interiors [55]. These regions possess strong interfacial energy driving forces and readily adsorb foreign atoms to reduce system energy. In addition, structural defects such as vacancies, dislocations, and dangling bonds are abundant in these areas, providing numerous active sites that facilitate the selective adsorption and segregation of active elements. As a result, elements such as Cu, Ti, and Nb tend to accumulate at triple junctions, which not only helps lower the grain boundary energy and reduces the resistance to boundary migration but also promotes grain sliding and rearrangement at elevated temperatures, thereby accelerating pore elimination and increasing the densification efficiency [56]. Moreover, the Cu, Ti, and Nb atoms adsorb at the Al2O3 grain boundaries and form an amorphous intergranular film, as shown in Fig. 10(b). The CTN-based thin interfacial layer has a eutectic temperature much lower than that of the corresponding matrix phases. During sintering, it exists in a quasiliquid state and serves as a fast mass transport path through short-circuit diffusion [57−59]. Owing to the similar ionic radii of Cu2+ and Zr4+, Cu2+ readily undergoes solid solution reactions with Zr4+ at high temperatures, forming the CuZrNb2O8 phase. This phase may exist in a liquid form in Zr-rich regions, and the presence of such a liquid phase significantly increases ionic diffusion rates and enhances mass transport and particle rearrangement. It not only fills pores and induces particle aggregation through capillary forces by wetting effects but also may trigger a dissolution–reprecipitation mechanism that promotes microstructural reconstruction and grain growth [60]. In summary, the spatial distribution behavior of the CTN sintering aid in different regions synergistically facilitates efficient densification and property retention of ZTA-based ceramics at relatively low sintering temperatures.

To verify the co-firing compatibility with the copper electrode, a custom-prepared copper paste was used for co-firing studies. The multilayer green bodies comprising ZTA + 5 wt% CTN green tape and copper paste are prepared through the LTCC process, which includes tape casting, slicing, screen printing, and lamination. After being degassed in a nitrogen atmosphere, the samples were sintered at 1050 °C for 30 min. The cross-sectional morphology and EDS analysis results indicate that no oxidation or diffusion of Cu occurred, as shown in Fig. 11(a). Owing to the use of mechanical cutting and the ductile nature of Cu, the Cu electrode layer appears to be unevenly distributed. The EDS results of the Cu layer reveal the presence of a certain amount of Al, which may result from the intentional addition of trace ceramic powder to the copper paste during its preparation to improve interfacial bonding with the ceramic. To investigate the effect of the sintering temperature on the electrical conductivity of the Cu layer, we measured the sheet resistance of a series of samples via the four-probe method. The results (Fig. 11(b)) indicate that the sheet resistance of the Cu layer does not significantly change with increasing sintering temperature but rather fluctuates within a narrow range, indicating good thermal stability. These results suggest that the ZTA + 5 wt% CTN ceramic has good co-firing compatibility with Cu. Since the optimal sintering temperature of the proposed ZTA + CTN ceramic (> 1000 °C) exceeds the melting point of silver (961 °C), this study selected copper for the co-firing experiment, as copper offers a low cost and high electrical conductivity. The co-firing compatibility of ZTA + 5 wt% CTN with Ag was also examined; it was found that Ag reacts with Nb2O5 during sintering to form the AgNbO3 phase, as shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM. This confirms that the ZTA + CTN ceramic system was not suitable for silver cosintering applications.

## 4 Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the promoting effect of a CTN composite oxide on the low-temperature sintering of ZTA ceramics. The results show that the addition of 5 wt% CTN significantly reduces the sintering temperature of ZTA ceramics to 1050 °C while maintaining good densification. The prepared samples exhibit excellent comprehensive properties, including high thermal conductivity (18.7 W/(m·K)), high bending strength (405 MPa), and low dielectric loss (9.97×10−4@11.97 GHz). The sintering kinetics analysis indicated that the introduction of CTN effectively lowered the activation energy and increased the densification rate. The microstructural observations combined with the XRD, XPS, and TEM analyses further revealed that CTN reacts in the ZrO2-rich regions to form liquid-phase domains and generates an amorphous intergranular film at the Al2O3 grain boundaries, jointly establishing rapid diffusion pathways. The co-firing experiments with Cu confirmed good interfacial compatibility with the copper electrodes, indicating that this material system holds significant promise for LTCC applications.
