## 1 Introduction

Ceria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia polycrystals (Ce-TZPs) are a family of tough ceramics with high fracture toughness (> 10 MPa·m1/2) [1,2], high Weibull modulus, exceptional mechanical reliability and biocompatibility [3,4], superior phase stability against low-temperature aging [3,5], and interesting shape memory effects [6,7]. Compared with tetragonal yttria-stabilized zirconia (t-YSZ), Ce-TZP has remarkable advantages in terms of toughness and aging resistance, making it a promising candidate for applications in the biomedical implant and restoration fields [3,8]. Its damage tolerance and insensitivity to flaws [4,9] are especially valuable for additive manufacturing (AM). The AM process has great potential in customized component structural design and fabrication for zirconia ceramics, even though it cannot produce high-quality green bodies of ceramics as well as optimized dry and wet forming techniques (e.g., cold isostatic pressing), at least at present [10−12]. However, Ce-TZP has a relatively low flexural strength of approximately 500–600 MPa, which is only ~1/2 that of t-YSZ and limits its practical application [1,3]. The reason for the flexural strength deficiency is closely related to the tetragonal–monoclinic phase stability. In Ce-TZP, the critical stress (*σ*ct–m) that drives the martensitic tetragonal–monoclinic phase transition is quite low and often well below the critical stress (*σ*R) that drives crack propagation and fracture [3,13]. Therefore, phase transformation occurs during fracture under extreme mechanical loading conditions, limiting the strength. Clearly, it is necessary to adjust the phase stability of Ce-TZP to enhance its strength.

There are two main ways to enhance the tetragonal phase meta-stability and increase *σ*ct–m. First, one can use more Ce4+ in Ce-TZP to lower the driving force for the phase transition compared to the widely used recipe of 12 mol% ceria-stabilized zirconia tetragonal polycrystal (12Ce-TZP) [1,14]. This approach is not often practiced because Ce is relatively expensive, and oversized Ce4+ is not a stabilizer as effective as lower-valence dopants such as Y3+. Second, one can refine the grain size to lower the nucleation probability/rate of the monoclinic phase [15]. This approach is challenging because Ce-TZP, unlike t-YSZ, experiences weak solute drag and fast grain growth [16−19]. Resultantly, Ce-TZP typically has an average grain size (*G*avg) of more than a few microns, while t-YSZ has *G*avg that is one order of magnitude smaller. In addition, Ce-TZP is easy to reduce and usually phase-separated into a Ce-rich Ce–Zr–O solid solution phase and a Ce-poor zirconia phase under a reducing atmosphere, accompanied by an undesirable t–m phase transformation during cooling [20−23]. Therefore, conventional hot pressing or hot isostatic pressing (HIP) techniques cannot be used to densify Ce-TZP due to the utilization of carbon dies and inert atmospheres that is quite reducing.

We proposed the following strategy for effective grain size refinement in Ce-TZP by coupling additive manufacturing and low-temperature HIP sintering. For HIP by high-pressure argon gas, a closed porosity with apparent threshold bulk density (*ρ*thold) (relative density with respect to the theoretical density) needs to be reached in the pressureless pre-sintering step [24]. The HIP schedule (e.g., temperature, holding time, and gas pressure) for full densification is related to *ρ*thold, *G*avg, and other microstructural parameters of the presintered samples [25]. It has been reported that stereolithographic additive manufacturing results in faster surface densification than bulk interior and lower *ρ*thold for zirconia ceramics [11,26]. Such a characteristic can be beneficially utilized to obtain pre-sintered samples with smaller *G*avg suitable for the follow-up HIP step. Because of the finer grain/pore size and more grain boundaries in the diffusional pathway, the HIP temperature decreases, which suppresses Ce-rich/poor phase separation (which requires the diffusion of slow-moving cations) and tetragonal phase destabilization. This enables us to use HIP to sinter Ce-TZP to full density. In a preliminary study [26], we demonstrated the feasibility of the strategy and produced dense ultrafine-grained Ce-TZP with *G*avg = 570 nm (using a statistical method consistent with this work, the grain size in Ref. [26] should be 570 nm instead of 420 nm), a flexural strength *σ*flex ≈ 770 MPa (measured by the three-point bending method), and a fracture toughness *K*IC = 12.0 MPa·m1/2 (measured by a single edge notch beam (SENB) method).

The present work seeks to further develop this method and realize more effective grain size refinement and strengthening. In addition to additive manufacturing and HIP, we coupled two-step sintering in the pressureless pre-sintering step before the final HIP treatment or two-step pre-sintering (TP) for short. This suppresses grain growth and leads to a smaller *G*avg when the *ρ*thold is reached in the presintered samples. Here, we distinguish TP from two-step sintering commonly discussed in the literature because we target a refined grain size and closed porosity rather than full densification. We now demonstrated dense ultrafine-grained Ce-TZP prepared by the TP–HIP method, with *G*avg = 480 nm (16% reduction compared to Ref. [26]) and *σ*flex ≈ 830 MPa (7.8% increase compared to Ref. [26]), while maintaining a similar *K*IC in the range of 11–12 MPa·m1/2. There has been significant progress made in refined Ce-TZP over literature reports by others, which typically have *G*avg values in the range of a few microns [27−31]. In the following, we first report the experimental details and data, and then discuss the underlying mechanisms, especially the critical role of mixed Ce3+/4+ valences in phase stability, fracture mode, and strengthening. Our thoughts on the future development of tougher and stronger Ce-TZP ceramics are provided.

## 2 Experimental procedures

Ce-TZP green bodies were prepared by stereolithographic additive manufacturing. The slurries were prepared by mixing ultraviolet-curable resins (consisting of acrylate monomers and prepolymers), 12Ce-TZP powders (CEZ-12-1, Daiichi Kigenso Kagaku Kogyo Co. Ltd., Japan), dispersant agents, plasticizers, and photoinitiators. The primary particle size of the CEZ-12-1 powders is approximately 50 nm (see scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). The mixtures were stirred at 600 r/min for 3 h to obtain a homogeneous slurry for three-dimensional (3D) printing. The solid loading of the Ce-TZP powder in the slurry was adjusted to 40 vol%. The green bodies were printed on a 3D printer (Cerafab 7500, Lithoz GmbH, Austria) with a single layer thickness of 25 μm and an exposure power of 52 mW/cm2. The exposure time was set to 7 s per layer for the first five layers and 5 s per layer for all the other layers. The dimensions of the printed green bodies were set as 37.5 mm × 3 mm × 2.25 mm (for flexural strength measurements of the sintered samples) or 30 mm × 6 mm × 4.5 mm (for fracture toughness measurements of the sintered samples). The printed green bodies were configured to ensure that the *z*-axis was parallel to the loading direction of the final mechanical tests. The printed green bodies were ultrasonically cleaned with iso-propyl alcohol and heat-treated at 600 °C for 3 h (heating rate: 0.8 °C/min) to remove the binders and then at 1000 °C for 2 h (heating/cooling rates: 5 °C/min) to obtain sufficient strength for handling. After the above heat treatment, the relative density of the debonded specimen is 45%. The microstructures of the starting powder and the debonded green body are shown in Fig. S1 in the ESM.

Pressureless sintering experiments were conducted in a muffle furnace in air with heating/cooling rates of 5 °C/min. The HIP (AIP, HP630, USA) experiments were conducted at 1250 °C for 1 h under 200 MPa argon pressure using molybdenum heating units. The relative density (*ρ*) and open porosity (*φ*a) (the ratio of the volume of the open pores to the bulk volume) were determined by the Archimedes method (ISO 18754–2020). The theoretical density of 12Ce-TZP was 6.26 g/cm3, as provided by the supplier.

The microstructures were characterized under a field emission SEM (TESCAN MIRA 3LMH, Brno, Czech Republic). Cross-sections of selected samples were prepared by an argon ion beam cross-section polisher (IB-09020CP, JEOL, Japan), followed by thermal etching (etching conditions: 100 °C, lower than the sintering temperature for 30 min). *G*avg was calculated by the standard lineal intercept method as described in ASTM E112-13 with > 200 intercepts and a correction factor of 1.56 on ion-polished and etched cross sections. The phases of the as-sintered samples were characterized on an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/max-2550, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (wavelength: 1.5418 Å) in the 2*θ* range of 20°–90° with a scanning rate of 1 (°)/min. Raman measurements were conducted on fracture surfaces using a Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR800, HORIBA JOBIN YVON, France) with a laser wavelength of 532 nm and a wavenumber range of 100–800 cm−1. The ratio of the monoclinic phase to the total on the fracture surface (*f*m) was calculated by Eq. (1):

where *I* denotes the integral area of a selected Raman peak after Lorentzian curve fitting; the subscripts m and t denote the monoclinic and tetragonal phases, respectively; the superscript numbers denote the wavenumbers (unit: cm−1) of the corresponding Raman peaks. Surface chemistry was characterized by an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; ESCALAB Xi+, Thermo Scientific, USA) with an Al Kα source (kinetic energy: 1486.7 eV). Samples for XPS testing were ultrasonically cleaned with ethanol and distilled water, and surface ion beam etching was performed prior to data acquisition. The C 1s peak at the kinetic energy of 284.6 eV was used for calibration.

The flexural strength was measured by the three-point bending method following ASTM C1161-13, with sample dimensions of 25 mm × 2 mm × 1.5 mm (span length (*L*1) = 20 mm). Five samples with identical processing conditions were measured to obtain the average and standard deviation. Fracture toughness was measured by the SENB method, with sample dimensions of 20 mm × 3 mm × 4 mm (span length (*L*2) = 16 mm). Eight samples with identical processing conditions were measured to obtain the average and standard deviation. A notch (length: 2 mm, width: ~0.2 mm) was cut in the middle of the sample with a U-shaped end by a diamond wheel of 0.1 mm in width. All samples were fine-machined and chamfered by diamond wheels to obtain the final dimensions (without mirror polishing), and the surface roughness (*R*a) of the samples was controlled below 0.2 for the tensile side. Three-point bending and fracture toughness tests were conducted on a universal mechanical testing machine (AG-IC 100 kN, SHIMADZU, Japan) with cross-head loading speeds of 0.2 and 0.05 mm/min, respectively. The Vickers hardness of the mirror-polished sample surfaces was measured using a Vickers hardness tester (Tukon2500B, Wilson, USA) following ASTM C1327-15. Each indentation was subjected to a 19.6 N load for 15 s. Three samples were prepared for each group, with a minimum of five indentations on each sample.

## 3 Two-step pre-sintering of additive manufactured green bodies

The proposal of the current work is the compatibility of two-step sintering in the pressureless pre-sintering step of additive manufactured Ce-TZP green bodies, which refines *G*avg in both the pre-sintered samples with closed porosity and HIP-fully densified samples. A typical two-step sintering (TSS) process, proposed by Chen and Wang [32], usually involves a higher temperature at *T*1 in the first step without holding or with a short holding time to obtain a thermodynamically sinterable state (usually with a critical relative density (*ρ*c) of 75%–92%) to render the pores unstable. In the second step, the materials are heated to a lower temperature at *T*2 (1) for long holding time to achieve full density with no or suppressed grain growth in the final sintering stage [32−35]. From a thermodynamic perspective, the critical densities (*ρ*c) after the *T*1 step and allowing for full densification in the *T*2 step depend not only on the material properties (e.g., the distributions of surface energy and grain boundary energy as well as their ratios) but also on the microstructure (e.g., the size ratios of a pore to its surrounding grains) [36,37]. Practicing two-step sintering in additive manufactured samples is challenging because of nonideal particle packing and macroscopic defects/flaws in the green bodies and debinded and partially sintered samples. Therefore, after pressureless TP, we targeted for a closed porosity and then applied HIP to obtain a dense ultrafine-grained microstructure.

To determine the proper TP conditions, we conducted normal sintering (NS) of the debonded Ce-TZP samples by heating them to a set temperature for 1 min. Table 1 summarizes the data. NS at 1300 and 1350 °C for 1 min gives *ρ* = 79% and 89% and *G*avg = 300 and 330 nm, respectively. For isotropic ceramic systems with cubic and near-cubic (e.g., a tetragonal structure with a weak tetragonality, *c*/*a* ≈ 1) structures, *ρ*c is in the range of 70%–80% [32,33], whereas ceramics with more anisotropic crystal structures (e.g., regular tetragonal and hexagonal structures) usually yield higher *ρ*c of 75%–92% [36,38]. In addition, a higher relative density of green bodies before sintering usually leads to lower *ρ*c, and vice versa. Thus, considering the tetragonal structure of Ce-TZP and the relatively loose particle packing in the debinded green body, we roughly estimated *ρ*c in the range of 80%–90% for additive manufactured Ce-TZP. Therefore, in the following two-step pre-sintering experiments, 1300 and 1350 °C were chosen as the first-step temperatures (*T*1). Moreover, we noted that samples with *ρ* = 79% and *ρ* = 89% had very similar *G*avg values, indicating that grain growth was relatively slow during the intermediate sintering stage. The reason for the large grain size in fully sintered Ce-TZPs is the absence of solute dragging, so that once the pore pinning effect vanishes at the final sintering stage, grain growth accelerates significantly. This is indeed why we hoped to combine TP and HIP to refine the microstructure of Ce-TZP ceramics.

For *T*1 = 1300 °C with holding time *t*1 = 1 min, we found that *T*2 = 1250 °C for *t*2 = 10 h (TP-B) gives a closed porosity with *ρ* = 91.7% and *G*avg = 380 nm. Instead, the samples experience rapid grain growth with little increase in *ρ*, probably due to the weakening of the pinning effect of the pores on the grain boundaries. The TP-A sample failed to reach a close porosity, with *φ*a = 11.7% and *ρ* = 85.9%. Therefore, *ρ*thold is between 86% and 91% for the TP samples and is estimated to be approximately 90%. This value is similar to that for normally sintered Ce-TZP ceramics, both from additive manufacturing [26]. We thus selected samples TP-B, TP-D, and TP-F for the final HIP treatment.

## 4 Microstructure and mechanical properties of fully densified samples

The selected TP samples (TP-B, TP-D, and TP-F) were HIPed at *T*HIP = 1250 °C for 1 h at 200 MPa in an argon atmosphere. This temperature was selected to match *T*2 of these samples on TP and was kept low to prevent phase decomposition of Ce-TZP in a reducing atmosphere. The obtained samples were labeled TP–HIP-B, TP–HIP-D, and TP–HIP-F, and they all had *ρ* > 99% (Table 3). Their microstructures are fine and uniform, as shown by the polished and thermally etched surfaces (Figs. 2(a)–2(c)) and the fractured surfaces (Figs. 2(f)–2(h)) (with mixed inter- and intragranular fracture modes). We compared them with two reference Ce-TZP samples sintered from additive manufactured green bodies under the same printing parameters reported in our previous work [26]: NS-HIP presintered at 1300 °C for 2 h and then HIPed at 1250 °C for 1 h at 200 MPa in an argon atmosphere, and NS was pressurelessly sintered at 1450 °C for 2 h. NS-HIP showed the smallest *G*avg of 570 nm reported in Refs. [3,27–31] (the *G*avg value was recounted using the standard lineal intercept method). Here, TP–HIP-B, TP–HIP-D, and TP–HIP-F have *G*avg = 480, 420, and 540 nm (Table 3), respectively, all of which are smaller than those of NS-HIP (Figs. 2(d) and 2(i)), not to mention NSs with *G*avg = 1650 nm (Figs. 2(e) and 2(j)). Therefore, the combination of TP and low-temperature HIP (referred to hereafter as TP–HIP) can successfully refine the grain size of dense Ce-TZP ceramics.

It is interesting to track the ρ*–*G*avg trajectories during the sintering process. As shown in Fig. 3, the open circles are the data for normal sintering with 1 min holding time, and the curve follows a concave shape with accelerated grain growth in the final sintering stage. For TP–HIP-B and TP–HIP-D, the TP–HIP technique effectively linearizes the *ρ*–*G*avg trajectory, which is consistent with the refined grain size in the fully densified samples. For TP–HIP-F at *T*2 = 1250 °C for 10 h and *T*HIP = 1250 °C for 1 h, the grain growth in the HIP step is completely suppressed. This is remarkable considering the very fast final-stage grain growth kinetics commonly known for Ce-TZP. Before HIP, the TP-F samples had higher *ρ* and *G*avg compared to the TP-B and TP-D samples, which indicates that the TP-F samples had a smaller surface area of pores and a denser grain boundary network. Under the same HIP conditions, this probably leads to greater grain boundary diffusion (leading to densification) and suppressed grain boundary mobility (leading to grain growth) for TP–HIP-F samples [25].

The mechanical properties of the TP–HIP samples are listed in Table 3 and compared with those of the NS-HIP and NS samples. As the grain size decreases, both TP–HIP-B and TP–HIP-F exhibit remarkable flexural strength (σ*flex) values of approximately 830 MPa, which are 7.8% and 60% greater than those of the NS-HIP and NS samples, respectively. TP–HIP-D has slightly lower *σ*flex of approximately 790 MPa, which is still higher than those of NS-HIP and NS. For the SENB fracture toughness (*K*IC), TP–HIP-F has the highest value of 12.4 MPa·m1/2, and all the HIPed samples are in the range of 11–12 MPa·m1/2, which is higher than that of 9.3 MPa·m1/2 for the NS sample. For the Vickers hardness (*H*), TP–HIP-D has the highest value of 10.8 GPa, apparently due to its finer grain size. Interestingly, among the three evaluated mechanical properties, only *H* shows a Hall‒Petch-type dependence on the grain size. This is easy to understand because hardness is a surface property sensitive to grain size, and the test did not involve crack propagation in our case (no cracks appeared at the indentation tip; see Fig. S2 in the ESM). With respect to strength and toughness, despite having the smallest *G*avg, TP–HIP-D has lower *σ*flex and *K*IC compared to TP–HIP-B and TP–HIP-F, and despite having larger *G*avg compared to TP–HIP-B and TP–HIP-D, TP–HIP-F has the highest *σ*flex and *K*IC. Compared to the NS-HIP sample, as *G*avg is refined from 570 to 420 nm, the strength and toughness of the TP–HIP samples both experience a promotion followed by a slight decrease, as illustrated in Fig. S3 in the ESM. Below, we show that it is related to the t–m phase stability and the phase transformability during the fracture event.

## 5 Phase stability and overstabilized Ce-TZP

After HIP, the Ce-TZP exhibited a dark brown color, which contrasted with its light yellow color after pressureless sintering in air. Photographs of TP–HIP-B, TP–HIP-D, and TP–HIP-F are shown in Fig. 4 and were compared with those of the NSs. The color change of Ce-TZP is usually attributed to the reduction of Ce4+ to Ce3+ under reducing conditions during HIP [39,40]. To confirm the valence change in the TP–HIPed samples, we conducted XPS measurements and fitted the data. Figures 5(a)–5(c) show the Ce 3d spectra for TP–HIP-B, TP–HIP-D, and TP–HIP-F, respectively. The subbands labeled v0, v'', and v''' can be attributed to Ce(IV) 3d5/2. The subbands labeled as u0, u'', and u''' can be attributed to Ce(IV) 3d3/2. The subbands labeled v0 and v' can be attributed to Ce(III) 3d5/2. The subbands labeled as u0 and u' can be attributed to Ce(III) 3d3/2 [41]. Therefore, Ce has mixed +3 and +4 valences in all three samples, and the Ce(III) peaks appear to be stronger than those observed in previous research on reduced Ce-TZP [20,42]. For phase analysis, we first performed XRD measurements on the as-sintered surfaces of the sample bars directly after HIP. Figure 6 shows the XRD patterns of TP–HIP-B, TP–HIP-D, and TP–HIP-F. The main phase can be attributed to the tetragonal zirconia phase (peaks marked with diamonds), with no observable monoclinic zirconia phase. Some minor impurity peaks can be attributed to the cubic Ce-rich phase Ce0.75Zr0.25O2 (peaks marked with squares). A Ce-rich phase is known to form when Ce-TZP is subjected to high-temperature heat treatments under reducing conditions, such as during hot pressing and HIP. Moreover, the Ce-depleted matrix easily transforms into the monoclinic phase, leading to an undesirable decrease in the mechanical properties [20−23,39]. This is indeed the reason why previous hot pressing and HIP studies cannot produce high-performance Ce-TZP ceramics with full density. In our case, because of the unique combination of additive manufacturing (which lowers ρ*thold) and TP as well as the fine microstructure after the pressureless presintering step, the HIP temperature decreases to 1250 °C. At such a temperature, cation diffusion—despite being along grain boundaries and enhanced to some extent by cation reduction—is sluggish enough to kinetically suppress the long-range Ce/Zr partition and the precipitation of Ce0.75Zr0.25O2 [44−46]. As further support, the signal of Ce0.75Zr0.25O2 is quite weak in all three TP–HIP samples due to the low temperatures in both the TP step and HIP final sintering step. This is the key to HIP-processed ultrafine-grained Ce-TZP ceramics.

We next conducted Raman measurements for local phase analysis to investigate the t–m transformation of the TP–HIP samples before and after fracture. Figure 7(a) shows the confocal Raman spectra of the as-sintered surfaces of TP–HIP-B, TP–HIP-D, and TP–HIP-F. All the peaks can be attributed to the tetragonal phase (marked with diamonds), and no monoclinic phase can be observed. Figure 7(b) shows the Raman spectra of the fracture surfaces of TP–HIP-B, TP–HIP-D, and TP–HIP-F after the bending test. While the main peaks can still be attributed to the tetragonal phase (marked with diamonds), there is a detectable monoclinic phase (peaks marked with circles) due to the stress-driven t–m phase transformation during fracture. As listed in Table 3, *f*m follows the rank of TP–HIP-D m shows a significant dependence on *G*avg of the samples. The smaller the grains are, the less the t–m transformation occurs during fracture (as plotted in Fig. S3 in the ESM). It is evident that reducing the grain size of Ce-TZP from the 1–2 μm level (NS samples) to the ~500 nm level (NS-HIP and TP–HIP-F) significantly enhances the mechanical properties due to the Hall‒Petch effect and the elimination of flaws. However, when the grain size is further reduced below 500 nm (TP–HIP-B and TP–HIP-D), competition arises between the weakened t‒m transformation toughening and the strengthening effect of the grain size refinement. The critical grain size for the t‒m phase transition of the reduced Ce-TZP is estimated to be approximately 400 nm. This explains why the mechanical properties tend to increase and then level off with the continuous refinement of *G*avg, as illustrated in Fig. S3 in the ESM.

On the other hand, for transformation toughening, such small f*m (below 10%) for Ce-TZP is not desirable, so the tetragonal phase in TP–HIP-B and TP–HIP-D can be considered “overstabilized”. Even for TP–HIP-F with high *σ*flex = 831 MPa and *K*IC = 12.4 MPa·m1/2, the *f*m is only 7.2%, which is much smaller than that of > 30% in conventional air-sintered Ce-TZP ceramics reported in Refs. [26,47,48]. For the latter, the high *f*m is attributed to high transformability because oversized Ce4+ (Shannon radius of 0.97 vs. 0.84 Å for Zr4+, both with 8 coordination sites) is the only stabilizing mechanism for tetragonal zirconia [49]. This leads to lower critical stress (*σ*ct–m) that drives the martensitic tetragonal–monoclinic phase transition than the critical stress (*σ*R) set by the largest flaw size, and yield-like mechanical failure occurs during brittle fracture (this increases the flaw tolerance in Ce-TZP but decreases the strength) [2,3,50]. Therefore, one may argue that the “conventional” Ce-TZP is “understabilized”. It is thus significant to achieve the other limiting case of overstabilization in the present work, as it offers ample opportunities to adjust between under- and overstabilized tetragonal phases and obtain optimal and desirable combinations of strength and toughness. This is the key insight and should be explored in the future.

Overstabilization is enabled by several factors. First, the martensitic t–m phase transformation in tetragonal zirconia is a nucleation-controlled process; thus, a refined grain size helps to reduce the single-grain volume and lower the probability of nucleation of the monoclinic phase during fracture [15,51,52]. Second, the reduction of Ce4+ to Ce3+ generates oxygen vacancies that stabilize the tetragonal phase. Using the Kroger–Vink notation, the defect reaction can be written as Eq. (3) [53,54]:

(3) 2CeZr×+OO×→2CeZr′+VO⋅⋅+12O2(g)

Third, the reduction of Ce4+ to Ce3+ further increases the cation size. Ce3+ has a Shannon radius of 1.143 Å, compared with 0.97 Å for Ce4+ and 0.84 Å for Zr4+ (all with 8 coordination sites). Such a large cation radius is likely to form first-nearest-neighbor Ce3+–oxygen vacancy pairs, deplete the amount of oxygen vacancies in the first-nearest-neighbor shell of Zr4+, and stabilize the tetragonal phase. Last, the suppressed precipitation of the Ce0.75Zr0.25O2−*δ* phase preserves the Ce content in the matrix, thus best utilizing the stabilization effects mentioned above.

Our results on the “overstabilized” Ce-TZP ceramics demonstrate the possibility of fine tuning the processing conditions to balance the competing factors underlying the t–m phase stability while realizing grain size refinement. To achieve better performance of the refined Ce-TZP in the future, more work should be done in the following aspects. First, we further explored the HIP parameters, including oxidation posttreatment to the TP–HIP procedure, to regulate the Ce4+/Ce3+ ratio in the reduced Ce-TZP. Second, the two-step sintering process of Ce-TZP was optimized to achieve densification to full density and zero grain growth at the second sintering step. This would eliminate the need for expensive HIP treatment or at least help to lower HIP temperature and shorten HIP time. Third, the additive manufacturing process and the debinding procedure were fine-tuned to obtain green bodies with more uniform particle packing and fewer defects. This would help enhance the densification efficiency in both pressureless presintering and HIP processes. A further decrease in the HIP temperature would suppress the amount of reduction-generated Ce3+ and oxygen vacancies, thereby achieving the optimal tetragonal phase stability. In conclusion, with the adoption of the above solutions, ultrafine-grained Ce-TZP ceramics with desirable mechanical properties, reliability, and stability against aging can hopefully be obtained.

## 6 Conclusions

Additive manufacturing, two-step pre-sintering, and HIP have been successfully coupled to produce ultrafine-grained Ce-TZP ceramics with *G*avg values down to 420 nm. The accelerated grain growth in the final sintering stage of Ce-TZP was effectively suppressed by the TP–HIP strategy. Compared to those of the normal sintered Ce-TZP ceramics, the mechanical properties of the TP–HIP Ce-TZP ceramics achieved comprehensive promotion, reaching a three-point bending strength of approximately 830 MPa, an SENB fracture toughness of 12.4 MPa·m1/2 and a hardness of 10.5 GPa. With the continuous refinement of the grain size below 500 nm, the mechanical properties of the TP–HIP samples initially increased and then stabilized due to the competition between the grain size and the suppressed t–m transformation. The tetragonal phase is demonstrated to be overstabilized because of Ce4+ reduction to Ce3+, the reduction of generated oxygen vacancies, and the refined grain size. These results suggest that the stability of the t–m phase of Ce-TZP can be adjusted by balancing these competing factors through proper processing conditions. Based on current findings on sintering protocols, the microstructure and composition/valence optimization of Ce-TZP ceramics require a more comprehensive strategy to fully utilize the potential of fine grains for optimized mechanical properties. The proposed TP–HIP schedule may also be useful for other materials and applications in the future, especially for additively manufactured parts with flexible structural designs.
