## 1 Introduction

Thermal protection materials are indispensable for critical applications such as hypersonic vehicles and nuclear fusion reactors [1]. As propulsion technologies continuously advance and the fusion power output increases, the extreme temperatures encountered by these systems become increasingly demanding [2]. Catastrophic failure occurs in currently available thermal protection systems when the ambient temperature exceeds 3000 °C, surpassing the melting points of even the highest-melting nonradioactive oxides (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) [3−5]. The development of novel oxide ceramics with melting points greater than 3000 °C has therefore become both a scientific and engineering priority [6]. However, the principles dictating oxide melting-point variations and their governing mechanisms remain poorly understood.

An analysis of established binary transition-metal oxide systems revealed that symmetry, structural type, valence electron concentration (VEC), coordination number (CN), cationic radius, and *d*-orbital characteristics are strongly correlated with melting temperature, as statistically validated in Fig. 1. High-melting-point oxides feature high-symmetry, cubic fluorite- or bixbyite-type crystal structures, a valence electron concentration of ca. 2.4–2.7, CN = 8, a small cation radius, and ionic‒covalent bonding formed by the overlap of transition metal *d* orbitals and O 2*p* orbitals. For high-melting materials, a small increase in the VEC generally indicates a strengthening of the chemical bonding and thus an increase in the melting temperature [7−9]. However, an excessive increase in VEC may weaken chemical bonding and reduce melting temperatures [10−12]. When VEC and CN are comparable, oxides with smaller cation radii tend to have shorter metal‒oxygen bond lengths, thereby increasing the bonding strength [13]. As a result, HfO2, which has a smaller cation radius than ZrO2, has a higher melting temperature. Moreover, cations with 5*d* orbitals such as Hf4+ and Ta5+ display relativistic effects, such as 6*s* orbital contraction and 5*d* orbital expansion [14]. These effects promote greater overlap between the *d* orbitals of the transition metal cations and the 2*p* orbitals of oxygen ions, leading to stronger covalent interactions and higher melting points than those of oxides containing cations with 4*d* or 3*d* orbital characteristics. In conclusion, HfO2 satisfies nearly all structural criteria, standing as the highest-melting oxide other than radioactive ThO2. However, a persistent temperature gap of approximately 200 °C still separates HfO2 from the 3000 °C threshold. Overcoming this limitation presents a significant scientific challenge.

The melting behavior of solid solutions is distinct from that of their parent compounds, providing critical opportunities for increasing the melting point of oxides [11]. However, conventional phase diagrams universally show that the melting points of solid solutions are lower than those of their parent components [15]. This limitation primarily stems from bond weakening induced by lattice distortion and symmetry reduction [16]. Low molar fraction doping may preserve structural symmetry but still typically fails to increase the melting point. For example, incorporating Y2O3 or Sc2O3 into ZrO2 maintains CNs and structural symmetry, yet the reduced VEC suppresses melting point elevation [17,18]. In stark contrast, minor additions of high-valence cations (e.g., Nb5+ in TiC) or anions (e.g., N3- in TiC) enhance VEC without compromising symmetry, ultimately increasing melting points [19,20]. The aforementioned phenomena provide a fruitful avenue for the quest for novel oxides with melting temperatures above 3000 °C. Therefore, we engineer solid solutions that preserve crystal symmetry through minimal doping while strategically enhancing VEC to breach the 3000 °C barrier in oxides. In line with what is known about the solute effects on the melting temperatures of oxides, ZrO2 and HfO2, with intrinsically high melting points closer to the target melting point of 3000 °C, were selected as parent oxides. Among the dopant candidates, Ta uniquely combines a minimal ionic radius, one more valence electron than Zr or Hf, and expends 5d* orbitals favorable for covalent bonding caused by relativistic effects. Accordingly, our preliminary investigation prompted a focused examination of the melting behavior of Zr–Ta–O (ZT) and Hf–Ta–O (HT) solid solutions.

### 2.1 Theoretical calculation of melting points

First-principles calculations were carried out within the framework of density functional theory (DFT) via the CASTEP code [21]. The interaction between the ionic nuclei and valence electrons was evaluated via the supersoft pseudopotential (USPP) method [22]. The exchange‒correlation function was described via the generalized gradient approximation of Perdew *et al.* (GGA-PBE) [23−25]. To ensure the convergence of the total energy of the ground state, the plane wave expansion cutoff energy was set at 630 eV. Brillouin zone sampling was performed via the Monkhorst–Pack method [26], and a separation of 0.04 1/Å was selected for the *k*-point grid. The Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) method was employed for geometric optimization [27]. The geometry optimization converged when the system satisfied the convergence criteria: the energy change within 5.0×10−6 eV/atom, the maximum ionic Hellmann–Feynman force on the atom within 0.01 eV/Å, the maximum stress within 0.02 GPa, and the maximum ionic displacement between cycles within 5.0×10−4 Å. Geometric optimization was first performed on cubic ZrO2 and HfO2 with *Fm** 3¯m* symmetry, yielding lattice constants (Table S1 in the ESM) with deviations 2 and HfO2 primitive cells, where gradient Ta5+ doping (3.125–12.500 mol%) was achieved via site-selective substitution (Fig. S2 in the ESM) [28]. After structural relaxation, all the doped systems exhibited lattice expansion rates constrained within 1% (Table S2 in the ESM). The electronic structure and elastic properties are calculated on the basis of the optimized structure. The elastic constants were calculated via linear fitting on the basis of the stress‒strain relationship following Hooke's law after structural optimization [29]. First, the elastic constants of cubic ZrO2 and HfO2 were calculated (Table S3 in the ESM), and the results were in good agreement with the literature values, thereby validating the computational parameters and methodology. The elastic constants of the optimized supercell structures with Ta5+ dopants were subsequently computed, and the results for various doping concentrations are listed in Table S4 in the ESM. The mechanical stability of the constructed supercells was evaluated via the Born–Huang lattice dynamics criterion [30]. ZrO2, HfO2 and the solid solution series all satisfy these criteria, confirming their mechanical stability in ground states. The polycrystalline elastic moduli were estimated via the Voigt–Reuss–Hill (VRH) approximation [31]. The Young's modulus (*E*) and Poisson’s ratio (*ν*) were derived from the bulk and shear moduli. The melting temperatures were calculated via Eq. (1):

(1) Tm=607+9.3B

where *T*m is the melting temperature, and *B* is the bulk modulus. The computed bulk modulus, Young’s modulus, and related data are summarized in Table S5 in the ESM.

### 2.2 Material preparation

Chemical composition gradients were designed by adjusting the Ta2O5 doping content in ZrO2, and the corresponding samples were labeled ZT1 to ZT12 ((Zr1–*x*Ta*x*)O2 series, *x* = 0.01–0.12). The raw powders were weighed according to predetermined stoichiometric ratios and then mixed with zirconia milling balls (ball-to-powder mass ratio of 1.5 : 1) and anhydrous ethanol (liquid-to-solid mass ratio of 1.2 : 1). The mixture was ball-milled for 12 h at 300 r/min via a planetary mill (DM-2 L, Nanjing Daren Technology Co., Ltd., China) to obtain homogeneous slurries. After drying at 60 °C for 12 h in a constant-temperature oven (101-2B, Shaoxing Yicheng Instrument Manufacturing Co., Ltd., China), the powders were pressed into green compacts under 100 MPa uniaxial pressure via a 30 mm die on a tablet press (769YP-15A, Tianjin Keqi High-tech Co., Ltd., China). Sintering was conducted in a muffle furnace (KSL1800X, Zhengzhou Kejing Electric Furnace Co., Ltd., China) with the following protocol: heating to 1700 °C at 5 °C/min, holding for 8 h, and then cooling to room temperature at 2 °C/min. On the other hand, (HfTa)O2 solid solutions (HT series, (Hf1–*x*Ta*x*)O2 series, *x* = 0.03–0.15)) were simultaneously prepared. The HT series samples with *x* = 0.03 and *x* = 0.15 were labeled HT3 to HT15, respectively.

### 2.3 Characterizations and measurement

The volumetric density of the ZT and HT series bulk samples was determined via Archimedes' principle via a density analyzer (DA-300R, Guangdong Hongtuo Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., China). The relative density was calculated from the theoretical densities derived from the XRD-determined lattice parameters. The measured relative densities were 94.4% for ZrO2, 97.4% for ZT3, 96.7% for ZT6, 97.1% for ZT9, and 99.9% for ZT12. Phase analysis of the ZT and HT series was performed via an X-ray diffractometer (TD-3500, Tongda Technology, China) with Cu Kα radiation and a scanning rate of 1 (°)/min (step size of 0.02°). Diffraction patterns were calibrated via Jade 6 software, followed by Rietveld refinement via the Reflex code in Materials Studio for phase quantification. For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis, TEM lamellae of the selected ZT12 sample were prepared via a focused ion beam (FIB; Auriga 950, Zeiss, Germany) and oriented with a Gatan double-tilt holder. Atomic-resolution high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) was conducted via spherical aberration-corrected TEM (Spectra 200, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 200 kV. The melting behavior was characterized via an integrated laser heating system equipped with four 300 W fiber-coupled semiconductor lasers (1070±10 nm wavelength, 5 mm beam diameter in concentric ring array) for uniform radiative heating combined with a pyrometer (600–3300 °C range, ImpacIGAR 12-LO, IMPAC Infrared GmbH, Germany) for temperature monitoring. Phase transition dynamics were recorded by a CMOS high-speed camera (1920 × 1200 pixels, 200 fps, acA1300-75gm, Basler, Germany) integrated with an 850 nm narrow-bandpass optical filter. The temperature rise curve of the sample is shown in Fig. S11 in the ESM. Nanoindentation tests were performed via a nanoindenter (Agilent G200, Keysight Technologies, USA) in quasistatic loading mode. Five randomly selected test points were measured per sample to obtain load‒displacement curves. The Young's modulus and hardness were derived from the experimental data through analysis based on the classical Oliver‒Pharr (O‒P) model [32]. Diffuse reflectance spectroscopy was performed via a UV‒Vis spectrophotometer (Cary 7000, Agilent Technologies, USA). The optical bandgap (*E*g) was calculated via the Tauc relation (Eq. (2)) [33]:

(2) αhν=A(hν−Eg)n

where *hν* is the photon energy, *α* is the absorption coefficient (*α* = 4π*k*/*λ*), *E*g is the bandgap energy (eV), *A* is a constant, and *n* depends on the nature of the electronic transition. The resistivity of the ZT-series samples was measured by a physical property measurement system (PPMS-9, Quantum Design, USA).

## 3 Results and discussion

Initially, the Zr–Ta–O (ZT) system was studied. First-principles calculations can provide theoretical guidance for optimizing doping concentrations [34]. The calculated results indicate that Ta5+ doping enhances the bulk modulus (*B*) of cubic ZrO2, in contrast to the negative effects observed with alternative cation dopants (Fig. S3 and Table S5 in the ESM). The bulk modulus is a direct indicator of the chemical bonding strength and is positively correlated with the melting point (Fig. S4 in the ESM). Using the empirical relationship in Eq. (1), we find that the melting temperature of ZrO2 increases to 2800 °C for ZT12 i.e., (Zr1−*x*Ta*x*)O2 with *x* = 0.12 (Fig. S5 in the ESM). The focus of subsequent research was the experimental realization of Ta5+ solid solubility in ZrO2. Therefore, Ta2O5–doped ZrO2 materials ((Zr1–*x*Ta*x*)O2, *x* = 0.01–0.12) were prepared via high-temperature solid-state synthesis. XRD analyses demonstrate that the monoclinic phase (*m*-ZrO2) is dominant in solid solutions doped for *x* 5+ (0.64 Å) substitution for Zr4+ (0.72 Å) (Figs. 2(a), Fig. 2(c) and Fig. S6 in the ESM) [35]. As the doping content increases, orthorhombic (*o*-ZrO2), tetragonal (*t*-ZrO2), and cubic (*c*-ZrO2) phases become predominant with concomitant *m*-ZrO2 depletion. Elevated Ta5+ doping concentrations effectively suppress ZrO2 phase transformations [36]. The increase in symmetry and superior incompressibility inherent to the *o*-ZrO2 and *c*-ZrO2 phases can enhance the stability of the solid solution, as demonstrated by complete crack elimination in the ZT9 sample (Fig. S7 in the ESM) [37−39]. In addition, UV‒vis spectroscopy (Fig. S8 in the ESM) and electrical conductivity measurements (Fig. S9 in the ESM) confirmed the increase in the Fermi level and narrowing of the bandgap upon Ta5+ doping [40]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of the ZT12 sample verified the cooccupancy of Zr4+ and Ta5+ at the cationic sites. Collectively, these results demonstrate the successful incorporation of Ta5+ into the ZrO2 lattice. We then measured the elastic modulus of the ZT solid solutions. Concomitant with bond length (reflected by lattice constant) contraction, the elastic modulus gradually increases with increasing dopant concentration, reaching a maximum value of 227 GPa for the ZT12 sample (Fig. S10 in the ESM).

A similar approach was subsequently applied to investigate the HT system. First-principles calculations revealed that HT15 exhibited the maximum bulk modulus (Fig. S6 in the ESM). XRD patterns of the HT series samples provide evidence that solid solutions of (Hf1–x*Ta*x*)O2 can also be achieved via a high-temperature solid-state synthesis method (Fig. 2(b)). Bulk modulus measurements of the HT system also confirmed that HT15 presented the highest value, in agreement with the computational results (Fig. S10 in the ESM). Since chemical bonding can be reflected by the elastic or bulk modulus, which can indirectly reflect the melting point, we anticipate that ZT12 and HT15 hold significant potential for achieving substantial enhancements in the melting point. We subsequently directly measured the melting points of ZT12 and pure ZrO2 via the laser heating method described in Section 2.3 (Fig. 3). The melting point is defined as the temperature at which samples undergo large volumetric collapse and complete melting. The undoped ZrO2 sample underwent volumetric collapse at 2632 °C, which is consistent with the reported values (2677–2717 °C) [41−45], verifying the accuracy of our measurement method. The ZT12 sample completely melted at 2885 °C, surpassing ZrO2 by 253 °C (Table S6 in the ESM). This very large increase is unexpected and is seldom found in solid solutions of oxide ceramics. The measured melting temperature of HfO2 was determined to be 2866 °C, which is in close agreement with the literature-reported range of 2800–2900 °C [46]. Notably, HT15 demonstrates a melting temperature increase to 3006 °C. This achievement establishes HT15 as the first experimentally verified nonradioactive oxide ceramic whose melting point exceeds the 3000 °C threshold and lays the groundwork for the design paradigm of high-melting-temperature solid solutions.

The experimental results confirm that the minor incorporation of high-valence Ta with minimal ionic radii yields oxide ZrO2 and HfO2 ceramics with significantly elevated melting points. The calculated bulk moduli of various Zr-based solid solution oxides are summarized in Fig. 4(a). The data points corresponding to the highest bulk modulus values were selected from each doping concentration (x* = 0.03, 0.06, 0.09, and 0.12). While most dopants degrade the bulk modulus of ZrO2, Ta5+ doping substantially enhances the bulk modulus, validating the strategic selection of Ta5+ as the optimal dopant in this study. Notably, *d*-orbital characteristics may serve as critical determinants of melting behavior. The charge density difference map of the ZT12 sample derived from first-principles calculations also demonstrated that the elevated valence electron concentration in Zr1−*x*Ta*x*O2 promoted enhanced overlap of the Zr/Ta *d*-O *p* orbitals, thereby strengthening the chemical bonds via orbital hybridization (Fig. 4(b)). A schematic diagram of the orbitals and chemical bonding characteristics of Zr–Ta–O is shown in Fig. 4(c). ZrO2 stabilizes in an eight-coordinate configuration where bonding occurs via Zr 4*dt*2*g*–O 2*p* orbital interactions. In contrast, the primary bonding of Ta2O5 with six coordination numbers arises from Ta 5*de**g*–O 2*p* orbitals. Low-concentration Ta5+ doping in ZrO2 induces multiaxial bonding along * dx2−y2,  dz2, d**yz*, *d**xz*, and *d**xy* orbital directions (Fig. S12 in the ESM). The higher-dimensional symmetry endows the strong bonding in the cubic unit (CN = 8) with enhanced structural stability. In brief, by preserving high structural symmetry and simultaneously achieving bond shortening and strengthening through doping with cations that have small ionic radii and high valence electron concentrations, the novel solid solutions exhibit a significant increase in the melting point. The validity of this mechanism is also confirmed in the Hf–Ta–O system. The coordinated tuning of structural descriptors through ionic doping in solid solutions offers a promising pathway to develop oxides with further enhanced melting points. The significance of these results cannot be overestimated. On the one hand, although elevated melting temperatures have been achieved in carbides through both cation and anion doping strategies [47,48], the 3000 °C melting temperature barrier for oxides has never been broken before. Since the upmost service temperature of ultrahigh-temperature ceramics (UHTCs, mainly carbide and boride-based ceramics) and their composites, relies on the temperature capability of the oxide scales, this pioneering work opens a new avenue for the composition design of UHTCs and their composites with higher temperature capabilities. On the other hand, unlike traditional strategies to stabilize ZrO2 and HfO2, we have provided a new pathway to stabilize and control the phase transformations of these oxide ceramics through high-valence element doping.

## 4 Conclusions

We pioneer the design of novel Ta-doped HfO2 solid solutions in this work, and an unprecedented increase in the melting point has been achieved, surpassing the 3000 °C threshold. Most importantly, a universal design paradigm for the development of ultrahigh-melting-temperature materials is established. This foundation ushers in a new era of oxide ceramics capable of withstanding even higher temperatures, enabling transformative advances in thermal protection systems for applications in hypersonic vehicles and fusion reactors.
