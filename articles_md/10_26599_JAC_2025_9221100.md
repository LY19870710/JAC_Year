## 1 Introduction

Gas turbine aero engines employ the Brayton cycle in their operation. A critical parameter for high thermal efficiency is the turbine flow path temperature, which depends on the high-temperature capability of structural materials. Refractory metals and their alloys are considered candidate materials for hot-section components because of their high melting point and high-temperature strength. Nevertheless, poor oxidation resistance, which can be attributed to the rapid growth of nonprotective or volatile oxides upon oxidation, has been the bottleneck hindering their practical application in engine environments for decades [1,2]. Generally, the dense and protective scales formed on metals and ceramics, which can prevent metals and ceramics from further oxidation, are Al2O3, Cr2O3, or SiO2. These protective scales, however, are seldom found upon the oxidation of refractory metals and their alloys [3−5]. Recent breakthroughs in refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) have demonstrated that although traditional protective scales such as Al2O3, Cr2O3, or SiO2 have difficulty forming upon the oxidation of RHEAs, the formation of rutile-structured complex oxides such as CrNbO4 and CrTaO4 has been found to protect RHEAs from further oxidation [6,7]. These results are appealing since they suggest that in addition to the well-established oxidation protective scales Al2O3, Cr2O3, or SiO2, there are some alternative oxide scales that can prevent oxidation of metals and ceramics. The above hypothesis was supported by the following facts. Using the kinetic data of the equiatomic alloy TaMoCrTiAl at 1000 °C, Gorr *et al*. [8] reported that the oxidation kinetics of CrTaO4 are quite close to those of Al2O3 and Cr2O3. Butler *et al*. [9] reported slow oxidation kinetics of the NbTiZrCr alloy at 1200 °C, which did not rely on the presence of traditional protective external oxide layers such as Al2O3, Cr2O3, or SiO2. Instead, the oxide layer contained a mixture of CrNbO4 and ZrO2, which protected the NbTiZrCr alloy from oxidation. It has come to light that ZrO2 does not have sufficient resistance to the ingress of oxygen and is dispersed throughout the oxide scale. Therefore, CrNbO4 is considered to play a key role in the slow oxidation of the NbTiZrCr alloy.

Notably, RHEAs not only have a high yield strength at the GPa level but also a high yield strength of 300–600 MPa at temperatures above 1200 °C [10]. Most profoundly, by replacing refractory Ta and W with lighter elements such as Cr, Al, and Ti, the densities of RHEAs can be adjusted to as low as 6.34–6.57 g·cm−3, which are comparable to those of Ti alloys [11]. The combination of high-temperature strength with low density indicates that weight reduction and high-temperature capability of engine components can be simultaneously achieved by using low-density Cr–Nb-containing RHEAs, which can lead to improved engine efficiency and less fuel burn. Furthermore, the density of CrNbO4 is also lower than that of CrTaO4, providing additional benefit to Cr–Nb-containing RHEAs over Cr–Ta-containing RHEAs because of the light weight of both the RHEAs and the protective oxide scale. These combinations of fascinating properties of Cr–Nb-containing RHEAs inspired us to further investigate the structure and properties of CrNbO4.

To understand the mechanism underlying the oxidation resistance of Cr–Ta-containing RHEAs, we systematically studied CrTaO4, focusing on its microstructure, elastic properties, mechanical properties, and thermal properties, in our previous work [12]. We not only elucidated the reasons for its exceptional oxidation resistance but also provided clear guidance for its potential applications. CrTaO4 has a low thermal conductivity, measuring only 1.31 W∙m−1∙K−1 at room temperature. Its thermal expansion coefficient (TEC) is (6.39±0.11)×10−6 K−1, which is very close to those of refractory metals and RHEAs. Furthermore, this material exhibits outstanding resistance to CaO–MgO–Al2O3–SiO2 (CMAS) corrosion [13], making it suitable as a novel thermal barrier material for Cr–Ta-containing RHEAs. However, to date, little is known about rutile-structured CrNbO4, although its role in the protection of Cr–Nb-containing RHEAs has been reported.

To fill this research gap, powders and bulk CrNbO4 were prepared, and the microstructure, elastic/mechanical, and thermal properties were systematically investigated in this work. The aims of this study are as follows: first, to gain insight into the structure‒property relationship of the rutile-structured complex oxide CrNbO4; second, to understand the role of CrNbO4 in the protection of RHEAs from oxidation; third, to find the high-temperature limit for the application of this new protective scale; and fourth, to provide fundamental property data that are instructive to future material design.

### 2.1 Preparation of CrNbO<sub>4</sub> powders and dense bulk

Chromic oxide (Cr2O3; 99% purity; Huawei Ruike Chemical Co., Ltd., China) and niobium pentoxide (Nb2O5; 99% purity; Huawei Ruike Chemical Co., Ltd., China) were utilized as the starting materials for synthesizing the CrNbO4 powders. In our previous work [12], we reported that Cr2O3 with a mass fraction of approximately 0.358% volatilizes at approximately 900 °C. To compensate for the mass loss of Cr2O3 during the heating process, we adjusted the molar ratio to Cr2O3 : Nb2O5 = 1.01 : 1.00. Cr2O3 and Nb2O5 powders were mixed in anhydrous ethanol at a molar ratio of 1.01 : 1.00, with ZrO2 balls serving as the grinding medium during ball milling. Notably, the mass ratio among the mixed powders, ZrO2 balls, and ethanol was maintained at 1 : 2 : 1. The mixture was agitated at 120–150 r·min−1 for 8 h, resulting in a uniformly dispersed slurry. This slurry was subsequently dried in an oven at a temperature of 80 °C for 12 h. Following drying, the mixed powders were sieved through a 400-mesh screen to obtain the final mixed powders. High-temperature synthesis was performed by heating the reactants to 1150 °C at a rate of 5 °C·min−1 and allowing them to react for 2 h according to Reaction (1).

(1) Cr2O3+Nb2O5→2CrNbO4

Phase identification was conducted via a powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Rigaku Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5406 Å) with a step size of 0.02° at a scanning rate of 1 (°)·min−1, and the microstructure and particle size distribution were analyzed via a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-7001F, JEOL, Japan). In addition, the crystal structure of CrNbO4 was refined via the Rietveld method (Reflex code, Materials Studio program, Accelrys Inc., USA).

Bulk CrNbO4 was prepared via the hot-press sintering method (ZT-50-22Y, Shanghai Chenhua Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China). After monitoring the temperature-dependent displacement curve during hot pressing, the following processing parameters were optimized for preparing bulk CrNbO4. The CrNbO4 powders were placed in a graphite mold of 50 mm in diameter and then heated to 1400 °C at a rate of 10 °C·min−1. The dwell time was 40 min, and the applied pressure was 30 MPa. After cooling to room temperature, surface contaminants were removed via a grinding mill. The final density of the bulk CrNbO4 was measured via the Archimedes method. The phase composition of the bulk CrNbO4 was analyzed via an XRD (Rigaku Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan). The microstructure and grain size distribution of the bulk CrNbO4 were observed via a SEM (JSM-7001F, JEOL, Japan). For in-depth and detailed microstructure analysis, atomic-resolution high-annular dark field (HAADF) and annular bright field (ABF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) analyses were carried out with an aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope (Spectra 300, ThermoFisher, USA).

### 2.2 Elastic/mechanical properties of bulk CrNbO<sub>4</sub>

The Young’s modulus (*E*) and shear modulus (*G*) were tested via the impulse excited resonance method (resonance frequency and damping analyzer (RFDA; RFDA system-24, IMCE, Belgium) via a rectangular bar with a size of 3 mm × 15 mm × 40 mm. The Young’s modulus and shear modulus were calculated from the flexural resonance frequency and the torsional resonance frequency, respectively, according to ASTM E1876-97 (Eqs. (2) and (3)) [14]:

(2) E=0.9465(mtfF2w)(L3δ3)C1

(3) G=4LmtfT2wδ(X1+Y)

where *E* and *G* represent the Young’s modulus and shear modulus in Pa, respectively; *m*t is the mass of the rectangular bar in kg; *f*F and *f*T are the fundamental flexural resonant frequency and torsional resonate frequency in Hz, respectively; *w*, *L*, and *δ* are the width, the length, and the thickness of the rectangular bar in m, respectively; *C*1 is a correction factor that depends on the thickness/length ratio (*δ*/*L*) and Poisson’s ratio; and *X* and *Y* are two correction factors that are dependent on the width/thickness ratio (*w*/*δ*). The bulk modulus (*B*) and Poisson’s ratio (*v*) were calculated from the Young’s modulus and shear modulus according to Eqs. (4) and (5) [15]:

(4) B=EG3(3G−E)

(5) v=E2G−1

The Vickers hardness (*H*v) was measured via a microhardness tester (HXD-1000TMC/LCD, Shanghai Taiming, China) at loads of 0.98, 1.96, 2.94, 4.90, and 9.80 N, with a dwell time of 15 s. The morphology of the indentation was observed via a SEM (JSM-7001F, JEOL, Japan). The *H*V was calculated according to Eq. (6) [16]:

(6) Hv=PS=2Psin(θ2)d2=1854.4Pd2

where *P* is the load, *S* is the surface area of the indentation, *θ* is the angle between the opposite faces of the indenter, and *d* is the diagonal length of the indentation.

The flexural strength (*σ*f) was measured via the three-point bending method with a span of 30 mm and a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm·min–1. The samples were cut into 3 mm × 4 mm × 40 mm pieces, polished, and chamfered with a polishing lap. The flexural strength was calculated via Eq. (7) [17]:

(7) σf=3Fl2bh2

where *F* is the load, *l* is the length of the span, *b* is the width, and *h* is the thickness of the sample. Fracture toughness (*K*IC) was measured via three-point bending in single-edge V-notched beam samples. The V-notches with a sharp tip radius of ca. 8 μm and notch depth of ca. 920 μm were cut at the center of the 3 mm × 40 mm surfaces of the rectangular samples by automated means with a razor blade coated with diamond paste before testing. The fracture toughness was calculated via Eqs. (8)–(10) [18]:

(8) θ=d/g

(9) Y=1.9887−1.326θ−(3.49−0.68θ+1.35θ2)θ(1−θ)⋅(1+θ)−2

(10) KIvnb=Y3Pmax(S0−Si)θ2wg1.5(1−θ)1.5

where *d* is the V-notch depth; *w* and *g* represent the width and height of the sample, respectively; *K*Ivnb is the stress intensity of a V-notched beam specimen, *Y* is a stress intensity shape factor; *P*max is the maximum force at which fracture occurred; *S*0 and *S**i* are the outer span and inner span, respectively.

### 2.3 Thermal properties of CrNbO<sub>4</sub>

The melting point of CrNbO4 was measured by directly observing the shape and volume change during the laser heating process in a system established at the Shanghai Institute of Ceramics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China. The bulk sample was placed in a graphite crucible located in the center of a vacuum chamber. The graphite crucible was heated by four symmetrically arranged laser beams, and the temperature was measured by an infrared pyrometer (Raynger 3iPlus1M, Raytek, USA). The shape and volume of the sample were monitored by an optical system. When the collapse of the solid sample was observed, the temperature (melting point) at that moment was recorded.

The average linear TEC of bulk CrNbO4 was obtained from temperature-dependent length (*L*) changes from 298 (*L*298K) to 1673 K in air via an optical dilatometer (Misura ODHT1600-50, Expert System Solutions, Italy). The average linear TEC of CrNbO4 was calculated according to Eq. (11):

(11) α=L−L298K(T−298K)L298K

The anisotropic TECs were determined from the high-temperature XRD data. The powder sample was heated from 298 to 1473 K at a rate of 10 K·min−1 under an air atmosphere in a heating plate of a high-temperature X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku-SmartLab SE, Rigaku, Japan), and the XRD patterns were collected at 298 K and then every 100 K interval from 373 to 1473 K. At each temperature, the powder sample was held for 10 min to achieve temperature equilibrium. The scanning range was selected between 2*θ* values of 25° and 70° at a speed of 1 (°)·min−1. The XRD patterns were refined via the Rietveld method (Reflex code in Materials Studio, Accelrys Inc., USA) to determine the lattice parameters at each temperature. The thermal diffusivity (*D*th) of bulk CrNbO4 was measured via the laser flash method (LFA457, NETZSCH, Germany) using a sample with a size of *ϕ*10 mm × 2 mm from 298 to 1473 K. Before the test, the surface of each sample was sprayed with a layer of graphite to prevent heat radiation from penetrating through it. The heat capacity (*c**p*) of CrNbO4 was obtained via the Neumann‒Kopp rule from the data of their constituent oxides (Cr2O3 and Nb2O5) [19]. The thermal conductivity (*κ*) was calculated according to the relationship between the thermal diffusivity, heat capacity, and density via Eq. (12) [20]:

(12) κ(T)=Dth(T)cp(T)ρ(T)

where *ρ* is the temperature-dependent density, which can be calculated via the volumetric TEC.

### 3.2 Phase composition and microstructure of bulk CrNbO<sub>4</sub>

The preparation of dense bulk CrNbO4 is a prerequisite for studying its mechanical and thermal properties. Therefore, we prepared bulk CrNbO4 by hot pressing the as-synthesized CrNbO4 powders in a graphite mold at 1400 °C under 30 MPa for 40 min. Subsequently, the phase composition of the hot-pressed bulk CrNbO4 was analyzed via XRD, as shown in Fig. 2(a). After hot-press sintering, there was no apparent change in the intensity or position of the diffraction peaks of CrNbO4, which matched well with PDF#38-1479. In addition to the original CrNbO4 phase, Cr2O3 was also detected. Cr2O3 has a corundum-type structure that crystallizes in trigonal symmetry with a space group *R** 3¯c* (No. 167). Therefore, after hot-press sintering at 1400 °C, a small amount of Cr2O3 phase precipitates from CrNbO4. For a two-phase material, the volume ratio between the two phases and their orientation relationship directly dictate its properties. To quantitatively calculate the two-phase ratio, the internal standard method [24] in JADE software was employed, yielding a mass ratio of CrNbO4 to Cr2O3 of 96.3% : 3.7%. Through further calculations, the volume ratio of the two phases was determined to be 96.2% : 3.8%. Figure 2(b) shows the SEM image of the fracture surface, wherein a two-phase microstructure is clearly observed, i.e., fine precipitated Cr2O3 grains (red circle area) distributed in microsized CrNbO4 grain boundaries. Notably, only a few pores and microcracks can be observed in the observed region, indicating that the bulk CrNbO4 has a high relative density. The theoretical density of CrNbO4 is 5.38 g·cm−3, whereas that of Cr2O3 is 5.22 g·cm−3 [25]. On the basis of the phase composition calculated from Fig. 2(a), the theoretical density of the two-phase material was calculated to be 5.37 g·cm−3. The density of the hot-pressed bulk material measured via the Archimedes method is 5.21 g·cm−3, which is 97% of the theoretical density. The grain size distributions of CrNbO4 and Cr2O3 were calculated from the grains shown in Fig. 2(b) via ImageJ software. The precipitated Cr2O3 has an average grain size of 0.35 μm, whereas the average grain size of CrNbO4 is 2.13 μm.

Through XRD and SEM analyses, we determined that second-phase Cr2O3 particles precipitated after hot-press sintering. Given that the microstructure features, including the grain size, distribution of the second phase, and interphase boundary, have important impacts on the mechanical and thermal properties of the material, we further confirmed the crystal structure of Cr2O3 and CrNbO4 and analyzed the interphase boundary between the two phases. Figure 3(a) shows a STEM image of hot-pressed bulk CrNbO4, from which two-phase contrast can be clearly seen. These two phases are assumed to be Cr2O3 (dark, rounded precipitates) and CrNbO4 (bright grains). To confirm the above hypothesis, STEM analysis across the interface (green rectangular region in Fig. 3(a)) is performed, and the results are shown in Figs. 3(b)–3(d). From the HAADF image in Fig. 3(b), one finds that there is no amorphous layer at the interphase boundary, i.e., the two-phase boundary is lattice-lattice directly matched. The EDS mappings are shown in Figs. 3(c) and 3(d). Combined with the XRD analysis in Fig. 2(a), the left phase is inferred to be Cr2O3, and the right phase is CrNbO4. In particular, we conduct a separate analysis of the element distribution of Nb. The results show that Nb is present mainly in CrNbO4 and that there is a small amount of Nb in Cr2O3. Figures 3(e) and 3(f) show HAADF and ABF-STEM images of CrNbO4 along the [001] zone axis. In the HAADF-STEM image, the bright spots in the image can reflect the real atoms or atomic pairs, and the intensity of the image points is proportional to the square of the atomic number, whereas the ABF image contrast is proportional to one-third of the atomic number [26]. Therefore, the atomic columns highlighted in Fig. 3(e) are Cr/Nb atoms, and the atomic columns highlighted in Fig. 3(f) are O atoms. Figure 3(g) shows the merged atomic resolution EDS mapping images of Cr and Nb, and Fig. 3(h) shows the EDS mapping of O, which matches well with the HAADF image in Fig. 3(e) and the overlaid atomistic structure in the figure. Notably, a nearly random distribution of Cr and Nb is obvious, revealing that CrNbO4 is actually a solid solution and can also be written as Cr0.5Nb0.5O2 to reflect that it is a rutile-type solid solution. Intriguingly, the HAADF and ABF images in Figs. 3(e) and 3(f) are very similar to those of TiO2 [001] [27] and CrTaO4 [001] [12], confirming the rutile-type crystal structure of CrNbO4. From Figs. 3(e) and 3(f), the distance between three Cr3+/Nb5+ atomic columns indicated by the yellow horizontal arrows is  2a, and the calculated lattice constant (a*) is 4.647 Å, which is closer to the theoretical value of 4.643 Å. Figure 3(i) is the HAADF image, and Fig. 3(j) is the ABF image of rutile-structured CrNbO4 in the [100] zone axis with the overlaid atomistic structure in Fig. 3(i). Figure 3(k) shows the merged atomic resolution EDS mapping images of Cr and Nb, and Fig. 3(l) shows the EDS mapping of O, which is consistent with the atom arrangement in CrNbO4. As shown in Figs. 3(i) and 3(j), Cr3+/Nb5+ cations are 6-fold coordinated with O2− and O2− is 3-fold coordinated with Cr3+/Nb5+ cations. The CrO6/NbO6 octahedra are alternatively stacked in the [100] direction, i.e., only one half of the octahedra are occupied by Cr3+/Nb5+ cations. Two types of Cr/Nb–O bond lengths can be distinguished: 1.979 and 2.002 Å. Combined with the XRD analysis in Fig. 2(a) and the STEM analysis in Fig. 3, we can conclude that the rutile-structured CrNbO4 bulk was successfully prepared.

### 3.3 Elastic/mechanical properties of bulk CrNbO<sub>4</sub>

The mechanical properties of CrNbO4 are investigated in this work since they can provide guidance for its potential applications. Importantly, the bulk material used for the subsequent mechanical and thermal tests was all CrNbO4, containing 3.7 wt% Cr2O3. For convenience, these materials are referred to as CrNbO4 bulk. For comparison, the mechanical properties of typical ceramics such as CrTaO4 [12], yttria stabilized zirconia (YSZ) [28], Y2SiO5 [29], Yb3Al5O12 [30], SiC [31], Al2O3 [32], and Cr–Nb-containing RHEAs CrMoNbTaTiVZr [33] are also listed in Table 3. For all the materials listed above, with the exception of CrMoNbTaTiVZr, both the elastic modulus and shear modulus were measured via the impulse-excited resonance method. The Pugh's ratio *G*/*B* and Poisson’s ratio *v* provide valuable information on the malleability (brittleness or ductility) and type of bonding in crystalline materials. In contrast, it is a damage-tolerant material. The measured elastic properties indicate that the *G*/*B* of CrNbO4 is 0.560, which is less than 0.571, whereas the Poisson’s ratio *v* is 0.266, which is slightly greater than 0.260. For comparison, the data of *G*/*B* and Poisson’s ratio *v* of CrMoNbTaTiVZr (0.317, 0.356) [33], CrMoNbTaTiVWZr (0.372, 0.335) [33], Y2SiO5 (0.44, 0.31) [29], CrTaO4 (0.59, 0.254) [12], YSZ (0.46, 0.3) [28], SiC (0.85, 0.16) [31], Al2O3 (0.65, 0.22) [32], TaC (0.63, 0.24) [36], HfC (0.81, 0.18) [37], Yb3Al5O12 (0.64, 0.24) [30], YbAlO3 (0.496, 0.287) [38], Ti3AlC2 (0.75, 0.2) [39], Mn2AlB2 (0.71,0.21) [40], YTaO4 (0.327, 0.35) [41], NdTaO4 (0.459,0.30) [41], and other materials are also plotted in Fig. 5. It is generally accepted that a material in the bottom right quadrant of Fig. 5 is considered brittle, whereas those in the opposite quadrant are considered ductile. On the basis of the data in Fig. 5, the two Cr- and Nb-containing RHEAs are judged as ductile materials, and CrNbO4 is more brittle than YSZ, Y2SiO5, YTaO4, NdTaO4, and YbAlO3. Compared with CrTaO4, which is also a complex oxide with a rutile structure, CrNbO4 is less brittle.

On the one hand, during the hot-pressing process, Cr2O3 precipitates as a second phase with a volume fraction of approximately 3.8% and an average particle size of approximately 350 nm, as shown in Fig. 2. It has come to light that grain boundaries (GBs) are usually strengthened because segregation and grain boundary segregation may constitute a universal mechanism for enhancing the strength of ceramics [44,45]. Thus, the segregation of Cr at the interphase boundary further contributes to the strengthening of CrNbO4.

(22) ln(XX0)=αX(T−T0)

(23) α′=αasin2θ+αccos2θ

where θ* is the spherical coordinate angle and *α**a* and *α**c* are the TECs along the principal axes (*a* and *c*). Like CrNbO4, the maximum linear expansion of CrTaO4 is also along the *c*-axis, and the minimum linear expansion coefficient is along the *a*-axis. The maximum-to-minimum TEC ratio is 1.38, with a difference between the two directions of 2.13×10−6 K−1. These results demonstrate that the anisotropy of CrTaO4 is greater than that of CrNbO4. Since elastic and thermal expansion anisotropies are the main causes of thermal stress and microcracking in polycrystalline ceramics [54], less anisotropy in thermal expansion favors the ability of polycrystalline CrNbO4 to maintain high strength, which is also helpful for explaining the higher strength of CrNbO4 than CrTaO4.

#### 3.4.3 Thermal conductivity of CrNbO<sub>4</sub>

In the rutile-type crystal structure of CrNbO4, the CrO6/NbO6 octahedra are alternatively stacked in the [100] direction, and only one half of the octahedral sites are occupied by Cr3+/Nb5+ cations. One can thus infer that CrNbO4 has a relatively low thermal conductivity. However, to date, there is still a lack of clear data supporting this hypothesis. Therefore, experimental measurement of the thermal conductivity is essential. Figure 9(a) shows the thermal diffusivity of CrNbO4 as a function of temperature. The thermal diffusivity data were subjected to linear fitting of 1/*T*, and the results are expressed as Eq. (24):

*

(24) Dth=0.378+397/T

Notably, the thermal diffusivity of CrNbO4 is 1.745 mm2·s−1 at 298 K, and as the temperature increases, the thermal diffusivity of CrNbO4 gradually decreases, approaching a minimum value. This phenomenon occurs because the phonon mean free path diminishes to the average atomic distance. Figure 9(b) depicts the curve of the heat capacity of CrNbO4 as a function of temperature. This curve is derived via the Neumann‒Kopp rule and data from its constituent oxides, namely, Cr2O3 and Nb2O5. The thermal conductivity of CrNbO4 as a function of temperature was subsequently calculated via Eq. (12), as shown in Fig. 9(c). CrNbO4 exhibited low thermal conductivity throughout the entire temperature range. Given that the thermal conductivities of most oxides are nearly inversely proportional to 1/T*, the data were fitted via the least-squares method to derive the thermal conduvtivity *κ* as expressed in Eq. (25):

(25) k=0.33+180/T

The *R*2 value of the fitted curve is 0.986. The thermal conductivity of CrNbO4 at room temperature is 1.09 W·m−1·K−1, which is much lower than that of other reported TBC materials, such as CrTaO4 (1.31 W·m−1·K−1) [12], YSZ (3.06 W·m−1·K−1) [55], Yb3Al5O12 (4.67 W·m−1·K−1) [30], and La2Zr2O7 (3.2 W·m−1·K−1) [56], and it is close to low-thermal conductivity materials, such as thin film SiO2 (1.2 W·m−1·K−1) [57], TiP2O7 (1.08 W·m−1·K−1), and ZrP2O7 (0.92 W·m−1·K−1) [58]. With increasing temperature, the thermal conductivity of CrNbO4 decreases gradually and reaches a value of 0.45 W·m−1·K−1 at 1473 K.

Debye used the analogy of gas dynamics theory to derive the lattice thermal conductivity of dielectric solids, which can be expressed as Eq. (26) [59]:

(26) κ=13Cvmλ

where *C* is the heat capacity, *v*m is the speed of sound, and *λ* is the phonon mean free path. Above the Debye temperature, the heat capacity and sound velocity are approximately constant, whereas the average phonon free path of the crystalline material is nearly proportional to 1/*T* [60]. In this context, the thermal conductivity of CrNbO4 can be modeled via a 1/*T* equation within the temperature range of 649–1473 K, as shown in Fig. 9(c). At temperatures exceeding 1473 K, the thermal conductivity is expected to reach a plateau, becoming independent of temperature. For most materials, this platform value of thermal conductivity approaches the minimum thermal conductivity (*k*min), which can be determined via Clarke’s model (Eq. (27)) [61]:

(27) κmin→0.87kBNA2/3m2/3ρ1/6E1/2M2/3

where *m* is the number of atoms in the primitive cell, and *M* is the molecular mass. To precisely estimate the lower limit of the intrinsic *k*min of CrNbO4, the modified Clarke model reported by Liu *et al*. [62] is adopted, and it can be written as Eq. (28):

(28) κmin→kBvm(MnρNA)−23

where *n* is the number of atoms in a molecule. According to the modified Clarke model proposed by Liu *et al*. [62], the estimated value of *k*min is 0.64 W·m−1·K−1. At 1473 K, the experimentally measured value of *k* is 0.45 W·m−1·K−1, which is slightly lower than the estimated value of *k*min. This discrepancy can be attributed to the fact that the model does not account for factors such as defects, resulting in the experimental value being lower than the estimated *k*min.

### 4.1 Solubility of CrNbO<sub>4</sub>

In the previous sections, we showed that phase-pure CrNbO4 powders were synthesized at 1150 °C for 2 h. However, during hot-press sintering at 1400 °C under 30 MPa for 40 min, Cr2O3 was found to have precipitated. To elucidate the mechanism for the precipitation of Cr2O3, the average linear TEC of CrNbO4 was measured from room temperature to 1673 K by an optical dilatometer. As shown in Fig. 10(a), below 1473 K, the length of the CrNbO4 sample expands almost linearly. According to Eq. However, when the temperature reaches 1500 K, the thermal expansion curve sharply decreases and then significantly increases. This phenomenon can be attributed to either the phase transformation or the decomposition (precipitation of the second phase) of the material during heating. Considering that the TEC of Cr2O3 (6.1–8.2)×10−6 K−1 from 600 to 1400 K is greater than that of CrNbO4 and that Cr2O3 was detected via XRD (Fig. 2) and STEM (Fig. 3) after hot pressing at 1673 K, the change in the thermal expansion curve can be attributed to the precipitation of *x* mole Cr2O3 from CrNbO4 following Reaction (29):

Intriguingly, the precipitation of the second phase was not observed during the hot pressing of CrTaO4. CrNbO4 and CrTaO4 are essentially analogous in structure, wherein the metal cations Cr/Ta or Cr/Nb occupy the octahedral sites in the rutile structure. The difference in ionic radius is one of the crucial factors influencing the solubility of solid solutions. Generally, the smaller the difference in ionic radius is, the greater the solid solubility, and the smaller the influence on the crystal structure distortion, making the crystal structure more stable. In CrTaO4, the ionic radius of Cr3+ is 0.615 Å, and the ionic radius of Ta5+ is 0.640 Å, with an ionic radius difference of 0.025 Å. In CrNbO4, the ionic radius of Nb5+ is 0.690 Å, and the ionic radius difference between the two is 0.075 Å, which is three times greater than that in CrTaO4. The large ionic radius difference influences the solid solubility and may cause greater distortion, which is responsible for the structural instability of CrNbO4 compared with CrTaO4. At this point, we can conclude that CrNbO4 is stable below 1500 K; above this temperature, the solubility decreases, and Cr2O3 may precipitate.

In the case of the solid solutions Cr0.5Ta0.5O2 (CrTaO4) and Cr0.5Nb0.5O2 (CrNbO4), lattice distortion inevitably occurs due to differences in atomic size. Furthermore, since the difference in ionic radii in Cr0.5Nb0.5O2 is greater than that in Cr0.5Ta0.5O2, we speculate that greater lattice distortion will occur in Cr0.5Nb0.5O2. To characterize this phenomenon accurately, we employed the geometric phase analysis (GPA) technique for strain quantification, conducting a quantitative investigation into the distribution of lattice strains along the [100] zone axis in Cr0.5Ta0.5O2, Cr0.5Nb0.5O2, and the interface between Cr0.5Nb0.5O2 and Cr2O3, as shown in Fig. 11. GPA analyses reveal that the maps of the horizontal normal strain (ɛ**xx*), vertical normal strain (*ɛ**yy*), and shear strain (*ɛ**xy*) are randomly distributed. Figures 11(a) and 11(e) show HAADF images of CrTaO4 and CrNbO4 on the [100] zone axis, respectively. As presented in Figs. 11(d) and 11(h), the GPA results indicate that the strain in CrNbO4 is greater than that in CrTaO4. The higher strain in CrNbO4 provides an additional driving force for the decomposition of the solid solution, prompting the precipitation of Cr2O3 from the solid solution to reduce the total energy of the system. This also explains why the precipitation of Cr2O3 occurs in CrNbO4 but is absent in CrTaO4. This precipitation reduces the lattice distortion of the solid solution, thereby decreasing the internal stress‒strain of the CrNbO4 bulk material. Figure 11(i) shows the HAADF image at the Cr2O3/CrNbO4 interface. Figures 11(j)–11(l) show the stress‒strain images near the two-phase interface. When calculated with GPA, the in-plane strain of Cr2O3 is greater than that of CrNbO4, indicating a localized strain concentration [63]. Furthermore, the CrNbO4 matrix is under compressive strain compared with the tensile strain in Cr2O3, which explains the high strength of bulk CrNbO4.

### 4.2 Dual effects of protective CrNbO<sub>4</sub> on RHEAs

CrNbO4 effectively prevents the oxidation of RHEAs once it forms on their surface upon oxidation. In the previous section, experimental measurements revealed that CrNbO4 has a low thermal conductivity, with a value of merely 1.09 W·m−1·K−1 at room temperature. This finding suggests that CrNbO4 can not only protect RHEAs from oxidation but also protect them from thermal attack since it is a high-temperature thermal insulation material. The low thermal conductivity of CrNbO4 can be explained by the following aspects. First, in the crystal structure of rutile-type CrNbO4, only half of the octahedral positions in each layer along the *c*-direction are occupied by Cr3+/Nb5+ cations. Additionally, for the solid solution of CrNbO4 (Cr0.5Nb0.5O2), there is a significant difference in the atomic masses of the Nb and Cr atoms (Nb5+ with a mass of 93 amu and Cr3+ with a mass of 52 amu). The partial occupation of octahedral positions and the large mass difference between Cr and Nb endow CrNbO4 with low thermal conductivity due to the scattering of transported phonons. Unexpectedly, after hot pressing, a small amount of Cr2O3 precipitated. Owing to the differences in the crystal structures and elastic properties between the two phases, phonons are scattered and change their propagation directions, thus impeding heat conduction and reducing the thermal conductivity. In addition, as shown in Fig. 11, the strain in CrNbO4 is greater than that in CrTaO4. This strain distorts the lattice and disrupts the symmetry of the crystal, thereby increasing the likelihood of phonon scattering and further reducing the thermal conductivity of CrNbO4. Consequently, CrNbO4 can be considered a dual-functional scale that can protect RHEAs from both oxidation and thermal attack. The TECs, room-temperature thermal conductivities, and Young’s moduli of common TBC/environmental coating (EBC) materials, as well as low-thermal-conductivity materials, were plotted in a three-dimensional (3D) graph, as depicted in Fig. 10(b). The thermal conductivity of CrNbO4 is much lower than that of some common TBC/EBC materials, such as YSZ [28], β-Lu2Si2O7 [64], γ-Y2Si2O7 [65], La2Zr2O7, Y2SiO5 [29], Yb3Al5O12 [30], La2Hf2O7 [48], TiO2 [24], and CrTaO4. Compared with Al2O3 [32], which is a well-established protective scale for metals and ceramics from oxidation, CrNbO4 exhibits a lower thermal conductivity, thereby demonstrating greater advantages in the fields of high-temperature oxidation and thermal protection. On the basis of the above analysis, CrNbO4 can be considered a promising dual-functional scale for the protection of refractory RHEAs from oxidation and thermal attack.

## 5 Conclusions

In summary, we report the synthesis, microstructure, and elastic, mechanical, and thermal properties of CrNbO4 with a rutile-type crystal structure. First, pure-phase CrNbO4 powders with an average particle size of 0.56 μm were synthesized via a solid-state reaction between Cr2O3 and Nb2O5 at 1150 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the CrNbO4 powders were hot-pressed at 1400 °C for 40 min under 30 MPa to produce nearly fully dense bulk CrNbO4. Notably, approximately 3.8 vol% of Cr2O3 precipitated after hot pressing, which is beneficial for increasing the strength and decreasing the thermal conductivity of CrNbO4. Using the HAADF and ABF techniques, we demonstrated that the precipitated secondary phase was Cr2O3, confirmed the rutile-type crystal structure of CrNbO4, and observed Cr segregation at the interphase boundary between CrNbO4 and Cr2O3. The *E*, *G*, and *B* of CrNbO4 are 253, 100, and 180 GPa, respectively, whereas *H*V, *σ*f, and *K*IC of CrNbO4 are 10.2±0.58 GPa, 205±8 MPa, and 1.54±0.12 MPa·m1/2, respectively. CrNbO4 has a relatively high melting point of 2053±20 K. The anisotropic TECs measured via high-temperature XRD are *α*a = (5.38±0.09)×10−6 K−1 and *α*c = (7.44±0.14)×10−6 K−1. From 649 to 1473 K, the volumetric TEC is *α*V = (18.20±0.36)×10−6 K−1, and the average TEC is (6.07±0.12)×10−6 K−1, which is close to the average linear TEC measured by an optical dilatometer ((5.84±0.04)×10−6 K−1). The room-temperature thermal conductivity of CrNbO4 is 1.09 W·m−1·K−1, which decreases to 0.45 W·m−1·K−1 at 1473 K. Below 1500 K, CrNbO4 demonstrates high thermal stability, with a thermal conductivity lower than that of most currently known thermal insulation materials, such as YSZ, rare earth silicates, rare earth aluminates, and rare earth zirconates. Therefore, CrNbO4 is anticipated to be a new high-temperature thermal insulating material for low-density Cr–Nb-containing RHEAs. Most importantly, it can either form *in situ* during service in an oxygen-containing environment or grow on the surface of RHEAs through heat treatment prior to service. On the whole, CrNbO4 is expected to be a promising dual-functional scale for RHEAs, providing both thermal and oxidation protection.
