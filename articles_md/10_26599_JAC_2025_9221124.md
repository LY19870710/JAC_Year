## 1 Introduction

Laser lighting is regarded as the next generation of solid-state lighting sources due to its advantages, such as high brightness, strong directionality, and long service life, showing significant potential for applications in the fields of automotive headlights, projection displays, and special lighting [1−3]. Compared with traditional light emitting diode (LED) lighting, the laser diode (LD) easily achieves high-brightness lighting application scenarios by increasing the power density of the laser owing to the lack of “efficiency droop” issues [4−7], providing an effective route to circumvent the physical limitations of LED lighting [8−10]. The blue LD excited phosphor color converter is one of the mainstreams for laser-driven white lighting because of its adjustable emission characteristics [11−13], rich material system [14−16], and excellent cost effectiveness [17,18]. Unfortunately, high power density and ultra-small spot area of blue LDs induces a significant heat accumulation in the phosphor color converter, leading to thermal quenching of the phosphor and a decrease in the luminescence saturation threshold [19−22].

To alleviate heat aggregation and phosphor quenching, all-inorganic color converters, including phosphor-in-glass (PiG) [23−25], PiG films (PiGFs) [14,26−28], single crystals [12,29,30], and transparent ceramics [31−36], have been developed. Among them, the PiGF converter containing a heat-conducting substrate is regarded as an advisable choice in laser-driven white lighting owing to its remarkable advantages of design adaptability, adjustable optical properties, and simple preparation methods. Although the optical and heat dissipation performances of transmissive and reflective laser lighting have been significantly enhanced by introducing a highly thermally conductive substrate (sapphire, Al2O3, AlN, and Al), commercial PiGF converters still face severe luminescence saturation at low laser power densities (2) because the low thermal conductivity of PiGF is incapable of quickly dissipating the heat generated near the laser excitation location, thereby inducing phosphor thermal quenching and an overheating gradient (Δ*T* > 150 K) [25,37]. Essentially, adding a heat-conducting channel near the laser excitation position can greatly improve the heat dissipation and luminescence of the PiGF converter [9,28]. Zhang *et al*. [38] and Peng *et al*. [14] designed a sandwich color converter of sapphire@PiGF@sapphire to achieve high-brightness laser lighting with a transmissive configuration, and the luminous intensity was exponentially increased compared with that of the traditional PiGF@sapphire color converter. However, these transmissive sandwich structures have severe backscattering effects and cannot be connected to the heat sink [39,40]. Additionally, Mou *et al*. [41] developed a reflective sandwich converter with a sapphire@PiG@planar alumina microstructure, and the luminescence saturation was greatly increased to 5197 lm at a laser power density of 30 W/mm2. However, the complicated preparation process and high cost of microstructure sapphire have limited its practical application, and the high thermal conductivity and high transmissivity of sapphire have not been fully utilized to further improve its luminescence performance. More crucially, the mechanism for internal heat dissipation enhancement of the reflective sandwich PiGF converter has not yet been explored.

Herein, a reflective sapphire@PiGF@alumina (S@PiGF@A) color converter was designed for high-brightness laser-driven white lighting. The S@PiGF@A color converter was fabricated by thermocompression bonding the La3Si6N11:Ce3+ (LSN) PiGF between the sapphire optical window and alumina substrate. The high thermal conductivity of sapphire and alumina provides double-sided heat dissipation channels; meanwhile, the high reflectance of alumina and the high transmissivity of sapphire enhance phosphor conversion and photon escape. The effects of sapphire thickness on the luminescence and thermal performance of the S@PiGF@A color converter were systematically investigated, and the heat dissipation enhancement mechanism of the S@PiGF@A color converter was demonstrated via finite element analysis (FEA). The prepared S@PiGF@A color converter achieves white light emission with an ultrahigh luminous ﬂux of 6749 lm at a laser power density saturation threshold of 47.70 W/mm2. Moreover, the S@PiGF@A color converter has a low working temperature of 62.9 °C, which is significantly lower than that of the traditional PiGF@ alumina color converter of 189 °C. These results demonstrate that the developed S@PiGF@A color converter has enormous potential for achieving high-brightness laser-driven white lighting.

### 2.1 Materials

Borosilicate glass powder (B2O3–SiO2–ZnO–Na2O) and La3Si6N11:Ce3+ (LSN) phosphor were purchased from Schott and Grirem Advanced Materials Co., Ltd., respectively. The alumina with high reflectance as the heat-conducting substrate was obtained from Wuhan LEDstar Co., Ltd. The sapphire with high transmissivity as the heat-conducting optical window was obtained from Shenzhen Feier Optical Technology Co., Ltd. The ethyl cellulose and terpineol were obtained from Sinopharm. The organic solvent of the phosphor glass paste was self-prepared by dissolving ethyl cellulose in terpineol at 80 °C for 30 min.

### 2.2 Fabrication process of the S@PiGF@A color converter

The S@PiGF@A color converter was fabricated by thermocompression bonding of the LSN PiGF between the sapphire optical window and alumina substrate, as shown in Fig. 1. First, LSN phosphor particles, glass powder, and organic solvent were mixed to obtain a phosphor-glass paste, and the mass ratio of the LSN phosphor to the glass powder was controlled at 1 : 1. The phosphor-glass paste was subsequently uniformly printed on the alumina substrate (15 mm × 15 mm × 1 mm) and then dried at 80 °C for 30 min. After that, a sapphire optical window (15 mm × 15 mm) with a thickness of 0.5–3 mm was placed on the surface of the dried phosphor-glass paste, and then thermocompression-bonded under 2 MPa at 680 °C for 60 min. Finally, the bonded sample was naturally cooled to obtain the S@PiGF@A color converter. To highlight the advantages of S@PiGF@A color converter, a traditional PiGF@alumina (PiGF@A) color converter without the sapphire optical window was fabricated under the same printing and thermocompression bonding conditions.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The size of the phosphor particles was measured via a Malvern Mastersizer 2000 (USA). A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Nova NanoSEM 450, FEI, the Netherlands) equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) was adopted to characterize the microstructure and element composition of the S@PiGF@A color converter. A fluorescence spectrometer (FP6500, JASCO, Japan) was utilized to analyze the photoluminescence emission (PL) and photoluminescence excitation (PLE) of the LSN phosphor and S@PiGF@A color converter. A steady/transient fluorescence spectrometer (FLS980, Edinburgh, UK) was used to record the decay curves of the LSN phosphor and S@PiG@A converter. The crystal phases of the LSN phosphor and S@PiG@A converter were detected via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Smartlab SE, Rigaku, Japan) in the 2*θ* angle range of 10°–80°. The transmission and reflection spectra of the sapphire optical window, alumina substrate, and S@PiGF@A color converters were measured via an ultraviolet (UV) spectrophotometer. A quantum yield spectrometer (Quantaurus-QY, Hamamatsu, Japan) was used to measure the quantum efficiency of the LSN phosphor, and PiGF and S@PiG@A color converters. A sphere-spectroradiometer system with a spectrometer detector (ATA-1000, EVERLINE, China) was used to measure electroluminescence (EL), luminous flux (LF), and chromatic coordinates of the PiGF@A and S@PiG@A color converters. The S@PiG@A color converters were placed at the center position of the integrating sphere on GB/T 26178-2010/CIE 84-1989 [42−44], and a blue laser with a circular spot area of 1.014 mm2 (RFL-B50D, 50 W, RAYCUS, China) served as the excitation source for high-brightness white laser lighting. The surface temperatures of the S@PiG@A converter driven by a blue laser were measured by a thermal infrared imager (E63, FLIR, USA).

## 3 Results and discussion

The XRD patterns of the LSN phosphor particles and S@PiGF@A color converter are shown in Fig. 2(a). It is evident that all the diffraction peaks of the S@PiGF@A color converter align with those of the LSN phosphor and the standard card (ICSD-248709), suggesting that the crystal structure of the LSN phosphor remains well-preserved during the thermocompression bonding process and the obvious intermediate products cannot be discovered. The PL and PLE spectra of the S@PiGF@A color converter are nearly identical to those of the LSN phosphor, as shown in Fig. 2(b). The PL spectrum reveals the allowed (536 nm) transition from 4f to 5d, which is significantly influenced by the crystal field environment and electron–phonon coupling [45−47]. The yellow-green emission peak is attributed to the weaker crystal field of the matrix or the smaller energy level spacing. The PLE spectra show that the PiGF and S@PiGF@A color converters have broad excitation bands centered at 467 and 471 nm, respectively. The LSN phosphor exhibits dual-peak emission at 467 nm (primary) and 384 nm (secondary), originating from energy transfer between the CeLa(1) and CeLa(2) sites during Ce3+ 5d→4f transitions [48−50]. The full width at half maximum (FWHM) is 58 nm, and the broad asymmetric emission band indicates that the S@PiGF@A color converter is more suitable for blue laser excitation. As presented in Fig. 2(c), the fluorescence lifetime of the LSN phosphor and S@PiGF@A color converter is *τ*PiGF = 41.28 ns and *τS@*PiGF@PA = 40.58 ns, respectively, which demonstrates that the lifetime of the LSN phosphor remains relatively stable after the thermocompression bonding process. These experimental results indicate that the LSN phosphor in the S@PiGF@A color converter effectively maintains its luminescence properties. Moreover, Fig. 2(d) displays the external quantum efficiency (EQE) of the LSN phosphor, PiGF, and S@PiGF@A color converter. The EQEs of the PiGF and S@PiGF@A color converters are lower than that of the LSN phosphor, which is mainly because the LSN phosphor undergoes thermal quenching during the thermocompression bonding process. In addition, the light scattering, interfacial Fresnel reflection, and self-absorption of the LSN phosphor interacting with glass also lead to a decrease in the EQE [51,52]. It is gratifying that the S@PiGF@A color converter still has a high EQE of 71%, fully meeting the requirements of phosphor-based converters for laser lighting.

Figure 3(a) shows the transmittance and reflectance spectra of the sapphire optical windows and alumina substrate. As the thickness increases from 0.5 to 3 mm, the transmissivity of the sapphire optical window slightly decreases from 88% to 86% within the visible light range of 400–750 nm, indicating that the influence of thickness on the visible light penetration capability of the sapphire optical window can be negligible, thereby reducing interface reflection loss and enhancing light extraction. Additionally, the alumina substrate exhibits a high reflectance of 84%, ensuring the effective reflection and recovery of light within the reflective phosphor-based color converter. Moreover, the reflectance spectra of the S@PiGF@A color converter with various sapphire thicknesses were further analyzed, as depicted in Fig. 3(b). After thermocompression bonding to the PiGF, the reflectances of the PiGF@A and S@PiGF@A color converters significantly decrease, which is attributed to the refractive index difference of sapphire–PiGF–alumina and the scattering effect of the LSN phosphor particles. Besides, the reflectance spectra of the S@PiGF@A color converter exhibit a decrease in the near-wavelength range of approximately 450 nm, which corresponds to the excitation band of the LSN phosphor. Furthermore, as the sapphire thickness increases from 0.5 to 3 mm, the average reflectance of the S@PiGF@A color converter decreases from 57% to 40%, indicating that the sapphire thickness affects the photon escape ability. It is essential to optimize the sapphire thickness to improve the optical and thermal performance of the S@PiGF@A color converter.

Figures 4(a) and 4(b) show the particle size distribution and SEM image of the LSN phosphor particles. The volume distribution D*50 value of the sample is 18.23 μm, and 80% of the particles are in the range of 8.7–39.8 μm (refer to *D*10/*D*90 data), and the LSN particles are uniformly dispersed in the glass matrix, which is owing to the uniform mixing and the flow of molten glass. Figure 4(c) shows a cross-sectional SEM image and element distribution images of the S@PiG@A converter. The PiGF is tightly bonded with the sapphire optical window and alumina substrate, and the micro gaps and cracks are not observed at the sapphire–PiGF bonding interface or PiGF–alumina bonding interface, thereby reducing the interfacial thermal resistance and enhancing the heat dissipation efficiency of the PiGF to the sapphire optical window and alumina substrate. In addition, the PiGF thickness is approximately 120 μm; the LSN phosphor particles are well embedded in the glass matrix; the crystal structure of the LSN phosphor maintains integrity based on the EDS spectra analysis. Meanwhile, a small number of pores exist inside PiGF, which can effectively serve as scattering centers to facilitate homogenization of the laser spot and enhance the phosphor conversion efficiency [28,48,53].

To demonstrate the feasibility of the S@PiGF@A converter applied in white laser lighting, the S@PiGF@A converters were tested in a test system equipped with a blue LD, as displayed in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The effect of the PiGF thickness on LF was studied, as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. At an optimized thickness of 120 µm, a maximum luminous flux of 6749 lm can be achieved under 47.70 W/mm² laser excitation. Additionally, the luminance performances of the S@PiGF@A converter with various sapphire thicknesses were evaluated at different input power densities, as shown in Figs. 5(a)–5(d). Before the luminous intensity reaches saturation, the LF increases linearly with the input power density, and then rapidly decreases as the input power density further increases, which is attributed to the large amount of heat accumulated in the color converter and the subsequent thermal quenching of photoluminescence. Notably, as the sapphire thickness increases from 0.5 to 3 mm, the saturation threshold for the laser power density of the S@PiGF@A color converter increases sharply from 19.53 to 47.70 W/mm2, and its maximum luminous efficiency also increases from 127.3 to 139.5 lm/W. Additionally, the luminous efficiency of the S@PiGF@A color converter gradually increases with increasing sapphire thickness, and the maximum luminous efficacy exhibits a broader range of input power densities. The reason is that the double-sided heat dissipation channels and high heat-conducting sapphire can reduce the working temperature and thermal quenching of the PiGF, thereby increasing the “effective radiation flux” (light-power-to-thermal-power ratio) available for phosphor converters. Although thin windows have potential advantages in terms of optical performance (reflectance, EQE), the “effective optical power gain” brought about by improved heat dissipation exceeds the impact of its optical disadvantages, which proves that thick window layers are beneficial for improving the luminescence property of S@PiGF@A color converters. It is evident that the EL intensity of yellow-green emission reduces gradually with increasing sapphire thickness, and the corresponding spectra show a similar trend to that of the PL spectra of the raw phosphor, as shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b). Moreover, the CIE chromaticity coordinates of the S@PiGF@A color converter gradually move from the yellow-white region to the blue-white region, and the spectrum distribution changes from the yellow-white region to the blue-white region and gradually passes through the Planckian locus. Considering both the luminescence properties and color quality, the optimized thickness of sapphire is 3 mm, and the corresponding S@PiGF@A color converter achieves white light emission with a maximum luminous ﬂux of 6749 lm at a laser power density saturation threshold of 47.70 W/mm2, which is 2.44 times that of the PiGF@A color converter (2522 lm@19.53 W/mm2).

Table 1 compares the luminous saturation thresholds with those of the reported reflective phosphor color converter driven by blue LDs, from which the advantages of the luminance performance of this work can be seen. The evaluation of the LF for the PiGF is significantly modulated by the laser spot size. A small spot size enables higher tolerance to power density, whereas a large spot size supports higher input power and enhanced LF output. Therefore, when evaluating the luminance performance of a PiGF, it is necessary to consider the LF, input power, and laser spot area. At a spot area of 2.5 mm2, the S@PiGF@A color converter performs an LF of 7938 lm (Fig. S3(a) in the ESM), and when the LP reaches the maximum power of 50 W, the LF still does not reach saturation. The increase in LF with increasing spot area can be attributed to the alleviation of the thermal quenching effect, as shown by the FEA simulations in Fig. S4 in the ESM. However, increasing the spot area reduces the input power density threshold (Fig. S3(b) in the ESM) and increases the beam divergence angle, thereby contradicting the laser’s small-emission-area advantage. Consequently, the luminance performance in the 1 mm2 spot area is more representative. By achieving an ultrahigh LF (6749 lm) at an ultrasmall spot (1.014 mm2), our S@PiGF@A converter demonstrates breakthrough potential for ultrahigh brightness white-laser lighting. This capability originates from the optimized sapphire thickness-enhanced thermal management, which suppresses phosphor thermal quenching and increases light saturation thresholds. The comprehensive evaluation of LF, laser spot, input power density, and other aspects confirms the great potential of the S@PiGF@A color converter in achieving ultrahigh-brightness white light laser illumination.

Furthermore, to deeply study the thermal performance of the laser-driven S@PiGF@A converter, the FEA simulations were performed at an input power density of 19.72 W/mm2. The FEA model of the laser-driven S@PiGF@A converter consists of a heat sink, an adhesive layer, an alumina substrate, a PiGF, and a sapphire optical window. The boundary conditions of the thermal simulation are shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM, and the effect of sapphire thickness on the thermal performance of the laser-driven S@PiGF@A converter was systematically investigated. A Gaussian distributed laser beam was used to irradiate the surface of the S@PiGF@A converter to achieve white laser lighting, and the generated thermal power was calculated as the difference between the laser power and the optical power of the S@PiGF@A converter (P*heat = *P*laser − *P*light, *P*laser = 19.72 W/mm2). By measuring the distance between the laser beam and the spot generated on the PiGF surface (*d*0 = 93.47 mm) and the spot area (*A*0 = 1.014 mm2), the divergence angle of the laser beam can be calculated to be *θ*1 = 0.18°, and the divergence angle of the laser beam in the sapphire layer can be obtained to be *θ*2 = 0.10° on the basis of the refractive index of the sapphire (1.76) and the refractive index formula, as illustrated in Fig. 7(a). The sapphire thickness has almost no effect on the spot area of the laser beam; the increment of the spot area (Δ*A*) is only 1.85% even when the sapphire thickness increases to 3 mm, and the change in the spot area value is less than 2% when the spot area is in the range of 0.5–2.5 mm2 (Table S1 in the ESM). On the basis of these results, the spot area of the laser-driven S@PiGF@A converter is fixed to 1.0 mm2 to simplify the FEA model and increase the computational speed. Additionally, the transient temperature of the laser-driven S@PiGF@A converter was evaluated at an input power density of 19.72 W/mm2, as shown in Fig. 7(b). The surface temperature of the PiGF layer rapidly increases within laser excitation time of 0.5 s and reaches a stable state after laser excitation time of 1 s. Figure 7(c) compares the FEA results and experimental results of the laser-driven S@PiGF@A converter with different sapphire thicknesses at an input power density of 19.72 W/mm2. The simulated maximum temperature change trend of the laser-driven S@PiGF@A converter is consistent with the actual temperature test results of the infrared thermometer, and the maximum error between the simulation and experimental results is 10.9%, which is within the allowable error range. More importantly, the double-sided heat dissipation structure formed by introducing a sapphire light window can significantly reduce the working temperature of PiGF. As the sapphire thickness increases, the maximum temperature of the laser-driven S@PiGF@A converter first rapidly decreases and then stabilizes, and the lateral heat distribution of the laser-driven S@PiGF@A converter becomes more uniform, as shown in Fig. 7(d). With an input power density of 19.72 W/mm2 and a sapphire thickness of 3.00 mm, the maximum temperature of the laser-driven S@PiGF@A converter is 62.9 °C, which is one-third that of the traditional laser-driven PiGF@A converter of 189 °C. Therefore, these results indicate that the heat generated by the phosphor conversion of PiGF can be quickly dissipated by highly thermally conductive double-sided heat dissipation structures, thus reducing the working temperature of PiGF and improving the phosphor conversion efficiency.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, a novel reflective S@PiGF@A converter with a high luminescence saturation threshold for high-brightness laser lighting was developed. S@PiGF@A was designed by introducing a double-sided heat dissipation channel for LSN PiGF, and the effects of sapphire thickness on the photothermal performance of S@PiGF@A were investigated. As the sapphire thickness increases to 3.0 mm, the input power density saturation threshold of S@PiGF@A improved to 47.70 W/mm2, facilitating white light emission with an ultrahigh LF of 6749 lm, which is 2.44 times that of the traditional PiGF@A converter (2522 lm@19.53 W/mm2). Furthermore, FEA simulations demonstrate that the double-sided heat dissipation channels effectively mitigate the thermal effect of the PiGF. Compared with the traditional PiGF@A converter, the S@PiGF@A converter reduces the working temperature of the PiGF from 189 to 62.9 °C at an input power density of 19.72 W/mm2. This work devises a novel method to improve the saturation threshold of laser-driven color converters, which will provide broad prospects for high-brightness laser lighting.
