## 1 Introduction

Piezoelectric ceramics have consistently drawn attention from academia and industry because of their ability to generate electric charge under mechanical stress and vice versa. In particular, the industrial demand for various applications, such as sonars and actuators, has driven researchers to focus on improving piezoelectric properties, as represented by the piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33) [1−3]. Through ongoing academic endeavors, the enhancement of piezoelectric characteristics has approached a high level [4−6]. Accordingly, the academic community is currently exploring not only the piezoelectric performance but also the additional functionalities of piezoelectric ceramics.

In recent years, transparency and flexibility have emerged as the foremost considerations in the choice of material in various fields [7,8]. Given the inherent nature of ceramics, acquiring flexibility is challenging. Thus, the realization of transparency is the most promising avenue for implementing new functionalities for existing piezoelectricity. Transparent piezoelectric ceramics meet the dramatically growing demand for ultrasound imaging, transparent microscopy, see-through displays, and other new and diverse applications to match rapid technological breakthroughs [9−11].

In transparent organic light-emitting diode (OLED) displays, a transparency level greater than 60% is considered highly transparent [12]. The maximum transparency in the transparent OLED display market at the time of the preparation of this report was 45%, and efforts are underway to achieve even higher transparency levels [13,14]. Therefore, the transparency of over 60% can be considered the minimum requirement for transparent piezoelectric ceramics.

However, achieving both piezoelectricity and transparency in ceramics is challenging because they are generally conflicting, as exemplified in (Pb,La)(Zr,Ti)O3 (PLZT) ceramics [15,16]. To overcome these challenges, extensive efforts have been made to develop materials with both transparent and piezoelectric properties. Given that light scattering, which disfavors transparency, occurs mostly owing to the pores in ceramics, most research has focused on removing pores, representatively through hot pressing. For example, improved transparency was recently reported for Sm-doped Pb(In1/2Nb1/2)O3–Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3–PbTiO3 (PIN–PMN–PT:Sm) via hot-press sintering, achieving 60% transparency with *d*33 of 690 pC/N [17]. However, hot-press sintering has certain limitations in the manufacturing of large-area ceramics.

In response to the current limitations on industrially viable transparent piezoelectric material, we presented piezoelectric ceramics that guarantee over 60% transparency via pressureless sintering. Because Sm enhances piezoelectricity, while La introduces transparency in perovskite-structured ferroelectrics, we adopted a co-doping strategy for both elements in 0.71Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3–0.29PbTiO3 (PMN–0.29PT) ceramics. To demonstrate the feasibility of mass production, all the samples were manufactured using a tape-casting process, and a series of doping concentrations were tested.

Although there are concerns regarding the use of lead-based piezoceramics, we implemented transparency on lead-based systems because property enhancement should precede the implementation of new functionalities in lead-free piezoceramics [18−22].

The Pb0.913La0.0145Sm0.0145(Mg1/3Nb2/3)0.71Ti0.29O3 (TP2.9) ceramics exhibited the best transparency of 61%, with a large piezoelectric coefficient of 1104 pC/N. A transparent piezoelectric speaker with a sound pressure of approximately 66 dB was fabricated to demonstrate its use in acoustic applications.

### 2.1 Matrix powder synthesis

Pb(1−3*x*)La0.5*x*Sm0.5*x*(Mg1/3Nb2/3)0.71Ti0.29O3 (TP*x*, where *x* represents the co-doping level of La and Sm (mol%)) was synthesized using a two-step columbite method through a solid-state reaction [23]. In the first step, to synthesize MgNb2O6, MgO (99%, Sigma Aldrich, USA) and Nb2O5 (99.9%, Sigma Aldrich, USA) were mixed by ball milling for 24 h with zirconia balls in ethanol. The mixed powders were dried at 100 ℃ for 24 h in an oven (Hantech, Republic of Korea) and then calcined at 1200 ℃ for 4 h in a box furnace (Hantech, Republic of Korea). In the second step, to synthesize TP*x*, the pre-calcined powders of MgNb2O6 were mixed with PbO (99.9%, Alfa Aesar, USA), TiO2 (99.9%, High Purity Chemicals, Japan), La2O3 (99.99%, Sigma Aldrich, USA), and Sm2O3 (99.9%, High Purity Chemicals, Japan) by ball milling for 24 h with zirconia balls in an ethanol medium. The mixed powders were dried at 100 ℃ for 24 h in an oven and then calcined at 850 ℃ for 2 h in a box furnace.

### 2.2 Tape-casting and sintering

Initially, the solvent was prepared by mixing 60 vol% toluene (99.5%, Daejung Chemical & Metals Co., Ltd., Republic of Korea) and 40 vol% ethyl alcohol (99.5%, Daejung Chemical & Metals Co., Ltd., Republic of Korea). Subsequently, polyvinyl butyral (Butvar® B-98, Solutia Inc., USA), dibutyl phthalate (94%, Daejung Chemical & Metals Co., Ltd., Republic of Korea), and BYK-111 (BYK Chemical, Germany) were added to the slurry as binder, plasticizer, and dispersant, respectively.

The slurry was mixed for 24 h by ball milling with zirconia ball medium and then tape-cast, resulting in a uniform green sheet thickness of 40 μm. Next, the green sheets were stacked to 15 mm in thickness and subjected to a uniaxial pressure of 700 MPa at 60 ℃ for 20 min. The laminated sheets were subsequently shaped into circular pieces with a diameter of 50 mm and rectangular pieces with dimensions of 45 mm × 50 mm using a laser cutting machine.

The prepared samples were heated at 650 ℃ for 2 h to eliminate organic elements, followed by sintering at temperatures ranging from 1100 to 1225 ℃ for durations of 2 to 30 h under an oxygen atmosphere. The samples were ground to the intended thickness using an autopolishing machine (MetPrep3, Allied, USA).

### 2.3 Characterizations

For electrical measurements, the Ag electrode was applied to the samples and then fired at 650 ℃ for 10 min. Direct current (DC) poling was performed at room temperature (2 kV/mm) for 10 min. The electromechanical coupling coefficient  (*k*p) and mechanical quality factor (*Q*m) were determined in the frequency range of 150–300 kHz using an impedance analyzer (HP4194A, Hewlett-Packard, USA). The dielectric permittivity and dielectric loss curves as a function of the temperature were measured using an impedance analyzer (HP 4192A, Hewlett-Packard, USA) in conjunction with a customized sample holder, as shown exemplarily for *x* = 0.29 (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary material (ESM)). The optimal sintering temperature for each composition was selected based on a relative density of > 99%. The density of each specimen was measured using a density analyzer (BELPycno, BEL Japan, Inc., Japan). *d*33 was measured at room temperature using a Berlincourt *d*33 meter (YE2730, SINOCERA, China). Polarization hysteresis and strain loops were measured at room temperature using a piezoelectric evaluation system (aixACCT Systems GmbH, Aachen, Germany). For optical measurements, all ceramics were polished, and an indium tin oxide (ITO) electrode was deposited at room temperature with a thickness of 200 nm by an radio frequency (RF) sputter (SRN-120, SORONA,  Republic of Korea) and fired at 450 ℃ for 1 h. The optical transparency was measured using an ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared (UV‒Vis–NIR) spectroscope (Cary 5000, Agilent, USA), and the surface of the ceramics was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Quanta200, FEI, USA) after thermal etching. The samples were thermally etched at 1100 ℃ for 1 h.

Figure 1(b) visually represents the production of the transparent piezoelectric ceramics, showing a diverse array of sizes, including diameters of up to 40 mm. This highlighted the scalability and versatility of the manufacturing process, which are crucial for industrial applications. In the context of display applications, Fig. 1(c) shows the red, green, yellow, white, cyan, magenta, and blue test results of the samples. This visual assessment offers vital insight into the actual visual properties of ceramics, thereby complementing the practical utility of the materials beyond numerical metrics.

Figure 1(d) shows the variation in the transparency and *d*33 with increasing the thickness from 0.2 to 1 mm. As the thickness increases, the transparency decreases from 75% to 61%, whereas *d*33 increases from 323 to 1104 pC/N. Despite a slight decrease in the transparency of approximately 19% with increasing the thickness, the notable increase in the *d*33 value of 242% provides the advantage of utilizing both high optical and piezoelectric properties.

Furthermore, Fig. 1(e) shows the sound pressure level (SPL) characteristics of transparent TP2.9 piezoelectric ceramics measured in a frequency range of 500 to 20,000 Hz (auditory perception range) for the thicknesses ranging from 0.2 to 0.5 mm. The first peak positioned at approximately 1.5 kHz corresponds to the fundamental thickness mode resonance. The position of the resonance mode is seen to shift to a lower frequency as the thickness decreases. All the samples, regardless of their thickness, exceeded the maximum SPL of 64 dB [24−26]. The method for measuring SPL of transparent piezoceramics is shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. This holds potential not only for speakers but also for a range of applications, including haptics, fingerprint sensors, and tactile realism.

The scalability of the manufacturing process and the adaptability of the material properties make it a promising candidate for a wide array of applications. Furthermore, they can be used in medical imaging, which requires simultaneous ultrasound and monitoring [10,11].

### 3.2 Optical and piezoelectric properties depending on doping levels

Figure 2(a) presents an array of doping concentrations for the TP*x* ceramics. The ceramics exhibited high transparency, allowing clear visibility of the letters behind them. Visually, it is apparent that the range of *x* = 2.6 to 4.0 demonstrates nearly identical transparency. This observation holds within the visible light wavelength range, where the TP*x* ceramics from *x* = 2.8 to 3.1 indeed possess nearly identical transparency values, as shown in Fig. 2(b). Notably, the transparency of the TP*x* ceramics increases with increasing doping concentration up to *x* = 2.9. However, with a further increase in the doping concentration, the transparency of the TP*x* ceramics decreases, as depicted in Fig. 2(b). Beyond *x* = 4.0, the transparency curves exhibit irregular shapes, contingent on the wavelength, deviating from the trends observed for the TP*x* (*x* = 2.5–3.1) ceramics. Importantly, the TP*x* ceramics not only feature better transparency within this doping range but also exhibit enhanced ferroelectric and piezoelectric properties (Figs. S3 and S4 in the ESM). *d*33 follows a similar trend, increasing with increasing *x* and reaching its peak value at *x* = 3.0. Subsequently, as *x* continues to increase, *d*33 decreases and ultimately becomes negligible after *x* = 5.0, as shown in Fig. 2(c).

*
Fig. 2Optical and piezoelectric characteristics of TPx* (*x* = 2.5–5.5) transparent piezoelectric ceramics. (a) Photograph of visual representation with varying doping levels. (b) Transparency at thickness of 0.2 mm: a comparative analysis of transparency levels across different doping amounts. (c) Transparency at 632 nm and *d*33 at thickness of 0.2 mm with various doping amounts.

The increase in the transparency and enhancement of the piezoelectric properties with increasing the doping concentration can be attributed to the incorporation of the rare-earth elements La3+ and Sm3+. La3+ is effective not only in reducing the distortion of oxygen octahedra in perovskite unit cells but also in enhancing microstructure densification [27−30]. This contributes to improving the transparency of the ceramics by reducing the light scattering caused by multiple scatterings at the boundaries of the randomly oriented lattices and residual pores. The porosity is a well-known factor that affects the transparency of ceramics and has been extensively studied in many ceramics, such as alumina, yttrium aluminum garnet (YAG), and PLZT. Thus, achieving nearly full density is crucial for securing transparency. In this study, only samples with relative densities exceeding 99% were tested. Therefore, porosity can be safely excluded as the origin of the changes in the transparency of samples with different compositions.

In addition, the grain size or grain boundary density appears to be ineffective. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the transparency increases with increasing *x*, culminating at *x* = 2.9. When comparing the grain sizes between *x* = 2.5 and 2.9, as shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM, it appears that the larger the grain size is, the greater the transparency. However, the transparency at *x* = 5.5 is the worst among the investigated compositions, although the grain size is practically the same between *x* = 2.9 and 5.5. This led us to conclude that grain size was, if ever, ineffective for transparency.

The enhancement of the piezoelectric properties of the TP*x* ceramics was linked to the augmented local structural heterogeneity through Sm3+ doping. Recent studies have demonstrated that the enhancement of local random fields through rare-earth element doping in relaxor–PT systems significantly contributes to improving piezoelectric properties by enhancing local structural heterogeneity at the nanoscale with minimal impact on ceramic density and grain size [31−34]. In brief, the incorporation of La3+ and Sm3+ into the TP*x* ceramics yields the dual benefit of enhancing both the transparency and piezoelectric properties. The observed trends in transparency and *d*33 provide valuable insights into the optimal doping levels for maximizing these key properties, thereby paving the way for the development of high-performance transparent piezoelectric ceramics.

### 3.3 Optical and piezoelectric properties at various thicknesses

Figure 3 presents a comprehensive analysis of the optical and piezoelectric properties of the TP2.9 ceramics at various thicknesses, which are crucial for determining the optimal thickness for device applications. Thickness-dependent transparency and piezoelectric properties are important for tailoring material with specific functionalities. Figure 3(a) shows almost full density, as presented in the SEM images of the polished and thermally etched surfaces. Figures 3(b) and 3(c) provide visual and transparency data, respectively, demonstrating a clear inverse relationship between the thickness and transparency. As the thickness decreased, the transparency increased significantly. The appearance of a broad maximum in the transmittance spectrum after being electroded with ITO could be due to the increased density of thermally generated Sn4+ carriers hindering the formation of black InO [35]. Figure 3(d) offers insights into the ferroelectric and piezoelectric behaviors of TP2.9 ceramics with different thicknesses. Regardless of the thickness, all ceramics exhibited saturated ferroelectric hysteresis and butterfly-shaped electric-field-induced strain curves. These features are characteristic of ferroelectric materials and indicate the presence of a well-defined ferroelectric phase in the ceramics. Although *d*33, strain, and *k*p decreased with decreasing the thickness, *Q*m exhibited the opposite trend, as shown in Fig. 3(e).

The piezoelectric coefficient shows a direct proportionality to the sample thickness, a phenomenon that has been previously observed in thin specimens (36,37]. To the best of our knowledge, this observation, particularly in bulk-scale ceramics of millimeter dimensions, has not been clarified, although it has been phenomenologically reported in Refs. [38−41].

We conducted a detailed comparison of the thickness-dependent piezoelectricity and transparency properties of the TP*x* ceramics with those reported in the literature (Fig. 4 and Table 1). Transparent piezoelectric ceramics are sensitive to the sample thickness, making them an important parameter to consider in the material design and processing. Previous research has demonstrated the impressive properties of ceramics produced using hot-press sintering methods. For example, Yan *et* *al*. [42] reported Eu-doped PMN–PT ceramics with a high piezoelectric coefficient of 1400 pC/N and 68% in the NIR region. Similarly, Qin *et* *al*. [43] prepared Pr3+-doped PMN–PT ceramics with even higher transparency at 900 nm, reaching 68%, in addition to a piezoelectric coefficient of 465 pC/N. However, notably, although Eu-doped PMN–PT exhibited a high piezoelectric coefficient, it has limited applicability since its high piezoelectric coefficient and transparency require hot press sintering. Furthermore, the high transparency of 68% observed for Pr3+-doped PMN–PT was measured at 900 nm, which falls outside the visible light range.

Our TP2.9 ceramics stand out in terms of transparency within the visible light range, surpassing the performances reported in previous studies. They also exhibit excellent piezoelectric properties. Remarkably, despite the use of a pressureless sintering process, the TP2.9 ceramics achieve significantly higher levels of transparency compared to the ceramics produced using the hot-press sintering method.

The comparative analysis shown in Fig. 4 highlights the complexity of achieving optimal transparency and piezoelectricity in these ceramics. This shows the need for an understanding of the intricate interplay among composition, processing techniques, and sample thickness. Our results demonstrate the potential for further advancements in the field of transparent piezoelectric ceramics with implications for a wide range of applications, including ultrasound imaging, transparent microscopy, and see-through displays. The properties of the TP2.9 ceramics make them promising candidates for various industrial and technological advancements, particularly in fields where the integration of transparency and piezoelectricity is crucial.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, we developed transparent piezoelectric ceramics via pressureless sintering. Co-doping with Sm and La has been demonstrated to be highly effective in enhancing both transparency and piezoelectricity. The transparency peaked at a total of 2.9 mol% of Sm and La co-doped in a ratio of 1 : 1. Among the investigated compositions, Pb0.913La0.0145Sm0.0145(Mg1/3Nb2/3)0.71Ti0.29O3 exhibited the highest transparency of 61%, with a piezoelectric coefficient of 1104 pC/N for the 1 mm thick samples. A sound pressure level of 66 dB was measured at a distance of 3 mm.
