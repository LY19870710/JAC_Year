## 1 Introduction

The proliferation of next-generation electronics, including 5G/6G systems, Internet of Things (IoT), and wearables, has dramatically increased human exposure to artificial electromagnetic (EM) waves. These EM waves, particularly in the microwave and radiofrequency ranges, have been linked to potential biological hazards, including DNA damage, oxidative stress, and neurological disorders [1−3]. Moreover, EM interference disrupts the normal operation of sensitive electronic equipment, affecting communication accuracy, medical diagnostics, and aerospace systems [4,5]. To mitigate these issues, the development of high-performance absorbers has become a critical research focus.

Among diverse candidate absorbers, metal-organic framework (MOF)-derived carbon materials have emerged as particularly promising candidates owing to their exceptional structural adaptability, which facilitates precise modulation of EM characteristics [6−8]. MOFs represent a class of crystalline porous coordination polymers formed through the spontaneous organization of metal clusters and multifunctional organic linkers. The well-defined periodic architectures of these materials endow them with extraordinary specific surface areas, tunable pore geometries, and abundant active sites [9,10]. More importantly, the chemical composition (e.g., types of metal nodes and ligand structures) and topological dimensions (one-dimensional (1D), two-dimensional (2D), or three-dimensional (3D)) of MOFs can be precisely regulated under controlled synthesis conditions, facilitating the directional design of dielectric and magnetic loss characteristics [11−13]. For example, the pyrolysis of zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs) centered on Zn or Co yields carbon-based composites with high dielectric loss [14,15], whereas Materials of Institute Lavoisier Frameworks (MIL)-series MOFs containing Fe or Cr tend to produce iron-based oxides with magnetic loss properties [16−18]. Additionally, strategies such as solvothermal synthesis, surfactant-assisted fabrication, or temperature regulation allow further optimization of MOF crystal orientation and microscopic morphology (e.g., core–shell structures, hollow polyhedra, or layered porous architectures), thereby enhancing multiple scattering and impedance matching characteristics of EM waves [19,20].

The pyrolytic transformation of MOFs is a critical step in obtaining high-performance absorbers. By precisely controlling pyrolysis conditions (e.g., temperature, atmosphere, and heating rate), MOF precursors can be converted into multicomponent composite materials, including metal/carbon hybrids, metal oxides, sulfides, or ferrites [21−23]. These materials typically exhibit significant synergistic effects. The carbon matrix provides high electrical conductivity and dielectric loss, while magnetic components (e.g., Fe3O4 and CoNi alloys) contribute to magnetic loss [24−26]. For example, Huang *et al*. [27] reported that an octadecahedral Co@N-doped carbon material achieved an effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) of 6.2 GHz at a thickness of 1.8 mm. Similarly, Deng *et al*. [28] developed Fe&TiO2@C composites that exhibit strong absorption capabilities due to multiple interfacial polarizations. These achievements underscore the immense potential of MOF-derived materials in EM protection applications.

Despite significant progress in experimental research on MOF-derived absorbers, the physical mechanisms underlying performance optimization remain inadequately understood. Most existing studies focus on characterizing macroscopic parameters (e.g., complex permittivity and permeability), while insufficient attention is given to the correlation between the microscopic electronic structure of materials (e.g., charge distribution, energy band characteristics, and density of states (DOS)) and EM response properties [29,30]. This cognitive gap severely hinders the rationalization of material design. Specifically, the intrinsic EM wave absorption mechanism of magnetic carbon materials involves complex multiscale physical processes. At the atomic scale, charge transfer at the metal–carbon hybrid interface may induce local dipole polarization. At the nanoscale, the size effect and dispersion of magnetic particles influence magnetic domain wall resonance. At the microscopic scale, multiple reflections caused by porous structures enhance EM energy dissipation [31,32]. Traditional experimental methods struggle to directly observe these microscopic phenomena, whereas theoretical simulations offer a crucial perspective for elucidating structure–property relationships. Notably, the related EM performance, which relies on the electronic/atomic structure of MOF-derived absorbers, has not been explored.

Herein, we report coral-like MOF-derived nickel–phosphorous@carbon (NP@C) nanocomposites by combining liquid-phase diffusion and high-temperature pyrolysis. Through density functional theory (DFT) calculations, we analyzed the electron DOS, charge density difference (CDD), and band structure of representative NP@C nanocomposites (e.g., C–Ni12P5 and C–Ni2P–Ni12P5), clarifying the contribution of the charge distribution at the metal–carbon interface to dielectric polarization. The local coordination environment of Ni in the coral-like NP@C materials was analyzed via X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectroscopy. This work reveals for the first time the interdependent relationship between the electronic structure and macroscopic EM parameters, providing a complete idea for the design of strong EM wave absorption materials.

## 2 Experimental

Reagents: 4,4'-Bismaleimide diphenylmethane (C21H14N2O4, 98%, BDM), 2,2'-diallyl bisphenol (C21H24O2, 90%, DBA), and phenylbis(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl)phosphine oxide (C26H27O3P, 97%) were purchased from Anage Chemical Co., Ltd. 1-Vinyl-2-pyrrolidone (C6H9NO, 99%, containing 100 ppm NaOH stabilizer), N,N-dimethylcarbamide (C3H7NO, 98%), and nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, 99.99%) was obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Co., Ltd.

Preparation of the MOF precursor: First, 20.0 g of DBA, 2.0 g of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, and 20.0 g of DMF were added to a beaker and heated to 135 °C. Next, 27.6 g BDM was added to the above liquor, and the mixture was vigorously stirred until BDM was completely dissolved, and then the temperature was reduced to 60 °C. After that, 2.0 g C26H27O3P and 20.0 g C6H9NO were added, and the mixed solution was placed in an ultraviolet environment for 30 min to obtain the MOF precursor via ultraviolet-assisted direct ink writing (UV-DIW).

MOF-derived NP@C nanocomposites: The obtained MOF precursor was heated at 5 °C/min to a preset temperature (800, 900, and 1000 °C) and kept in flowing argon for 4 h. Magnetic materials consisting of Ni2P–Ni12P5 nanoparticles embedded within a carbon matrix (denoted as NP@C) were obtained. The prepared samples were designated NP@C-800, NP@C-900, and NP@C-1000 according to the heating temperature.

Characterization: The crystalline structure of the MOF-derived NP@C nanocomposites was inspected via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X'Pert PRO, PANalytical B.V., Netherlands). Morphologies were observed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM), and Cs-corrected TEM. The band gap of the material was measured via an ultraviolet and visible spectrophotometer (UV–Vis; UV-3600 plus, Shimadzu, Japan). To investigate the EM properties of the products, the as-prepared samples were mixed with paraffin wax and then cut into a ring shape with an outer diameter of 7.0 mm and an inner diameter of 3.04 mm. The EM parameters were determined via a vector network analyzer (VNA; E8362B, Agilent, USA). In this work, 40 wt% NP@C products were evenly mixed with a paraffin wax matrix and measured at 2–18 GHz. The radar cross section (RCS) plots were simulated via the CST Studio Suite.

First-principles calculations were performed within the framework of density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Vienna *ab* *initio* simulation package (VASP). Electronic structure calculations were performed via periodic DFT within the CP2K software package, utilizing its Gaussian and plane wave basis set implementation in the Quickstep module. The exploratory study of the catalyst structure employed the molecular-optimized basis sets DZVP–MOLOPT–SR–GTH for each atom and the Goedecker–Teter–Hutter (GTH) pseudopotential. We implemented the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof-generalized gradient approximation (PBE-GGA) exchange-correlation functional with van der Waals (DFT–D3) corrections, enforcing an SCF convergence threshold of 5×10−6 Hartree for all calculations. An energy truncation value of 400 Ry was used throughout the calculation process. The input file was generated by Multiwfn.

## 3 Results and discussion

At the atomic scale, analysis of the chemical properties and coordination of MOF-derived NP@C phases is crucial for adjusting the cell fraction and grain boundaries. In this work, the synthetic procedure for NP@C is initially demonstrated in Fig. 1. The MOF precursor was first prepared via the liquid phase diffusion method and then hydrogenated to obtain coralloid NP@C products at different temperatures ranging from 800 to 1000 °C (Fig. 1(a)). Typically, the MOF precursor can be formed through the coupling of metallic Ni ions with organic ligands. Figure 1(b) shows the XRD patterns of the MOF-derived NP@C nanocomposites produced at different pyrolysis temperatures. The distinct peak clusters of the NP@C samples emerge after high-temperature calcination, suggesting the presence of amorphous carbon. Moreover, the diffraction peak intensity is high because of the thin carbon layer on the surface of the NP@C nanocomposites. The diffraction peaks of the (310), (112), (400), (330), (420), and (312) crystal planes correspond to Ni12P5 (JCPDS card No. 74-1381) [33]. The diffraction peak of the (111) crystal plane belongs to Ni2P (JCPDS card No. 03-0953), and the characteristic peaks of Ni3(PO4)2 (JCPDS card No. 70-1796) appear at approximately 2*θ* = 25.9° and 35.5° [34]. The XRD patterns show that the crystallinity of the samples improves significantly with increasing calcination temperature, as evidenced by the sharpening and intensification of the diffraction peaks.

Raman spectroscopy was performed to investigate defects or disordered structures in the NP@C nanocomposites prepared at different pyrolysis temperatures (Fig. 2(a)). The Raman spectrum of the composite exhibited two characteristic bands at 1350 cm−1 (D-band) and 1580 cm−1 (G-band), corresponding to disordered carbon structures and graphitic domains, respectively. The intensity ratio I*D/*I*G of the characteristic peaks can characterize the degree of defects in carbon materials to a certain extent. The higher the ratio is, the greater the defect density. The *I*D/*I*G values of NP@C-800–NP@C-1000 samples are 0.56, 0.69, and 1.17, respectively. Graphite is highly ordered, with an *I*D/*I*G close to 0, whereas the reduced graphene oxide (rGO) group has many defects, with an *I*D/*I*G typically ranging from 1.0 to 1.5. The degree of graphitization of NP@C-800 and NP@C-900 is between that of graphite and rGO, whereas that of NP@C-1000 is more inclined toward reduced graphene oxide (rGO). Ni, as the magnetic component, attenuates EM waves through natural resonance and eddy current loss. The carbon matrix in NP@C-800 is highly ordered, and the conductive network is relatively complete. This structure mainly results in conductive loss through the migration of free electrons, but the interfacial polarization effect is relatively weak, resulting in dielectric loss that mainly relies on the conductivity of the material itself. The NP@C-900 product introduces moderate defects. These defects not only increase the dielectric loss through the relaxation polarization of the sp2/sp3 hybrid interface but also may serve as anchor sites for Ni particles, optimizing the interfacial coupling between the magnetic components and the carbon matrix. NP@C-1000 is a highly defective carbon matrix in which the carbon matrix has many disordered structures and defects. The high defect density significantly enhances the interfacial polarization effect, thereby greatly increasing the dielectric loss. However, too many defects disrupt the continuity of the carbon skeleton, resulting in reduced conductivity and weakening the contribution of conductance loss. Therefore, under the synergy of polarization loss and electrical loss, the NP@C-900 sample may be more suitable for wideband absorption.

To further analyze the microstructure of the composites, TEM analysis was performed on NP@C-900 and NP@C-1000. As shown in Figs. 3(a)–3(c), the NP@C-900 sample is composed of amorphous carbon, and many Ni nanoparticles are distributed within it. This indicates that after high-temperature pyrolysis, the metal atoms undergo agglomeration. While the carbon layer connects and supports the nanoparticles, it also forms a conductive network system among the metal particles. When the incident electron beam direction of the NP@C-900 sample is aligned with the crystal zone axis, the interplanar spacings of 0.22, 0.19, and 0.30 nm correspond to the (2* 2¯0), ( 1¯1¯2¯), and ( 13¯2¯) crystal planes of Ni12P5, respectively [35]. In Figs. 3(d) and 3(e), the diffraction ring belongs to Ni12P5, and the diffraction points belong to the ( 23¯1¯), ( 112¯), and ( 1¯41¯) crystal planes. Ni-based nanoparticles are mainly composed of Ni12P5 and Ni2P. As shown in Figs. 3(f)–3(h), when the pyrolysis temperature increases to 1000 °C, the Ni nanoparticles decompose and disperse in the amorphous carbon, but their crystal structure does not change significantly and is still dominated by Ni12P5. Figures 3(i)–3(l) show the elemental mapping of the NP@C-900 material. The distributions of Ni and P are clearly consistent with the contours of the TEM image. Ni and P together form NP@C nanoparticles. The other elements are uniformly distributed in the NP@C-900 material. There is no agglomeration of elements in any place.

As shown in Fig. 4, the EM parameters of the MOF-derived NP@C nanocomposites were tested in the frequency range of 2–18 GHz. As shown in Figs. 4(a)–4(c), the real part of complex permittivity (ε'*) values of the NP@C samples gradually increased as the pyrolysis temperature increased. Moreover, the *ε'* values of the NP@C samples show a fluctuating downward trend, whereas the imaginary part of complex permittivity (*ε''*) values of the samples fluctuate more. However, when the pyrolysis temperature increases to 1000 °C, *ε''* decreases sharply and tends to 0. It could be inferred that the formation of coral-like structures in the material promoted the formation of a conductive network. After the pyrolysis temperature increases, the value of the NP@C sample first increases and then decreases, and the degree of fluctuation decreases at the same time. This is mainly because the change in the material morphology results in the formation of numerous heterogeneous interfaces, enhancing the interfacial polarization and conductance loss [36,37]. In addition, when the pyrolysis temperature reached 900 °C, three resonance peaks gradually appeared in the composite, more than at the other two pyrolysis temperatures, indicating the presence of more multipolarization losses in NP@C-900, which, to some extent, contributed to the improvement in the absorbing capacity of the material.

Figure 5 shows the reflectance plots and 3D reflectance plots of NP@C at different pyrolysis temperatures. For NP@C-900, there is an effective absorption of less than –10 dB within the frequency range of 7.34–9.36 GHz (2.02 GHz), and the minimum reflective loss (RLmin) is –68.2 dB at 8.6 GHz (2.49 mm) (Figs. 5(d) and 5(g)). In summary, compared with the other two pyrolysis temperatures, NP@C-1000 achieves a smaller peak reflectance and a larger effective bandwidth and has better wave absorption performance.

The differences in the absorption properties of the NP@C-800–NP@C-1000 products were analyzed as follows. In Fig. 5(c), the attenuation constant (α*) of NP@C first increases but then decreases with increasing pyrolysis temperature. The NP@C-900 sample has the highest attenuation coefficient, which is attributed to the fact that as the carbonization temperature increases, the size of the conductive surface domain increases, resulting in increased conductivity, thus increasing conductivity loss. The area of the NP@C-1000 sample that meets the normalized input impedance required for effective absorption (RL ˂ −10 dB) is larger than that of the other samples. Therefore, it seems that the absorbing performance of these NP@C samples is related to the pyrolysis temperature in view of their phase evolution. In short, among these annealed NP@C materials, the optimal NP@C-1000 sample is derived from the appropriate EM attenuation ability and best impedance matching.

The local electronic and geometric structure of the Ni sites in NP@C was probed via X-ray absorption spectroscopy, which combines X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) techniques for comprehensive analysis. Figure 7(a) shows the XANES spectra corresponding to the Ni edge of NP@C as a reference. The absorption edge of NP@C shifts toward higher energy (blueshift) relative to that of the Ni foil, indicating that Ni is in a higher oxidation state in the composite. If the spectral line of NP@C is smoother and the EXAFS oscillation is weak, combined with the previous analysis, nickel is highly dispersed in the amorphous carbon. As shown in Fig. 7(b), the intensity of the front peak of NP@C is significantly greater than that of the Ni foil. As shown in Figs. 7(c) and 7(d), the DOSs of both samples are non-zero near the Fermi level, with electronic states crossing the Fermi level directly, indicating the absence of a significant band gap. The material is metallic or semimetallic. The material mainly experiences conductivity loss due to free electron migration. There are sharp DOS peaks formed by the 3d orbitals of Ni near the Fermi level. Moreover, the carbon matrix contributes wide peaks within the range from −10 to −5 eV, indicating that the conductance loss, magnetic loss, and dielectric loss of the NP@C nanomaterials. The synergistic effect of this multiloss mechanism enables it to have a relatively low reflectivity over a wide frequency band. Especially at a specific thickness, efficient wave absorption can be achieved through impedance matching. The DOS absolute value of the NP@C samples near the Fermi level is as high as −200–200, showing typical metallic properties, with a high concentration of free carriers and dominant conductive losses. The DOS of NP@C is asymmetric near the Fermi level, the DOS fluctuates greatly in the high-energy region, the spin polarization characteristics are more prominent, and the spin transitions related to magnetic domains may increase the mid- and high-frequency magnetic loss. The magnetic domain effect of the sample is relatively weak, and the loss is based mainly on the conductivity mechanism [38,39]. Its reflectance curve may weaken, and the reflectance decreases at high frequencies; however, at low frequencies, the reflectance is relatively high because EM waves have difficulty penetrating the material surface. In addition, owing to the lack of magnetic element contributions, the magnetic loss of the sample is negligible, resulting in the absorption performance being dependent mainly on conductive loss and the band adaptability and absorption efficiency being weaker than those of NP@C.

The difference in charge transfer reflects the influence of the electronic behavior between the interfaces of different materials on the dissipation of EM wave energy [40]. The lower charge transfer value (0.66e) of C–Ni12P5 indicates that electrons are enriched in a specific area at the interface, which may increase the conductivity of the material and reduce resistance loss (Fig. 7(e)). This charge distribution promotes the conduction of EM waves within the material, reduces reflection, and thereby enhances the absorption efficiency of EM waves. However, if the charge is overly localized, it may cause a local concentration of the electric field, resulting in an increase in dielectric loss. The lower charge transfer value (0.20e) of C–Ni2P–Ni12P5 in Fig. 7(f) indicates that electron transfer at the interface is relatively efficient, with a small interfacial potential barrier, suggesting that EM dissipation may be facilitated through a dielectric polarization mechanism. In this case, the reflection and transmission of EM waves at the interface tend to be balanced, and the energy loss may be mainly dielectric relaxation or dipole polarization, which is suitable for wideband EM wave absorption applications. The negative charge transfer of C–Ni2P–Ni12P5 implies the loss of electrons from the interface region, which may lead to the formation of charge defects or local electric field distortion at the interface. This characteristic can promote the multipath dissipation of EM wave energy by enhancing the interfacial polarization effect or introducing defect states and can be absorbed through defect-induced electronic transitions or interfacial resonance [41–44]. Negative charge transfer can also suppress the overall conductivity of a material, increase eddy current loss, and promote its absorption of EM waves in the high-frequency band.

In Fig. 7(g), the different color areas reflect the differences in charge density. The distribution of the yellow and blue regions in the C–Ni12P5 system indicates the existence of an uneven distribution of charges, which is conducive to the formation of electric dipoles. Under the influence of EM waves, these electric dipoles attempt to align with the direction of the electric field, constantly flipping and adjusting their directions, thereby generating polarization losses, converting EM energy into thermal energy, and enhancing the absorption of EM waves [45–47]. The C–Ni2P–Ni12P5 system has a multilayer structure, and the charge density distribution between different layers is different, which causes multiple reflections and refractions of EM waves between the layers. During each reflection and refraction process, polarization loss and conductance loss occur due to charge polarization and charge transfer at the interface [48, 49]. At the interfaces of different layers, charges are redistributed to form electric dipoles and generate electron migration to form current, thereby absorbing EM wave energy.

The electronic states of Ni and P in C–Ni12P5 exhibit relatively high density values near the Fermi level, indicating that the material has significant electronic activity in this energy region (Fig. 7(h)). The hybridization of the p orbitals of Ni and the p/d orbitals of p may result in extensive band overlap, enhancing the electrical conductivity of the material. High electrical conductivity usually corresponds to lower resistance loss, which is conducive to the conduction of EM waves within the material and energy dissipation [50–52]. However, the state density near the Fermi level is overly concentrated, which may lead to electronic localization, limit the polarization ability, and thereby weaken the contribution of dielectric loss. The DOS distribution of the C–Ni2P–Ni12P5 heterostructure shows that the electronic states of N and P extend over a wider energy range, especially with multiple hybrid peaks near the Fermi level (Fig. 7(i)). This characteristic indicates that band reconstruction at heterogeneous interfaces may introduce interface polarization centers or defect-induced local states. These additional electronic states can significantly increase dielectric loss through dipole polarization or defect-related electronic transitions. Moreover, owing to the transfer of negative charges, the loss of electrons at the interface results in charge defects or local electric field distortions, further promoting the interface polarization effect [53]. The band matching characteristics of heterogeneous structures may optimize the impedance matching of materials.

To investigate the far-field EM wave absorption performance in practical applications, different NP@C absorbers were simulated in the form of coatings by the CST Studio Suite. From the 3D RCS diagram (Figs. 8(a)–8(c)), the radar scattering signal of the NP@C-1000 sample was weaker than that of the other NP@C absorbers, implying appropriate EM absorption for NP@C-1000. In addition, the RCS curves (Fig. 8(d)) indicate the scattered areas at different incident angles. As expected, the minimum RCS value of NP@C-1000 reaches −21.94 dBm2. In short, the stable RCS curve of NP@C-1000 at various incident angles and the simulation results that align with the experimental absorption characteristics indicate that NP@C-1000 has significant potential for practical applications in microwave absorption.

As shown in Fig. 8(e), the EM wave absorption mechanism of the coral-inspired NP@C absorber is attributed primarily to its multicomponent heterostructure and hierarchical architecture, which work synergistically to increase EM energy dissipation through conductive, dielectric, and magnetic loss mechanisms. The composite material consists of Ni nanoparticles with a Ni12P5 core/Ni2P shell heterostructure that is uniformly dispersed within an amorphous carbon matrix. The distinct crystallographic orientation and interfacial coupling within this system contribute to enhanced EM loss across multiple scales. Theoretical calculations indicate that the charge transfer (0.66e) occurring at the C–Ni12P5 interface significantly improves conductive loss by facilitating electron migration through the interconnected carbon framework. Concurrently, the C–Ni2P–Ni12P5 heterostructure promotes interfacial polarization and the formation of defect states due to negative charge redistribution (−0.20e), thereby enhancing dipole polarization and relaxation losses associated with structural defects. Electron structure analysis further revealed that the coexistence of sharp Ni 3d orbital peaks near the Fermi level and broad electronic states from the carbon matrix enables dual-loss mechanisms: Ni-derived electronic states contribute to spin-related magnetic loss processes, such as natural resonance and exchange resonance, whereas the conductive carbon network supports Ohmic loss via Joule heating. In total, the thermally processed NP@C composite exhibited a biomimetic coral-like architecture that established an interconnected conductive network for EM waves. This network facilitates the transformation of EM energy into electrical current while simultaneously creating extended pathways for electron transport, thereby enhancing conductive dissipation [54]. Furthermore, the porous design of the material amplifies the multiple reflections and scattering phenomena of incident EM waves [55]. By harmonizing microwave attenuation mechanisms across multiple scales, the absorber achieves synergistic improvements in energy dissipation, impedance matching, and EM field concentration, resulting in broad bandwidth absorption performance. Overall, the NP@C absorber integrates multiscale EM loss mechanisms, delivering superior wideband absorption with strong attenuation capabilities.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, magnetic MOF-derived carbon materials were prepared via self-templating and restricted conversion strategies. Through precise component and structural design, a perfect magnetoelectric balance was achieved, demonstrating excellent absorbing capacity. These findings indicate that the pyrolysis temperature has a profound effect on the crystallinity and morphology of the material. The Ni12P5/Ni2P heterostructure and spherical carbon matrix formed at 1000 °C provide abundant heterointerfaces and conductive networks for effective EM wave interactions. The moderate carbon defect level of NP@C-1000 achieves an optimal balance between conductance loss and interfacial polarization, whereas the synergistic effect of Ni nanoparticles embedded in the carbon matrix enhances natural resonance and eddy current loss. NP@C-1000 exhibits full-band effective absorption (RL 12P5/carbon interface enhances the electrical conductivity, whereas the relatively low charge transfer (0.20e) within the C–Ni2P/Ni12P5 heterostructure promotes dielectric polarization, further elucidating its broadband absorption mechanism. In summary, the NP@C-1000 sample demonstrates remarkable potential for wideband EM wave absorption through structural design and compositional control, offering new insights into the development of high-performance absorbing materials.
