## 1 Introduction

Recently, solid-state lighting (SSL) has experienced explosive growth worldwide, and in particular, phosphor-converted SSL technology has been broadly implemented in various fields, benefiting from its revolutionary features of high brightness and efficiency, reliability, long service life, energy savings, environmental friendliness, compact size, etc. [1−6]. In the current SSL market, the prevailing lighting scheme involves a blue light-emitting diode (LED) or laser diode (LD) chip (450–460 nm) at the junction with a broadband (full width at half maximum; FWHM ≈ 125 nm) yellow-emitting Y3Al5O12(YAG):Ce3+ material to produce cool white light; however, its performance is usually compromised by poor spectral continuity, leading to duller and unrealistic colors [7−9]. In addition, it may also give rise to a chronic blue light exposure hazard, which has been proven to be harmful to physiological and behavioral rhythms, especially for eyesight [10−12]. In the context of increasing focus on a high-quality and healthy lifestyle, the pursuit has evolved from high brightness toward full consideration of high luminance, an ultrahigh color rendering index (CRI) and health for next-generation lighting [13]. Thus, human-oriented full-spectrum lighting that simulates the solar spectrum can be expected, providing visual comfort and presenting objects in more vivid and realistic colors. In this case, a full-visible-spectrum lighting scheme, which is based on violet LED/LD (400–420 nm) chips coupled with polychromatic luminescent materials, is a compelling solution, featuring high color fidelity and marvelous similarity to sunlight [14,15]. Impressively, it not only compensates for the absence of spectral ingredients, especially the violet components, but also avoids the risk of blue light exposure [16]. On the other hand, it can be more efficient than the “near-ultraviolet (NUV) chip coupled with polychromatic materials” lighting scheme since the external efficiency of violet chips is approximately 80%, which is much greater than that of 365 nm NUV chips (~44%) [17,18].

Unfortunately, for such a proposal, most existing luminescent materials have been developed for conventional lighting schemes and are efficiently excited by blue or NUV light, but their optical properties are far from impressive under violet light irradiation [19]. Taking the well-known YAG:Ce3+ phosphor as an example, the photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) value decreases from 97% to 75% (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) when the excitation wavelength is altered from 450 nm blue light to 410 nm violet light, owing to unsatisfactory absorption of violet light. Indeed, it would become less competitive and may lose market dominance with the advent of the full-spectrum lighting era. To the best of our knowledge, there are few excellent violet-light-excitable yellow luminescent materials for SSL (Table 1), highlighting the pressing need to develop high-performance materials of this type.

As an alternative, the yellow-emitting Ba2Sc2B4O11(BSB):Ce3+ phosphor reported by Du *et al*. [20] creates the perfect fit for violet light excitation but is extremely limited by the modest PLQY value of 53.3%. The standard BSB lattice belongs to the monoclinic space group, which consists of [BaO7], [Sc1O6], [Sc2O6], and [BO3] polyhedrons. However, resonance bands attributed to the [BO4] tetrahedrons emerge in the 11B magic angle spinning nuclear magnetic resonance (MAS-NMR) spectrum of the BSB:Ce3+ phosphor (Fig. S2 in the ESM), since an amorphous phase is likely produced in the form of [BO3] and [BO4] polyhedrons and mixed with the crystalline BSB phase during high-temperature sintering. This usually leads to low crystallinity and high-density defects in the BSB:Ce3+ phosphor, which are detrimental to luminescence. However, every cloud has a silver lining. The characteristics of borates that are easily compatible with glass provide an opportunity for growing a BSB:Ce3+ lattice within a borate glass matrix, enabling the fabrication of BSB:Ce3+ glass ceramic (GC) composites via *in situ* glass crystallization. Unlike the high-temperature solid-state method, the glass network structure serves as an optimal medium for the nucleation and growth of micro/nanocrystals (NCs) while inevitably restricting ion diffusion, thereby effectively regulating the overall properties of GC composites [21]. Notably, glass structural rearrangement plays a vital role in facilitating the precipitation of desired micro/nanocrystals within the glass matrix and the partitioning of activators [22,23]. Moreover, the unique composite structure of GC composites enables highly desirable advantages, such as adaptable design features, low cost, ease of manufacture, remarkable luminescent properties, and excellent physical/chemical/thermal stabilities, positioning them as promising candidates for advancing SSL technology [24−27]. As a consequence, accessing violet-light-excitable BSB:Ce3+ GC composites for full-spectrum SSL is a challenging but rewarding endeavor.

Next, glass network engineering of the B2O3–BaO–Sc2O3 system is proposed to optimize the *in situ* glass crystallization process of BSB:Ce3+ GCs. Structural and spectroscopic characterization revealed that regulation of the [BO3]/[BO4] ratio facilitates heterogeneous nucleation and improves the crystallinity of BSB NCs, enabling a record PLQY of 95.0% among various reported violet light-excitable luminescent materials (Table 1). Under 410 nm violet light excitation, BSB:Ce3+ GC exhibited bright yellow emission centered at 540 nm with an FWHM of 127 nm, perfectly matching the requirements for violet-light-excitable full-spectrum light. Moreover, as a bulk material, it offers superior suitability for SSL applications compared with other materials. Finally, full-spectrum LED/LD lighting with superior performance can be achieved by adopting BSB:Ce3+ GC as the yellow-emitting color converter. These results suggest that the BSB:Ce3+ GC composite is highly promising for violet-light-excitable full-spectrum lighting, benefiting from glass network engineering. This work not only deepens the understanding of GC composites but also advances their potential application for high-performance full-spectrum SSL.

### 2.1 Materials and methods

Fabrication of BSB:Ce3+ GCs: Precursor glasses were prepared via the traditional melt-quenching method, with carefully designed compositions of *x*B2O3–2BaO–Sc2O3–*y*CeO2 (*x* = 2.0–3.0, *y* = 0.01–0.15). The raw materials were weighed and ground in an agate mortar for 60 min, after which the mixture was melted at 1100 °C for 90 min in a reducing atmosphere (10% H2/90% N2). After cooling with the furnace naturally to room temperature, the precursor glasses were formed and marked as PGs. For *in situ* glass crystallization, the PGs were maintained at 900 °C for 10 h in a reducing atmosphere (10% H2/90% N2 to obtain GC samples, which were denoted as GCs. The related PG and GC samples were cut and polished to specific dimensions for subsequent characterization.

Fabrication of GC-based white LED/LD devices: A typical GC-based white LED (GC-WLED) is constructed to generate high-quality white light by adopting BSB:Ce3+ GC as the yellow-emitting color converter, which is concurrently coupled with a 410 nm violet LED chip, commercial blue-cyan BaZrSi3O9:Eu2+ (BZS:Eu2+), and red CaAlSiN3:Eu2+ (CASN:Eu2+) phosphors. To fabricate a GC-based white LD (GC-WLD) device, a 1.0 mm-thick BSB:Ce3+ GC substrate was prepared via the phosphor-in-glass film (PiGF) technique and subsequently coated with commercial blue BaMgAl10O17:Eu2+ (BAM:Eu2+) and red CASN:Eu2+ phosphors.

### 2.2 Characterization

A differential scanning calorimeter (STA449F3, NETZSCH, Germany) at a heating rate of 10 °C/min was used to plot the differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curve. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the PG and GC samples were collected via an X-ray diffractometer (D8, Bruker, Germany) equipped with Cu-Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5406 Å) operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. XRD Rietveld refinements were obtained using the General Structure Analysis System (GSAS) program. For morphological characterization, a field emission scanning electron microscope (SU8010, HITACHI, Japan) and a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM; JEM-2010, JEOL, Japan) in combination with an energy dispersive spectrometer were used to record the morphology and element mapping of the GCs. To determine the elemental valence, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data were obtained with an XPS (K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific, USA). A Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR; VEC-TOR33, Bruker, UK) was used. MAS-NMR spectra were recorded with a MAS-NMR spectrometer (Avance Neo 400WB, Bruker, UK) with boric acid (H3BO3) solution used as the reference. For optical characterization, photoluminescence excitation (PLE), photoluminescence (PL), and PLQY spectra of the PG and GC samples were recorded via a fluorescence spectrophotometer (FLS1000, Edinburgh, UK) equipped with a 450 W continuous xenon lamp as the excitation source, whereas a pulsed laser at 375 nm was used for measuring fluorescence decay curves and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL). For the temperature-dependent emission spectra measurements, the sample temperatures were controlled by a heating/cooling workbench (Linkam THMS600E, 300–475 K). The electroluminescence (EL) spectra and related parameters of the constructed LED devices were measured with an LED measuring instrument (PMA-12, Hamamatsu, Japan) equipped with an optical fiber integrating sphere (30 cm in diameter; Labsphere Inc., USA). To assess the lighting performance, a charge-coupled device (CCD) spectrometer (OHSP-350 M, HopooColor Technology Co., Ltd., China) integrated with an integrating sphere was employed to record the relevant parameters.

## 3 Results and discussion

As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), the ternary phase diagram of the B2O3–BaO–Sc2O3 system elucidates the intricate relationship between composition and crystal structure [32], serving as a guide for compositional design to achieve the BSB lattice. Therefore, the chemical composition (in molar ratio) of PGs is specifically designed as *x*B2O3–2BaO–Sc2O3–*y*CeO2 (*x* = 2.0–3.0, *y* = 0.01–0.15), and PGs are prepared via a traditional melt-quenching method. According to the DSC curve of the B2O3–BaO–Sc2O3 system (Fig. S3 in the ESM), the related thermodynamic parameters, including the glass transition temperature *T*g, the initial crystallization temperature *T*x, the maximum crystallization temperature *T*p, and the melting temperature *T*m, are estimated as 522, 596, 903, and 1073 °C, respectively. Notably, the low *T*x temperature and small temperature difference ∆*T* (*T*x − *T*g) indicate that PG has great potential for *in situ* glass crystallization [33]. As shown in Fig. 1(b), via *in situ* glass crystallization, the nucleation and growth of BSB NCs occur at interstitial positions within the glass network, which is composed of [BO3] and [BO4] polyhedrons, to form the BSB:Ce3+ GC composite.

Furthermore, Fig. 1(c) shows the PLE and PL spectra of the PG and GC samples, as well as those of the YAG:Ce3+ phosphor, where broadbands assigned to typical 4f–5d transitions of Ce3+ activators can be observed. Notably, for PG, the blue emission band centered at 420 nm originates from the disordered environment of Ce3+ in glass; in contrast, the characteristic yellow emission band centered at 540 nm for the GC, deconvoluted into two bands centered at 16,835 cm−1 (594 nm) and 18,832 cm−1 (531 nm) (Fig. S4 in the ESM), originates from the transitions from the 5d level to the ground 2F7/2 and 2F5/2 states of Ce3+-activators in the ordered local environment of BSB NCs [34]. The same trend is observed for the excitation spectra, which is powerful evidence for the partition of Ce3+ into the BSB NCs. In addition, the emission band profile is extremely similar to that of the YAG:Ce3+ phosphor. However, it is widely divergent from the excitation bands. The optimal excitation band centered at 410 nm of BSB:Ce3+ GC coincides exactly with violet LED chips (Figs. S5 and S6 in the ESM), contributing to violet-light-excitable full-spectrum SSL [35].

Remarkably, Fig. 1(d) shows that the BSB:Ce3+ GC achieves a record internal quantum efficiency (IQE) of 95.0% and an external quantum efficiency (EQE) of 46%, significantly outperforming the BSB:Ce3+ and YAG:Ce3+ phosphors. As discussed above, this can be attributed to the inherent limitations of the BSB:Ce3+ and YAG:Ce3+ phosphors, which suffer from poor crystallinity and low violet light absorption, respectively. Notably, glass network engineering by regulating the B2O3 content (*x* = 2.0, 2.2, 2.4, 2.6, 2.8, and 3.0) is responsible for the remarkable optical performance of the BSB:Ce3+ GC. Both the emission intensity and PLQY followed identical trends of initial increase followed by a decrease, with an optimal point at *x* = 2.6 (Figs. S7 and S8 in the ESM). The PL decay curves are plotted in Fig. S9 in the ESM, and the average lifetimes can be obtained, as tabulated in Note S1 and Table S1 in the ESM. Correspondingly, the radiative transition rate (*A*rad) and nonradiative transition rate (*A*nnon-rad) can be estimated via Eqs. (1) and (2) [36]:

(1) Arad=ητave

(2) Anon−rad=1τave−Arad

where *η* refers to the value of the PLQY and * τave represents the average lifetime. Compared with the BSB:Ce3+ phosphor, BSB:Ce3+ GCs presented significantly increased A*rad but decreased *A*nnon-rad (Fig. 1(e) and Table S1 in the ESM), contributing to their optical performance. In particular, the optical performance of the BSB:Ce3+ GC composite reaches its optimum at *x* = 2.6, which is consistent with both the emission intensity and PLQY. It is concluded that regulating the glass network through B2O3 modification is feasible for optimizing the luminescence of BSB:Ce3+ GC composites.

Figure 2(a) shows the XRD patterns that indicate the precipitation of BSB NCs within the glass matrix. For PG, amorphous humps originating from borate glass can be observed, whereas after *in situ* glass crystallization, sharp diffraction peaks coincide precisely with the BSB (#ICSD-86436) phase, verifying the successful precipitation of BSB NCs in the borate glass matrix [37]. As shown in Table S2 in the ESM, the refinement results with detailed crystallographic parameters of the BSB:Ce3+ GC and phosphor samples are reliable (Fig. S10 in the ESM). Additionally, the crystallinity of BSB:Ce3+ GCs tends to increase first but then decreases, with a maximal crystalline fraction of 92.3%, as the B2O3 content increases via glass network engineering (Fig. S11 in the ESM). Notably, the gradual shift of the XRD diffraction peaks of the GCs toward larger angles with increasing Ce3+ content (Fig. S12 in the ESM) indicates that the lattice shrinks, confirming that Ce3+ activators prefer to occupy the larger Ba2+ sites in the BSB lattice rather than the smaller Sc3+ sites. Additionally, the TRPL spectra of the BSB:Ce3+ GC verify the exclusive lattice occupation of Ce3+ ions at the Ba2+ site (Fig. S13 in the ESM). As illustrated in Fig. S14 in the ESM, the optimal doping concentration of Ce3+ is *y* = 0.09. Moreover, Fig. 2(b) shows digital photographs of the PG and GC samples under daylight and ultraviolet (UV) light irradiation. As recognized by the naked eye, the PG is transparent, whereas the GC is opaque and yellow, indicating the existence of BSB:Ce3+ NCs in the glass. Under UV light irradiation, blue and bright yellow emissions for PG and GC, respectively, were detected. TEM image (Fig. 2(c)) confirms the coexistence of the amorphous and crystalline BSB phases. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (inset in Fig. 2(c)) shows discrete single-crystal diffraction features, confirming the high crystallinity of the BSB:Ce3+ particles. In the HRTEM image shown in Fig. 2(d), explicit and uniformly arranged lattice fringes can be observed with a lattice spacing of 0.3308 nm (Fig. 2(e)), matching precisely with that of the (221) crystal plane of the BSB phase. As presented in Fig. 2(f), the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) lattices are attributed to the (221), (002), (311), and (202) crystal planes of the BSB phase, in agreement with the XRD results. Furthermore, the element map shown in Fig. 2(g) confirms the homogeneous distributions of Ba, Sc, B, O, and Ce. Hence, all the results revealed the precipitation of BSB NCs with high crystallinity in the glass matrix.

To gain insight into the mechanism of glass network engineering, structural characterizations of the PG and GC samples were performed, as depicted in Fig. 3. Furthermore, XPS full survey profiles confirmed the presence of Ba, Sc, B, O, and Ce in the PG and GC samples (Fig. S15 in the ESM). Notably, typical peaks at binding energies of 889.0 and 903.1 eV correspond to Ce3+:3d5/2 and 3d3/2, indicating the mere existence of Ce3+ rather than Ce4+ [38] (Fig. S16 in the ESM). As shown in Fig. 3(a), the high-solution XPS profiles of B 1s for all the PG and GC samples can be deconvoluted into two peaks at binding energies of 191.9 and 192.7 eV, coinciding with [BO3] triangles and [BO4] tetrahedrons [39]. Evidently, the intensity of the [BO4] tetrahedrons for the GCs increases with increasing B2O3 addition. From the 11B MAS-NMR spectra in Fig. 3(b) and Fig. S17 in the ESM, the typical resonance bands are assigned to the [BO3] (5–20 ppm) and [BO4] ((−10)–5 ppm) groups, respectively [40]. Moreover, there is a systematic increase in the [BO4] tetrahedral intensity compared with that of the [BO3] triangular units. As illustrated in Fig. 3(c) and Fig. S18 in the ESM, the FTIR spectra can be categorized into three primary regions at 550–800, 800–1250, and 1330–1525 cm−1, corresponding to the B–O–B bending vibrations, [BO4] stretching vibrations, and [BO3] stretching vibrations, respectively [41−43]. The pronounced difference in spectral line broadening for PG and GCs further corroborates the structural evolution from disordered glass to ordered NCs [44]. These results indicate the successful precipitation of BSB:Ce3+ NCs and confirm the evolution of the glass structure upon B2O3 addition.

The mechanism underlying the increase in luminescence via glass network engineering can be attributed to the adjustment of the relative contents of the [BO3] and [BO4] groups, which fundamentally affects the *in situ* glass crystallization process. For borate glass systems, [BO3] triangles usually transform into [BO4] tetrahedrons with the assistance of BaO, a phenomenon known as the “boron anomaly” [45]. This transformation inevitably induces structural evolution from a relatively loose layered architecture to a more compact three-dimensional framework for the glass network [46]. Hence, [BO3] triangles and [BO4] tetrahedrons coexist in the PG, as confirmed by XPS, NMR, and FTIR analyses. On the other hand, in GC, boron functions not only as glass network builders in the form of coexisting [BO3] triangles and [BO4] tetrahedrons but also as BSB NC participants exclusively involving [BO3] triangles. In other words, the glass matrix accommodates the [BO4] tetrahedrons instead of the BSB lattice, significantly differing from the BSB:Ce3+ phosphor fabricated via the high-temperature solid-state method. As illustrated in Fig. 3(d), the glass network initially contains a relatively high proportion of [BO3] triangles and homogeneous nucleation domains, allowing the disordered [BO3] groups in the glass to precipitate into the BSB NCs along with *in situ* glass crystallization. Nevertheless, this nucleation process typically occurs within localized [BO3]-enriched regions, which may arise from transient compositional inhomogeneities caused by molecular motion or thermal perturbations [47]. Owing to the inherently high energy barrier or the absence of preferential nucleation sites for homogeneous nucleation [48], it suffers from relatively low nucleation density, leading to suboptimal crystallinity of BSB NCs. As the B2O3 content increases, the [BO3]/[BO4] ratio decreases, and the enhanced interfaces, structural defects, and compositionally heterogeneous microregions function as preferential nucleation centers for heterogeneous nucleation, offering thermodynamically favorable sites for *in situ* glass crystallization [49]. This results in a marked increase in the nucleation density compared with homogeneous nucleation, thereby promoting the rapid formation of BSB NCs (Fig. 3(d)). However, when the B2O3 content exceeds the optimal level, excessive formation of [BO4] tetrahedra relative to [BO3] triangles leads to an overly dense glass network with a compact and rigid three-dimensional framework-like structure, limiting ion diffusion and nucleation/growth processes [50]. This, in turn, makes crystallization more difficult than before, which is detrimental to luminescence. Figure 3(e) depicts the crystallization behavior of BSB GCs with increasing B2O3 content, revealing an initial increase in crystallinity followed by a decrease, with a maximum at *x* = 2.6. XRD analysis confirmed this trend, revealing a maximal crystalline fraction of 92.3% at the same composition (Fig. S11 in the ESM). As a consequence, the appropriate regulation of the [BO3]/[BO4] ratio on the glass network contributes positively to enhancing the crystallinity of the precipitated BSB lattice within the borate glass matrix, thereby maximizing the optical performance of BSB:Ce3+ GCs with a record PLQY of up to 95.0%.

Furthermore, comprehensive stability tests were performed on BSB:Ce3+ GC to evaluate its thermal, moisture, and irradiation resistance properties, as shown in Fig. 4. The PL intensities of both BSB:Ce3+ GC and phosphor decrease as the temperature increases, owing to the thermal quenching effect (Fig. 4(a)). Impressively, the PL intensity of BSB:Ce3+ GC remains at 43%@150 °C of its initial value at room temperature with an activation energy Δ*E* = 384 meV, significantly outperforming that of the BSB:Ce3+ phosphor, which maintains only 29% and Δ*E* = 273 meV under identical conditions (Fig. 4(b)). Additionally, excellent repeatability during three heating/cooling cycles indicates the stable emission performance of BSB:Ce3+ GC in response to temperature variations (Fig. 4(c)). Additionally, Fig. 4(d) shows the PL spectra under accelerated aging conditions of 85 °C and 85% relative humidity, and the PL intensity of BSB:Ce3+ GC remains impressive at 92% of its initial value after 20 days. Notably, all the PL spectra exhibit similar profiles, and the emission intensity of BSB:Ce3+ GC tends toward stabilization without noticeable weakening, even under consecutive exposure to violet light irradiation at 6 W for 30 days (Fig. 4(e)). Upon immersion in water for 30 days, there was only a minimal loss (−7%) in emission intensity for the BSB:Ce3+ GC, in stark contrast to only 44% retention for the BSB:Ce3+ phosphor (Fig. 4(f)). Overall, BSB:Ce3+ GC exhibits superior resistance to heat, moisture, and irradiation, since glass network engineering facilitates the high crystallinity of BSB:Ce3+ NCs, thereby increasing the radiative transition rates while suppressing the nonradiative rates. Moreover, the rigid glass network encapsulates the BSB:Ce3+ NCs for structural support while serving as a protective barrier against external elements such as moisture and oxygen. This significantly enhances material stability, making it highly suitable for high-power LED/LD lighting applications.

To assess the full-spectrum lighting performance, the EL spectrum of the GC-WLED device (Fig. 5(a)) closely resembles the solar spectrum, and the chromaticity coordinates of the full-spectrum white light fall at (0.3964, 0.3947) in the CIE 1931 color space with a low correlated color temperature (CCT) of 3902 K (Fig. S19 in the ESM). Impressively, the R*a value reaches as high as 95.2, and all R1–R15 values exceed 90 (Fig. 5(b)), indicating exceptional color rendering capability. Correspondingly, Fig. 5(c) compares the lighting effects in actual scenes, utilizing blue-light-excitable YAG-WLED and violet-light-excitable GC-WLED as light sources. From a visual perspective, GC-WLED lighting appears more realistic, vivid, and expressive, benefiting from its continuous sun-like spectrum. Furthermore, as the operating current increases from 50 to 650 mA, the EL spectral profiles remain unchanged (Fig. S20 in the ESM) with stable CCT and CRI parameters (Fig. 5(d)), suggesting excellent color stability of the GC-WLED. Therefore, BSB:Ce3+ GC is exceedingly competent as a yellow-emitting color converter for violet-light-excitable full-spectrum lighting.

Furthermore, LD lighting, regarded as one of the significant development directions of SSL, is extensively utilized in specialized scenes because of its high brightness, excellent beam quality, and long-distance transmission capabilities [51]. As depicted in Fig. 5(e), the light path for LD lighting is carefully simulated, comprising a 410 nm violet laser source, an optical filter, a lens, a BSB:Ce3+ GC bulk composite, and a spectral acquisition system. Here, the modulated 410 nm laser beam is focused onto the surface of the BSB:Ce3+ GC, with an illuminated area of 1 mm2. The spectral intensity of the GC-LD gradually increases as the incident power density increases from 15 to 133 mW/mm2 (Fig. 5(f)), with the corresponding luminous flux reaching 17.4 lumens at 105 mW/mm2 (Fig. 5(g)). To evaluate the long-term stability of BSB:Ce3+ GC under LD radiation, LD spectra were recorded over an extended operation period. Notably, the output intensity of the GC-LD device remains constant in the time range of 0–1800 s without any noticeable light saturation or degradation (Figs. 5(h) and 5(i)), thereby demonstrating the reliable stability of the BSB:Ce3+ GC for LD lighting. The high crystallinity and dense glass network enable the BSB:Ce3+ GC to possess remarkable resistance to laser irradiation. Moreover, as illustrated in Fig. 5(j), the resulting GC-WLD device presents excellent EL properties with an R*a of 93.3, a CCT of 6596 K, and chromaticity coordinates of (0.3119, 0.3232), which are close to noon daylight (Fig. 5(k)). Therefore, these results verify that BSB:Ce3+ GC is an outstanding candidate for violet-light-excitable LED/LD lighting.

## 4 Conclusions

To address the growing demand for violet-light-excitable luminescent materials with the advance of full-spectrum SSL, glass network engineering via regulation of the [BO3]/[BO4] ratio is proposed to improve the crystallinity of yellow-emitting BSB:Ce3+ GCs, enabling a remarkable PLQY of up to 95.0% under 410 nm violet light excitation. Structural and optical characterization evidence that optimization of the [BO3]/[BO4] ratio facilitates heterogeneous nucleation during *in situ* glass crystallization, resulting in improved BSB NC crystallinity and optimized optical performance. Finally, BSB:Ce3+ GC-based full-spectrum LED/LD lighting devices with remarkably outstanding performance are constructed. This work validates the feasibility of glass network engineering for optimizing the optical performance of GC composites and advancing the development of full-spectrum SSL.
