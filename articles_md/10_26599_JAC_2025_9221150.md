## 1 Introduction

Electromagnetic wave-absorbing materials (EMWAMs) are essential for radar stealth technology and electromagnetic pollution protection and play an irreplaceable role in defense systems and civilian applications [1−5]. Research on EMWAMs often highlights their impressive EMWA properties, such as superb absorption performance (minimum reflection loss, (RLmin)), wide absorption frequency band (maximum effective absorption bandwidth (EABmax), typically requiring RL 6−8], thin, and lightweight application [8−11]. Most current strategies focus on improving single- or multicomponent materials with known EMWA properties [12−14]. However, this methodology remains semiempirical and lacks theoretical modeling support. A critical limitation lies in its heavy reliance on traditional trial-and-error approaches, which suffer from inefficiency, high costs, and an inability to systematically address the complex interplay of material parameters [15,16]. For example, optimizing the absorption performance across multiple variables, such as dielectric/magnetic loss mechanisms, impedance matching, and structural hierarchy, requires iterative experimental adjustments that consume significant time and resources [17−19].

To address the above dilemma, researchers have proposed a method of inversion. The dynamic electromagnetic parameters (*ε*r and *μ*r) of the desired material are deduced inversely from the wave-absorbing properties. On the basis of the transmission line principle and the metal backplane model, the single-layer absorber RL is calculated as Eqs. (1) and (2) [20−23]:

(1) RL(dB)=20log|(Zin−Z0)/(Zin+Z0)|

(2) Z=|Zin/Z0|=|μr/ϵr|tanh(j(2πfd/c)μrϵr)

where *Z*in is the input impedance and *Z*0 is the free-space wave impedance (~377 Ω). * μr ( μr =  μ′ −  jμ′′) and  ϵr ( εr =  ε′ −  jε′′) are the relative permeability and permittivity, respectively.  μ′ and  μ″ represent the real and imaginary parts of permeability, respectively.  ε′ and  ε″ represent the real and imaginary parts of permittivity, respectively. i and j both represent the imaginary units of complex numbers.f*, *d*, and *c* are the EMW frequency, absorber thickness and speed of light, respectively. If the influence of *μ*r is disregarded, variations in the permittivity will directly affect the EMWA properties [24]. For example, in the design of “lightweight” and “thin layers” of MXene-based EMWAMs, Li *et al*. [25] calculated the necessary permittivity values for achieving EAB. Similarly, Yan *et al*. [26] designed carbon-based EMW absorbers to model the distribution of permittivity values taken by dielectric materials for effective absorption. Nevertheless, a fundamental limitation of this inversion strategy lies in the fact that frequency-dependent electromagnetic parameters (*μ*r and *ε*r) cannot be treated as independent design variables for material improvement. The frequency-dependent permittivity of EMWAMs exhibits significant dispersion characteristics, which vary with the EMW frequency [27]. Thus, extracting and analyzing the intrinsic electromagnetic parameters of EMWAMs is crucial for revealing their dynamic electromagnetic behavior.

Consequently, from the perspective of dielectric physics, it is reasonable to use the Debye relaxation model for resolving the permittivity, whose equations are in the basic form shown as shown in Eq. (3) [28]:

(3) ϵr=ϵsta−ϵinf1+iωτ+ϵinf−iσωϵ0

where *ε*sta and *ε*inf represent the permittivity at the static and ultimate optical frequencies, respectively. *ε*0 and *τ* are the vacuum permittivity and the relaxation time, respectively. i is an imaginary unit. *ω* and *σ* represent the angular frequency (*ω* = 2π*f*) and direct-current (DC) conductivity, respectively. The *σ* and polarization (*ε*sta, *ε*inf, and *τ*) are key factors controlling the permittivity, thereby ultimately determining the material’s EMWA properties. Although the Debye model provides theoretical guidance for designing the inherent electromagnetic parameters of materials, challenges remain in synergizing conductivity loss and polarization relaxation [29].

Represented by the two-dimensional transition metal carbide Ti3C2T*x* MXene, the remarkable conductivity (*σ* > 4×103 S·cm−1) of the conducting materials endows them with strong conductivity loss to EMWs at low frequencies. Unfortunately, its high carrier mobility leads to a short *τ*, which shifts characteristic dielectric relaxation peaks toward higher frequencies [30,31]. This inherent conflict fundamentally arises from the dual roles of Debye parameters, where *σ* dominates charge transport while *τ* governs the polarization delay. Herein, the Debye parameters (*σ* and *τ*) represent the core physical variables governing the dielectric response, where their distinct physical roles induce a *σ*–*τ* incompatibility that critically limits EMWA performance. The final manifestation of the *σ*–*τ* imbalance is that many incident EMWs are reflected, resulting in a serious impedance mismatch phenomenon. Hence, simply relying on inherent conductivity or polarization properties cannot overcome the bottleneck of Debye parameter tuning, and it is necessary to introduce functional interfaces in the material through microstructural design to synergistically optimize the Debye parameters. For example, Cui *et al*. [32] constructed a Ti3C2T*x* MXene/ZnS heterostructure that cleverly utilizes Schottky interface engineering to optimize the polarization effect at heterophase boundaries, while defects are introduced to control carrier transport properties. In addition, a composite structure with electrostatic self-assembled hollow polypyrrole nanocubes embedded between MXene layers was developed by Ding *et al*. [33]. Due to the approach of interface engineering, a three-dimensional conductive network was successfully constructed, while numerous heterogeneous interfaces increased the interfacial polarization [33]. These reports confirm that interface engineering is able to effectively modulate *σ* and *τ*, further improving the EMWA properties.

Despite these advancements, the fundamental question of how interface engineering modulates the intrinsic Debye parameters in MXene-based composites (particularly the heterointerface and tunable surface groups) remains mechanistically unclear. Therefore, the integration of human‒computer interaction (HCI) for Debye-parameter extraction not only accurately decouples the competing mechanisms between hybrid conductivity and polarization but also bypasses the empirical trial-and-error method in the design of MXene composites. Using Python, we translate the mathematical models (Eqs. (1)–(3)) into code, implementing a “mathematical model-Python code” HCI framework tailored for MXene systems. This approach provides two key advantages: at the material design level, the form of the dispersion of the material’s permittivity is first evaluated. The benefit lies in the ability to determine the direction of initial MXene modification according to the desired performance needs to rationally screen composite systems through performance-driven criteria. At the parametric analysis level, the dynamic permittivity of the MXene material system is deconstructed into the intrinsic Debye parameters, isolating the effect of frequency. Therefore, by correlating interface engineering strategies with Debye-parameter evolution, this HCI framework establishes a quantitative pathway to optimize MXene-based materials for enhanced EMWA performance.

To improve the EAB performance at the target thickness (2 mm), we propose an “interface engineering–HCI” synergistic framework, which was successfully implemented through the fabrication of nitrogen (N)-doped SiO2-coated Ti3C2Cl*x* MXene (SMX) composites. On the one hand, the introduction of insulating SiO2 inhibits the high *σ* performance of Ti3C2Cl*x* MXene, whereas the heterogeneous interface triggers overall *τ* hysteresis, which ultimately results in bidirectional modulation of the EMWA effect of the composite. On the other hand, the amount of N doping directly affects the dipole polarization process and unidirectionally modulates the *τ* of the heterogeneous dipole. On the basis of our inversion calculations, the EAB at a 2 mm thickness (EAB2mm) is successfully broadened (113% improvement over pristine MXene), which confirms the feasibility of “interface engineering–HCI”. The final SMX-S2-N1 obtained has an RLmin value of −31.4 dB and an EABmax of 5.2 GHz (12.56–17.76 GHz). This work not only solves the problem of the narrow absorption band for single-component MXenes but also offers innovative insight into the microscopic mechanism of dielectric loss by examining the inherent Debye parameters of the material. This provides a valuable framework for the future development of MXene-based EMWAMs.

### 2.1 Theoretical computing and material synthesis mechanisms

The preparation of EMWAMs is performance oriented, necessitating inverse permittivity calculations that require intensive computational involvement. To bridge natural language with machine-readable formats, we transform the mathematical models of EMWAMs into Python-based interactive code, thereby establishing an HCI mode. The computational tool addresses two key challenges in designing traditional EMWAMs: (i) It reduces the blindness in material selection by converting complex RL computational derivations into a real-time screening tool, providing immediate feedback on parameters. (ii) It helps determine performance optimization by quickly predicting the material’s EMWA properties within a specific frequency band, facilitated by visualization. As detailed in Appendix 1, users can obtain the required permittivity range (RPR) for achieving effective absorption within a set frequency band by adjusting the absorption thresholds and simulated thicknesses (Fig. 1(a)). Inverse calculations reveal that effective absorption requires *ε'* to be more sensitive to frequency while tolerating broader variations in *ε''*. Additionally, increasing the matching thickness leads to a decrease in the RPR (Fig. S1 and Video S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)).

For permittivity deconstruction, the Havriliak–Negami (H–N) model, a modified Debye relaxation equation, was employed through Python code fitting to extract intrinsic Debye parameters (Appendix 2). The independent Debye parameters resulting from the decoupling process more clearly distinguish the effects of conductivity and polarization on the RL values. This separation leads to a more accurate design of EMWA performance. Parameter variation simulations reveal two fundamental electromagnetic modulation mechanisms: (i) σ* modulation: *σ* enhancement induces a proportional increase in *ε''* under constant *ε'*, yielding an optimal RLmin at intermediate *σ* levels (Fig. 1(b1)). (ii) *τ* extension: *τ* increases and inversely decreases *ε'*, whereas *ε''* exhibits a parabolic trend, which is eventually accompanied by an RL extreme-point shift toward higher frequencies (Fig. 1(b2)). This dielectric decoupling analysis, which is based on the H‒N model, not only quantifies the independent regulatory effects of *σ* and *τ* on EMWA performance but also offers clear physical mechanism guidance for designing interface engineering strategies. Therefore, for the implementation of EAB2mm widening, it is necessary to control the range of the *σ* and *τ* values. Furthermore, the HCI results define the interface engineering approach: *σ* suppression caused by the dielectric wave-transparent layer enhances impedance matching for RLmin optimization, whereas heterointerface-induced *τ* regulation enables EAB expansion through *τ* dispersion.

The structural evolution of hybrid SMX is schematically illustrated in Fig. 1(c). Initially, Ti3C2 MXene is obtained by etching Ti3AlC2 MAX, where Ni2+ ions selectively etch the Al atoms according to the chemical potential in the redox reaction, resulting in an accordion-like structure. In addition, most of the end-groups of Ti3C2 are Cl-terminations (Cl*x*) due to the oxygen-free environment and the use of molten chloride salts. Next, through the intercalation of Li+ ions and polarity changes in different organic solvents, Ti3C2Cl*x* MXene is exfoliated into monolayer/thin-layer lamellar structures to increase the number of active chemical sites for subsequent composite reactions (labeled MX) [34]. Finally, during the hydrolysis of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) to generate SiO2 under solvent heating, SiO2 forms Si−O−Ti bonds on the surface of the Ti3C2Cl*x* MXene, resulting in the creation of N-doped SiO2-coated MXene hybrids. In contrast, the solvothermally obtained N-doped SiO2 without added MXene is named N-SiO2.

### 2.2 Bidirectional regulation of the conductive polarization method via SiO<sub>2</sub> content modulation

On the basis of the principle of control variables, ClH5N2 addition in SMX is consistent in this chapter (Section 4.3 for details), and SMX-S*x*-N1 is simplified to SMX-S*x* for simplicity. The characterization of the composites begins with a crystalline phase analysis, as evidenced by the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of SMX-S*x* (*x* = 1, 2, and 3) in Fig. 2(a), which demonstrate the properties of the composite crystalline phase. Compared with the standard PDF card (00-052-0875) of the MAX phase, the original Ti3AlC2 phase diffraction peak of the prepared MXene disappears. Moreover, the (002) diffraction peak of the MXene shifted toward a small angle, reflecting the phenomenon of expanded interlayer spacing from the etching/exfoliation processes. The broadened N-SiO2 diffraction peaks (18°–30°) further verify the MXene/SiO2 composite structure. In addition, the capping effect of SiO2 influences the (002) crystal plane spacing of MXenes (Fig. S2(a) in the ESM) [35]. Moreover, Raman spectroscopy (Fig. S2(b) in the ESM) revealed a Ti−C in-plane vibration peak at 151 cm−1 and broad peaks (200–600 cm−1) corresponding to surface functional groups. Detailed information on the chemical state of the SMX-S2 surface was obtained via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy. The full-spectrum scan shown in Fig. S2(c) in the ESM reveals six distinct elemental characteristic peaks in SMX-S2: Si (104 eV), Cl (199 eV), C (284 eV), N (401 eV), Ti (458 eV), and O (533 eV). All the fine X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectral calibration peaks correspond to C‒C bonds (284.8 eV), whereas the C‒Ti characteristic peak (281.9 eV) can be observed in the C 1s spectra (Fig. S2(d) in the ESM), confirming the primary Ti‒C structure of Ti3C2Cl*x* in SMX-S2 [36]. The Si 2p spectra (Fig. 2(b)) revealed Si–N (102.4 eV) and Si–O (103.7 eV) bonds, indicating that N-doped SiO2 capped the surface [37]. Additionally, the N-SiO2 (400.3 eV) and N-Si2O (401.4 eV) characteristic peaks in the N 1s spectra (Fig. 2(c)) reaffirmed the doping state of the surface N. Figure 2(d) shows that the XPS spectra of Ti 2p have eight subpeaks due to energy splitting. Among them, Ti–C(I) and Ti–C(II) confirm the fundamental [TiC6] octahedral structure of Ti3C2 MXene [38]. The Ti–Cl (2p3/2: 458.4 eV, 2p1/2: 464.4 eV) and Ti–O (2p3/2: 458.9 eV, 2p1/2: 464.9 eV) peaks indicate different surface species. The characteristic Cl−Ti peaks (2p3/2: 198.7 eV, 2p1/2: 200.2 eV) in the Cl 2p spectra (Fig. S2(e) in the ESM) are consistent with the Ti−Cl bonds observed in the Ti 2p spectra. Figure 2(e) shows O−Ti bonds (529.9 eV) and O−Si bonds (532.9 eV), matching the Ti 2p and Si 2p features [39,40]. In addition, as shown in Fig. S2(f) in the ESM, quantitative characterization of elemental Si was carried out on SMX-S*x*, and the results indicate that the Si content of the hybrid increased with increasing TEOS addition.

In addition, as shown in Fig. S3(b) in the ESM, N-SiO2 exhibited a block-like morphology with rough surfaces. The bonding of N-SiO2 with the MXene sheets was observed via high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM), and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping was used to determine the distributions of Ti, Cl, Si, O, and N (Fig. 2(l)). The concentrated distributions of Ti, Cl, Si, and O illustrate the composite structure of the SiO2-coated MXene, and the uniform distribution of N proves the successful implementation of the N-doping process.

The EMWA ability is typically evaluated through RL, where more negative values indicate stronger absorption performance [43]. As shown in Fig. S4(a) in the ESM, the conductive nature of the MXene contributes to electronic conductivity loss, achieving an EAB of 3.44 GHz in the Ku-band at 1.2 mm, whereas N-SiO2 retains the inherent wave-transparent properties of silica (Fig. S4(b) in the ESM). Comparative *ε*r dispersion analysis (Figs. S4(c) and S4(d) in the ESM) revealed that MXene requires downward shifting of the *ε'* curve for EAB broadening, whereas N-SiO2 needs simultaneous *ε'* and *ε''* enhancement [44]. On the basis of the laws presented in Figs. 1(b1) and 1(b2), the EAB2mm optimization direction of the MXene is to prolong *τ* appropriately. Since *σ* does not directly affect *ε'*, it can be appropriately reduced. For SMX-S*x*, as shown in Figs. 3(a)–3(c), SMX-S1 has an RLmin of −39.5 dB at 1.6 mm and an EABmax of 3.28 GHz at 1.5 mm, whereas SMX-S2 has RLmin = −31.4 dB (4.0 mm) and EABmax = 5.2 GHz (2.2 mm). SMX-S3, however, exhibits reduced performance (RLmin = −13.4 dB at 4.0 mm and EABmax = 1.92 GHz at 2.9 mm), indicating a limit to the modulation efficiency of SiO2. These findings suggest that both insufficient and excessive SiO2 fail to effectively improve the EMWA characteristics of SMX-S*x* (Table S1 in the ESM). Finally, the SiO2 interface engineering strategy enhances EAB2mm by 113% (3.92 GHz vs. 1.84 GHz for the original MXene).

The RL behavior of a material should be studied in terms of intrinsic physical parameters, with further in-depth exploration of *ε*r being necessary. The Debye parameters were deconstructed via the calculation method of Appendix 2, with the fitted *ε*r curves and trends in the conductivity and polarization components (*ε*c*''* and *ε*p*''*, *ε''* = *ε*c*''* + *ε*p*''*) shown in Figs. 3(d)–3(f) [48−50]. At low SiO2 contents, *ε*c*''* dominates dielectric loss, whereas *ε*p*''* dominates at higher SiO2 loadings. Moreover, according to the H‒N model (Eq. (A1)), *α* represents the relaxation time distribution (0 51]. The Debye parameters of SMX-S*x*, shown in Fig. 3(g) and Table S2 in the ESM, quantify the reduction in conductivity loss and the increase in polarization loss. Given the weak polarization ability of SiO2, as the SiO2 content increases, *ε*sta and Δ*ε* significantly decrease [52]. Figure 3(h) shows the model of a wrapped structure under an alternating electromagnetic field, where N-SiO2 introduces heterogeneous interfaces, triggering abrupt polarization norm transitions and thereby inducing strong interface polarization effects (Fig. 3(i)). Moreover, the actual HRTEM images (Fig. 3(j)) and corresponding lattice strain distributions (Figs. 3(k) and 3(l)) confirm that interfacial polarization originates from N-SiO2/MXene heterointerface distortions [53]. These interfaces, resulting in space-charge polarization, exhibit slower polarization responses than does the intrinsic dipole relaxation of MXenes, increasing the average relaxation time from 0.032 to 0.096 ns. In addition, polarization norms of different strengths lead to the occurrence of multiple relaxation types, introducing multiple relaxation times and broadening *τ* distributions (*α*). The resistive loss power map (Fig. 3(m)) reflects the MXene-dominated conductivity loss, with minimal N-SiO2 contribution. Furthermore, the fitted *σ* is consistent with the trend of the experimental SMX-S*x* conductivity (Fig. 3(n)), indicating that SiO2 capping limits the carrier mobility of the composites.

### 2.3 Unidirectional polarization optimization through an N-doping strategy

Similar to the naming in Section 2.2, the naming of SMX-S2-N*y* with consistent TEOS additions is simplified to SMX-N*y* in this section. Prior studies have shown that the amount of surface N doping is one of the factors influencing the electromagnetic characteristics of SiO2 [54,55]. Hence, it can be hypothesized that N-doping also impacts the EMWA properties of SMX. SMX-N*y* (*y* = 0, 1, 2, and 3) with varying degrees of N doping were synthesized according to the amount of N source added. As shown in Fig. 4(a), the XRD patterns of SMX-N*y* reveal that all SMX-N*y* samples maintain the biphasic MXene/SiO2 structure. Notably, the increase in N-doping does not induce the formation of any other crystalline phases. Moreover, the Raman spectrum (Fig. S6(a) in the ESM) reaffirms the presence of Ti3C2 MXene by the distinct Ti−C characteristic peaks. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to identify the types of surface functional groups present, as shown in Fig. 4(b). The broad band at approximately 3430 cm−1 corresponds to the −OH species on the SMX-N*y* surface [56]. The absorption peak at 1540 cm−1 is related to the N−H bending vibration mode. Note that the intense peak located at 955 cm−1 corresponds to the Si–N stretching mode, indicating the surface N-doping state. The characteristic peaks of SiO2 appear at 1050, 795, and 440 cm−1, which are attributed to the symmetric stretching and asymmetric bending vibrations of Si–O–Si and the stretching vibrations of Si–O, respectively [57−59]. To clarify the relationship between N source addition and N doping on the SMX-N*y* surface, the split-peak area of XPS was used to determine the N content, as shown in Figs. S6(b)–S6(e) in the ESM. A comparison between the N content and the sum of other elements revealed that the N content decreased with decreasing *y* values (Fig. 4(c)). The microscopic morphology of SMX-N*y* (Fig. S7 in the ESM) clearly shows that, regardless of N doping, exfoliated MXene undergoes aggregation induced by SiO2 during the solvothermal process. These structural analyses confirm tunable N incorporation without altering the crystalline phases, enabling a systematic study of doping-dependent EMWA performance.

Although the N-doping strategy successfully enhanced the EAB2mm of SMX-N*y* (Table S3 in the ESM), the EMWA performance (Figs. 4(d)–4(f)) shows that increased surface N doping fails to proportionally increase EABmax and RLmin. The EABmax of SMX-N0 is 2.32 GHz at low N-doping, whereas samples with higher N loadings (SMX-N2 and SMX-N3) exhibit comparable EABmax values (3.20 and 3.04 GHz). Excluding the influence of permeability (Fig. S8 in the ESM), the variation in *ε*r directly affects the EMWA properties of SMX-N*y*, as depicted in Figs. 3(e) and 4(g)–4(i). A sharp *ε*r reduction occurs between SMX-N0 and SMX-N1, with marginal changes at higher doping levels (SMX-N1–SMX-N3). Dielectric loss deconvolution revealed that conductivity loss was dominant at low frequencies for SMX-N0 (Fig. 4(j)). As N doping increases, conductivity loss stabilizes and is no longer the primary contributor to dielectric loss (the decoupled fitted *σ* is consistent with the tested conductivity shown in Fig. 4(k)). Conversely, polarization losses govern the dielectric losses of the other samples over the whole frequency range. Therefore, the evolution of the relaxation time (*τ*), which characterizes the polarization response, is of particular importance. In addition to the slow relaxation caused by the MXene/N-SiO2 heterogeneous interface, surface N doping also plays a crucial role in dipole relaxation (Fig. 4(l)). The average relaxation time gradually increases from SMX-N0 to SMX-N3, whereas the distribution of relaxation times shows little variation (Table S4 in the ESM).

To elucidate the evolution of *τ*, an analysis of the atomic electronic population is employed [60]. In [SiO2] orthotetrahedral structures, polar covalent Si–O bonds generate symmetric electric dipoles (O–Si–O/Si–O–Si). In this crystalline material, electron population statistics (Table 1) reveal intrinsic charge separation. Si atoms in crystalline SiO2 exhibit +2.35 charge depletion, whereas O atoms attract electrons through cloud displacement (−1.17). However, surface-engineered N-SiO2 systems alter the equilibrium (Fig. S9(a) in the ESM). Owing to the reduced electronegativity of nitrogen, Si‒N bonds exhibit a lower charge disparity (2.83 vs. 3.51 for Si‒O) [55,61]. Moreover, the surface O atoms are more chemically active, leading to the formation of defects and the generation of asymmetric O−Si-vacancy dipoles (3.21 charge difference). Thus, heterogeneous dipoles formed by combinations of Si-connected surface O/N atoms introduce new microscopic loss mechanisms for polarization effects. On the one hand, the inherent electronegativity differences prevent the dipole moments formed by surface Si−N or Si vacancies from neutralizing internal Si‒O, generating enhanced net dipole moments that amplify polarization losses. On the other hand, with the help of band structure and density of states (DOS) distribution (Figs. S9(b) and S9(c) in the ESM) diagrams, hetero/defect-induced localized states within the bandgap can be identified. According to the partial DOS (PDOS) graphs, the p-orbitals of surface Si, N, and O create a trap that hinders the migration of free carriers (Figs. S9(d1)–S9(d4) in the ESM), resulting in deep-level capture. However, an obvious bandgap clearly exists in the electronic structure of the internal Si and O atoms in bulk SiO2 (Figs. S9(d5)–S9(d7) in the ESM). Consequently, increased N-doping increases the trap state density, making *τ* primarily governed by the slow detrapping process. As shown in Fig. S10 in the ESM, the macroscopic characterization reveals that the impedance matching ability of SMX-N*y* is similar. However, the AC value of SMX-N1 was superior. Thus, despite their similar impedance matching characteristics, SMX-N1 results in greater reflection loss, leading to greater electromagnetic energy attenuation [62,63]. To summarize, as shown in Fig. 4(m), SMX-N1 has the best EMWA performance on the basis of the combined evaluation of two performance criteria (RLmin and EABmax). For tuning the EAB2mm, the interface engineering strategy is crucial. SiO2 capping modulates the *ε*r distribution by suppressing conductivity and regulating *τ*, whereas N doping fine-tunes *τ* through dramatic heterogeneous dipole polarization. This synergistic approach allows customized electromagnetic signature management via bidirectional regulation of conductive-polarization and unidirectional polarization optimization. Furthermore, the SMX-N1 synthesized in this work demonstrates superior performance compared with other reported EAB values of single-component MXenes and MXene-based composites (Fig. S11 and Table S5 in the ESM).

### 2.4 Radar cross section (RCS) simulation and mechanism summary

The stealth performance of SMX was evaluated through RCS simulations via CST2020 software. Section S4.7 in the ESM illustrates the finite element modeling setup, where SMX coatings on perfect electrical conductors (PECs) were analyzed. The detector angular sweep spans from −90° (normal incidence) to +90° in the *x*O*y* plane. In the 3D bistatic scattering RCS plots, the scattering signals of the SMX samples are similar in shape, but the RCS intensities vary considerably. In comparison, the 3D RCS signals are stronger for PEC (Fig. S12(a) in the ESM), MX (Fig. S12(b) in the ESM), and N-SiO2 (Fig. S12(c) in the ESM). Notably, the strong reflection signal received, as shown in Fig. S12(d) in the ESM, is essentially the same for MX and N-SiO2 as it is for PEC, illustrating the weak EMWA capability of MX and N-SiO2 at the simulated frequency. For the SMX samples (Figs. 5(a)–5(f)), SMX-S2 clearly has the weakest scattered signal strength, and although the other samples also attenuate the signal strength to some extent, they are far inferior to the stealth effect of SMX-S2. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 5(g), SMX-S2 presents the lowest bistatic scattering RCS value across the whole detection angle. These results align with the RL trends of the SMX-S*x* and SMX-N*y* series, confirming the critical role of SiO2 content and N-doping in optimizing EMWA properties.

Combining parameter deconstruction and theoretical modeling, we establish an “interface engineering-Debye-parameters” correlation framework to interpret the electromagnetic loss mechanisms of SMX, as shown in Fig. 5(h). First, the SiO2 content bidirectionally modulates the conductivity loss and polarization loss of SMX. On the one hand, the insulating property of SiO2 hinders σ*. The cladding layer not only blocks the migration path of the transferred electrons but also elevates the energy barrier of the interlayer electrons, reducing the hop probability of the electrons [64,65]. On the other hand, the SiO2 content controls the dielectric loss provided by the interfacial polarization. The dielectric behavior of MXenes is similar to that of metals, whereas SiO2 is an insulator. The difference in permittivity between the two materials strongly influences the charge arrangement at the interface. An increase in SiO2 content introduces more heterogeneous interfaces and increases the number of different types of relaxation, leading to a broader relaxation time distribution (*α*). This heterogeneous interface polarization tends to occur at much lower frequencies than the inherent electronic polarization frequency of MXene and SiO2, and the increased number of interfaces results in a slower response to electrical relaxation (*τ*). Second, at low doping levels, surface N doping of SiO2 unidirectionally modulates the contribution of dipole polarization to polarization loss. In contrast to the intrinsic electric dipoles (O–Si–O) within the crystal, where there is a nearly uniform electronic offset, the heterogeneous dipoles (O–Si–N) involve elemental atoms with a significant difference in electronegativity, which tends to cause an unbalanced electron cloud offset, as seen in the surface doping defects. The polarization response rate induced by heterogeneous dipoles and defective dipoles (O‒Si vacancies) is slower than the intrinsic electronic polarization rate is, thereby delaying the overall average relaxation time of SMX (*τ*). Finally, these microscopic mechanisms directly tailor macroscopic electromagnetic responses through permittivity modulation. As *α* and *τ* covary with *ε*r, optimized Z and AC are achieved via controlled SiO2/N doping, explaining the superior EMWA performance of SMX-S2.

Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; C2H6SO, 99%) was provided by Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China. TEOS ((C2H5O)4Si, 98%) and potassium chloride (KCl, 99.5%) were purchased from Tianjin Beilian Fine Chemicals Development Co., Ltd., China. Absolute ethanol (C2H6O, 99%) was supplied by Yantai Shuangshuang Chemical Co., Ltd., China. The deionized (DI) water used in the experiments was sourced from an ultrapure water purification system.

### 3.2 Preparation of Ti<sub>3</sub>C<sub>2</sub>Cl<sub><i>x</i></sub> MXene

Typically, Lewis acid etching of Ti3AlC2 MAX to obtain Ti3C2Cl*x* MXene was performed as follows: Initially, a mixture of MAX, NiCl2, NaCl, and KCl was prepared at a molar ratio of 1 : 6 : 2 : 2 [38]. This mixture was then subjected to heat treatment under an argon atmosphere and stabilized at 700 °C for half a day. Afterward, FeCl3 solution (5 mol·L−1) was used to remove unreacted materials and the generated Ni metal. The mixture was then washed three times with deionized water and ethanol until the washing solution reached a neutral pH.

The dried MXene (labeled MX) was dispersed in a DMSO solution (10 mL) containing LiCl (1.2 g) and magnetically stirred for one day at 1000 r·min−1. The dispersion was then purified by washing twice with DMSO to remove residual LiCl. Following solvent polarity gradients, sequential centrifugation was performed: primary exfoliation in DOS at 4000 r·min−1 for 10 min, followed by secondary treatment in NMF under identical centrifugal conditions. Finally, the MXene was subjected to centrifugation with NMF at 1600 r·min−1 for 10 min. The resulting mixture was allowed to stratify, enabling the removal of the supernatant and facilitating the collection of the precipitate [66].

### 3.3 Fabrication of SMX-S<i>x</i> (<i>x</i> = 1, 2, and 3) and SMX-N<i>y</i> (<i>y</i> = 0, 1, 2, and 3)

The stripped MXene (0.168 g) was dispersed in NMF (30 mL). Different amounts of TEOS and ClH5N2 were added to the dispersion, which was thoroughly mixed before being heated to 130 °C and maintained for 13 h. The obtained gel was washed three times with anhydrous ethanol and then dried and milled into powdered samples. At the same time, the solvothermal sample without added MXene was N-doped SiO2 and was named N-SiO2. The samples in the TEOS variable group were sequentially labeled SMX-S1, SMX-S2, and SMX-S3, with the labels corresponding to increasing amounts of TEOS. Similarly, the samples in the ClH5N2 variable group were labeled SMX-N0, SMX-N1, SMX-N2, and SMX-N3, in order of increasing ClH5N2 addition (Table S6 in the ESM). Notably, SMX-S2 and SMX-N1 were the same samples.

### 3.4 Characterization

Crystallographic information was obtained via an XRD (DX-2700, HAOYUAN, China). XPS (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA) was used with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source. The FTIR transmission spectrum was characterized with a TENSOR II instrument (Bruker, Germany). Raman spectra were examined by a Raman microscope (inVia-Reflex, UK) using an Ar ion laser (*λ* = 633 nm). An inductively coupled plasma spectroscope (ICP; 725-ES, Agilent, USA) was used to analyze the elemental content of the materials quantitatively. The surface morphology of the material was observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; ZEISS, Germany). The microstructure and elemental distribution of the material were analyzed via TEM (JEOL-F200, JEOL, Japan). The conductivity of the samples was obtained by testing the current‒voltage curve at an electrochemical workstation (ECW, LICHEN, China). *ε*r and *μ*r of the samples were tested via a vector network analyzer (VNA; N5245A, Agilent, USA). Sixty percent of the samples mixed with paraffin were pressed into coaxial rings, whose inner diameter, outer diameter, and thickness were 3.0, 7.0, and 2.0 mm, respectively.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, we enhanced the electromagnetic wave absorption properties of SMX hybrids to overcome the narrow effective absorption properties of typical MXene materials via the “interface engineering–HCI” strategy. The optimized SMX-S2-N1 achieved a minimum reflection loss of −31.4 dB and a maximum absorption bandwidth of 5.2 GHz. Benefiting from the insights of the “mathematical model-Python code” HCI approach, we designed a range of electromagnetic parameters for MXene-based materials according to the effective absorption bandwidth at 2 mm. We subsequently deconstructed the permittivity expression and obtained the Debye parameters (*σ* and *τ*) of SMX. Finally, the systematic characterization and simulations revealed the fundamental reason for the excellent electromagnetic absorption of SMX. On the one hand, the increase in SiO2 in the bidirectional modulation strategy limited the conductivity *σ* and simultaneously retarded the relaxation time *τ*, broadening the distribution of *τ*. On the other hand, in the unidirectional modulation strategy, N doping tailored the response rate of polarization relaxation. This work not only advances the frontier of MXene-based absorbers but also establishes a material design concept bridging macroscopic performance optimization with microscopic electromagnetic loss mechanisms.

In general, the Debye relaxation model is an idealized equation. However, in many practical scenarios, the intrinsic permittivity of materials is often not well adapted to the Debye relaxation model. For this reason, we adopt the Havriliak‒Negami model (H‒N model) [51], which has a broader range of applications. The expression of this model is shown in Eq. (A1):

(A1) ϵr=ϵinf+ϵsta−ϵinf(1+(iωτ)α)β−iσωϵ0

where the parameters *α* and *β* (both values less than or equal to 1) control the nonideality of the system. If *α* = *β* = 1, the H−N model reduces to the classical Debye model. If *β* = 1, the H−N model becomes the Cole‒Cole equation. If *α* = 1, the H−N model becomes the Davidson‒Cole equation. When *α* or *β* is less than 1, the model becomes more complex, representing the distribution of relaxation times [67]. On the basis of Eq. (A1), we developed a corresponding Python code to fit the relevant parameters, including the static permittivity (*ε*sta), optical frequency permittivity (*ε*inf), relaxation time (*τ*), conductivity (*σ*), and relaxation factors (*α* and *β*). The Python code used for this purpose is shown below.
