## 1 Introduction

The proliferation of high-frequency electronic systems has transformed electromagnetic (EM) interference from a simple disturbance into a multifaceted challenge involving reliability, stealth, and thermal stability [1−5]. Conventional microwave absorbers such as carbon-based frameworks and magnetic metals are constrained by intrinsic drawbacks: carbon materials often suffer from impedance mismatch and thermal degradation [6−11], whereas metallic systems are constrained by high density, corrosion susceptibility, and limited dielectric or magnetic tunability [12−14]. These drawbacks restrict their applicability in scenarios where broadband absorption, strong attenuation, and lightweight design must be maintained at elevated temperatures. To overcome these challenges, material platforms with structural adaptability and robust high-temperature performance are urgently needed.

Perovskite oxides, a class of functional materials with unique crystal structures and a general formula of ABO3 [15−18], provide a particularly promising solution owing to their flexible crystal chemistry and defect tolerance. From a structural perspective, perovskite oxides exhibit a highly regular cubic crystal structure: A-site cations occupy the eight vertices of the unit cell and form a three-dimensional network framework by connecting with oxygen ions at face-centered positions; B-site cations are precisely embedded in the center of a regular octahedron composed of six oxygen ions [19]. Unlike traditional absorbers, the perovskite lattice can stably accommodate cation substitutions, mixed valence states, and a wide range of vacancies. This structural flexibility enables precise control over EM loss mechanisms: A-site modulation alters the lattice symmetry and dielectric behavior, B-site tuning governs charge transport and magnetic exchange, and cation and oxygen vacancies act as active centers for dipole polarization and carrier migration [20,21]. Such defect-driven loss modulation, combined with intrinsic structural stability, positions perovskite oxides as an attractive alternative for next-generation absorbers requiring both broadband efficiency and high-temperature robustness.

Recent studies have further consolidated the immense potential of perovskite oxides in EM wave absorption, with a series of representative works advancing the field from different technical perspectives. For example, Li *et al*. [22] implemented entropy-driven regulation in iron-based perovskite oxides, and their developed (Ba1/3Sr1/3Ca1/3)FeO3 material achieved a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −40.58 dB and an effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) of 4.16 GHz at a mere thickness of 1.2 mm, demonstrating the efficacy of entropy engineering in optimizing the absorption performance. Similarly, Zhao *et al*. [23] utilized off-axis holography to uncover significant structural distortions in high-entropy perovskites and further clarified that such structural features play a pivotal role in increasing dipole polarization, which is a key mechanism underlying enhanced microwave absorption (MA). In addition to high-entropy systems, rare-earth manganese-based perovskite oxides have also emerged as promising candidates owing to their unique crystal structures, abundant defect chemistry, and tailorable electrical/magnetic properties [24]. In the LaMnO3 system, the multivalent characteristic of Mn ions at the B-site enables precise defect regulation through element doping and atmosphere annealing, providing flexible room for optimizing EM parameters. Moreover, this system can simultaneously regulate polarization loss and conductive loss, forming an efficient loss synergy effect and overcoming the performance limitations of a single loss mechanism. Additionally, its excellent high-temperature structural stability is suitable for high-temperature EM protection scenarios. To validate this potential, Su *et al*. [25] adopted a simple solid-phase doping strategy to optimize the impedance matching of calcium manganate, yielding an impressive absorption intensity of −52.63 dB at a thickness of 1.14 mm. While these advances collectively underscore the exceptional tunability of perovskite oxides for MA, a critical limitation persists in existing strategies: Most rely on either increasing composition complexity or adopting single-site doping, approaches that often fail to precisely regulate the interplay between polarization loss and conduction loss, two core factors determining absorption performance. Given that vacancy chemistry inherently governs the balance between these two loss mechanisms, a vacancy-centered design strategy thus presents a more direct, universal, and effective pathway to address the aforementioned bottleneck.

In this context, Cr3+-doped LaMn1−*x*Cr*x*O3 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.25) is explored as a model system, where Cr3+ substitution induces Mn vacancies and concomitantly tunes oxygen–vacancy populations, enabling vacancy ratio-guided balancing of polarization and conduction losses to deliver broad bandwidth and deep attenuation. LaMn0.85Cr0.15O3 achieves an outstanding absorption performance of −75.37 dB at 10.40 GHz and an effective bandwidth of 6.00 GHz at a thickness of only 2.8 mm. Control experiments combined with density of states (DOS) calculations further revealed the effects of Cr doping on vacancy generation, the regulation of conductivity, and the role of differential charge density at vacancies in enhancing absorption. Compared with existing materials, this composite demonstrates superior absorption bandwidth and efficiency, establishing a foundation for high-temperature microwave-absorbing materials with significant value in EM compatibility engineering.

### 2.1 Material preparation

LaCl3·7H2O, CrCl3·6H2O, MnC4H6O4·4H2O, citric acid, and absolute ethanol were used in this experiment, all of which were purchased from Macklin with analytical reagent (AR) purity. The perovskite samples were prepared via a simple sol–gel method. First, LaCl3·7H2O, CrCl3·6H2O, and MnC4H6O4·4H2O were accurately weighed according to the chemical formula LaMn1−*x*Cr*x*O3 (*x* = 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, and 0.25). The weighed reagents were then mixed and dissolved in 30 mL of deionized water in stoichiometric proportions. After all the reagents were completely dissolved, citric acid was added at a molar amount twice that of the total metal cations, which ensured sufficient coordination of the metal salt ions and maintained the overall solution at a low pH. The beaker was then placed in an oil bath at 85 °C and continuously stirred for 3 h until the solution in the beaker transformed into a homogeneous dark blue colloid. The beaker was transferred to a vacuum drying oven and dried at 90 °C for 24 h, resulting in a gel-like sample. The gel was placed in a muffle furnace, where it was first incubated at 250 °C for 3 h and then heated to 900 °C and incubated for 4 h (as revealed by the preliminary verification experiments shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), a single stable phase could not be formed when the temperature was too low, whereas excessively high temperatures had a significant negative impact on the MA performance. Therefore, 900 °C was selected as the optimal temperature. The heating rate throughout the entire process was maintained at 5 °C/min, and the cooling method adopted was furnace cooling to room temperature. The samples were named LMCO-0, LMCO-1, LMCO-2, LMCO-3, LMCO-4, and LMCO-5, corresponding to Cr3+ doping amounts of *x* = 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, and 0.25, respectively.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The phase compositions of the samples were measured via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; DX2700, Dandong Haoyuan, China). A Fourier transform infrared spectroscope (FTIR; TENSOR II, Bruker, Germany) and an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Kα, Thermo Fisher, USA) were employed to detect the chemical bonds and structures of the materials. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; MERLIN Compact, Zeiss, Japan) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEOL-2100, Hitachi, Japan) were utilized to characterize the morphology and structure of the materials microscopically. To investigate their EM wave absorption performance, the prepared powders were uniformly mixed with paraffin at a mass ratio of 6:4. The composite mixture was then molded into cylindrical samples with an inner diameter of 3.04 mm, an outer diameter of 7.00 mm, and a thickness of 1.53 mm. The EM parameters were measured via a vector network analyzer (VNA; E8363B, Agilent, USA) within the frequency range of 2–18 GHz. The corresponding RL was calculated via transmission line theory. Magnetic hysteresis loops were recorded at room temperature via a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM; Lakeshore 7407, Lakeshore, USA).

### 2.3 Computational methods

All density functional theory (DFT) [26,27] calculations were performed via the Quantum ESPRESSO [28] software package. The electron‒ion interactions in the system were accurately described by the projected augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotential, whereas the exchange‒correlation effects were treated within the framework of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) via the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional [29]. A cutoff energy of 50 Ry was set for the plane-wave basis set, and the corresponding cutoff energy for the charge density was increased to 400 Ry to ensure the accuracy of the electronic structure calculations. The Monkhorst-Pack scheme was employed for k-point sampling throughout the calculations. On the basis of systematic convergence tests, a k-point grid density of 0.03 × 2π/Å was adopted for self-consistent field (SCF) calculations and lattice optimization. For non-self-consistent field (NSCF) calculations, including DOS and charge density analyses, a denser *k*-point grid with a density of 0.02 × 2π/Å was used to guarantee the precision of Brillouin zone integration. During the relaxation of the crystal structure, strict convergence criteria were imposed: the change in the total energy of the system was required to be less than 10−6 eV, and the residual force on each atom was restricted to below 0.03 eV/Å. These criteria ensure that the optimized crystal structure corresponds to a stable thermodynamic state, laying a reliable foundation for subsequent electronic property analyses.

### 3.1 Powder preparation and characterization

The preparation process of LMCO-*x* is illustrated in Fig. 1(a). The phase composition of LaMn1−*x*Cr*x*O3 (*x* = 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, and 0.25) was analyzed via XRD, as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. All the XRD patterns of LaMn1−*x*Cr*x*O3 are consistent with those of standard LaMnO3 (PDF#86-1228). The distinct diffraction peaks at 32.396°, 32.724°, 46.713°, and 57.993° are attributed to the (110), (104), (024), and (214) crystal planes of LaMnO3, respectively. These results further confirmed the formation of the perovskite-type lanthanum manganite (LaMnO3) structure. In addition, no impurity peaks appear in the crystals with different Cr3+ doping contents, indicating that Mn is well substituted by Cr and that the samples exhibit good single-phase characteristics. As shown in Table S1 in the ESM, the structure belongs to the trigonal crystal system with a space group of *R** 3¯C*, and the lattice parameters are *a* = *b* = 5.523 Å, *c* = 13.331 Å, *α* = *β* = 90°, and *γ* = 120°. The crystal structure and fitting data of LMCO-*x* with different doping amounts were obtained by refining the XRD diffraction data via GSAS II software (Figs. 1(b)–1(d)). The length of B–O first decreases but then increases with increasing Cr content. The relative shortening of the B‒O bond length intensifies the octahedral distortion. Lattice distortion leads to a decrease in the degree of overlap between oxygen ion orbitals and metal ion orbitals, which ultimately reduces the super-exchange interaction energy and further weakens the super-exchange interaction between Mn−O−Mn [30]. In LaMnO3, the existence of the Mn3+/Mn4+ mixed-valence system can enhance the ferromagnetic correlation through the double-exchange interaction. However, since the redox potential of Cr3+/Cr4+ is greater than that of Mn3+/Mn4+, Cr3+ cannot form valence fluctuations with Mn3+, increasing the energy barrier for electron transition between Mn−Cr−O chains and the failure of the double-exchange interaction [31]. The t2g electrons of Cr3+ are nonbonding orbitals, and their spin‒orbit coupling strength (~300 cm−1) is lower than that of Mn3+ (~400 cm−1). Moreover, the hybridization symmetry of the Cr‒O‒Mn bond is different from that of the Mn‒O‒Mn bond, leading to the breaking of the spatial symmetry of magnetic orbital coupling and making it difficult to maintain long-range magnetic order. The material changes with the external magnetic field, resulting in relatively low magnetic loss. The above analysis clarifies the EM wave absorption mechanism of the LMCO absorbent, that is, the loss mechanism dominated by the coordination of cation and anion vacancies. During the *in situ* formation of LMCO, the introduction of chromium replaces part of the manganese. To balance the overall charge of the material, metal manganese vacancies and oxygen vacancies in the product form dynamic coordination, which effectively improves the overall EM wave absorption performance of the material.

Chromium doping alters the charge balance of perovskites; to maintain electrical neutrality, the valence states of manganese and oxygen vacancies seem to reach a balance, thereby changing the electrical conductivity.

In the early synthesis stage, metal salt ions are oxidized at high temperatures. Among them, manganese is preferentially oxidized to manganese oxide, and chromium is oxidized to chromium oxide. During high-temperature processes, as LaMnO3 continues to form, the oxidation of various metal salt ions occurs simultaneously. The manganese oxide at the interface of the preferentially formed LaMnO3 can be replaced by chromium oxide. To maintain electrical neutrality, vacancies of the metal cation manganese appear in part of the manganese oxide, as seen from the corresponding defect in Eq. (1). As the entire process progresses, the defective manganese-chromium oxide will be incorporated into the lanthanum manganite matrix while balancing the charge in the matrix. With increasing chromium doping amount, more manganese will be replaced in early synthesis, and the oxygen content introduced into the lattice will also increase synchronously, which is consistent with the XPS results showing that the content of defect oxygen decreases and the content of lattice oxygen increases.

The above process dynamically adjusts the quantities of oxygen vacancies and metal manganese vacancies, which can effectively consume EM waves through the cyclic reversal of dipoles at the vacancies when EM waves are incident on the material. Owing to chromium doping, according to Zener’s double-exchange theory, Mn4+ ions appear after doping. The electrons of Mn3+ can jump to the empty orbitals of Mn4+, generating free electrons through O2−, thus forming the metallic conduction phenomenon of carrier migration. Moreover, all the samples exhibit superparamagnetic characteristics, which is consistent with the previous analysis (Fig. 2(d)). The most prominent peak in the FTIR spectrum is located at 604 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching or bending vibration mode of the metal‒oxygen bond in the octahedron (MnO6) of the ABO3 structure (Fig. 2(e)), which also indicates the successful synthesis of perovskite-type lanthanum manganite [34].

### 3.2 Dielectric parameter analysis

Cation/anion vacancies play a crucial role in increasing the dielectric constant of composite materials [35]. The MA performance was investigated by examining EM parameters, specifically, the energy storage capacity (real part of the permittivity (*ε'*), real part of the permeability (*μ'*)) and attenuation capacity (imaginary part of the permittivity (*ε"*), imaginary part of the permeability (*μ"*)). In Fig. S6 in the ESM, the complex permeabilities (*μ'* and *μ"*) of samples LMCO-0 to LMCO-5 are close to 1 and 0, respectively, and their magnetic loss tangent values are negligible compared with the dielectric loss tangent values, indicating that the degree of magnetic loss in all the composites is negligible. Therefore, only the relatively complex permittivity was studied in detail.

Owing to the incorporation of trivalent chromium ions, the continuous generation of manganese ion vacancies and the continuous reduction in intrinsic oxygen vacancies in the material lead to changes in the relaxation time of dipoles in the material with frequency, resulting in different degrees of frequency dispersion effects on the dielectric parameters among materials with different proportions in the 2−18 GHz range [36,37]. Figures 3(a) and 3(b) clearly show that the real part (*ε'*) and imaginary part (*ε"*) of the dielectric constant of LMCO-0 to LMCO-5 generally decrease with increasing frequency, which is attributed to the polarization lag phenomenon at high frequencies, i.e., the frequency dispersion effect. Generally, the more significant the frequency dispersion effect is (the larger the numerical span of the dielectric constant at 2−18 GHz), the stronger the MA performance is [38,39]. For LMCO-0, the variation range of *ε'* is 7.8−5.7 (span 2.1), and that of *ε"* is 1.44−1.16 (span 0.28); for LMCO-1, the variation range of *ε'* is 10.95−6.18 (span 4.77), and that of *ε"* is 3.37−2.07 (span 1.3); for LMCO-2, the variation range of *ε'* is 12.68−6.48 (span 6.2), and that of *ε"* is 4.07−2.48 (span 1.59); for LMCO-3, the variation range of *ε'* is 11.85−6.08 (span 5.77), and that of *ε"* is 3.78−2.27 (span 1.51); for LMCO-4, the variation range of *ε'* is 11.74−6.31 (span 5.33), and that of *ε"* is 3.45−2.02 (span 1.43); for LMCO-5, the variation range of *ε'* is 10.25−5.26 (span 4.99), and that of *ε* Compared with the other samples, LMCO-2 has a large variation in the real and imaginary parts of the dielectric constant in the 2−18 GHz range and always maintains high values. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 3(d), all LMCO samples except LMCO-0 exhibit polarization relaxation peaks, which are related to the polarization relaxation effect of dipoles. The frequency range of the polarization relaxation peak of LMCO-1 is 12−14.8 GHz (span 2.8 GHz), that of LMCO-2 is 8−16 GHz (span 8 GHz), that of LMCO-3 is 8−14.4 GHz (span 6.4 GHz), that of LMCO-4 is 10−14.4 GHz (span 4.4 GHz), and that of LMCO-5 is 10−14.4 GHz (span 4.4 GHz). LMCO-2 clearly has the largest frequency span of the polarization relaxation peak and the largest change in the dielectric loss tangent value. This is because with the incorporation of chromium ions, the intrinsic oxygen vacancies in the material are continuously reduced, and manganese vacancies are continuously generated, resulting in multiple dipoles with different relaxation times in the material system, which enhances the polarization relaxation phenomenon.

where τ*, *ε*s, σ and *ε*∞ represent the relaxation time, relative permittivity at static frequency，electrical conductivity and “infinite” high frequency, respectively.

As shown in Fig. 3(e), the polarization loss value indicates that under the ratio of manganese vacancies to oxygen vacancies in LMCO-2, its polarization loss in the 2‒18 GHz range is greater than that of the other samples. In summary, its widest parameter variation range and multiple relaxation processes are conducive to meeting the requirements of microwave absorbing material parameters at different frequencies, thereby broadening the absorption frequency band [44,45]. With the continuous doping of chromium ions, owing to the continuous increase in manganese vacancies, the electron exchange between cation vacancies and anion vacancies is subsequently enhanced, and the ordering of the arrangement of different types of vacancies in LMCO-3 is improved [46]. This weakens the scattering effect on carriers, thus achieving optimal conduction loss, which lays a foundation for achieving RLmin (Fig. 3(f)). Moreover, owing to the increased ordering of the arrangement of the same type of vacancies, the polarization loss decreases. Thereafter, with the continuous increase in the number of manganese vacancies, the spacing between oxygen ion vacancies increases, and the ordering of their arrangement weakens. In contrast, more oriented dipoles are formed between the cation and anion vacancies in LMCO-4, which enhances the polarization effect and correspondingly weakens the conduction ability. With the continuous decrease in anion vacancies, the ordering of the cation vacancy arrangement is enhanced, which weakens carrier scattering and promotes an increase in conduction loss. These data indicate that LMCO materials can regulate the ratio of cation and anion vacancies through the microdoping of chromium ions, thereby regulating the dielectric parameters.

The absorber enables efficient EM wave absorption, which relies primarily on two core mechanisms: impedance matching and EM attenuation. Impedance matching is a core principle that needs to be focused on when designing high-performance absorbers. When EM waves propagate from the air to the surface of the absorber, the impedance of the absorber should be close to that of the air. At this time, EM waves tend to penetrate into the absorber rather than being reflected, which is called impedance matching; otherwise, impedance mismatch will occur. The impedance value can be derived via Eq. (4) [47]:

(4) Zin=Z0⁡μrεrtanh⁡(j2πfdcμr⁡εr)

where *Z*in and *Z*0 represent the input impedance and free space impedance, respectively, *μ*r represents the relative permeability, *ε*r represents the relative permittivity, and *c* represents the speed of light in vacuum. The *Z* value (*Z* = * |Zin/Z0|) [48] is related to the frequency (f*) and thickness (*d*).

When the impedance matching degree reaches a good state, its *Z* value approaches 1, which can not only effectively suppress EM wave reflection but also maximize the penetration of EM waves into the composite material [49,50]. As shown in Figs. 3(g) and 3(h), the impedance matching performance of the pure-phase LMCOs is extremely poor, and most of its impedance values in the 2−18 GHz range are far from *Z* = 1. However, when chromium ions are introduced, leading to the formation of cation vacancies, the impedance matching performance is significantly improved. Figures S7(a)‒S7(f) in the ESM show two-dimensional distribution diagrams of the impedance matching performance of LMCO-0 to LMCO-5. The orange curve marks the *Z* = 0.8 region, and the pink curve marks the *Z* = 1.2 region. The larger the area enclosed by the two curves is, the better the impedance matching effect. The effective area of impedance matching for LMCO-0 to LMCO-5 changes. This proves that through chromium ion doping, changes in the number of manganese ion vacancies/oxygen ion vacancies can ultimately regulate the impedance matching performance of a material.

Moreover, *α* determines the attenuation properties of composites, which can be calculated via Eq. (5) [51]:

(5) α=2πfc×(μ″ε″−μ′ε′)+(μ′ε′+μ″ε″)2+(μ″ε″−μ′ε′)2

As shown in Fig. 3(d), LMCO-0 has the lowest real attenuation constant, which is attributed to its extremely low conduction loss and polarization loss. With the incorporation of chromium ions, the real attenuation constants of LMCO-1 to LMCO-5 increase significantly. Moreover, owing to the introduction of new dipoles formed between cation and anion vacancies, polarization relaxation peaks appear, leading to a rapid change in the attenuation constant, which is beneficial for improving the EM attenuation capability of the material. It can also be observed that the real loss values of LMCO-3 and LMCO-5, where conduction loss accounts for a greater proportion, are less affected by the polarization relaxation effect. Thus, the sharp increase in their real loss values is not as obvious as that of LMCO-1, LMCO-2, and LMCO-4, where polarization loss dominates. The consistently high real attenuation constant of LMCO-2 in the 2−18 GHz range lays a foundation for its ability to achieve the optimal effective MA bandwidth, which will be further discussed in the subsequent analysis of EM absorption performance. The variation in the real attenuation constants of the LMCO series samples indicates that the microwave attenuation capability of the material can be regulated through the influence of dipoles by adjusting the ratio of microscopic cation and anion vacancies.

### 3.3 EM wave absorption performance

To further analyze the MA performance of LMCO0-5 composites with different cation‒anion vacancy ratios, Figures 4(a)−4(f) show the measured results of their RL. For LMCO-0, the RLmin value is −18.87 dB, with a peak frequency of 15.76 GHz. The optimal EAB is 2.88 GHz, corresponding to a thickness of 6 mm. For LMCO-1, at a thickness of 3.4 mm, the RLmin reaches −48.99 dB with a peak frequency of 7.84 GHz, and the optimal EAB is 6.24 GHz. For LMCO-2, at a thickness of 4.9 mm, the RLmin is −45.06 dB with a peak frequency of 4.88 GHz, and the optimal EAB is 6.96 GHz, which is the widest among all samples. When the chromium ion doping amount continues to increase, LMCO-3 achieves the RLmin value of the entire series (−75.37 dB) at a thickness of 2.8 mm, with a peak frequency of 10.4 GHz and an optimal EAB of 6 GHz. The RLmin of LMCO-4 decreases to −42.00 dB at a thickness of 2.2 mm, with a peak frequency of 12.24 GHz, and its optimal EAB increases to 6.32 GHz. The RLmin of LMCO-5 increases to −54.91 dB at a thickness of 2.8 mm, with a peak frequency of 11.60 GHz, whereas its optimal EAB decreases to 6.00 GHz. It is easy to observe that with the incorporation of chromium ions, the RLmin and optimal EAB of the materials generally tend to first increase and then decrease (Fig. 4(g)). The corresponding peak frequency also tends to shift from high frequency to low frequency and then returns to high frequency, which is consistent with the law of changes in the arrangement of cation‒anion vacancies caused by the introduction of chromium ions. The system transitions from initially having manganese ion vacancies scattered in an oxygen ion vacancy-dominated system to having oxygen ion vacancies scattered in a manganese ion vacancy-dominated system, thus realizing the regular regulation of microwave performance [52]. Moreover, it is evident that LMCO-1, LMCO-2, and LMCO-4, where polarization loss accounts for a larger proportion, have significantly higher optimal EABs than LMCO-3 and LMCO-5, where conduction loss dominates. In contrast, the RLmin values of LMCO-3 and LMCO-5 (with greater conduction loss) are significantly greater than those of LMCO-1, LMCO-2, and LMCO-4 (with greater polarization loss). This finding indicates that in the LMCO system, the effective MA bandwidth of the material is strongly affected by polarization loss, whereas the RLmin value is more obviously influenced by conduction loss. Moreover, the polarization relaxation behavior and conduction loss behavior of the material can be further demonstrated via Cole-Cole plots.

The Cole‒Cole diagram of dielectric loss is analyzed on the basis of Debye theory. Theoretically, if a sample undergoes a polarization relaxation process, then the curves plotted by ε'* and *ε"* will form a semicircle, each of which represents a Debye relaxation process. A period is assigned to a polarization relaxation process (including interfacial/dipole polarization), whereas long smoothing lines are associated with conduction losses. The specific equations are shown in Eqs. (6) and (7) [53−55]:

(6) ε′=ε∞+εs−ε∞1+(2πf)2⁡r2

(7) ε″=ωτ(εs−ε∞)1+(2πf)2⁡r2

where *ε*s is the static dielectric constant, *ε*∞ is the optical dielectric constant, *f* is the frequency, and *t* is the polarization relaxation time. The Cole‒Cole equation is expressed as Eq. (8) [56,57]:

(8) (ε′−εs+ε∞2)2+(ε″)2=(εs−ε∞2)2

If the sample undergoes a polarization relaxation process, then the curves plotted by *ε'* and *ε"* will form a semicircle, each of which represents a Debye relaxation process. Owing to the relatively simple phase structure of LMCO-0, which contains only oxygen ion vacancies, its charge exchange capability is poor, and the degree of structural distortion is slight, resulting in poor polarization loss. Therefore, no obvious dielectric relaxation characteristics are observed in Fig. S8(a) in the ESM, which corresponds well with the poor EM wave absorption performance of the LMCO-0 sample. After the introduction of chromium ions, the polarization relaxation effect is significantly enhanced, and the conduction loss is also strengthened. In Fig. S8(c) in the ESM, LMCO-2 exhibits two obvious large-scale relaxation polarizations: the first occurs in the microwave frequency range of approximately 6.5–7.8 GHz, and the second occurs at approximately 7.8–12.7 GHz. Under this doping ratio, the sample clearly achieves the optimal polarization relaxation response. This polarization effect occurring over a wide range greatly enhances the continuous microwave loss capability of the sample, which is the intrinsic mechanism enabling LMCO-2 to achieve the optimal absorption bandwidth. With the continuous addition of chromium ions, the electron exchange effect between the cation and anion vacancies is subsequently enhanced, and the influence of conduction loss becomes increasingly apparent. In LMCO-3, in addition to the polarization relaxation processes between 6.7–7.7 and 7.8–11.8 GHz, there is an obvious linear part representing conduction loss, which benefits from charge exchange and polarization relaxation between anion vacancies and cation vacancies inside the material. Under the combined action of conduction loss and a relatively wide polarization relaxation effect, LMCO-3 achieves the RLmin. Thereafter, with the continuous increase in chromium ions, the conduction loss effect becomes increasingly obvious overall, whereas the polarization relaxation effect weakens accordingly. In LMCO-5, the polarization relaxation phenomenon is significantly weakened, and the loss is attributed mainly to conduction loss. Therefore, through the dynamic regulation of manganese ion vacancies and oxygen ion vacancies, the MA performance of LMCO-0 can be modified by adjusting the defect ratio of the phase structure without changing the crystal phase of the material.

As shown in Fig. 4(h), compared with other ceramic powder absorbents [22,58−66], the EM wave absorbent prepared in this study can achieve an optimal absorption bandwidth of 6.96 GHz (for LMCO-2) and an excellent RLmin value of −75.37 dB through trace element doping. The specific material parameters are presented in Table S2 in the ESM. To assess the EMW absorption capabilities of the materials accurately, we conducted supplementary testing via the arch-shaped method, which is shown in Fig. S9 in the ESM. By comparison, we found that although the calculated results were slightly different from the experimentally measured data, both datasets generally reflected the excellent EMW absorption capability of LMCOs. To verify the oxidation resistance of the materials, we conducted cyclic oxidation experiments on the LMCO samples. Although the EM wave absorption performance of the materials decreased to some extent after multiple oxidation cycles, it still remained at a level meeting the requirements of practical applications, which fully demonstrates the excellent high-temperature oxidation resistance of the samples (Fig. S10 in the ESM).

For a perfect lanthanum manganite crystal, the charge distribution is relatively uniform. However, when a chromium atom is introduced into the lanthanum manganite matrix to substitute for a manganese atom, the charge distribution equilibrium around the doping site is disrupted (Figs. 5(a) and 5(b)). A distinct charge exchange occurs between the Cr atom and its adjacent oxygen atoms, where the yellow regions in the figure denote electron accumulation and the blue regions represent electron depletion. This leads to the formation of permanent dipoles between the Cr atom and its bonded neighboring O atoms, generating a local electric field. These permanent dipoles oscillate under an external alternating EM field, thereby promoting the enhancement of polarization loss inside the material under microwave conditions [67,68]. Moreover, the defect chemistry equations demonstrate that the introduction of high-valence Cr atoms induces the formation of Mn vacancies within the material, as illustrated in Fig. 5(c). The generation of vacancies breaks the originally balanced charge distribution. Coulomb attraction causes the separation of positive and negative charge centers, resulting in the distortion of the electron cloud distribution around atoms near the vacancies [69,70]. As shown in Fig. 5(d), the O atoms surrounding the Mn vacancies exhibit significant charge transfer toward the vacancies—specifically, the Mn vacancies gain electrons, whereas the O atoms lose electrons. This also results in the formation of defect dipoles around the vacancies, which can further increase polarization loss. Additionally, the presence of vacancies provides sites for carrier migration, reducing the carrier transport barrier and thus facilitating the improvement of conduction loss. XPS analysis revealed that oxygen vacancies inherently exist in the pristine lanthanum manganite matrix. These oxygen vacancies exhibit positive polarity, enabling them to form vacancy-defect pair dipoles with nearby Mn vacancies (which have negative polarity). These dipoles also provide sites for carrier transfer and scattering, further enhancing both the conduction loss and polarization loss effects of the material under alternating EM fields. The influence of the aforementioned structural changes is also remarkably evident in the total DOS. As observed in Figs. 5(g) and 5(h), compared with pure-phase lanthanum manganite, the introduction of Cr atoms leads to the emergence of new characteristic DOS peaks at −1, 1.5, and 1.6 eV in the DOS curve. These new peaks indicate that the incorporation of heteroatoms introduces additional defect energy levels into the system, promoting electron exchange within the system.

In conclusion, the introduction of Cr atoms induces the formation of Mn vacancies, which in turn generate local dipoles. This process enhances both the conduction loss and polarization loss effects of the material under microwave fields, ultimately enabling the structural regulation of the MA performance.

To investigate the actual response of LMCO-0 to LMCO-5 under radar signals, we used CST microwave simulation software to model the radar cross section (RCS) of the LMCOs. The unit cell boundary conditions were applied in the X* and *Y* directions, and the open boundary condition was used in the vertical direction. As shown in Fig. 5(i), the model adopts a double-layer structure design, including a base layer (perfect electric conductor (PEC)) and an absorption layer, on the basis of the EM parameters of LMCO-0 to LMCO-5. The specific thickness of each layer corresponds to the thickness at which each sample achieves the RLmin. Taking the case of an EM wave incident angle of 0 degrees as an example, the RCS value of the substrate coated with LMCO-0 is −5.25 dB·m2. When the absorption layer of LMCO-0 is replaced by a PEC of the same thickness, the RCS value at this angle reaches 15.9 dB·m2. The RCS value of the substrate coated with LMCO-1 is −16.9 dB·m2; replacing the absorption layer of LMCO-1 with PECs of the same thickness results in an RCS value of 10.2 dB·m2 at this angle. For the substrate coated with LMCO-2, the RCS value is −21.6 dB·m2, and after the absorption layer of H3 is replaced with PECs of the same thickness, the RCS value at this angle increases to 5.89 dB·m2. The RCS value of the substrate coated with LMCO-3 is −13.5 dB·m2; replacing its absorption layer with PECs of the same thickness leads to an RCS value of 12.5 dB·m2 at this angle. The RCS value of the substrate coated with LMCO-4 is −13.5 dB·m2, and replacing its absorption layer with PECs of the same thickness results in an RCS value of 13.9 dB·m2. For the substrate coated with LMCO-5, the RCS value is −5.62 dB·m2, and replacing its absorption layer with a PEC of the same thickness results in an RCS value of 13.5 dB·m2 at this angle (Figs. 5(j) and 5(k)). These data indicate that LMCO materials can effectively absorb or dissipate a large amount of EM wave energy. The experimental results not only verify the accuracy of the theoretical predictions but also confirm the feasibility of LMCO materials in practical applications.

In summary, the introduction of trivalent chromium ions into LMCO materials leads to the generation of manganese ion vacancies, accompanied by changes in the valence state of manganese. This promotes electron exchange between different valence states of manganese. The newly formed manganese vacancies can form polar dipoles with oxygen vacancies, inducing polarization relaxation effects and thus generating polarization loss (Fig. 6). Additionally, owing to the difference in electronegativity between manganese vacancies and oxygen vacancies, electron exchange can occur between them, enhancing the conduction loss of the material system. During the high-temperature sintering process, chromium enters the LMCO-0 system in the form of chromium trioxide (Cr2O3), which fills the original oxygen vacancies in the system, reducing the number of oxygen vacancies and consequently altering their arrangement. By continuously adjusting the ratio of manganese vacancies to oxygen vacancies, the arrangement of cation and anion vacancies within the material system changes accordingly, resulting in varying proportions of conduction loss and polarization loss under different doping ratios. This separately achieves broadening of the EAB and enhancement of the RLmin value. Ultimately, excellent MA performance is realized through the polarization relaxation of dipoles and the conductive transport of carriers. Moreover, this study provides a new approach for the development of high-temperature antioxidant EM stealth materials.

## 4 Conclusions

Taking LaMnO3 perovskite as a model system, we pioneer the concept of “vacancy-mediated cooperative modulation of EM losses”. From the perspective of loss mechanisms, many reversible defect dipoles formed by the coupling of Mn vacancies and oxygen vacancies undergo directional alignment and relaxation motion under an external alternating EM field, generating significant polarization loss due to relaxation lag; moreover, lattice distortion induced by vacancies further strengthens the interfacial polarization effect, providing an additional contribution to polarization loss. In terms of conductive loss, the formation of vacancies effectively reduces the transport barrier of charge carriers, minimizing carrier scattering. Moreover, the improved order of vacancies significantly enhances the electron exchange efficiency, maintaining high conductive loss over a broad frequency range. Experiments confirm that conductive loss dominates the “strong absorption” characteristic of the material, whereas polarization loss governs the “broad bandwidth” characteristic; both are precisely regulated and synergistically enhanced via vacancy engineering. The results of the performance optimization show that when the ratio of Mn vacancies to oxygen vacancies reaches the optimum value (*x* = 0.15, LMCO-3), the material achieves an ultra-strong RLmin of −75.37 dB at a thickness of 2.8 mm; when *x* = 0.10 (LMCO-2), the EAB is expanded to 6.96 GHz, successfully meeting the dual core requirements of “strong absorption” and “broad bandwidth”. Verified by CST RCS simulation, they demonstrated outstanding microwave attenuation performance in the practical radar frequency band, providing a universal design scheme for high-performance, low-cost EM protection materials capable of serving at high temperatures. The absorption capacity of a material in the low-frequency band remains limited. However, this vacancy engineering strategy can be extended to other ABO3-type perovskite systems, opening a new path for the development of next-generation broadband and lightweight microwave-absorbing materials.
