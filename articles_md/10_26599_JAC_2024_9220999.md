## 1 Introduction

Dielectric capacitors are the optimal choice among currently available energy storage technologies to offer high power density and fast charging–discharging speed in pulse power systems [1,2]. However, the energy storage capability of dielectric capacitors is generally poor. Therefore, numerous efforts to improve the energy density/efficiency of dielectric materials have been made to meet the demands of advanced electronics and pulse power systems for integration and compactness [3−5].

The energy storage properties of dielectric materials can be determined by the polarization and electric field as follows: *W*rec* =∫PrPmaxEdP; η* =* Wrec/(Wrec+Wloss)×100%, where W*rec, *W*loss, *η*, *P*max, *P*r, and *E* are the recoverable energy density, energy loss, energy efficiency, maximum polarization, remnant polarization, and applied electric field, respectively. A slim *P‒E* loop with high *P*max, low *P*r, and high *E* (breakdown strength *E*b) is desirable to obtain high recoverable energy density and efficiency. On the basis of Refs. [6,7], relaxor ferroelectric and antiferroelectric materials are potential candidates for dielectric energy storage. (Bi05Na0.5)TiO3 (BNT), as a ferroelectric crystal, possesses a complicated phase structure [8,9] and variable dielectric/ferroelectric behavior, attracting increased attention for piezoelectric actuators and energy storage applications [10−12]. For example, the frequency-dependent dispersion behavior and double dielectric peak phenomenon of a 0.94(Bi05Na0.5)TiO3–0.06BaTiO3-based solid solution located at the morphotropic phase boundary (MPB) are considered to be related to the thermal competition between the rhombohedral *R*3*c* phase and the tetragonal *P*4*bm* phase [13]. Furthermore, several experimental methods, including *in* *situ* transmission electronic microscopy (TEM) and neutron diffraction, have been used to analyze the phase structure of BNT-based materials [9,14]; however, there are still some controversies regarding the phase/domain structure of the specified BNT-based ceramics due to their structural complexity.

Recently, high-quality BNT-based relaxor ferroelectrics have achieved high recoverable energy density via many methods, such as chemical doping [15], hierarchical structure design [16], advanced sintering technology [17], and defect structure engineering [18]. Unfortunately, the energy efficiency of BNT-based ceramics is still at a level of 60%–70%, which means that a large portion of stored energy is dissipated, generating more Joule heat [19,20]. Notably, low energy efficiency is a long-term neglected but important issue, and corresponding solutions need to be developed. An acceptable approach to achieve the high energy efficiency of BNT-based relaxor ferroelectrics is to form solid solutions using nonpolar phases, including paraelectric and linear dielectric phases, as end members, by which the long-range polar structure is broken into polar nanoregions (PNRs). A representative example is paraelectric BaZrO3-modified BNT-based relaxor ferroelectric ceramics, which have a high energy efficiency of 94% [21]. Nevertheless, the bottleneck for the achievable energy efficiency of this approach is strong phase competition with the polar structure. In other words, the ratio of the polar/nonpolar phase structure in BNT-based materials needs to be adjusted to improve energy efficiency. For the rare earth ion-doping strategy, the phase structure and dielectric/piezoelectric properties, such as those of trivalent ion(s) La3+, Gd3+, Sm3+-doped SrTiO3 [22], PbZrO3 [23], and BaTiO3 [24] solid solutions at the A-site, are easy to adjust, decreasing the phase transition temperature and increasing the nonpolar phase content. In addition, Sm3+ doping is an effective strategy for obtaining an ultrahigh piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33) in Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3–PbTiO3 (PMN–PT) single crystals/ceramics, as Sm3+ has a similar ionic radius to Pb2+ and changes the order degree of A-site ion(s) [25,26]. Therefore, aliovalent rare earth ion doping is feasible for realizing high energy efficiency for BNT-based ceramics, but the effects of defect-induced phase structures need to be further investigated in future research.

On the basis of our previous works [27,28], a new relaxor ferroelectric (Bi0.5Na0.5)0.6(Ba0.3Sr0.7)0.4TiO3 (Ba0.12Na0.3Bi0.3Sr0.28TiO3 (BNBST)) solid solution has a high *P*max at room temperature, but a relatively large hysteresis loss reduces its energy efficiency. To solve this issue, herein, we propose designing a strategy for defect-engineered phase/domain structures by aliovalent rare earth ion doping, i.e., Sm3+ replaces Sr2+ at the A-site to fabricate a series of Ba0.12Na0.3Bi0.3Sr0.28−1.5*x*□0.5*x*Sm*x*TiO3 (abbreviated as Sm*x*–BNBST, *x* = 0.00, 0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.07, and 0.08) solid solutions. Sm0.07–BNBST ceramics achieve a high energy efficiency of 91% together with a recoverable energy density of 2.1 J/cm3 at a low electric field of 114 kV/cm, which exceeds those of other reported dielectric materials at the same electric field. Sm0.07–BNBST ceramics also exhibit good stability and endurance in terms of temperature-, frequency-, and fatigue-dependent energy storage properties and fast charging‒discharging speed of 119 ns, demonstrating their great potential for pulse power applications.

### 2.1 Sample synthesis

Sm*x*–BNBST (Sm*x*–BNBST, *x* = 0.00, 0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.07, and 0.08) ceramics were fabricated via a solid-state reaction. Raw materials of Bi2O3 (99.9%), Na2CO3 (99.9%), TiO2 (98.9%), BaCO3 (99.9%), SrCO3 (99.9%), and Sm2O3 (99.9%) (purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were weighed according to the stoichiometric formula and mixed in a planetary ball for 24 h. After drying, the powder was calcined at 850 °C for 3 h and then milled again for 24 h. The mixed powder was compacted into green pellets 13 mm in diameter and 1 mm in thickness under 120±10 MPa pressure after granulation by adding polyvinyl alcohol solution (PVA, 5 wt%). Those disks were preheated at 650 °C for 2 h to remove the PVA organic binder and finally sintered at 1200–1260 °C for 2 h in an air atmosphere.

### 2.2 Microstructure characterization

The crystal structures of Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics were measured via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, SMARTLAB, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation. X-ray diffraction refinement was conducted by using GSAS-2 software. Surface scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics were obtained by an SEM (JSM-6700F, JEOL, Japan). The polarization switching behavior of Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics was analyzed via a piezoelectric force microscope (PFM; Jupiter XR, Oxford Instruments, USA) at different electric fields/relaxation durations.

### 2.3 Electrical property measurements

The temperature-dependent dielectric properties of Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics were measured via a precision impedance analyzer (HP4294A, Agilent, USA) over the temperature range from −60 to 400 °C. For *P‒E* loop and charging‒discharging measurements, Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics were polished to a thickness of approximately 0.32 mm and covered on a central electrode with a margin blank. The *P*–*E* hysteresis loops were measured via a ferroelectric analyzer (Trek model 609B, Radiant, USA) at 10 Hz via a standard Sawyer–Tower circuit. The temperature-, frequency-, and cycle-dependent *P*–*E* hysteresis loops of Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics were measured by the same ferroelectric workstation. The temperature-dependent charging‒discharging capability was tested via a designed RLC circuit (CFD-003, Tongguo Technology, China) connected to a temperature control chamber.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(a1) shows XRD patterns of Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics at 2*θ* = 20°–80°. When *x* ≤ 0.07, Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics have a single perovskite structure. As the Sm-doping content increases, the (200) diffraction peak gradually shifts toward a high angle, indicating a decreased *d* value, as shown in Fig. 1(a2), which is due to the ion radius of Sm3+ (1.24 Å, CN = 12) being smaller than that of Sr2+ (1.44 Å, CN = 12) [29] and the formation of Sr vacancies. Chemical modification leads to distortion and compression of the crystal structure, which influences the change in the phase ratio in BNT-based ceramics. With further increases in the *x* value, a secondary Bi4Ti3O12 phase occurs for the *x* = 0.08 composition. Considering the complicated phase structure in BNT-based ceramics, XRD refinements of all Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics were conducted. Herein, the tetragonal *P*4*bm* and pseudocubic *Pm** 3¯m* are chosen as reference phase structures for Rietveld refinement [28]. The fitted results of *R*-weighted pattern (*R*wp) and chi-square distribution (Chi2) for the representative *x* = 0.07 composition are shown in Table 1 and Fig. 1(b), respectively. All the Rietveld refinements of Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics are well-matched, which means that the fitted results are convincing. Figure 1(c) shows the phase fraction change vs*.* the *x* value for Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics. As Sm-doping content increases, the dominant pseudocubic phase content slightly increases and then decreases with further increases in the *x* value. The variation in the tetragonal phase content is the opposite. Sm doping can induce phase composition fluctuations to some degree, which can generate localized heterostructures. To evaluate the energy storage properties of this work, a comparison between *W*rec and *η* in this work and other reported dielectric materials at electric fields of 100–200 kV/cm was performed, as shown in Fig. 1(d). This work achieves a high energy efficiency of 91% and a discharge density of 2.1 J/cm3 at an electric field of 114 kV/cm, which exceeds those of other lead-free dielectric materials at the same electric field.

Figures 2(a)–2(f) show SEM images of Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics. All Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics have clear grains, grain boundaries, and no obvious pores. The average grain size of Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics is approximately 1–3 μm. As Sm-doping content increases, the average grain size first decreases but then gradually increases. This phenomenon can also be found in other rare earth ion-doped dielectric materials, which should be related to the high melting point of those rare earth ions [22,30].

The *P‒E* loops of Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics were measured at 80 kV/cm, as shown in Fig. 3(a). Compared with other BNT-based ceramics, pure BNBST ceramics exhibit a “clamped” *P‒E* loop [27]. Notably, antiferroelectric materials such as PbZrO3 has an obvious double *P‒E* loop characterized by a low *P*r and high *P*max [31,32]. As the Sm-doping content increases, *P‒E* loops of Sm*x*‒BNBST ceramics gradually decrease, and *P*max and *P*r gradually decrease. However, it should be noted that *P*max of Sm-doped BNBST ceramics with *x* = 0.07 has a sudden increase compared with that with *x* = 0.05 (Fig. 3(b)). This phenomenon effectively enhances the polarization difference of Δ*P* (*P*max − *P*r) for Sm*x**‒*BNBST ceramics; hence, Sm0.07*‒*BNBST ceramics achieve a high discharge density *W*rec of 2.1 J/cm3 at 114 kV/cm (Fig. 3(d)). This sudden increase in *P*max should be closely related to the synergetic contribution of tetragonal/pseudocubic phase competition and reduced domain size. More importantly, the energy efficiency of Sm-doped BNBST ceramics quickly increases from 56% to > 90% at the optimized composition (Fig. 3(c)), which should be closely related to a decreased domain size to form PNRs [33]. In fact, the modification mechanism is still suitable for high electric fields in BNT-based ceramics. If dielectric materials have a small domain size, polarization switching would be easy to finish after applying or removing the electric field, which is demonstrated in the next section.

PFM is an effective way to analyze the dynamics of polarization switching for ferroelectric/antiferroelectric materials [34]. A negative DC voltage is applied to the tip at a scanning area of 5 µm × 5 µm, and a positive voltage of the same amplitude is applied to a scanning area of 2 µm × 2 µm. Figures 4(a) and 4(c) show the out-of-plane phase images of Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics with *x* = 0 and 0.07 at different voltages. For the *x* = 0 composition, when the voltage increases from 5 to 10 V, the polarization direction achieves 180° switching, which demonstrates that its domain easily corresponds to strong ferroelectricity. With further increasing voltage, the polarization switching is unchanged. By comparison, for *x* = 0.07 composition, the domain could be written when the voltage approaches 20 V, which is related to the reduction in domain size caused by aliovalent doping rather than tetragonal/pseudocubic phase competition. The reduced domain size determines *P*r, whereas the tetragonal/pseudocubic phase competition determines *P*max. To further analyze the dynamic response of the domain after removing the electric field, out-of-plane PFM phase images of Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics with *x* = 0 and 0.07 at different relaxation durations are measured, as shown in Figs. 4(b) and 4(d). The domain structure is still maintained for the *x* = 0 composition when the relaxation duration reaches 5 min and gradually disappears at 10 and even 15 min (Fig. 4(b)). This phenomenon means that the direction of polarization for the *x* = 0 composition has difficulty switching its initial state and corresponds to a high *P*r. For the *x* = 0.07 composition, the direction of polarization quickly switches to the initial state when the applied voltage is removed (Fig. 4(d)), which corresponds to a low *P*r. The fast response of polarization switching in Sm0.07–BNBST ceramics demonstrates that they possess highly dynamic PNRs, which generate an imperceptible *P*r and moderate *P*max, contributing to an increased energy density and efficiency [23,35].

Figures 5(a)–5(f) show the temperature-dependent dielectric properties of Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics at frequencies of 1 kHz, 10 kHz, 100 kHz, and 1 MHz. All Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics display obvious frequency dispersion behavior [36]: As the frequency increases, the phase transition temperature at the dielectric peak shifts to a high temperature, and the corresponding dielectric constant decreases. For pure BSBNT ceramics, there is only one dielectric peak below 100 °C. For Sm*x*–BNBST ceramics, there is a new weak dielectric peak at ~200 °C. This phenomenon should be related to the defect-induced thermal evolution of the tetragonal *P*4*bm* phase and pseudocubic *Pm** 3¯m* phase competition in BNT-based ceramics [13]. The defect equation of Sm-doped BNBST-based ceramics is as follows: * Sm2O3→3SrO2SmSr⋅+VSr''+3OO. An increase in the number of Sr vacancies with increasing Sm doping content influences the phase structure change. As the Sm-doping content increases, the dielectric double peaks gradually broaden, and the corresponding transition temperature, especially T*m1, shifts to a low temperature, which is beneficial for improving the dielectric temperature stability. In other words, designing a defect structure could be used to adjust the temperature-induced phase competition in BNT-based materials.

For dielectric materials, the stability and reliability of energy storage properties play a vital role in determining their applications [37−40]. The temperature-dependent *P*‒*E* loops of Sm0.07‒BNBST ceramics were measured at 60 kV/cm, as shown in Fig. 6(a). As the temperature increases from 20 to 160 °C, Sm0.07*‒*BNBST ceramics exhibit a slim *P‒E* loop, and the energy efficiency *η* slightly varies at Fig. 6(b)). The decrease in *W*rec is attributed to the measured temperature exceeding the phase transition temperature *T*m1, leading to the generation of more high-temperature paraelectric phases. Similarly, frequency- and fatigue-dependent *P*‒*E* loops of Sm0.07‒BNBST ceramics are measured at 60 kV/cm, as shown in Figs. 6(c) and 6(e). As the frequency or number of cycles increases, *P*‒*E* loops of Sm0.07‒BNBST ceramics essentially remain unchanged, indicating good frequency stability and fatigue endurance. The variation in *η* for the frequency-/fatigue-dependent Sm0.07‒BNBST ceramics is less than 2% (Figs. 6(d) and 6(f)), indicating that it possesses good stability and reliability under different frequency-/fatigue-field conditions.

To evaluate the energy storage properties of Sm0.07‒BNBST ceramics dynamically, fast charging‒discharging measurements at underdamped/overdamped circuits have been performed [41]. Generally, the underdamped discharge properties can be calculated as Eqs. (1) and (2) [42]:

(1) j=ImaxS

(2) PD=E×Ima×S

where *j*, *I*max, *S*, and *P*D are the current density, maximum current, effective electrode area, and power density, respectively. Figure 7(a) shows the electric field-dependent *I*max, *J*, and *P*D of the Sm0.07–BNBST ceramics at the underdamped circuit. As the electric field increases, *J* and *P*D both linearly increase and reach values of 365.5 A/cm2 and 14.3 MW/cm3, respectively. The temperature-dependent Sm0.07–BNBST ceramics at the underdamped circuit are shown in Fig. 7(b). As the temperature increases, *I*max, *J*, and *P*D of Sm0.07–BNBST ceramics both increase, with *J* and *P*D values of 414.6 A/cm2 and 16.6 MW/cm3 (@100 °C), respectively.

*

The discharge density (*W*d) under overdamped conditions is calculated via Eq. (3) [5]:

(3) Wd=R∫i2dtV

where *R*, *i*, and *V* are the load resistance (100 Ω), discharge current, and effective electrode volume, respectively. Figure 7(c) shows the electric field-dependent *W*d of Sm0.07–BNBST ceramics at the overdamped circuit (@100 Ω). As the electric field increases, Sm0.07–BNBST ceramics achieve a high discharge density (*W*d) of 1.5 J/cm3 at 80 kV/cm together with a *t*0.9 of 119 ns, indicating their fast charging‒discharging speed. The temperature-dependent overdamping properties of Sm0.07–BNBST ceramics were measured at 80 kV/cm, as shown in Fig. 7(d). As the temperature increases, *W*d increases to 1.8 J/cm3, and a *t*0.9 of 160 ns is reached. Combined with their underdamped- and overdamped-dependent energy storage properties, Sm0.07–BNBST ceramics exhibit fast charging–discharging properties.

## 4 Conclusions

A high 91% energy efficiency with a recoverable energy density of 2.1 J/cm3 is achieved in Sm0.07–BNBST ceramics at a low electric field of 114 kV/cm, which is closely related to the reduced *P*r demonstrated by PFM measurements. Defect-induced phase competition between the tetragonal *P*4*bm* and pseudocubic *Pm** 3¯m* phases not only strengthens the polarization switching ability but also improves the dielectric temperature stability via thermal evolution. Sm0.07–BNBST relaxor ferroelectric ceramics exhibit good energy storage temperature and frequency stability, fatigue endurance, and fast charging–discharging capabilities. These features endow aliovalent rare earth-doped BNT-based relaxor ferroelectric ceramics at the A-site with high energy efficiency and provide new insight into phase/domain structure evolution via defect engineering for energy storage.
