## 1 Introduction

Ceramic matrix composites (CMCs) have attracted significant interest for high-temperature structural applications in aircraft engines to meet the stringent requirements of enhanced efficiency and the thrust-to-weight ratio [1−3]. Compared with Ni-based superalloys, silicon carbide fiber-reinforced SiC ceramic matrix composites (SiCf/SiC CMCs) offer high crack growth resistance, low density, and exceptional thermal and chemical properties are currently considered promising materials for turbine blades, combustor lines, and nozzles in aeroengines [4−6]. When subjected to high-temperature combustion environments, SiC-based CMC components undergo oxidation and develop a thermally grown oxide (TGO) layer on their surface. This TGO layer subsequently reacts with water vapor at high temperatures, generating gaseous silicon hydroxides, such as Si(OH)4. This reaction poses a significant challenge and results in a marked decrease in the lifespan of SiCf/SiC composites [7,8]. In addition, molten oxide particles (e.g., dust, volcanic ash, and desert sand) with a low melting point of approximately 1200 °C can be ingested with the intake air and deposit on the surface of hot-section components, leading to a continuous calcium–magnesium–alumino–silicate (CMAS) reaction, infiltration, and, ultimately, material failure [9,10]. Consequently, the application of environmental barrier coatings (EBCs) has been recognized as a viable approach for protecting SiCf/SiC CMC components against corrosive media such as high-temperature water vapor and CMAS in harsh combustion environments [11−13].

With the development of EBCs for SiCf/SiC CMCs, rare earth (RE) silicates have been extensively investigated as promising candidates because of their excellent high-temperature stability, reduced silica activity, and superior corrosion resistance to water vapor [14−16]. However, the application of rare-earth silicates as advanced EBCs is limited by their insufficient resistance to CMAS corrosion at elevated temperatures. Two primary failure mechanisms of rare-earth silicates exposed to CMAS have been reported: (1) dissolution into molten CMAS, followed by the precipitation of crystalline reaction products [17−20], and (2) CMAS infiltration via grain boundaries or pores, which solidifies upon cooling and reduces coating durability [21−23]. To increase the corrosion resistance of rare-earth silicates against CMAS, recent studies have employed two distinct strategies. One strategy involves modifying the composition of EBC materials, such as rare-earth silicates, leveraging the synergistic effect of the different reaction activities with CMAS of various rare-earth ions. This encompasses not only multicomponent rare-earth silicate modifications, such as (Er0.25Tm0.25Yb0.25Lu0.25)2Si2O7, (Y0.25Yb0.25Er0.25Sc0.25)2Si2O7, and (Gd0.2Ho0.2Er0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)2Si2O7 [24−26] but also rare-earth doping strategies, such as * Yb2xYxSi2O7 (x* = 0, 0.2, 1, or 2), γ-Y1.5Yb0.5Si2O7, and * YxYb2−xSi2O7 (x* = 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0) [27−29]. The other strategy focuses on investigating innovative EBC materials through compositional design, such as alumina-based aluminates like YAlO3, Al2O3/garnet coatings, and directionally solidified eutectics (DSEs) Al2O3/garnet composites, as well as REAlO3/RE2Zr2O7 composites [30−34]. These alumina-based materials exhibited significantly enhanced resistance capacity against CMAS. For example, the recession depth of an Al2O3/Y3Al5O12 DSE after CMAS corrosion at 1500 °C for 100 h was approximately 130 μm [32]. The fundamental standing point is that materials containing Al3+ in their composition can facilitate the formation of CMAS crystalline phases, such as anorthite (CaAl2Si2O8) and spinel (MgAl2O4), thereby minimizing coating material consumption [34,35]. This is attributed to the fact that the liquid phase concentration range of AlO1.5 is less than 10 mol% in the equilibrium phase diagram of the CaO–MgO–AlO1.5–SiO2 system at 1300 °C, whereas the liquid field encompasses changes spanning approximately 25 mol% in both SiO2 and CaO concentrations [36].

On the basis of the aforementioned criteria, materials such as Y4Al2O9 (YAM)/Y2O3 are expected to hold great promise as potential candidates for advanced EBCs. The concept for this hypothesis is described as follows: First, YAM has a unique combination of a high melting point (2020 °C), low density (4.44 g/cm3), low high-temperature thermal conductivity (the minimum thermal conductivity is 1.13 W/(m·K), and good damage tolerance ability. These characteristics render YAM a promising material for EBC applications [37−39]. In addition, the measured average linear coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of YAM is 7.37×10−6 K−1 [37], which is close to that of the commonly referred EBC material RE2SiO5 [40]. Additionally, YAM incorporates 13.3 mol% Al3+ in its composition, surpassing the liquid phase concentration range of AlO1.5, as indicated in the equilibrium phase diagram of the CaO–MgO–AlO1.5–SiO2 system, thereby identifying it as a promising CMAS-resistant EBC material. Second, YAM, characterized by an optical basicity (OB or *Λ*) value of 0.87, is expected to exhibit high reactivity with CMAS melt and actively consume CMAS during the corrosion reaction, as suggested by the screening criterion for selecting CMAS-reactive EBC ceramics [41−44]. The results of the corrosion behavior of YAM exposed to CMAS at 1250 °C illustrated that YAM could interact with CMAS and form a dense reaction layer rapidly [45]. Moreover, Y2O3, which also boasts a relatively high OB value of 0.99, is selected as an additional active phase for CMAS consumption and can coexist in equilibrium with YAM [44]. Therefore, these novel EBC materials may exhibit high reactivity with molten CMAS, enabling the rapid consumption of CMAS and the effective precipitation of various products with less consumption of material. Consequently, it is anticipated that the YAM/Y2O3 ceramics will exhibit excellent CMAS resistance.

In this study, novel YAM/Y2O3 ceramics were designed and prepared via the hot-pressing method, and the interactions of the as-sintered YAM/Y2O3 composite with CMAS at 1300 and 1500 °C were investigated in detail. After corrosion for different durations, the phase compositions, morphologies, and thicknesses of the reaction layers between the molten CMAS and the YAM/Y2O3 composite were characterized. The chemo-thermal mechanisms underlying these interactions between the YAM/Y2O3 ceramics and CMAS are discussed on the basis of the above results. This work may provide insights that can guide the design and development of superior CMAS-resistant EBC materials.

## 2 Experimental

High-purity commercially available submicron-sized Al2O3 (Sumitomo Chemical Co., Ltd., Japan) and Y2O3 powders (Rare-Chem. Hi-tech. Co., Ltd., China) were used as raw materials in this work to synthesize YAM/Y2O3. First, Al2O3 and Y2O3 powders with eutectic compositions (29.5 mol% Al2O3 and 70.5 mol% Y2O3) were precisely weighed and mixed with ethanol as the solvent and subsequently milled in a Si3N4 mill jar using Si3N4 balls. After 24 h of ball milling, the as-received slurry was dried at 80 °C for another 24 h and then sifted through an 80-mesh sieve. The pressure-less sintering method was employed to synthesize phase-pure YAM/Y2O3 powders with a two-phase mole ratio of 0.72 : 0.18 at 1300 °C for 3 h. For the fabrication of the bulk materials, the as-synthesized YAM/Y2O3 powders were placed into a boron nitride (BN)-coated graphite mold with a diameter of 50 mm. After being cold pressed under a pressure of 10 MPa for 5 min, the powders were further hot pressed at 1750 °C for 1 h under a pressure of 30 MPa. The density of the hot-pressed sample was measured via the Archimedes principle, with distilled water used as the immersion medium. For the phase distribution and grain size investigations, the polished surface of the as-prepared sample was thermally etched at 1500 °C for 3 h in a muffle furnace (KSL-1800X, Shenzhen Kejing Star Technology, China).

For the CMAS corrosion experiments, we adopted a representative CMAS composition consisting of 33 mol% CaO–9 mol% MgO–13 mol% AlO1.5–45 mol% SiO2, following the same procedure as in our previous study [24]. First, powders of CaO, MgO, Al2O3, and SiO2 (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) were precisely weighed on the basis of the stoichiometric ratios mentioned earlier and thoroughly mixed through ball-milling for 24 h. After drying, the resulting mixture was transferred into an alumina crucible and heated at 1200 °C for 24 h. Samples with dimensions of 8 mm × 8 mm × 3 mm were cut from the as-received YAM/Y2O3 bulk material, and the testing surfaces were polished with diamond paste with a 1.5 μm particle size. The as-prepared CMAS powders were subsequently homogeneously dispersed in ethanol and uniformly coated on the testing surfaces of the samples, with an average coating weight of approximately 30 mg/cm2 across the treated area. The YAM/Y2O3 samples were then placed inside a box furnace and subjected to a heating process at a rate of 10 °C/min until they reached 1300 and 1500 °C. These temperatures were maintained for durations of 1, 4, 25, and 50 h. For the characterization of cross-sectional morphologies, samples after CMAS corrosion were sectioned along the midline by a dicing saw and subsequently polished for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations.

The phase compositions of the as-prepared YAM/Y2O3 powders and CMAS-coated surfaces were determined via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/max-2400, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5406 Å). The XRD patterns were recorded in the 2*θ* range of 10°–70° with a scan speed of 10 (°)/min. Microstructural characterization of the cross section of the hot-pressed sample, as well as the CMAS interaction surfaces and polished cross sections, was conducted with an SEM (SUPRA 35, Zeiss, Germany) equipped with an energy-dispersive spectroscope (EDS; Azteclive, Oxford Instruments, UK). The composition of each sample was characterized via an electron probe X-ray microanalyzer (EPMA; JXA-iHP200F, JEOL Ltd., Japan). A transmission electron microscope (TEM; FEI Talos F200X, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to characterize the internal microstructure of the cross-sections of the samples that interacted with CMAS.

### 3.1 Characterizations of sintered YAM/Y2O3 bulk

Figure 1 shows the XRD patterns and SEM micrographs of the polished and thermally etched surfaces of the YAM/Y2O3 samples prepared via the hot-pressing method. The indexed XRD patterns presented in Fig. 1(a) indicate that the as-prepared YAM/Y2O3 sample is composed of two phases: Y4Al2O9 (PDF No. 34-0368) and Y2O3 (PDF No. 86-1107). Among the two phases, the YAM phase is dominant and aligns with the eutectic components of YAM and Y2O3. In Fig. 1(b), the surface of the YAM/Y2O3 sample has no discernible pores and displays a uniform distribution of two distinct phases with contrasting shades. The density of YAM/Y2O3 was measured to be 4.56 g/cm3 (~98.7% of its theoretical density) via the Archimedes technique. Figure 1(c) shows that the grains in the hot-pressed bulk YAM/Y2O3 are equiaxial grains, with an average grain size of 0.91±0.09 μm. This value is much smaller than the EDS detection threshold (2–3 μm in diameter), and EPMA was chosen for phase identification. According to the EPMA point analysis results presented in Table 1 and the previous XRD results shown in Fig. 1, it can be inferred that the darker phase, which comprises Y and Al in a mole ratio close to 2.57 : 1, corresponds to the YAM phase. Conversely, the lighter phase, characterized by a Y : O ratio of approximately 2 : 3, is identified as Y2O3.

### 3.2 Characterizations of YAM/Y2O3 bulk coated by CMAS at 1300 °C

Figure 2 shows the X-ray diffraction patterns of the YAM/Y2O3 surfaces after CMAS corrosion at 1300 °C for durations of 1, 4, 25, and 50 h. For the sample exposed to CMAS corrosion for 1 h, as indicated by the orange line in Fig. 2(a), diffraction peaks corresponding to apatite (Ca2Y8(SiO4)6O2 (PDF No. 27-1054), Ca4Y6(SiO4)6O (PDF No. 27-0093)), Y3Al5O12 (PDF No. 33-0040), and Ca2Al2SiO7 (PDF No. 74-1607) could be identified. Among these phases, Y3Al5O12 is the predominant product with the highest peak intensity. When the corrosion time is extended to 4 h, as indicated by the purple line, all three aforementioned reaction products are discernible. Compared with that of the sample corroded for 1 h, the diffraction peak intensity of Y3Al5O12 decreased, whereas that of Ca2Al2SiO7 and apatite increased. Upon extending the corrosion time to 25 h, as indicated by the green line, the diffraction peak intensity of the Ca2Al2SiO7 phase decreases significantly, and the Y3Al5O12 phase is no longer detectable. Apatite emerges as the predominant product. The corrosion products after 50 h, as depicted by the blue line, are primarily composed of apatite with trace amounts of Ca2Al2SiO7. In addition, to improve the visibility of local features, XRD patterns on a logarithmic scale within 20°–45° range are shown in Fig. 2(b). Notably, obvious broad humps are visible within the orange and purple lines, which correspond to the results after CMAS corrosion for 1 and 4 h, respectively. These humps indicate the existence of amorphous phases, likely resulting from residual CMAS retained on the surface after the reaction for 1 and 4 h.

The surface observations of YAM/Y2O3 after CMAS corrosion at 1300 °C for 1, 4, 25, and 50 h are displayed in Fig. 3. Figures 3(a) and 3(b) show that the dark gray phases (point 2) accommodate both white stick-like phases (point 1) and light gray block phases (point 3) on the surfaces of YAM/Y2O3 after CMAS corrosion at 1300 °C for 1 and 4 h. As revealed by the EDS analysis results presented in Table 2, it is reasonable to conclude that these stick-like phases are Ca–Y–Si apatite phases. The dark gray phase could be recognized as residual CMAS. According to Godbole et al*. [46], the substitution mechanisms of garnet solid solutions include the substitution of Ca2+ or Mg2+ for Y3+ and the substitution of Si4+ or Mg2+ for Al3+. The light gray block phases, characterized by an approximate mole ratio of (Ca + Y) : (Al + Si + Mg) close to 3 : 5, can probably be identified as garnet phases ((Ca0.70Y0.31)3(Al0.30Mg0.12Si0.58)5) rather than Y3Al5O12, which represents a pure garnet phase containing no additional elements. This identification is consistently applied to the garnet referred to in the subsequent text. Importantly, only cations, excluding the anion O2−, are considered in this chemical formula, and the subsequent molecules studied herein employ analogous expressions. In addition, Fig. 3(b) also reveals the presence of several arrow-shaped crystalline phases (point 4), which are distinctly highlighted with yellow circles. Through the integration of EDS analysis with XRD results, it can be speculated that these phases correspond to melilite (Ca2Al2SiO7) with a higher Al : Ca ratio than CMAS, which is an intrinsic crystallization product of CMAS [47]. Notably, garnet constitutes the primary reaction product, with traces of apatite present on the surface after CMAS corrosion for 1 h. As the reaction duration is extended to 4 h, the garnet phases decrease in abundance, whereas the melilite phases increase, which supports the XRD results depicted in Fig. 2. Upon further extending the reaction time to 25 and 50 h, only stick-like apatite phases (point 5) and residual CMAS are observable on the sample surface.

The cross-sectional images of YAM/Y2O3 and CMAS at 1300 °C with different exposure times are shown in Fig. 4. Figures 4(a1)–4(a4) present low-magnification images corresponding to CMAS corrosion at 1300 °C for 1, 4, 25, and 50 h, respectively. Figures 4(b1)–4(b4), 4(c1)–4(c4), and 4(d1)–4(d4) show the regions delineated by black, red, and yellow dashed boxes in Figs. 4(a1)–4(a4), respectively. As illustrated in Figs. 4(a1)–4(a4), it is evident that the reaction products can be stratified into multiple layers, which undergo gradual transformation as time progresses. After 1 h of the CMAS reaction, as shown in Fig. 4(a1), only continuous and evenly distributed product layer 1 can be found, consisting primarily of white stick-like phases (point 4). In addition, there exists an obvious and intact dark gray phase (point 2) situated above this product layer. On the basis of the EDS results tabulated in Table 3, the white stick-like phase represents apatite, and the dark gray phase corresponds to residual CMAS. In addition, the light gray block phases (point 3) shown in the apatite layer are identified as the garnet phase [(Ca0.65Y0.29)3(Al0.27Mg0.17Si0.60)5]. Beneath the apatite layer, there are several phases with darker contrast (points 5 and 6), which cannot be accurately determined by the current SEM-EDS analysis because their sizes are below the resolution threshold of EDS. When the corrosion time increases to 4 h, a continuous apatite layer can also be discovered beneath the residual CMAS layer. In addition, a novel reaction layer 2 becomes visible beneath the apatite layer in Fig. 4(a2). This reaction layer in Fig. 4(c2) was found to be composed of a multitude of flocculent phases (point 7), accompanied by a scattering of white stick-like phases (point 8), as well as dark gray phases (point 9). On the basis of the EDS results listed in Table 3, these phases correspond to garnet, apatite, and CMAS. As the reaction time is further extended to 25 h, a new dual-phase zone labeled layer 3 in Fig. 4(a3) appears below layer 2, whereas the residual CMAS situated above the apatite layer disappears. This zone comprises densely arranged gray phases (point 10) and white phases (point 11), and the gray phase with (Ca + Y) : (Mg + Al + Si) at an approximate mole ratio of 3 : 5 is identified as garnet [(Ca0.40Y0.65)3(Al0.46Mg0.11Si0.40)5]. Previous works have reported that the apatite phase has a general formula  M4IM6II(SiO4)6Ox and that Ca atoms can only occupy MΙ sites, whereas rare earth atoms can reside on either MΙ or MII sites. This implies that the mole ratio MII : MΙ is highly variable when defects are introduced [45,48,49]. Consequently, the white phase is identified as the apatite phase. The results obtained after 50 h of reaction are comparable to those observed at 25 h, and the reaction products can also be divided into an apatite layer, a tri-phase layer (garnet, apatite, and CMAS), and a dual-phase layer (apatite and garnet). The product interfaces in this work demonstrate enhanced uniformity compared with the YAM-CMAS reaction [45]. In addition, this enhancement is primarily due to the accelerated formation of dense apatite layers, facilitated by the presence of Y2O3 as the active phase interacting with CMAS, thereby slowing the diffusion rate of CMAS and reducing the subsequent reaction rate. The apatite product layer formed after the reaction between CMAS and YAM bulk at 1350 °C for 1 h exhibited a thickness of approximately 11.5 μm [50], which is less than that observed in the reaction between YAM/Y2O3 and CMAS at 1300 °C for 1 h (32.4 μm). Therefore, the presence of Y2O3 promotes the formation of a thicker reaction layer, even at a lower temperature. Furthermore, the coexistence of YAM and Y2O3 may promote the formation of garnet, but it is challenging for single Y2O3 or YAM to form garnet during the reaction [51]. For example, Liang et al*. [51] studied the reaction behavior between Y2O3 and CMAS, and the results revealed that only the Y-apatite phase formed at temperatures ranging from 1250–1400 °C. The garnet phases contain alkaline constituents (CaO and MgO) derived from CMAS, leading to a reduction in viscosity and mitigated CMAS penetration [52,53]. These findings provide evidence that the presence of Al3+ facilitates the formation of garnets.

Compared with rare earth disilicates (RE2Si2O7), which are completely penetrated by CMAS along their grain boundaries at 1500 °C [21−23], the YAM/Y2O3 composites in this study exhibit excellent resistance to CMAS attack, with a reaction thickness of merely 197.71 μm after 50 h of exposure.

### 4.2 Reaction mechanism of YAM/Y2O3  and CMAS at 1300 °C

On the basis of the aforementioned findings regarding the reaction products of various corrosion reactions, the time-dependent CMAS corrosion process of YAM/Y2O3 at 1300 °C is schematically illustrated in Fig. 12. These images illustrate that the reaction mechanism between YAM/Y2O3 and CMAS involves the preferential precipitation of apatite, accompanied by an increase in the amount of Al3+ in the melt, resulting in the formation of garnet. Subsequently, garnet and apatite undergo competitive precipitation, which is controlled by the Al : Si ratio in the CMAS melt after the formation of the garnet layer. Furthermore, garnet serves as an efficient product for CMAS consumption, significantly reducing the reactivity of CMAS by consuming more Ca2+ and Si4+ than other products do. As shown in Fig. 12(a), the original state of the YAM/Y2O3 sample exposed to CMAS is clearly observed. The reaction initiates with the dissolution of YAM and Y2O3 into the molten CMAS, accompanied by an increase in the concentrations of Y3+ and Al3+ within the melt. Figures 12(b)–12(e) correspond to YAM/Y2O3 undergoing CMAS corrosion at 1300 °C for 1, 4, 25, and 50 h, respectively. After a reaction duration of 1 h, the reaction products are garnet Ι, apatite (layer 1 in Fig. 4(a1)), and residual CMAS. When the corrosion time increases to 4 h, the same products persist on the surface of YAM/Y2O3, with no noticeable changes in the thickness of the apatite layer. However, the garnet II layer (layer 2 in Fig. 4(a2)) is observed beneath the apatite layer. With time further elongating to 25 and 50 h, garnet Ι cannot be observed on the surface, whereas the dual-phase region (layer 3 in Figs. 4(a3) and 4(a4)) containing garnet II and apatite appears near the substrate. The similar change tendencies of garnet Ι and residual CMAS may suggest that garnet Ι precipitates from the residual CMAS upon cooling. Therefore, only changes in apatite and garnet II occur during the reaction process. As time progresses further, the stability of the garnet decreases due to the diminished concentration of Al (or reduced Al : Si ratio), and apatite re-emerges in the garnet layer (as illustrated in Fig. 12(d)). Subsequently, garnet II and apatite are generated from the CMAS competitively, depending on the concentration of Al (or the Al : Si ratio). A dense layer consisting of a dual-phase zone with apatite and garnet II forms at the interface near the substrate (Figs. 12(d) and 12(e)) because of the complete depletion of the CMAS within the interval between apatite and garnet. A large amount of Y3+ and Al3+ ions supplied by the substrate exist in the CMAS melt near the substrate, rendering the CMAS melt susceptible to the formation of apatite and garnet phases. The mole fraction of Al3+ among all the cations in the top region of layer 2 is 22.2%, which is lower than the 25.9% observed in the bottom region of layer 2, indicating more dissolution of YAM near the substrate. This uniform distribution of apatite and garnet II can be attributed to the homogeneous dispersion of Y2O3 and YAM in the YAM/Y2O3 composite.

Notably, according to Reaction (2), the formation of garnet requires the consumption of 10.93 moles of CMAS melt with one mole of YAM/Y2O3, whereas the formation of apatite necessitates the consumption of 5.73 mol of CMAS, as indicated in Recation (1). These findings demonstrate that garnet is a more efficient reaction product for reducing the reactivity of CMAS than apatite is. For the majority of EBC materials, including β-Yb2Si2O7, RE2Si2O7 (RE = Y, Yb, and Lu), various multicomponent rare-earth silicates, and Y2O3 (one of the components of the designed material in this work), apatite functions as the predominant reaction product [18−26]. However, the formation of apatite consumes merely 1.5 mol of CMAS melt per mole of rare-earth silicates or Y2O3, as reported by Zhao *et al*. [55], which is significantly less depleted in CMAS than in YAM/Y2O3.

### 4.3 Reaction mechanism of YAM/Y2O3 and CMAS at 1500 °C

The CMAS reaction with YAM/Y2O3 at 1500 °C proceeds via a distinctly different mechanism than that at 1300 °C, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 13. At 1500 °C, the apatite layer still forms first, increasing the Al3+ concentration in the CMAS melt, which subsequently leads to the generation of garnet. Then, competitive precipitation of garnet and cuspidine occurs, which is controlled by the Al : Si ratio in the CMAS melt. The replacement of apatite by cuspidine is attributed to the presence of a relatively high concentration of Ca2+ in the melt at 1500 °C. In addition, garnet and cuspidine function as highly efficient products for consuming CMAS, reducing its reactivity. Figure 13(a) depicts the original state of the YAM/Y2O3 sample exposed to CMAS, where the dissolution of YAM and Y2O3 into the molten CMAS occurs, accompanied by an increase in the concentrations of Y3+ and Al3+ within the melt, which is similar to that observed at 1300 °C. As the reaction progresses, the reaction products after one hour of corrosion can be divided into three layers: apatite at the upper layer (layer 1 in Fig. 8), garnet I at the middle layer (layer 2 in Fig. 8), and a dual-phase zone (layer 4 in Fig. 8) consisting of garnet II and cuspidine I near the substrate, as evident from Fig. 13(b). The formation of cuspidine requires the consumption of 5.75 moles of CMAS melt per mole of YAM/Y2O3, and it is also an efficient reaction product for reducing the reactivity of CMAS, similar to garnet. A similar microstructure, comprising cuspidine, garnet, and fluorite, was observed in the work of Poerschke et al*. [56], who investigated the reaction between Yb4Hf3O12 and CMAS at 1500 °C.

Upon increasing the corrosion time to 4 h, a novel cuspidine II layer (layer 3 in Fig. 8(a2)) emerges between garnet Ι and the dual-phase zone in Fig. 13(c). This emergence is accompanied by a reduction in the thickness of the garnet Ι layer and a simultaneous increase in the thickness of the YAM layer lacking Y2O3 (Fig. 8(d2)). The total thicknesses of the garnet Ι layer plus the cuspidine II layer after 4, 25, and 50 h are 32.34, 40.38, and 31.95 μm, respectively, which are all less than the thickness of the garnet Ι layer of 46.48 μm after 1 h in Fig. 11(b), indicating that the entire cuspidine II layer is derived from the garnet Ι layer. The formation of this cuspidine II layer may be associated with the reaction between garnet Ι and Y2O3, which provides the necessary Y3+ required for the transformation of garnet Ι into cuspidine II. The phase transition from garnet to cuspidine is attributed to the solid diffusion of Y3+. The Y3+ concentration gradient across various reaction layers serves as the driving force for this solid diffusion process. The Y : Al ratio in garnet II within the two-phase zone is greater than that in garnet I. Specifically, the EDS results for the Y : Al ratios in garnet Ι are 0.80, 0.75, 0.78, and 0.69, corresponding to 1, 4, 25, and 50 h, respectively, whereas those in garnet Ⅱ are 0.89, 0.97, 0.90, and 0.91, respectively. Consequently, Y3+ diffusion initiates from Y2O3 and eventually reaches garnet Ι, leading to the transformation of garnet (in both the garnet layer and the dual-phase zone) to cuspidine. The volume fraction of the cuspidine phase (from 0.97 to 1.50 and finally to 2.12, corresponding to 1, 4, and 25 h reactions) within the dual-phase zone increase as the reaction progresses, indicating that the transformation of garnet to cuspidine also takes place within the dual-phase zone. However, the garnet phases within the dual-phase zone are separated by cuspidine phases. Consequently, the phase transformation manifests as an increase in the volume fraction of the cuspidine phase rather than the formation of a pure cuspidine product layer. Finally, a pure cuspidine product layer is transformed from a pure garnet layer as the middle layer. This speculation is also supported by the fact that an increase in the thickness of the YAM layer devoid of Y2O3 (Figs. 8(d2)–8(d4)) could be identified from 10.40 μm at 4 h to 36.04 μm at 25 h and then to 48.12 μm at 50 h.

The accelerated reaction and formation of garnet and cuspidine could be advantageous for the YAM/Y2O3 composite in terms of CMAS resistance, which can significantly reduce the reactivity of CMAS and impede the penetration of CMAS glass along grain boundaries at elevated temperatures. Furthermore, the YAM/Y2O3 composite has a Si activity of zero, which is a significant advantage over Si-containing EBC materials, especially from the perspective of high-velocity water‒vapor corrosion. However, the moderate CTE mismatch of YAM and SiC-based CMCs is a disadvantage. Nonetheless, cracking at sharp interfaces induced by CTE mismatch can be mitigated by incorporating a CTE-graded bond coat between the CMCs and the YAM/Y2O3 EBC. Alternatively, the YAM/Y2O3 composite can be applied as an outermost layer over traditional RE-disilicates EBC to provide protection against CMAS corrosion.

## 5 Conclusions

In summary, a novel Y4Al2O9/Y2O3 composite was designed and subjected to CMAS corrosion testing at temperatures of 1300 and 1500 °C. The CMAS corrosion resistance ability and the reaction mechanism between Y4Al2O9/Y2O3 and molten CMAS were systematically investigated. The key findings are summarized as follows:

(1) At 1300 °C, the maximum reaction product thickness was 137.50 μm after 50 h of corrosion. An apatite layer forms within the first hour, followed by the formation of a garnet layer beneath it after 4 h. Ultimately, after 25 and 50 h, the reaction crystallization products can be delineated into three distinct layers: an apatite layer, a three-phase layer comprising garnet, apatite, and CMAS, and a dense two-phase layer containing garnet and apatite in the sequence moving away from the substrate. Additionally, the thickness of various layers varies with corrosion time, except for the apatite layer, which remains constant throughout the duration of 1–50 h.

(2) At 1500 °C, the maximum thickness of the reaction products is 198.88 μm after 25 h of corrosion, with no noticeable changes observed after 50 h. The reaction crystallization products can be categorized into three layers, moving away from the substrate: an apatite layer, a garnet layer, and a dense two-phase layer (comprising garnet and cuspidine) after 1 h of reaction. By 4 h, a novel cuspidine layer emerges between the garnet layer and the two-phase layer, which is attributed to the transformation of garnet facilitated by the incorporation of Y3+ ions originating from Y2O3 in the YAM/Y2O3 substrate.

(3) The YAM/Y2O3 composite investigated in this work exhibited significant CMAS resistance at both 1300 and 1500 °C, with no significant grain-boundary penetration by CMAS glass. The rapid formation of dense product layers reduces the infiltration of CMAS into the substrate along the grain boundaries and the reaction rate. The analysis of the reaction mechanism demonstrates that the incorporation of Al3+ and Y3+ into the composition promotes the formation of garnet and cuspidine phases, which are enriched with high silica and alkaline earth contents (CaO and MgO). Moreover, the formation of garnet and cuspidine necessitates the consumption of 10.93 and 5.75 moles of CMAS, respectively, for every mole of YAM/Y2O3. Consequently, this significantly reduces the reactivity of CMAS and the consumption of EBC materials. This study confirms the feasibility of selecting high-reactivity materials with CMAS at elevated temperatures in terms of chemical composition during material design, enabling rapid crystallization phase precipitation and effective CMAS melt consumption, and may provide valuable insights to guide the design and development of excellent CMAS-resistant materials.
