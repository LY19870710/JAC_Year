## 1 Introduction

Relays are extensively utilized in accelerators, satellites, rockets, and various advanced technology sectors. They play crucial roles in signal transmission, long-distance control implementation, and protection circuits, directly impacting the safety of aerospace and defense equipment systems. The selection of electrical contact material in a relay is crucial for its performance [1]. Arc discharge, characterized by high temperature, heat, and energy, commonly occurs during operation. Consequently, the arc erodes the electric contact material, causing craters, spattered particles, compositional changes, and a decrease in performance [2]. Traditionally used silver-based electrical contact materials, such as AgCdO [3] and AgSnO2 [4,5], suffer from chromium toxicity or poor wettability and severe temperature increases [6]. Therefore, it is necessary to find a new reinforcement phase material to replace CdO and SnO2 materials.

MAX phase materials are a type of ceramic material known for their good electrical conductivity [7−11], and the arc erosion performance of MAX phase-enhanced silver-based electrical contact materials has recently been investigated. The good wettability between Ag and MAX (Ti3SiC2, Ti3AlC2, and Ti2SnC) restricted the flow and splashing of Ag in the molten pool, suggesting that Ti3SiC2, Ti3AlC2, and Ti2SnC have the potential to replace CdO as reinforcements in the Ag matrix [12−17]. Ti3AlC2 material underwent degradation through a rapid “decomposition−oxidation” mechanism [18]. Furthermore, the Ag–Ti3AlC2 material produced by equal channel angular pressing (ECAP) exhibited the best arc erosion resistance [19]. Ultimately, the thick oxide layer formed on the surface of Ti3AlC2 enhanced the mechanical properties and overall arc erosion resistance of the Ag–Ti3AlC2 material within the range of the 1000th to 6200th arc erosion cycles [8]. Notably, compared to the Ag–Ti3AlC2 composite, the Ag–Ti3SiC2 material demonstrated superior arc erosion resistance due to the stable nature of Ti3SiC2 and the lower resistivity of the composite [20]. The diverse microstructure and chemical composition of the Ag–Ti2AlC material were attributed to the melting and vaporization of Ag, absorption of O2, deposition of Ag–O vapor, and interdiffusion of Ag–Al [21].

However, the influence of the work function on the arc erosion resistance of Ag‒MAX electrical contact materials has not been thoroughly explored in the literature. The work function is defined as the energy needed to overcome the surface barrier for an electron to escape from a solid surface [22]. When particles collide with the cathode surface, the atoms or molecules on the surface absorb energy from the collision. If the energy surpasses the work function of the materials, electrons will be released from the surface, leading to ionization of the cathode material [23]. This results in more particles being available to sustain arc burning. Hence, the arc tends to preferentially strike the phase with a lower work function [24]. Ta2AlC has a hexagonal crystal system, and the *P*63/*mmc* space group is a typical MAX material that can be used as an enhancement phase for Ag-based contact materials [25,26]. Ta2AlC exhibits excellent thermal conductivity (28.4 W∙m−1∙K−1) and electrical conductivity (3.91 ×106 Ω−1∙m−1) and low Vickers hardness (4.67–7.46 GPa) and shear modulus (121 GPa) [26]. It can be machined like metal and exhibits plasticity at higher temperatures but also possesses ceramic properties such as high yield strength, high melting point, high thermal stability, and good oxidation resistance. First-principles calculations revealed that the work function of Ta2AlC is 6.7192 eV, which is greater than that of common MAX phase contact materials, such as Ti3SiC2 (6.5203 eV), Ti3AlC2 (6.1264 eV), Ti2AlC (6.1251 eV), Ti2SnC (6.2498 eV), Cr2AlC (6.3556 eV), and Nb2AlC (6.5697 eV). This higher work function suggests a lower probability for electrons to escape from the material surface, indicating that Ta2AlC may be a more suitable reinforcement phase for the Ag-based electrical contact materials.

In our previous study, Ti3SiC2 or Ti3AlC2 dispersed the arc, preventing concentrated arc erosion in specific areas and reducing its impact on the material [27,28]. Herein, Ag–Ta2AlC composites with volume fractions of 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% were fabricated by hot pressing sintering. The arc responsible for the eroded surface was generated by a strongly nonuniform field. A comparative analysis of the arc erosion properties of Ag–Ta2AlC composites with various compositions was conducted. Additionally, the microstructural and chemical changes in the Ag–Ta2AlC composites were observed and recorded experimentally. The mechanism of arc erosion and morphology were systematically explained. The findings of this study may broaden the application of MAX phase materials in the field of electrical contact materials.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

Ag powder (> 99.8%, 200 mesh) was acquired from Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. An X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the original Ta2AlC powder (> 98%, 200 mesh, Laizhou Kai Kai Ceramic Materials Co., Ltd.) is illustrated in Fig. S1(a) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). A certain mass of Ag powder and Ta2AlC powder was weighed according to Ta2AlC volume percentages of 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40%. The two powders were mechanically mixed for 2 h, and Fig. S1(b) in the ESM displays the XRD pattern of the mixed powders.

The mixed powder was sintered into a bulk material by hot-pressing sintering (ZT-40-20Y, Shanghai Chenhua Science Technology Co., Ltd.). The process of hot-pressing sintering was carried out by heating to 700 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min and applying a pressure of 30 MPa for 30 min. Then, the bulk cooled inside the furnace. The preparation process of the Ag–Ta2AlC composite is shown in Fig. 1(a). According to the emission center model, the field strength at the micro protrusions on the cathode surface was very strong, and the heat generated by the field electron emission current in the area increased the temperature at the micro protrusions [29]. To minimize this impact, the samples were carefully polished until the arithmetical mean deviation of the profile (*R*a) was smaller than 0.5 μm. The obtained samples were then cleaned and dried for arc erosion testing. The obtained *R*a values are shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. The arithmetical mean deviation of the profile (*R*a) of the obtained samples is shown in Table S1 in the ESM, which was measured using a surface roughness tester (MarSurf PS 10, Mahr). The relative density was determined by Archimedes’ principle. A microhardness tester (YZHV-IZP, Shanghai Aolong Xingdi Testing Instrument Co., Ltd.) was used to measure the hardness of the AgTa2AlC composite material under 100 g for 30 s. The electrical conductivity was measured by a multifunction digital four-probe tester (ST2258C, Suzhou Lattice Electronics Co., Ltd.).

### 2.2 Arc erosion test

The Ag–Ta2AlC composites with different compositions were placed in a self-made arc erosion device, which was carried out in an atmospheric environment. The equipment setup, as illustrated in Fig. 1(b), included systems for arc generation, arc parameters, and circuit control. In the system of arc generation, the cathode consisted of the Ag–Ta2AlC composite, and the anode was a W-rod with a 2 mm tip. Prior to initiating the arc, a voltage of 10 kV was applied between the two electrodes using the circuit control system. The cathode moved toward the anode at a speed of 0.2 mm·s−1. The arc formed before the two electrodes came into contact, accompanied by visible light and sound. The arcing time and breakdown current were tested at the time of arc occurrence by a digital memory oscilloscope (ADS1102CAL, Shenzhen AXTEK Technology Co., Ltd.) by an arc parameter analysis system.

The eroded surface of the Ag–Ta2AlC composite was characterized by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; JEM-2100F, JEOL) equipped with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The surface composition of the eroded Ag–Ta2AlC was recorded and analyzed by a Raman spectrometer (Lab RAM-HR, HORIBA), which was used to detect the type of bonding over the wavenumber range of 100 to 1100 cm−1. The Raman spectra were excited with a Nd:YAG laser, with an incident power of 4 mW. The surface topographic features of the eroded samples with different Ta2AlC contents were analyzed using a three-dimensional laser scanning confocal microscope (3D LSCM, VK-X1000, Keyence) in a laser mode.

### 2.3 Work function of Ta<sub>2</sub>AlC

The work function of the Ta2AlC material was calculated by first-principles calculations. Spin-polarized first-principles calculations were performed according to the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof exchange–correlation functional, as implemented in the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP). The projector augmented wave method and the corresponding pseudopotentials were employed, and the convergence thresholds for the energy and force were 10−5 eV and 0.01 eV·Å−1, respectively. A cutoff energy of 600 eV was set for the plane wave basis set. The calculations used a 12 × 12 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack *k*-point mesh to sample the first Brillouin zone. To avoid interactions between the slabs, a vacuum space of 15 Å was applied along the *z*-direction of the (0001) structure, and dipole correction in the *z*-direction was considered in the calculation of the slab model. The plots of relative density, hardness and electrical conductivity are shown in Fig. 5. The composites consisted of Ag–10 vol% Ta2AlC, Ag–20 vol% Ta2AlC, Ag–30 vol% Ta2AlC, and Ag–40 vol% Ta2AlC. The relative densities of these composites are 98.2%, 96.4%, 94.5%, and 91.3%, respectively. It is evident that as the Ta2AlC content increases, the density decreases. This phenomenon is attributed to the increase in the reinforcement phase, leading to greater interfacial areas and more pores. The microhardness of Ta2AlC initially increases and then decreases as its content increases. This trend is attributed to the greater hardness of the Ta2AlC powder compared to that of metallic silver. The hardness shows an increasing trend with increasing Ta2AlC content, reaching a peak of 97.4 HV at a volume fraction of 30%. However, as the Ta2AlC content continues to increase, the hardness decreases to 93.9 HV due to the presence of increased interfaces and pores. When the Ta2AlC volume fraction is 10%, the highest conductivity is 0.141 MS·cm−1. With an increase in the Ta2AlC content to 20%, 30%, and 40%, the conductivity decreases to 0.130, 0.106, and 0.089 MS·cm−1, respectively. This decrease in conductivity can be attributed to the greater presence of the reinforcement phase, leading to a greater interface that hinders electron transfer. Moreover, an increase in the Ta2AlC content leads to a decrease in the density and an increase in the number of holes, further impeding electron transfer and consequently reducing the conductivity.

## 4 Discussion

Without external factors, neutral gas molecules do not typically conduct electricity. Due to the presence of some radiation from space in air, gases are weakly ionized and produce a small number of charged particles, generally approximately 500–1000 pairs of positively and negatively charged particles per cubic centimeter in the atmosphere [35]. This number of charged particles will be very small compared with the density of atmospheric molecules because air serves as an excellent insulator. Therefore, the arc erosion experiment was conducted under open-air conditions. Under the action of external factors, the originally insulated air became electrically conductive, accompanied by gas discharge. Arc erosion essentially serves as a form of gas discharge.

### 4.1 Breakdown of gas and morphological formation

When a voltage of 10 kV was applied to the anode (tungsten rod) and cathode (Ag–Ta2AlC composite), as shown in Fig. 12(a), the two electrodes experienced slow relative motion. Because the radius of curvature of the anode head was 2 mm, the cathode consisted of a massive planar block 15 mm in diameter, and at a certain distance, a strongly nonuniform field formed, as shown in Fig. 12(a). A large number of electrons and positive ions are produced, as shown in Fig. 12(b). Consequently, an increasing number of electrons moved toward the anode. The number of charged particles increased rapidly, similar to an avalanche, which is known as a primary electron avalanche [36]. Figure 12(c) shows a schematic of this process.

*
Fig. 12(a) Anode and cathode, (b) ionization by collision, (c) electron avalanche, (d) field distortion due to space charge, (e) photoionization, (f) secondary electron avalanche, (g) positive streamer, (h) breakdown of gas, (i) formation of electromagnetic force, (j) formation of plasma flow force, and (k) arc erosion.

The space charges produced their own field (E*sp), which amplified the electric field toward the cathode, as illustrated in Fig. 12(d). Inside the primary electron avalanche, an electric field was present in the opposite direction to the external field (*E*ex), which weakened the strength of *E*ex. Subsequently, many photons were released to the surrounding area. The electrons produced by photoionization are known as photoelectrons, as depicted in Fig. 12(e). The photoelectrons continued to collide with the particles, producing positive ions and electrons; namely, the photoelectrons produced a secondary electron avalanche, as shown in Fig. 12(f). A large number of positively and negatively charged particles generate a plasma, known as a positive streamer [37], as shown in Fig. 12(g). After a voltage of 10 kV was applied between the electrodes, the positive streamer toward the cathode rapidly developed due to the continuous participation of the secondary electron avalanche, as shown in Fig. 12(h). Significantly, the conductivity of the gap between the two electrodes was enhanced, resulting in the breakdown of gas. The current versus time curve showed a current spike in the form of a breakdown current, as clearly visible in Fig. 6.

During gas discharge, the formation of streamers produces a significant amount of energy and photons, which contributes to the high temperature of the arc (6700–8300 K) [38]. This intense arc heat interacting with the cathode surface caused the material to melt, resulting in the formation of a molten pool, as shown in Fig. 12(k). An electromagnetic force is created when currents flow in the same direction through two parallel wires in close proximity, resulting in mutual attraction. When the gas breaks down, the plasma forms. When the plasma flows between the cathode and the anode, it can be seen as consisting of multiple current lines, as shown by *i*1 and *i*2 in Fig. 12(i). These current lines interact with their own magnetic fields, resulting in attraction forces (*f*1 and *f*2). By decomposing *f*1 and *f*2 shown in Fig. 12(i), the electromagnetic forces (*F*1 and *F*2) are produced in the vertical direction. *F*1 and *F*2 act on the molten pool, leading to a pit-like morphology in the pool. The size of the electromagnetic force gradient (*F*t) is related to the width of the arc (*R*b). *R*b changed continuously in the axial direction, resulting in *F*t that caused the plasma to flow from the area A near the tungsten anode to the area B near the cathode, as shown in Fig. 12(j). This led to the formation of high-speed plasma flow in the arc. To maintain flow continuity, a new gas medium from the area C was drawn into the arc column, where it was ionized and continued to flow toward the area B, creating a plasma flow force. This force impacted the molten pool, causing splashing of the melt. Once the arc was extinguished, the sputtered particles solidified, resulting in a morphology similar to that shown in Figs. 12, 10, and 9. Similar phenomena were found in previous studies [39].

### 4.2 Metallic vapor arc

The electron avalanche caused by collision ionization and the streamer was caused by field distortion due to the space charge, which maintained the arc combustion. Furthermore, when the positive and negative ions were in directional motion, they collided with the cathode surface, and the atoms or molecules on the cathode surface accepted energy during the collision. Consequently, some of the energy exceeded the work function of the material, and the cathode material was ionized [40]. The work function, which involves overcoming the surface barrier when an electron escapes from a solid surface, is commonly measured in electron volts (eV) [22]. The electron work function can be defined as Eq. (2):

(2) ϕ=Evacuum−EFermi

where * ϕ is the work function (eV), E*vacuum is the vacuum energy level (eV), and *E*Fermi is the Fermi energy level (eV). When the energy reached the work function of the Ta2AlC and Ag materials, they decomposed into negative cations. The work function of silver typically ranges from 4.26 to 4.47 eV [41,42], which is much smaller than the ionization energy of oxygen and nitrogen. The calculated work function of the Ta2AlC material was 6.7192 eV. Hence, the cathode material could decompose into Ag, Ta, and Al ions after accepting the energy. A higher value of the work function meant that the electrical contact material was less prone to arc decomposition and provided better resistance to arc erosion. The calculated work function of the Ta2AlC material was higher than those of electrical contact materials described in the literature, such as ZrO2 (4.76 eV) [43,44], SnO2 (3.54 eV) [45], and Ti3SiC2 (6.5203 eV). The work function of Ti3SiC2 was lower than that of Ta2AlC. Therefore, the electrons more easily escaped from the cathode surface, resulting in a greater breakdown current for Ag–30 vol% Ti3SiC2 (51.1 A) [46] at a voltage of 10 kV than for Ag–30 vol% Ta2AlC (42.0 A). Remarkably, the Ag–Ta2AlC material possessed a better resistance to arc erosion.

The electrons on the surface of the Ta2AlC material escaped and transformed into ions, which proceeded to sustain the combustion of the arc. The escaped electrons and these positive ions formed metal vapor. In 1971, Boddy and Utsumi [47] discovered that the arc combustion process can be divided into two phases, namely, the metallic and gaseous phases, under atmospheric pressure. Subsequently, this phenomenon was spectroscopically observed in the break arcs of resistive circuits [48]. The gaseous phase was also found to be absent in vacuum arcs [48,49]. Kiyoshi and Sunao experimentally studied the arc transition process in Ag, AgCu, and AgSnO2 materials [50,51] and concluded that the gaseous phase did not occur at a voltage of 24 V or less [52]. In this work, the arc voltage was 10 kV, which was significantly greater than 24 V. In addition, arc erosion testing was performed under open-air conditions. Therefore, the arc combustion process of Ag–Ta2AlC occurred via the combination of metal vapor and gas ionization. The transition boundary from the metallic to the gaseous phase was found to be closely related to the arc voltage, air pressure, gas type, and material [53−55]. Considering the complexity of the factors influencing the arc transition from the metallic to the gaseous phase, it was necessary to further explore the arc transition of Ag–Ta2AlC under air conditions.

### 4.3 Effect of Ta<sub>2</sub>AlC content

The arc erosion mechanism of Ag–Ta2AlC composites with varying contents was analyzed. Specifically, the work function of Ag ranges from 4.26 to 4.47 eV [42], while the work function of Ta2AlC was calculated to be 6.7192 eV, indicating that Ag has a lower work function than Ta2AlC. The arc tends to preferentially strike the phase with a lower work function. Zhang *et al*. [56] reported that the movement of the cathode spot was related to the Cu phase. The arc selectively occurred on the Cu phase. The size of the cathode spot depended on the size of the Cu phase. The movement distance of the cathode spot was the distance between the Cu phases. This phenomenon was also observed in experiments conducted in Refs. [57−61]. For composites with a 10% volume fraction of Ta2AlC, the silver matrix gathered together, and the arc formed a large area of ​​erosion. As the Ta2AlC content increased, the Ag matrix was divided into smaller areas. A higher Ta2AlC content corresponded to a smaller silver area. Upon reaching a critical value, the arc dispersed on the surface of the Ag–Ta2AlC material. This dispersion may be influenced by factors such as the size of the arc root or the cathode spot, which our current equipment is unable to measure. Further investigations will be carried out once a more accurate measurement method is developed.

## 5 Conclusions

Ag–Ta2AlC composites containing Ta2AlC volume fractions of 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% were prepared by hot-pressing sintering. W rods and Ag–Ta2AlC composites were used as the anodes and cathodes, respectively; a load voltage of 10 kV was applied to the two electrodes; then, the Ag–Ta2AlC composites were eroded by arc erosion.

1) The relative density of the Ag–10 vol% Ta2AlC composite material was 98.2%. As the Ta2AlC content increased, the relative density of the Ag–Ta2AlC composite material gradually decreased. The hardness initially increased and then decreased. The Ag–30 vol% Ta2AlC composite exhibited the highest hardness at 97.4 HV.

2) The work function of the Ta2AlC material was 6.7192 eV, as calculated by the first-principles calculations, and this value was smaller than that of ZrO2, SnO2, or common MAX phase contact materials.

3) The Ag–30 vol% Ta2AlC composite material exhibited the lowest arc energy (3.395 kJ) and the shortest arcing time (33.26 ms). Additionally, the arc was dispersed on the surface of the Ag–30 vol% Ta2AlC composite material, preventing concentrated arc erosion and demonstrating higher arc erosion resistance among the four components.

4) The surface atoms of the Ag–Ta2AlC composite absorbed energy that exceeded the work function of Ag and Ta2AlC, resulting in their ionization. Ionized oxygen atoms combined with Ta and Al ions to form Ag2O, AgO, Ta2O5, and Al2O3.
