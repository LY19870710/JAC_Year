## 1 Introduction

Recently, due to their broad technological applications, including high-density dynamic random access memories (DRAMs), embedded capacitors and gate layers in thin film transistors (TFTs) [1,2], and dielectric materials with a high dielectric constant (*k*), i.e., high *k* dielectrics, have attracted a lot of research interests [1−5]. Specifically, following the miniaturization trend in IC chips, a high *k* dielectric with its *k* value much larger than 3.9 is being sought to replace the conventional SiO2 gate layer, thereby achieving a higher specific capacitance without incurring an intolerable tunneling leakage [3,4]. An important parameter named “equivalent oxide thickness” (EOT) has been used to rate these high *k* dielectrics [5]. It is the equivalent thickness of a SiO2 layer with the same specific capacitance as that of the high *k* dielectric. The EOT is calculated by EOT=* (kSiO2/khigh-k)⋅thigh-k, where  kSiO2is the dielectric constant of SiO2 (= 3.9);  khigh-k and  thigh-k are the dielectric constant and physical thickness of the high k* dielectric, respectively. According to above equation, a high dielectric constant *k*, i.e., a strong “polarizability”, and a low physical thickness *t*, i.e., a good “scalability”, are two key factors to achieve a low EOT for a gate dielectric. Meanwhile, to meet the requirements for the operation of the gate and consumption of low power [6], there are limits on the maximum leakage current density of a gate dielectric, which put constraint on its lowest achievable physical thickness *t*. This “insulation robustness” is the 3rd factor outside of the two for EOT that is critical for the CMOS gate performance. For the native SiO2, it shows a stable low *k* value and an excellent insulation, leading to an EOT/physical thickness *t* of ~1.2 nm [7]. Further thinning down of the SiO2 gate layer will induce a direct tunneling current, causing an electric breakdown and gate damage (Fig. 1(a)).

Binary high *k* oxides, such as HfO2, ZrO2, and Ta2O5, have been extensively investigated and broadly used as gate dielectrics [6,8−12]. Similar to SiO2, they are good insulators [6] and shows a good thickness scalability in their *k* values (10–25) [8−11]. However, due to a reduced breakdown strength *E*bd [13], their lowest achievable physical thicknesses are about 3–10 nm [8−11], leading to EOT values of a few nanometers (Fig. 1(a)). On the other hand, complex oxides of a polar nature (polar oxides), including (Hf,Zr)O2 [14] and perovskite oxides (BaTiO3, SrTiO3, etc.) [15,16], display much larger dielectric constants in their films (*k* is from ~40 to a few hundred) with a nano-scale thickness (14,17], thermal instability [11], and a poor thickness scalability [18−20]. The nonlinear dielectric response and thermal instability are characteristics of an electric polar structure, such as a ferroelectric or antiferroelectric. Moreover, an intrinsic crystalline size dependence of the long-range polar order [21], surface/interface dead layer effects [22], and depolarization effects [23,24], all contribute to a sharp declination of the dielectric constant in a high *k* polar oxide with a decreasing thickness (Fig. 1(b)) [21,23,24]. Formation of localized defects during the build-up of the polar structure in a small scale reduces the dielectric constant and increases the leakage current [11,25], which further exacerbates this downscaling issue [11]. Their theoretically predicted and experimentally verified low *E*bd values only make the matter worse [13].

To solve this trilemma between a high *k* value, a good thickness scalability of *k*, and insulation robustness, we propose to use a ferroelectric oxide in its size-driven superparaelectric (SPE) state as the gate dielectric. As shown in Fig. 1(c), with a microstructure consisting of well-dispersed nanometer polar clusters (NPCs) and an amorphous matrix, this state is endowed with a large dielectric constant and a stable, pseudo-linear dielectric response [26−28]. The average diameter of the NPCs, by the definition of the SPE state, is smaller than the polarization correlation length of the ferroelectric material. This diameter was found to be as small as a few nanometers in our recent experimental study [27]. This extremely small feature size warrants a good thickness scalability of the *k* value. Furthermore, the lack of mesoscale crystalline defects, including grain boundaries and interface dislocations [11,29], in this insulator complex consisting of dispersed nanocrystallites and an amorphous matrix, helps minimize carrier recombinations and leakage paths [29,30]. This will contribute to a robust insulation with a high breakdown strength.

In this work, high-*k* superparaelectric (Ba0.95,Sr0.05)(Zr0.2,Ti0.8)O3 (BSZT) ultrathin films (with thicknesses of 45, 24, 13, 7 and 4 nm) were successfully sputter-deposited on commercially available platinized Si substrates. Such a composition was chosen based on the experimental results of the bulk material (showing one of the highest *k* values at room temperature (RT)) [31], and thermodynamic calculations. Furthermore, the doping of Sr and Zr into BaTiO3 will increase its band gap and improve the insulating property. Moreover, the choice of the sputtering technique is based on its superior capability in tuning the nanograin structure of a resulting film. Compared with other commonly-used experimental methods for the fabrication of high-*k* gate layers, including epitaxial thermal growth [32], atomic layer deposition [33,34], liquid–metal chemistry [35,36], and evaporative driven assembly [37], sputtering is a physical vapor deposition process whose film’s microstructure can be readily dictated by the growth temperature (*T*) [27,38]. Using a buffer layer of conductive perovskite LaNiO3 [27], we were able to deposit these superparaelectric films with a well-controlled NPC size in a RT sputtering process. The average diameter of the NPCs was tuned to ~1.8 nm in all films, either in an as-grown state (in the 45, 24, 13, and 7 nm films) or in an annealed state (in the 4 nm film, via rapid thermal annealing (RTA)). These films showed about the same *k* value (*~*37±3@1 kHz), as well as a pseudo-linear dielectric response under a varying electric field. The thinnest film, 4 nm-RTA, showed a low EOT value of ~0.46 nm. Whereas in the as-grown 4 nm film, the *k* value dropped to ~24 by ~33% due to a smaller NPC size (~1.3 nm) from an insufficient deposition time under a slow growth kinetics in a RT sputtering process. Consequently, its EOT was increased to ~0.65 nm. These observations suggest that the dominant factor for a scalable *k* in a SPE dielectric is its NPC size, not the film thickness usually being investigated. It is such a small feature size that has led to a good thickness scalability of *k* in an SPE ultrathin film, as opposed to a non-scalable *k* in its ferroelectric counterpart [21−24]. Furthermore, through studies of the temperature dependence of *k* (*k*–*T* curves), we estimated the critical NPC size for the superparaelectric-to-paraelectric (SPE-PE) transition in the BSZT film, i.e., its theoretical scalability limit as a gate layer. This limit is between 1.3 and 1.8 nm, which is consistent with the thermodynamic prediction for the BSZT material. Lastly, excellent electrical fatigue resistances, i.e., charge–discharge stabilities (through an electric field cycling test up to 2×109 alternating current (AC) field cycles at 1 V), was demonstrated by these SPE BSZT films.

### 2.1 Material design and structural characterizations

Thermodynamic calculations were carried out to predict the size limits of a stable SPE state in (Ba0.95,Sr0.05)(Zr0.2,Ti0.8)O3, using a size-modified Landau–Ginsberg–Devonshire thermodynamic potential and a “spherical nanoparticle” model. An upper limit of the nanoparticle’s radius *R* for the disruption of a long-range polar order, i.e., the ferroelectric correlation length *R*c, can be estimated by equaling the Gibbs free energy *F*(*T*, *R*c) of the nanoparticle to the thermal fluctuation energy *k*B*T* [39], where *k*B is the Boltzmann constant and *T* is the temperature, K. On the other hand, a complete disappearance of the polar order occurs when *F*(*T*, *R*) approaches zero in a nanoparticle with a critical radius *R* = *R*cr. *R*c and *R*cr are the upper and lower limits for the existence of a SPE state, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2, ferroelectric (FE)/SPE/paraelectric (PE) phase diagrams for Ba(Zr0.2,Ti0.8)O3 and (Ba0.95,Sr0.05)(Zr0.2,Ti0.8)O3 were constructed in the coordinates of *T* and particle radius (*R*). As the radius of a spherical nanoparticle decreases, its FE/SPE and SPE/PE transition temperatures decrease due to the polarization correlation effect. Similarly, the SPE region is expanded as the temperature increases. Compared with those in Ba(Zr0.2,Ti0.8)O3 (BZT), the correlation length *R*c for the FE-SPE transition in BSZT increases to 5.3 nm (vs. 4.1 nm), and the critical radius *R*cr for the SPE-PE transition in BSZT increases to 0.8 nm (vs. 0.5 nm), respectively (Fig. 2). This up-shifted expansion of the stable SPE region along the crystallite-size axis (0.8 nm ≤ *R* ≤ 5.3 nm) by the addition of Sr can be attributed to the much-enhanced dielectric constant *k*, which stabilizes polar clusters of a larger volume [27]. Such an expansion enables an experimentally achievable feature size (2*R*) 37]. Furthermore, the lower limit of the NPC size (2*R* ≈ 1.6 nm) is still far below that of the thickness of a hetero-grown oxide film (~3–5 nm) [40,41], and hence satisfying the dispersion/non-interacting requirement for the NPCs in the SPE state [39]. Therefore, use of the (Ba0.95,Sr0.05)(Zr0.2,Ti0.8)O3 composition, not only will promote the formation of a stable SPE state in a nanocrystalline film, but can also endow such a film a high polarizability with a good thickness-scalability. The key to realize such a material design, is to deposit nanocrystalline (Ba0.95,Sr0.05)(Zr0.2,Ti0.8)O3 films with a slow growth kinetics to achieve a good control of the size and distribution of the NPCs.

Based on this design, BSZT ultrathin films with thicknesses of 45, 24, 13, 7, and 4 nm were sputter-deposited at RT on LaNiO3 buffered Pt(111)/Ti/SiO2/(100)Si substrates in a high-vacuum magnetron sputtering system. A high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of the 7 nm thick BSZT film is shown in Fig. 3(a), which reveals the existence of well-dispersed, {110}-oriented NPCs (2*R* ≈ 1.8 nm) in an amorphous matrix. This macroscopically amorphous but locally nano-crystalline nature of these BSZT films was also revealed via X-ray diffraction (XRD) 2*θ*-scan and grazing incidence XRD (GIXRD) (Figs. S1 and S2 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). NPCs with a close average diameter were revealed in the 7 nm and thicker films via a detailed TEM analysis (Figs. S3–S5 in the ESM). A similar nanostructure is observed in the 4 nm thick film (Fig. 3(b) and Fig. S6 in the ESM) deposited under the same conditions, but with a significantly decreased average NPC diameter (2*R* ≈ 1.3 nm). This can be attributed to an insufficient deposition time (~30 s for the 4 nm film) under a slow growth kinetics at RT. Adjustment of the growth time/sputtering power did not result in a noticeable growth of the NPCs in the 4 nm thick film (Fig. S7 in the ESM). However, a substantial growth of the NPCs (2*R* from ~1.3 to ~1.8 nm) was observed in the 4 nm film which underwent a rapid thermal annealing (4 nm-RTA, Fig. S8 in the ESM).

Through surface analyses, we revealed a smooth and dense morphology of the BSZT films. An scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of the as-grown 4 nm BSZT film (Fig. 3(c)) shows a dense and homogeneous surface morphology, without any visible void or crack. Moreover, the inset of Fig. 3(c), which is a representative atomic force microscopy (AFM) image of this ultrathin film, shows a root mean square (RMS) roughness of ~0.66 nm. These surface features are typical for sputtered oxide films [42] and have an important impact on device performance. For example, a dense dielectric layer with a smooth surface is essential to achieve a reliable TFT performance, while a rough and porous one would lead to an inferior TFT performance [43,44]. The surface AFM images of the BSZT films with different thicknesses shown in Fig. S9 in the ESM reveal a similar RMS roughness of ~0.5–0.6 nm. Moreover, energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental surface scans (Figs. 3(d)–3(h)) reveal a homogeneous distribution of the constituent elements, Ba, Sr, Zr, Ti, and O. To further validate the chemical bonding states in the BSZT films, high-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra were collected and comparatively analyzed (Fig. S10 in the ESM). The metallic elements Ba, Sr, Zr, and Ti were found to exist in their native oxidation states of Ba2+, Sr2+, Zr4+, and Ti4+, respectively, in these BSZT films. Moreover, there were only lattice and surface-absorbed oxygen being detected via XPS (Fig. S10(b) in the ESM), indicating a suppression of the formation of oxygen vacancies in these sputtered BSZT films. The homogeneous distribution and stable valence states of the constituent elements are beneficial for achieving an excellent dielectric performance in these films.

### 2.2 Dielectric performance and scalability limit

Figure 4(a) displays the dielectric constant–direct current (DC) bias voltage (*k*–*V*) curves and polarization–electric field (*P*–*E*) hysteresis loops (inset, @10 kHz) of the BSZT films. For the as-grown films, their *k* values (~37) demonstrate an excellent thickness scalability down to ~7 nm. Meanwhile, the 4 nm-RTA film displays a *k* value close to those of the thicker films, as is expected from its similar NPC size to the latter. Moreover, an excellent linearity/electric field stability of *k* is demonstrated by these BSZT films, which can be attributed to their small NPC sizes (*R* c) [27,39]. From the *k*–*V* curves, the variations in *k* were within ±5% for the as-grown films and ~±10% for the 4 nm-RTA film. The variations in *k* were much reduced when it was measured using an AC voltage. This is shown by the *P*–*E* loops in the inset of Fig. 4(a) (*k* being the slope of a *P*–*E* loop). Furthermore, there is no polarization saturation displayed by any of the BSZT films (Fig. S11 in the ESM), nor did they show any switchable polarization in their switching current–electric field (*I*sw–*E*) curves (Fig. S12 in the ESM). These observations indicate the absence of a long-range polar order in these films, which is consistent with their XRD (Figs. S1 and S2 in the ESM) and TEM results (Figs. 3(a) and 3(b) and Figs. S3–S8 in the ESM).

Figure 4(b) illustrates thickness dependences of *k* and the NPC size 2*R*. The *k*–*t* and 2*R*–*t* curves showed the same trend with a decreasing film thickness *t*, i.e., both *k* and 2*R* displayed a good thickness scalability (*k* ≈ 37, 2*R* ≈ 1.8 nm) down to ~7 nm. This is characteristic of a superparaelectric state whose dielectric property is determined by the average diameter of its highly polarizable NPCs [39]. For the 4 nm films, the as-grown one showed a decreased *k* at ~24, consistent with its reduced NPC size of ~1.3 nm. Nevertheless, an RTA process promoted the growth of the NPCs in the 4 nm film (to ~1.8 nm), and recovered its dielectric constant back to ~34. These observations revealed the dependence of *k* on the feature size of a superparaelectric film, i.e., its average NPC diameter 2*R*.

The electric breakdown strengths *E*bd reached ~7.0, ~7.0, ~7.2, ~8.5, and ~10.5 MV·cm−1, for the as-grown 45, 24, 13, 7, and 4 nm thick BSZT films, respectively, as analyzed via a two-parameter Weibull distribution model (based on the *P*–*E* test at 10 kHz, Fig. S13 in the ESM) [45]. These ultrahigh breakdown strengths can be attributed to the small size of the NPCs [27,46], which manifested themselves as well-dispersed nanocrystallites in an ultrathin amorphous film (with a thickness approaching the lower limit for the commonly used deposition methods) [40,47], and a low deposition temperature which suppressed the proliferation of electrical defects [27]. Furthermore, these films showed an increasing *E*bd with a decreasing film thickness, which is consistent with Refs. [38,48]. The 4 nm-RTA film, on the other hand, showed a reduced *E*bd of ~8.0 MV·cm−1 in comparison with the as-grown 7 and 4 nm films, which can be attributed to its thermally-induced electrical defects from the RTA process. The dielectric properties (*k* and loss tangent tan*δ*) as functions of the measuring frequency are illustrated in Fig. S14 in the ESM, which supported the scalability results shown in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b), and confirmed a more defective film in 4 nm-RTA than the as-grown ones.

As shown in Fig. 4(c), thermal evolutions of the short-range polar structures in these BSZT films are revealed via temperature-dependent dielectric constant measurements (*k*–*T*), i.e., how do the nanometer polar clusters (NPCs) eventually lose their stability with an increasing temperature, turning the whole film into a paraelectric state [27]. The temperature points corresponding to the dielectric maxima of the films (*T*m) can be considered as the “Curie temperatures” for the transition from a superparaelectric to a paraelectric state. Such a transition occurs when the critical radius *R*cr, which increases with temperature increasing, reaches the average diameter *R*avg of the NPCs (*R*cr ≈ *R*avg), and hence these short-range polar structures begin to lose their stability [27]. From the *k*–*T* curves in Fig. 4(c), three distinct features were observed with a decreasing film thickness. Firstly, *T*m shifts to a lower temperature. Secondly, the dielectric peak value *k*m becomes lower. Lastly, the dielectric peak becomes broader. These thickness-dependent characteristics were also found in other BaTiO3-based materials [49]. Furthermore, the high-temperature sides of the *k*–*T* curves in the vicinity of *T*m can be fitted to the Curie–Weiss law (Fig. 4(d)) [27], validating a transition from the super-paraelectric state to the paraelectric one. It is noted that the fitted Curie–Weiss temperature *T*0 shifted from *T*m to a lower temperature due to the small NPC size and film thickness [49]. *T*0 values for the 45, 13, and 7 nm thick films are 410 ℃ (close to *T*m), −51 ℃, and −262 ℃ (close to 0 K, i.e., −273 ℃), respectively. However, for the as-grown 4 nm film, the fitted *T*0 was −789 ℃, which lost its physical meaning. Just like the *k*–*T* relationships of CaTiO3 and (Ln1/2Na1/2)TiO3 studied by Cross’s group [50], this failed fitting to a classic model indicates that the 4 nm film may have a different crystalline structure/state from that defined by the model. Meanwhile, after the RTA process, the 4 nm-RTA film showed an up-shifted *k–T* curve with a higher *T*m. The fitting of this *k–T* curve yielded a *T*0 of ~−112 ℃, which is between those of the 7 and 13 nm as-grown films. These observations revealed the existence of a NPC-size driven polar state, i.e., the superparaelectric state, at a NPC size (2*R*) of ~1.8 nm, and its transition to the non-polar paraelectric state [27]. The critical NPC size for the SPE BSZT film to lose its stability, i.e., two times the critical radius (2*R*cr), was found between 1.3 and 1.8 nm. This is fairly consistent with the predicted result from the thermodynamic calculation (2*R*cr ≈ 1.6 nm, Fig. 2(b)). Such a scalability limit is a significant improvement against those reported for typical high *k* polar oxides, such as SrTiO3 and Ba(Sr,Ti)O3 [2,20,51].

### 2.3 Leakage current and cycling stability

Figure 5(a) shows the representative electric field dependent leakage current densities (*J–E* curves) of the BSZT films up to a common gate electric field of 2.5 MV·cm−1. First of all, the leakage currents of all the as-grown films are far below the low-power limit (1.5×10−2 A·cm−2) [20]. Specifically, for the 7 nm and thicker films, their leakage currents are relatively low (*J* −4 A·cm−2) and thickness-scalable, which can be attributed to the low sputtering temperature for their integration on Si. The RTA process, on the other hand, increased the leakage current as commonly observed in Ref. [52]. Nevertheless, the 4 nm-RTA film still shows a leakage current density (~1.5 or 5.6×10−2 A·cm−2@2.5 MV·cm−1) far below the MOSFET gate limit (10 A·cm−2) [20]. Furthermore, the leakage current densities of our sputtered BSZT films, when compared under the same electric field (~10−6 A·cm−2@1 MV·cm−1), are in par with those of the recently reported HfO2 gate layers deposited via low-temperature atomic layer deposition (ALD) [34].

(a) Leakage current density–electric field (J–E*) curves and (b) cycling stability test results for the 4 nm-RTA film, represented by its areal charge density and specific capacitance as functions of a bipolar cycling ac field of 2.5 MV·cm−1.

Moreover, a high electrical cycling stability/fatigue resistance of the SPE BSZT films is demonstrated. As shown in Fig. 5(b), there is no observable trend of declination in its areal charge density and specific capacitance after 2×109 times of bipolar charging and discharging, for the 4 nm-RTA film under a cycling AC field with an amplitude of 2.5 MV·cm−1. The room-temperature EOT value for the 4 nm-RTA film, based on its dielectric performance, is 0.46 nm. This EOT is robust against the above electrical cycling test. A similar cycling performance was achieved in the 4 nm as-grown film (EOT ≈ 0.65 nm) under an AC field of 3 MV·cm−1 (Fig. S15 in the ESM). It is noted that a scalable *k* value of the gate layer corresponds to a specific capacitance (*C*s) inversely proportional to its thickness. The 4 nm-RTA film showed a large *C*s value of ~6.9 μF·cm−2, leading to a high areal charge density of ~6.9 μC·cm−2 at 1 V.

## 3 Experimental methods

Raw materials: Single crystalline substrates of SiO2/(100)Si, as well as the LaNiO3 ceramic target (*ϕ* = 50 mm, *t* = 5 mm, 99.9%) were provided by Anhui Institute of Optics and Fine Mechanics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China. Pt (*ϕ* = 50 mm, *t* = 3 mm, 99.99%) and Ti (*ϕ* = 50 mm, *t* = 5 mm, 99.99%) metal targets were provided by Beijing Goodwill Metal Technology Co., Ltd., China. The BSZT ceramic target was prepared in-house with the same shape, size and purity as the LaNiO3 target via a solid-state reaction method [31].

Film growth: BSZT ultrathin films with thicknesses of 45, 24, 13, 7, and 4 nm were sputter-deposited on LaNiO3-buffered Pt(111)/Ti/SiO2/(100)Si substrates in a high-vacuum magnetron sputtering system. Before the film deposition, a base pressure of 2.0×10−4 Pa was achieved. The conductive LaNiO3 buffer layer (~100 nm) [27], and the BSZT films were sequentially deposited at RT on the platinized (100)Si substrate (no heat was supplied to the substrate). A sputtering atmosphere with an Ar/O2 flow ratio of 4:1 and a fixed pressure at 1.4 Pa was used. The details of the sputtering deposition process were provided in Ref. [27]. To overcome the effect of insufficient growth time on the size of NPCs, the 4 nm thick films were annealed in an RTA furnace (RTP-300, Beijing East Star Applied Physics Research Institute, China) at 700 ℃ in an ambient atmosphere for 60 s [53]. To measure their electrical properties, circular gold top electrodes with a diameter of 200 μm and a thickness of ~100 nm were sputtered on the surface of the BSZT films via a shadow mask at RT.

Film characterizations: The crystalline information of the BSZT films was analyzed by using XRD 2*θ*-scans with an XRD diffractometer (Dmax-2500PC, Rigaku, Japan) equipped with a Ni-filtered Cu Kα radiation source, and small angle GIXRD with a XRD diffractometer (Smartlab9KW, Rigaku, Japan). A TEM microscope (Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN, FEI, USA) was used to investigate the nanostructures of the films. It is noted that carbon was used as the top layer for TEM sample preparation to provide protection as well as a high contrast for film thickness measurement. A Schottky field emission scanning electron microscope (JSM-7610F, FESEM, JEOL, Japan) and a scanning probe microscope (Dimension Icon 3100, Bruker, USA) were used to analyze the surface morphologies of the BSZT films. Chemical composition and valence states of the elements were analyzed via EDS equipped in the FESEM, and an XPS spectrometer (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA).

The RT polarization–electric field (*P–E*) hysteresis loops, switching current–electric field (*I*sw*–E*) loops and leakage current density*–*voltage (*J–V*) curves were measured using a ferroelectric tester (Multiferroic, Radiant Technology, USA). The voltage-dependent dielectric constants (*k–V*) were measured from the capacitance*–*voltage tests, i.e., *C–V* tests, using a precision LCR meter (TH2838H, Tonghui Electronics, China). The frequency-dependent dielectric properties of the films were measured by using a precision LCR meter (QuadTech 7600 plus, Chroma Systems Solutions, USA) from 1 kHz to 1 MHz. The dielectric constant *k* was determined by using the equation for a parallel plate capacitor, *C* = (*k*·*ε*0·*S*)/*t*, where *C* is the measured capacitance; *t* is the film thickness; *S* is the electrode area, and *ε*0 is the permittivity of free space. The temperature-dependent dielectric properties of the BSZT films were measured by connecting a temperature-controllable probe station (HFS600E-PB2, Linkam, UK) to the QuadTech LCR meter. The heating rate is 30 ℃·min−1 for the temperature-dependent dielectric measurements. When the testing temperature point was reached, a 3-min holding at that temperature was used before starting the dielectric measurement. Lastly, the electrical cycling tests were carried out by using the ferroelectric tester under increasing frequencies of 1 kHz (1 to 103 cycles), 10 kHz (1 to 104 cycles), 100 kHz (1 to 105 cycles), and 1 MHz (1 to 106, 1 to 107, 1 to 108, 1 to 109, and 1 to 2×109 cycles). The starting and ending *P–E* hysteresis loops of the cycling tests were all measured at 10 kHz.

Thermodynamic calculation: As the size of a ferroelectric nanoparticle decreases, it will be confined by surface tension, polarization correlation, and depolarization field. Thus, the ferroelectric–paraelectric phase transition temperature of a nanoparticle varies with its size. For a spherical nanoparticle (with radius * R), the modified phase transition temperature can be described by Eq. (1) [54]:

(1) TSP(R)≈TC[1−4μ(Q11+2Q12)αTTCR−k12δαTTCR2]

where  TSP is the modified transition temperature;  TC is the Curie temperature of the bulk material;  μ is the surface tension coefficient between the nanoparticle and its interface;  Q is the electrostrictive coefficient;  αT is the Landau coefficient of the second order;  R is the particle’s radius;  k1 and  δ are the characteristic parameters coming from the polarization correlation effect respectively.

Analogous to the superparamagnetic state, an energy barrier for polarization reorientation  ΔF(T,R) can be estimated to define characteristics of the superparaelectric state from the following condition [39]:  ΔF(T,R)≤kBT, where  kB is the Boltzmann constant and  T is the temperature in Kevin. A Landau–*Ginsberg*–*Devinshire thermodynamic potential was employed to study the size effects in Ba(Zr*x*Ti1−*x*)O3-based nanoparticles. For a Ba(Zr*x*Ti1−*x*)O3 bulk ceramic, its Gibbs free energy * F(T,R) can be expanded in a power series of the electric polarization, assuming an isothermal condition:  F(T,R)=F(T,R,P=P0), where  P=P0 is obtained via  ∂F(T,R,P)/∂P=0, and

(2) Fbulk(P)=a1(Px2+Py2+Pz2)+a11(Px4+Py4+Pz4)+a12(Px2Py2+Px2Pz2+Py2Pz2)+a111(Px6+Py6+Pz6)+a112[Px4(Py2+Pz2)+Pz4(Px2+Py2)+Py4(Px2+Pz2)]+a123Px2Py2Pz2−12s11(σ12+σ22+σ32)−s12(σ1σ2+σ1σ3+σ3σ2)−12s44(σ42+σ52+σ62)−Q11(σ1Px2+σ2Py2+σ3Pz2)−Q12[σ1(Py2+Pz2)+σ3(Px2+Py2)+σ2(Px2+Pz2)]−Q44(PyPzσ4+PxPzσ5+PxPyσ6)

where  Px,  Py, and  Py are components of the polarization vector  P;  P0 is the equilibrium polarization at temperature  T and particle size  R;  a1−a123 are the Landau coefficients;  sij are the elastic compliance coefficients; and  σi(i=1−6) are the applied stresses. The parameters were constructed by Peng et al.* [55] to account for all possible transitions with different BaZrO3 volume fractions.

The parameters for Ba(Zr0.2,Ti0.8)O3  (x=0.2) are as follows [54]:  a1=3.12(T−305)×105,  a11=−15.1×108,  a12=0.12×108,  a111=370×109,  a112=−5.28×109,  a123=−0.75×109,  Q11=0.092,  Q12=−0.024,  Q44=0.033,  s11=7.3×10−12,  s12=−2.3×10−12, and  s44=8.24×10−12. To take into consideration the effect of Sr doping, the Landau coefficients for SrTiO3 [56] are used to interpolate the parameters for BSZT. Due to the size effect, the second-order parameter needs to be modified:  a1=3.12(T−TSP(R)). For estimation of the correlation length  Rc for the SPE-FE transition,  ΔF(T,R)=F(T,Rc,Pc)−F(T,Rc,P=0)=F(T,Rc,Pc), while for estimation of the critical radius  Rcr for the PE-SPE transition,  Rcr was obtained via  F(T,Rcr,Pcr)→0.

## 4 Conclusions

In a summary, ultrathin films of macroscopically amorphous (Ba0.95,Sr0.05)(Zr0.2,Ti0.8)O3 with well-dispersed nanometer polar clusters (NPCs) were successfully prepared on LaNiO3 buffered Pt(111)/Ti/SiO2/(100)Si substrates. These films are in a superparaelectric state based on analyses of their nanostructures and dielectric properties. A high dielectric constant was well preserved when decreasing the film thickness from 45 nm down to 4 nm. This has led to a substantial reduction of the EOT thickness (from 4.7 to 0.46 nm). Due to their superparaelectric nature and a low processing temperature, these high *k* films showed a pseudo-linear dielectric response, a high electrical cycling stability/fatigue resistance, and a low leakage current. This work reveals the origin of the good thickness scalability of a high *k* superparaelectric, i.e., a small feature size (average diameter of its NPCs) in the order of a few nanometers. An estimated lower limit of the NPC diameter, i.e., two times the critical radius (2*R*cr), for a stable superparaelectric state in the (Ba0.95,Sr0.05)(Zr0.2,Ti0.8)O3 film, was about 1.6 nm and supported by the experimental results.
