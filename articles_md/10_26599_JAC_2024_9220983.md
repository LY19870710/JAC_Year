## 1 Introduction

MAX phase ceramics have received increasing attention in recent decades because of their unique nanolamellar structure [1,2]. The lattice of MAX phase consists of alternatively stacked M*n*+1X*n* transition metal carbide slabs and main group A atomic layers [2,3]. Owing to the heterodesmic bonding system, which involves a strong covalent nature between M–X atoms and weak interactions between M–A species, the MAX phase integrates advantages from both alloy and ceramic materials [4]. In particular, the strength of the MAX phase is controlled by both M–X and M–A bonding, while the weak M–A interaction is also responsible for the ductility [5,6]. Moreover, the anisotropic properties of the MAX phase are also related to the nanolamellar structure and heterodesmic bonding characteristics [7]. Therefore, the chemical composition of M and A has been widely investigated to establish guidance for the design and property regulation of the MAX phase [8−11].

By increasing the *n* in M*n*+1X*n* slabs to 2 or 3, two different Wyckoff sites of transition metal atom M, i.e., adjacent to the A layer and away from the A layer, are distinguished. Depending on the intrinsic properties of different transition metals, such as the atomic radius, electronegativity, and electron concentration, the metal atoms are found to tend to occupy different Wyckoff sites. By taking the M3AC2 phase as an example, on the one hand, different elements in M layer with distinctly different properties separately reside at the 4*f* (adjacent to the A layer) and 2*a* (away from the A layer) sites, thus forming so-called “out-of-plane ordering” in the M3C2 slabs [12−14]. Correspondingly, the varied M–X and M–A bondings further give rise to the unique mechanical and thermal properties of the MAXs [15]. For instance, when the Ti‒Al bond was substituted for the Cr‒Al bond, the out-of-plane-ordered Cr2TiAlC2 exhibited better mechanical properties than Ti3AlC2. Moreover, an increase in the mechanical properties was also achieved by adding an additional Ti‒C slab in Cr2TiAlC2 compared with that in Cr2AlC [16]. Moreover, Li *et al*. [17] reported the correlation between the strength and reduced mass of M(4*f*)–C/Al bonding and the phonon eigenvectors of the Raman response in a series of out-of-plane ordered MAX (o-MAX) through the First-principles calculations.

On the other hand, M atoms with similar properties can form solid solutions at the same Wyckoff sites, which results in an increasing configurational entropy of the lattice [18]. Eventually, a medium- or high-entropy structure might be achieved, for which the solid solution strengthening effect and phonon scattering effect are expected [19,20]. As reported recently [21], the introduction of V in Cr2TiAlC2 o-MAX can lead to a solid solution of Ti and V at both 4*f* and 2*a* sites, while Cr still resides only at the 4*f* site. The partially broken out-of-plane ordering results in a medium-entropy structure as well as a solid solution-strengthening effect. In contrast, in the (Ti0.2V0.2Cr0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)2AlC high-entropy MAX ceramics, Liu *et al*. [22] reported a sharp decrease in electrical and thermal conductivity, which was one to two orders of magnitude lower than that reported for nonhigh-entropy MAX ceramics. However, current studies on M3AC2 phase MAX with multiple transition metal constituents are still at an early stage. Only a few cases of o-MAX have been synthesized thus far [23−26], whereas the discovery of high-entropy M3AC2 MAX occurred as late as 2023 [27,28]. Given the rather sluggish research progress on multicomponent M3AC2 MAXs, a large research gap remains in the study of their physicochemical properties. Therefore, there is an urgent need for the design and construction of multicomponent M3AC2 MAX, as well as a systematic investigation of their properties corresponding to the chemical composition and ordering.

Very recently, we prepared a series of binary and ternary o-MAXs based on Cr2TiAlC2 and further achieved a high-entropy Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2 MAX, which all prove the fascinating composition-dependent structure and performance of the family. Herein, three quaternary MAXs (referring to the M site elements), Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 (*x* = 1.25, 1, and 0.75) MAXs with controllable transition metal compositions, are synthesized, and an unprecedented transition in atomic occupancy from out-of-plane order to solid solution is observed. Along with the composition-triggered transition in atomic occupancy, an increasing configurational entropy is also detected, which corresponds to the transition from a medium- to high-entropy structure. Furthermore, the mechanical properties and thermal properties of the three MAXs were evaluated. Through experimental observations and theoretical simulations, the influence of atomic occupation on mechanical and thermal properties was investigated.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

The Cr, Ti, Mo, V, and Al metal powders with a purity of 99.9% were purchased from Qinghe Benyu Metal Material Co., Ltd., China, whereas flake graphite with a purity of 99.9% was purchased from Alfa Aesar, USA. All powders were used as received. Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 (*x* = 1.25, 1, and 0.75) were prepared via the hot-pressing method. Briefly, powders of each element were mixed according to the formula, in which 20% excess Al and 5% deficient C were used. The powder mixture was ball milled for 2 h under Ar protection in a high-energy ball mill (SPEX 8000 M Mixer/Mill, USA) and then hot pressed in a graphite die under flowing Ar at 1400 °C for 3 h at a pressure of approximately 10 MPa. Owing to the low melting point of Al (660 °C), an excess of Al was introduced to compensate for the sublimation. The inner surface of the graphite die was precoated with the hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) release agent before loading. All sintered disks were machined via electrical discharge wire-cutting, and the resulting samples were approximately 25 mm long, 5 mm wide, and 2 mm thick. All the samples were polished with SiC paper sequentially to 2000 grit and ultrasonically cleaned in ethanol for 10 min before further use.

### 2.2 Material characterization

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on an XRD diffractometer (D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, USA). The 2*θ* range was set to 5°–120°, and the step of the scan was 0.02°. GSAS II software was adopted for Rietveld structural refinements [29]. A double-spherical aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope (ACTEM; Themis Z, FEI, USA) was used to collect the atomic resolution images of the new MAXs. For the ACTEM observations, the thin-foil sample from each MAX was extracted via dual-beam focused ion beam processing, which was performed on an instrument (Helios G4 CX, FEI, USA). The atomic resolution image of the lattice and elemental distribution was probed under high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) mode. The chemical composition of the samples was verified via an electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA), which was performed on an instrument (JXA-iHP200F, JEOL, Japan). The homogeneity of each sample was verified via a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Helios G4 CX, FEI, USA) with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) attachment. To observe the grains, the polished surface of each sample was etched with 48% hydrofluoric acid (HF) for8 min and then cleaned in deionized (DI) water, followed by air drying. The density of the sintered samples was determined via Archimedes’ method, whereas the Vickers hardness was tested on a Vickers diamond indenter by holding 9.8 N load for 5 s. For each sample, at least 10 measurements were carried out. The elastic modulus (*E*) and indentation hardness (*H*) of each MAX were determined through the nanoindentation technique, which is performed on a Hysitron nanoindenter at a load of 5 mN. For the indentation test, the loading, dwell, and unloading times were 5, 2, and 5 s, respectively, and at least 10 measurements were performed. The thermal diffusivity (*D*T) of the specimens was determined on a laser flash analyzer (LFA1000, Linseis, Germany) within a temperature window of 25–800 °C under argon protection. The samples were machined and polished into a shape of *φ*13.0 mm × 1.5 mm. To reduce the radiant thermal conductivity, all the samples were sprayed with a graphite coating. The electrical conductivity of the samples was determined with a four-probe tester (ST2258C, Suzhou Lattice Electronics Co., Ltd., China).

### 2.3 Theoretical calculation

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed on Material Studio 2018 software by using the Cambridge serial total energy package code (CASTEP) [30,31]. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) scheme was adopted to describe the exchange–correlation functional energy [32]. The interaction between electrons and core ions was described by ultrasoft pseudopotential-plane-ware. The total energy and internal forces during calculation were minimized according to the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) scheme [33]. For all calculations, a plane-wave cutoff of 700 eV and a *k*-point separation of 0.01 Å−1 were adopted. The maximum force tolerance was 0.03 eV·Å−1, and the convergence precision for each iteration was set to 1.0×10−7 eV·atom−1. The criteria for stress and displacement convergence were set as 0.05 GPa and 1.0×10–3 Å, respectively.

### 3.1 Phase purity and lattice parameters

The phase composition and purity of the hot-pressed (Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*)AlC2 (*x* = 1.25, 1.00, and 0.75) MAXs were first investigated by refining the XRD patterns via the Rietveld method (GSAS II software). As shown in Figs. 1(a) and 1(b) and Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) [27], according to the Rietveld refinement, the dominant phase of all three MAX samples can be assigned to the typical Cr2TiAlC2 (M3AlC2) phase. The equivalent atomic positions of the three MAXs from refinement are shown in Table S1 in the ESM. As summarized in Table 1, Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2 (*R*wp = 5.578%) has the parameters of *a* = *b* = 2.95381 Å and *c* = 18.18614 Å for its lattice. The corresponding parameters are *a* = *b* = 2.95646 Å and *c* = 18.21594 Å for Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2 (*R*wp = 3.994%), and *a* = *b* = 2.95912 Å, *c* = 18.23609 Å for Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2 (*R*wp = 5.546%). With decreasing Cr content but increasing V content, the *a*-axis and *c*-axis of the MAXs expand slightly. The Cr2TiAlC2 phase delivers a weight fraction (wt%) of approximately 91.7% in the synthesized Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2 sample, with 8.3% Cr5Al8 impurities. For Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2, the weight fraction of the Cr2TiAlC2 phase increases to 94.0%, accompanied by the Cr5Al8 impurity phase (6.0%). The Cr2TiAlC2 phase content in Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2 is 92.7%, with Cr5Al8 (6.7%) and TiC (0.6%) impurities.

### 3.2 Atomic distribution and composition

HAADF-STEM was used to determine the atomic arrangement of the quaternary MAXs. The HAADF-STEM image of Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2 is shown in Fig. 2(a). As observed along the 2¯0> zone axis of the grains, a typical lamellar structure of the M3AlC2 lattice is observed. Moreover, EDS mapping of the corresponding region depicts the chemically ordered distribution of Cr, Mo, Ti, and V atoms across multiple layers. To further display the ordered atomic distribution span of the lamellae, the EDS line profile was recorded along the c*-axis. As plotted in Fig. 2(b), out-of-plane atomic ordering can be verified by the periodic fluctuating signals of Cr, Mo, Ti, V, and Al along the scan path. In particular, Cr, Mo, and V are located in the same layer (4*f*, Wyckoff site), whereas Ti and V are located in the other layer (2*a*, Wyckoff site). In one period (marked in orange), Cr/Mo/V atoms are sandwiched between Ti/V and Al layers. Among them, the small amount of V detected at the 4*f* site is almost negligible. The Mo atoms clearly exhibit ordering at the 4*f* site in Fig. 2(b). The fluctuations clearly indicate that Mo is unevenly distributed at the 4*f* sites of different lattices. As defined in a binary Cr2TiAlC2 system [26], this kind of elemental distribution can be ascribed to typical out-of-plane chemical ordering. Figure 2(c) shows the SAED patterns obtained by an electron beam parallel to the 2¯0> direction and the electron diffraction characteristics of the hexagonal crystal system in Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2 can be observed.

HAADF-STEM image and corresponding EDS mappings of Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2 are shown in Fig. 3(a). HAADF-STEM analysis via double Cs-corrected transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figs. 2 and 3 and Fig. S2 in the ESM) revealed that the out-of-plane ordered atomic occupancy gradually disappears in the system along with the changes in M-site constituents. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 4, consistent with the representative out-of-plane ordering in Cr2TiAlC2 system with Cr and Ti in 4f* and 2*a* Wyckoff sites, respectively [26], the out-of-plane ordering in Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2 can be described as the preferential occupation of Cr, Mo, and V in 4*f* site, whereas Ti and V reside at the 2*a* site. Among them, the small amount of V detected at the 4*f* site is almost negligible. With reduced Cr and increased V content, as demonstrated in Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2, the distribution of Cr appears at the 2*a* site accompanied by the occupation of V at the 4*f* site. In this phase, the out-of-plane ordering between the 4*f* and 2*a* sites starts to be disturbed, and intermixing between Cr and V at the 4*f* and 2*a* sites can be observed. However, the respective occupation of Mo and Ti at the 4*f* and 2*a* sites is still maintained, which is in accordance with our previous observations of element intermixing at both Wyckoff sites in the ternary (TiVCr)AlC2 system [21]. With a further decrease in Cr and increase in V, the equimolar introduction of the Ti, V, Cr, and Mo atoms in Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2 results in the formation of a solid solution at both 4*f* and 2*a* sites [27].

To determine the locations of impurities and verify the chemical compositions of Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2 and Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2, electron probe X-ray micro-analysis (EPMA) and backscattered electron (BSE) imaging accompanied by EDS elemental mapping and point analysis were then adopted. The atomic percentage of each element in MAXs can be obtained by analyzing the experimental EPMA data (Table 2). The result for Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2 is consistent with the theoretical value. As shown in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b), light and dark areas can be clearly observed in BSE mode. Through EDS (Table 3), it can be determined that the brightly layered structures are Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2 (spectra 1 and 2) and Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2 (spectra 5 and 8). The dark intergranular region with higher Cr and Al contents corresponds to Cr5Al8 (spectra 3, 4, 6, and 7). The homogeneous distributions of Ti, V, Cr, Mo, Al, and C can be confirmed by EDS mapping of the intragranular region. Figures S3–S5 in the ESM display the HF-etched surfaces of the three sintered MAX samples. The three MAXs possess a similar lamellar geometry with a lateral size of approximately 4 μm and a thickness of 1–3 μm, which is approximately one-fifth of the grain size of Ti3AlC2 (approximately 20–40 µm in length and approximately 10–15 µm in width) [15]. The results show that a finer grain size can be obtained by introducing multiple elements into the MAX phase material [8]. Moreover, an enrichment of carbides and oxides can be found on the surface, which can be attributed to the impurities formed by sintering or etching. The remaining small amount of F comes from the HF solution. Similarly, the chemical homogeneity of the three MAXs in the depth direction was also determined. As shown in Figs. S6–S8 in the ESM, SEM images, and corresponding EDS line profiles were acquired from the top surface of the samples to depths greater than 100 µm, which revealed a homogeneous grain size and chemical composition along the depth direction. Moreover, with increasing Cr content and decreasing V content, the density of Cr‒Ti‒Mo‒V MAX ceramics increases (summarized in Table S2 in the ESM). In addition, the relative densities of (Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*)AlC2 (*x* = 1.25, 1.00, and 0.75) MAXs were calculated (Table S2 in the ESM), which were 95.7%, 99.7%, and 98.5%, respectively. A higher relative density is achieved in Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2 than in the other two MAXs, which is in accordance with its higher purity.

### 3.3 Entropy and viability map

Given the transition in atomic occupancy in the (Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*)AlC2 system, the influence of configurational entropy is then taken into consideration. In the fields of alloys and traditional ceramics, the high-entropy system was initially defined as a single phase with a configurational entropy higher than 1.61*R*, where five or more chemical species exist in the lattice with a molar content between 5% and 35% [34,35]. However, with the increasing complexity of the unit cell, the definition of high entropy has become more ambiguous. Thus, the initially established criteria for high, medium, and low entropies have also become inapplicable. Recently, a new entropy metric (EM) was developed to describe the entropy states of a complex structure with multiple sublattices. It is based on Richard’s rule [36] and uses the sublattice model as a method to calculate the entropy of atoms per mole to measure the configurational entropy of MAX ceramics. For the calculation, the configurational entropy of atoms per mole is determined according to Eq. (1):

(1) SSLconfig=−R(∑S∑iasXisln(Xis))∑sas

where for the *s* sublattice, *a**s* is the number of sites on it; * Xis is the fraction of the i*-th element species that is randomly distributed on the *s* sublattice or the fraction of the site. The unit of * SSLconfig is J·K−1·mol of formula units−1. Moreover, the entropy measure of MAX is calculated via Eq. (2):

(2) EM=SSL/molatomsconfigR×L

where  SSL/molatomsconfig refers to the configurational entropy according to the sublattice, in units of J·K−1·mol atoms−1), R* is the ideal gas constant, and *L* is the total number of sublattices.

In the lattice of M3AlC2 phase MAXs, the formula of a unit cell can be described as * M′2M″AlC2, where  M′ and  M″ represent the 4f* and 2*a* sites used to distinguish the sublattice. Depending on the kinds of metals that reside at the same Wyckoff site, the formula of multi-elemental MAXs can be rewritten. For example, the formulas of Cr1.8Ti0.8Mo0.4AlC2 and (CrTiV)AlC2 can be described as * (2M’)2 (2M′′)AlC2 and  (3M′)2 (2M″)AlC2, respectively. Accordingly, the formula of Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2 MAX can be characterized as  (3M′)2 (2M′′)AlC2, whereas the formulas of Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2 and Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2 can be described as  (3M′)2(3M″)AlC2 and  (4M′)2(4M″)AlC2, respectively. Here, all of the atoms at the  M′ and  M″ sites are assumed to be distributed uniformly, according to Eqs. Moreover, Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2 and Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2 are high-entropy MAXs. Therefore, along with the changes in Cr and V contents in the lattice, the quaternary Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 MAXs not only present a transition in atomic occupancy but also demonstrate evolution from a medium-entropy to high-entropy structure.

Moreover, although some pioneering works based on the First-principles calculations have successfully predicted the phase stability of MAXs with various binary metal combinations [37−39], many challenges remain. In particular, with more than two M-site elements, the distribution of multiple metals in the same symmetrical position is inevitable, which makes it rather difficult to model or predict the formation energy of a new phase. In contrast, as demonstrated in alloy systems, Hume–Rothery’s rules have shown great potential in predicting the formability of new metallic phases by comparing the atomic radius (*r*), electronegativity (*χ*), electron concentration, etc. In particular, for the MAX phase, the abovementioned indicators also show an advantage for the empirical estimation of chemical ordering [34,37].

Therefore, the size factor is calculated on the basis of the average atomic radius difference between the M and A sites, and the electron concentration of the MAX phases is first adopted to map the transition of chemical ordering in the title MAXs. According to Hume–Rothery’s rules, the size factor of (Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*)AlC2 MAXs is determined via Eq. (3):

(3) Sizefactor=∑i(|(ri−rAl|)×ni∑iri×ni

where *r**i* is the atomic radius, and *n**i* is the molar ratio of the *i*-th metal. The electron concentration is calculated according to Eq. (4):

(4) Electronconcentration=∑i(VECi×ni)+3×nAl+4×nc6

where VEC*i* is the number of valence electrons for the *i*-th metal. For M3AlC2 phase, the denominator is constantly 6. The atomic radius and valence electrons adopted for the calculations in this work are summarized in Table S3 in the ESM.

As shown in Fig. 6(b), the viability maps of the quaternary (Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*)AlC2 MAXs and some of their binary and ternary counterparts are plotted. The viability of Cr2TiAlC2, Mo2TiAlC2, Cr1.8Ti0.8Mo0.4AlC2, and (CrTiV)AlC2 MAXs is in accordance with DFT prediction [12] and has been successfully confirmed by experiments [10,15,21].

### 3.4 Mechanical properties

Given the distinct differences in the chemical ordering of the three quaternary (Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*)AlC2 MAXs, nanoindentation was performed on MAXs to determine their mechanical properties. As shown in Figs. 7(a)–7(c), the load−depth curves of Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2, Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2, and Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2 show the characteristics of typical brittle solids. Some steps indicate cracks in the sample during loading. *E* of the three MAXs was calculated following the Oliver‒Pharr method and is plotted in Fig. 7(d). Under an indentation load of 5 mN, Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2 presented the highest *E* value (~169.52±6.2 GPa). While *E* of Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2 and Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2 are 129.29±4.2 GPa and 154.21±5.6 GPa, respectively. Interestingly, *H* values of Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2, Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2, and Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2 are 11.2±0.9, 11.1±0.6, and 10.9±1.2 GPa, respectively (Fig. 7(d)), which are less different than those of *E*.

*H*/*E* and *H*3/*E*2 ratios of the three MAXs were calculated and are plotted in Fig. 7(e). The two ratios are closely related to the mechanical properties of materials and are used to evaluate the elastic recovery ability and plastic deformation resistance of materials [40,41], i.e*.*, a high value of these two parameters indicates better deformation resistance [42]. As shown in Fig. 7(e), with decreasing Cr content and increasing V content, *H*/*E* decreases from 0.087 to 0.066 and then increases to 0.071, whereas the *H*3/*E*2 decreases from 0.085 to 0.048 GPa and then increases to 0.054 GPa. Along with the transition in atomic occupancy from out-of-plane ordered to solid solution, the variation in the mechanical properties of the MAXs is nonlinear. In contrast, a ternary analog with a similar ternary transition metal composition and out-of-plane ordering has *H*/*E* (0.061) and *H*3/*E*2 (0.046) values similar to those of Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2, which demonstrates the impact of out-of-plane ordering on the mechanical properties.

Furthermore, the mechanical properties of the three quaternary MAXs were compared on the basis of their Vickers hardness. As shown in Fig. 7(f), Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2 has a Vickers hardness of 6.99±0.22 GPa, which is close to that of Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2 (6.81±0.46 GPa) and Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2 (6.94±0.18 GPa). Moreover, the Vickers hardness of the three MAXs presents a similar trend to that of the indentation hardness, which demonstrates the consistency of hardness at both the micro- and macro-scale. Compared with that of Cr2TiAlC2 (4.98±0.30 GPa) [15], the Vickers hardness of the three quaternary MAXs is approximately 40% greater, which might be associated with the strengthening effect caused by the solid solution lattice structure.

### 3.5 Thermal properties

Figure 8(a) depicts *D*T of the quaternary Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 MAXs and Cr2TiAlC2 in the temperature window of 25–800 °C. *D*Ts of the four MAXs strongly depend on temperature. In particular, with increasing environmental temperature, *D*T of the quaternary Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 MAXs decreases within the range of 25–100 °C and then increases in the range of 100–800 °C. *D*T of Cr2TiAlC2 decreases from 25 to 300 °C and increases with increasing temperature until 800 °C, where the ramping rate is lower than that of Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 MAXs. The decrease in *D*T with increasing temperature in low-temperature windows can be attributed to the increase in phonon‒phonon scattering [43]. *D*T increases further when the temperature increases because of photon-conductive thermal radiation. Moreover, *D*T of the quaternary Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 MAXs remains lower than that of Cr2TiAlC2 in the temperature window of 25–800 °C. At room temperature, *D*T of the high-entropy Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2 is the lowest (3.03 mm2·s−1), whereas *D*T values of Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2, Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2, and Cr2TiAlC2 are 3.20, 3.25, and 4.35 mm2·s−1, respectively.

*
Fig. 8(a) D*T and (b) thermal conductivity of three MAXs and Cr2TiAlC2 over a wide temperature range from 25 to 800 °C. (c) Electrical conductivity of four MAXs at 25 °C. (d) Thermal conductivity is affected by phonons and electrons.

Figure 8(b) shows the corresponding thermal conductivities (*κ*) of the quaternary Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 MAXs and Cr2TiAlC2. *κ* of lattice conduction can be described as Eq. (5):

(5) κ=13Cp×v×l

where *C**p* is the heat capacity calculated from the molar percentage of oxides according to the Neumann‒Kopp rule [44], *v* is the sound velocity, and *l* is the average free path of phonons. As shown in Fig. 8(b), Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2 and Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2 present low *κ* values of 13.49 and 13.85 W·m−1·K−1 at 25 °C, respectively. Correspondingly, the *κ* values of Cr2TiAlC2 and Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2 are 14.90 and 20.53 W·m−1·K−1, respectively. As summarized recently, M2AlC MAXs can have a *κ* of 20–60 W·m−1·K−1 at room temperature, whereas a low *κ* of 16.6 W·m−1·K−1 was reported for the solid solution (Ti,Nb)2AlC MAX. When the type of M-site element increases, the thermal conductivities of (TiVNb)2AlC and (Ti,V,Nb,Ta)2AlC are even lower, at 12.04 and 14.8 W·m−1·K−1, respectively [45,46]. For M3AlC2 phase, *κ* can reach 40 W·m−1·K−1 for Ti3AlC2 and Ti3SiC2 but dramatically decreases to 8 W·m−1·K−1 for Mo2TiAlC2. Moreover, the thermal conductivity contributed by electrons (*κ*electron) can be evaluated according to the Wiedemann–Franz law (Eq. (6)):

(6) κelectron=L0×σ×T

where *L*0 is the Lorenz number (2.45×10−8 W·Ω·K−2), *σ* is the electrical conductivity, and *T* is the temperature on the Kelvin scale, as shown in Fig. 8(c), Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 and Cr2TiAlC2 MAXs present typical metal-like electron conductivities, whose conductivities range from 0.6×106 to 0.8×106 S·m−1. Accordingly, the calculated *κ*electron values are 5.36, 4.58, 4.27, and 6.17 W·m−1·K−1 at 25 °C for Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2, Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2, Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2, and Cr2TiAlC2, respectively. Thus, the thermal conductivity contributed by phonons (*κ*phonon) can be obtained (Eq. (7)):

(7) κphonon=κ−κelectron

As plotted in Fig. 8(d), the *κ*phonon values of Cr1.25Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.25AlC2, Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2, Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2, and Cr2TiAlC2 at 25 °C are 15.16, 8.91, 9.58, and 8.73 W·m−1·K−1, respectively. As mentioned above, the increasing type of M-site metal in each layer not only dramatically interrupts the ordering in the layer but also eliminates the out-of-plane ordering. Therefore, the low *k* values detected in Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2 and Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2 can be ascribed to the enhanced scattering of both electrons and phonons dominated by the solid solution structure [47].

### 3.6 Electronic structure and bonding

To gain a better understanding of the chemical composition, lattice structure, and physical properties of the quaternary Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 MAXs, the electronic structure and bonding of four out-of-plane ordered counterpart MAXs, M2TiAlC2 (M = Ti, V, Cr, and Mo), were investigated through the First-principles calculations. The total density of states (TDOS) and corresponding projected density of states (PDOS) of the four binary MAXs are shown in Fig. 9. As shown in the TDOS plots, the Fermi energy level (vertical dashed line) of each MAX indicates a metallic nature [48]. Moreover, the PDOS proves the influence of the 4*f* metals on the bonding states of the Ti (2*a*), C, and Al atoms. The states from −7.50 eV to near the Fermi level can be mainly ascribed to the d–p hybridization of * M’–C,  M’’–C, and  M’–Al. For Ti3AlC2, the  M’–Al d–p hybridization is located in the region between −2.56 and 0.16 eV (Fig. 9(a)), which indicates the partially occupied nature of the states [49]. For the other three MAXs, a downshift of the M–C and  M’–Al d–p hybridization states can be observed (Figs. 9(b)–9(d)). In particular, Cr2TiAlC2 and Mo2TiAlC2 present a lower energy level of hybridization states than V2TiAlC2 does, which is in accordance with the increased valence electron concentration (VEC) [49]. Eventually, the occupation of Cr and Mo at the 4f* site contributes to a higher bulk modulus and, thus, hardness [48,50].

When the quaternary Crx*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 system is used, the transition in atomic occupancy is accompanied by the distribution of Cr and Mo at 2*a* as well as Ti and V to 4*f* sites. As summarized in Table S4 in the ESM, the bond lengths of * M’–C,  M’’–C, and  M’–Al from the four predicted M2TiAlC2 MAXs and the three Crx*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 MAXs are compared. According to the varied bond lengths of Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 MAXs, the redistribution of Ti, V, Cr, and Mo at the 4*f* and 2*a* sites along with the transition in atomic occupancy can be clearly mapped. Moreover, the reduced Cr and Mo contents at 4*f* site are in accordance with the slightly decreased hardness of the MAXs observed from both nanoindentation and Vickers hardness (Fig. 7).

## 4 Conclusions

In conclusion, three quaternary Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 (*x* = 1.25, 1, and 0.75) MAXs with an unprecedented transition in atomic occupancy from out-of-plane order to solid solution are synthesized. Their mechanical and thermal properties are evaluated. Through experimental observations and theoretical calculations, the influence of the atomic occupancy transition on the mechanical and thermal properties of these materials is illustrated. The main conclusions are as follows:

1) With decreasing Cr content and increasing V content, the quaternary Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 MAX shows a transition in atomic occupancy from out-of-plane order to a solid solution.

2) The hardness of the quaternary Cr*x*Ti0.75Mo0.75V1.5−*x*AlC2 MAXs slightly decreases along with the transition of atomic occupancy, whereas their Vickers hardness is still approximately 40% greater than that of Cr2TiAlC2, which can be attributed to the complex chemical composition and solid solution strengthening.

3) Along with the transition of atomic occupancy, a high-entropy structure is achieved in Cr1Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.5AlC2 and Cr0.75Ti0.75Mo0.75V0.75AlC2. The two MAXs present low thermal conductivities of 13.49 and 13.85 W·m−1·K−1 at 25 °C, which is attributed to the enhanced scattering of both electrons and phonons dominated by the high-entropy structure.
