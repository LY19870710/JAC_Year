## 1 Introduction

With the advent of the 5G era and the widespread application of wireless communications and electronic devices, electromagnetic (EM) waves have become increasingly significant in both civilian and military domains [1−4]. However, excessive EM radiation can interfere with the normal operation of electronic equipment, undermine the stealth capabilities of military assets, and pose risks to information security and human health. To mitigate these challenges, microwave-absorbing materials (MAMs) have attracted increasing attention worldwide [5−9]. MAMs dissipate incident electromagnetic energy into heat or other forms of energy, thereby minimizing reflection and scattering and effectively reducing EM pollution [10−14]. MAMs are generally classified into two types on the basis of their energy dissipation mechanisms: dielectric loss materials (e.g., carbon-based materials, ceramics, and metal sulfides) [15−18] and magnetic loss materials (e.g., ferrites, magnetic metals, and their oxides) [19−22]. Although magnetic loss materials typically exhibit stronger absorption performance, their properties tend to deteriorate at elevated temperatures because of the loss of magnetism above the Curie temperature. In contrast, dielectric loss materials have relatively stable performance under high-temperature conditions.

To meet the dual demands of stealth and thermal stability in defense applications, ceramic-based MAMs have emerged as a promising research focus owing to their excellent corrosion resistance and high-temperature tolerance [23,24]. Moreover, the advent of the “high entropy” concept has further enhanced the performance of ceramic MAMs, benefiting from the synergistic effects of high configurational entropy, lattice distortion, sluggish diffusion [25], and the cocktail effect [26,27]. For example, Zhou *et al*. [28] prepared (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C ceramics via carbothermal reduction, achieving RL of −38.5 dB at 9.5 GHz (thickness of 1.9 mm) and EAB of 2.3 GHz (11.3–13.6 GHz) at 1.5 mm. Wang *et al*. [29] prepared (Ti0.2Zr0.2Mo0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C with RL of −32.1 dB and an EAB covering 13–18 GHz at 1.5 mm. Chen *et al*. [30] synthesized HE(Tm0.2Y0.2Pr0.2Gd0.2Dy0.2)3Si2C2/HE(Tm0.2Y0.2Pr0.2G0.2Dy0.2)2O3 composite ceramics with the minimum RL of −40.7 dB and 3.4 GHz EAB at 2.9 mm. In our previous study [31], (Hf0.25Zr0.25Nb0.25Ta0.25)C high-entropy ceramics with rock-salt structures were successfully prepared via a solid-state reaction at 2200 °C. Although the material exhibited excellent EM wave absorption, its EAB remained narrow. To address this limitation, we introduced SiC into the (Hf0.25Zr0.25Nb0.25Ta0.25)C matrix via the polymer-derived ceramic (PDC) route at relatively low temperatures [32]. This approach not only reduced the synthesis temperature but also improved the absorption performance through enhanced interfacial polarization and conductivity loss. Nonetheless, the EAB has remained limited. To further optimize the microwave absorption performance, incorporating magnetic elements into high-entropy ceramic matrices has emerged as a promising strategy. Among various magnetic transition metals, cobalt (Co) stands out owing to its strong magnetic properties and high Curie temperature. However, systematic studies on the incorporation of Co into high-entropy carbide ceramics—including compositional regulation, effects on magnetic permeability, and synergistic interactions between dielectric and magnetic losses—are still lacking. In this context, Zhou Fu (Master’s thesis, unpublished) utilized a liquid precursor method to introduce Co into a high-entropy ceramic matrix, successfully synthesizing equimolar (Hf0.2Zr0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Co0.2)C ceramics and preliminarily investigating their microwave absorption behavior. However, the effects of varying the Co content on the microwave absorption performance—particularly whether tuning the Co concentration can regulate the matrix permeability, suppress the dielectric constant dominance, and promote synergistic dielectric–magnetic loss mechanisms—remains unexplored.

In this study, we successfully introduced varying Co contents into (Hf0.25Zr0.25Nb0.25Ta0.25)C ceramics via the PDC method, resulting in (Hf(1−*X*)/4Zr(1−*X*)/4Nb(1−*X*)/4Ta(1−*X*)/4Co*X*)C ceramics (*X* = 0.14, 0.18, and 0.20), all of which adopted a rock-salt structure. The microstructure and surface chemical states were systematically characterized. Furthermore, the effect of Co content on the microwave absorption properties was thoroughly investigated, with a particular emphasis on the synergistic mechanisms between magnetic and dielectric losses. The results indicate that Co can form a solid solution with host cations in a near-equimolar configuration, yielding single-phase high-entropy ceramics with significantly enhanced microwave absorption performance.

### 2.1 Materials and sample preparation

Tantalum pentachloride (TaCl5) (CAS: 7721-01-9, purity: 98%), niobium pentachloride (NbCl5) (CAS: 10026-12-7, purity: 99%), hafnium tetrachloride (HfCl4) (CAS: 13499-05-3, purity: 99.5%), zirconium n-propanol (CAS: 23519-77-9 70 wt% n-propanol solution), and cobalt acetylacetonate (CAS: 21679-46-9, purity: 98%) were selected as raw materials. n-propanol (CAS: 71-23-8, purity: 99%), triethylamine (CAS: 121-44-8, purity: 99.5%), and acetylacetone (CAS: 123-54-6, purity: 99%) were used as solvents and complexing agents. Phenolic resins served as the carbon source throughout the synthesis. All reagents were supplied by Shanghai Maclean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.

Initially, TaCl5, NbCl5, HfCl4, and zirconium n-propoxide were dissolved in n-propanol and stirred magnetically in a glass container. Triethylamine was then added dropwise to the solution under continuous stirring to react completely with the chlorine ions, resulting in the formation of a precipitate. After standing for 24 h, the precipitate was filtered to obtain a clear transition metal alkoxide solution. Cobalt acetylacetonate and acetylacetone were subsequently added to the mixture, which was then thoroughly mixed. Phenolic resin was then introduced under vigorous stirring to ensure homogeneity. The final homogeneous solution was dried at 120 °C to remove residual solvents. The resulting solid was ground into a fine precursor powder. These powders were then heated at 1600, 1700, 1800, and 1900 °C for 1 h in an argon atmosphere at a heating rate of 8 °C/min. After naturally cooling to room temperature, the sintered products were milled to yield Hf(1−*X*)/4Zr(1−*X*)/4Nb(1−*X*)/4Ta(1−*X*)/4Co*X*)C high-entropy carbide ceramic powders with Co molar fractions of *X* = 0.14, 0.18, and 0.20.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The phase compositions of the samples were analyzed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan). The surface morphology, microstructure, and elemental distribution were characterized via a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Apreo 22, Thermo Fisher, USA) equipped with an energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images were obtained via transmission electron microscope (TEM; CM300, Philips, the Netherlands) with an integrated EDS system to further investigate the internal nanostructure. The surface chemical states of the constituent elements were determined via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific, USA). The magnetic properties of the samples were measured via a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM; 8604, Lake Shore, USA).

The electromagnetic properties of (Hf(1−*X*)/4Zr(1−*X*)/4Nb(1−*X*)/4Ta(1−*X*)/4Co*X*)C (*X* = 0.14, 0.18, and 0.20) ceramic powders were evaluated in the frequency range of 2–18 GHz using the transmission/reflection (T/R) coaxial line method with a vector network analyzer (N5230A, Agilent, USA). Before testing, the ceramic powders were homogeneously mixed with paraffin (30–50 wt%) and pressed into toroidal samples with a thickness of 2.0–4.0 mm, an inner diameter of 3.04 mm, and an outer diameter of 7.00 mm.

### 3.1 Phase composition and microstructure of the prepared samples

The XRD patterns of the ceramic samples sintered at different temperatures ranging from 1600–1900°C are shown in Fig. 1. As shown in Figs. 1(a) and 1(b), all (Hf(1−*X*)/4Zr(1−*X*)/4Nb(1−*X*)/4Ta(1−*X*)/4Co*X*)C (*X* = 0.14, 0.18, and 0.20) ceramics exhibit a typical face-centered cubic (FCC) structure with a rock‒salt lattice across the entire temperature range. However, distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to residual metal oxides are observed in the samples sintered at 1600 °C, indicating incomplete carbothermal reduction and/or insufficient reaction kinetics at this temperature. Additionally, even at 1700 °C, oxide-related peaks remain visible in the sample with an equimolar composition (Hf0.2Zr0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Co0.2)C, further suggesting that this temperature is inadequate to achieve complete phase purity. In contrast, as shown in Figs. 1(c) and 1(d), samples sintered at 1800 and 1900 °C exhibit only diffraction peaks corresponding to the FCC rock-salt phase, with no detectable secondary or oxide phases. These results indicate that a single-phase high-entropy carbide with a rock-salt structure was successfully synthesized at ≥ 1800 °C after 1 h of heat treatment under an inert atmosphere.

### 3.2 XPS analysis

To elucidate the surface chemical state of the (Hf(1−*X*)/4Zr(1−*X*)/4Nb(1−*X*)/4Ta(1−*X*)/4Co*X*)C high-entropy carbide ceramic powders prepared at 1800 °C, XPS was performed on the samples with *X* = 0.14, 0.18, and 0.20. The high-resolution XPS spectra are shown in Fig. 6. The fitted binding energy positions of all the constituent elements in the (Hf0.215Zr0.215Nb0.215Ta0.215Co0.140)C and (Hf0.2Zr0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Co0.2)C samples are nearly identical, indicating comparable chemical environments. In contrast, the (Hf0.205Zr0.205Nb0.205Ta0.205Co0.180)C sample exhibited a pronounced shift in the binding energies of all the peaks toward lower values of ~1.5 eV. This shift suggests electronic structure or chemical state modifications, possibly arising from changes in local bonding configurations or oxidation behavior. In the following discussion, we focus on the detailed peak-fitting analysis of the (Hf0.215Zr0.215Nb0.215Ta0.215Co0.140)C sample as a representative case.

*
Fig. 6High-resolution XPS spectra of high-entropy ceramics (Hf(1−X*)/4Zr(1−*X*)/4Nb(1−*X*)/4Ta(1−*X*)/4Co*X*)C (*X* = 0.14, 0.18, and 0.20): (a) C 1s; (b) Hf 4f; (c) Ta 4f; (d) Nb 3d; (e) Zr 3d; (f) Co 2p.

As shown in Fig. 6(a), the high-resolution XPS spectrum of C 1s reveals two primary fitted peaks. The peak at 283.64 eV is attributed to the C‒C bond, whereas the peak at 280.93 eV corresponds to the C‒M bond, indicating chemical bonding between carbon and transition metals [33,34]. In the Hf 4f spectrum (Fig. 6(b)), three distinct peaks are observed at 13.25, 15.4, and 17.48 eV. The peak at 13.25 eV is assigned to the Hf‒C bond, whereas the latter two peaks correspond to the Hf‒O bonds, suggesting partial surface oxidation of hafnium [35]. The Ta 4f spectrum (Fig. 6(c)) presents fitted peaks at 22.17, 24.35, and 26.66 eV. Among them, the peak at 22.17 eV is attributed to Ta–C bonding, whereas the remaining peaks arise from Ta–O bond species, further indicating surface oxidation [36]. In the Nb 3d spectrum (Fig. 6(d)), three peaks are observed: the peak at 202.67 eV is attributed to the Nb–C bond, whereas the peaks at 205.68 and 209.77 eV are attributed to the Nb–O bonds [29]. Similarly, the Zr 3d spectrum (Fig. 6(e)) displays peaks at 177.92, 180.98, and 183.71 eV, which are assigned to (Zr–C) and (Zr–O), respectively [37]. The Co 2p spectrum (Fig. 6(f)) is deconvoluted into four peaks: the 2p3/2 peak at 780.59 eV and the 2p1/2 peak at 796.26 eV correspond to Co2+ species, whereas two satellite peaks at 785.48 and 801.84 eV further confirm the oxidized state of cobalt [38]. Collectively, the XPS peak fitting results confirmed the formation of M‒C (metal‒carbon) bonds in the samples, indicating the effective incorporation of transition metals into the carbide framework. Moreover, the detection of metal‒oxygen (M‒O) bonds indicates slight surface oxidation, which is common in high-entropy carbides upon exposure to ambient conditions.

### 3.3 VSM analysis

The magnetization properties of the (Hf(1−*X*)/4Zr(1−*X*)/4Nb(1−*X*)/4Ta(1−*X*)/4Co*X*)C high-entropy carbide ceramics with varying Co contents (*X* = 0.14, 0.18, and 0.20) sintered at 1800 °C were evaluated via a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) at room temperature. The magnetization versus applied magnetic field (M–H) curves are presented in Fig. 7. According to the VSM results, the (Hf0.215Zr0.215Nb0.215Ta0.215Co0.140)C sample exhibited a saturation magnetization (*M*s) of 2.522 emu/g and a coercivity (*H*c) of 161 Oe. In contrast, the Ms values increase to 4.515 and 10.976 emu/g for the (Hf0.205Zr0.205Nb0.205Ta0.205Co0.180)C and (Hf0.2Zr0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Co0.2)C samples, respectively, while their corresponding Hc values decrease to 135 and 61 Oe. These results demonstrate that all samples exhibit weak ferromagnetic behavior, with both saturation magnetization and coercivity strongly dependent on the Co content. As the Co concentration increases, the magnetic response increases, which may be attributed to the increased presence of Co2+ species and improved magnetic exchange interactions within the rock-salt structured lattice.

where  εs is the static permittivity, and  ε∞ is the permittivity at high frequencies. As shown in Fig. 9(a), multiple semicircles are observed for each sample, indicating the occurrence of polarization relaxation processes. Magnetic loss in microwave-absorbing materials generally arises from three primary mechanisms: eddy current loss, resonance loss, and hysteresis loss. Among them, eddy current loss is caused by the circular currents induced within the materials when subjected to a time-varying magnetic field. This type of loss can be quantitatively described by Eq. (2) [46,47]:

(2) Co=μ′′(μ′)−2f−1=2πμ0d2σ

where *C*o is the eddy current coefficient,  f is the frequency of the electromagnetic wave,  μ0 is the magnetic permeability of free space,  d is the sample thickness, and  σ is the electrical conductivity. According to Eq. (2), if the thickness ( d) remains constant, a frequency-independent  C0 indicates that eddy current loss is the dominant mechanism of magnetic loss. Conversely, a frequency-dependent  C0 suggests the involvement of other mechanisms, such as resonance loss. As illustrated in Fig. 9(b), the  C0 values for the (Hf0.215Zr0.215Nb0.215Ta0.215Co0.140)C samples vary with frequency, implying that eddy current loss is not the sole contributor. Resonance loss typically originates from magnetic anisotropy and energy exchange between magnetic domains or between grain interiors and their surface. Hysteresis loss, on the other hand, is associated with magnetic domain reversal and is proportional to the area enclosed by the hysteresis loop. However, on the basis of VSM measurements, the sample exhibits relatively low coercivity and narrow hysteresis loops, indicating that the hysteresis losses are minimal and can be neglected. Therefore, combining the analysis of Eq. (2), the frequency-dependent behavior of  C0, and the magnetic hysteresis characteristics, it can be concluded that the dominant magnetic loss mechanisms in the (Hf0.215Zr0.215Nb0.215Ta0.215Co0.140)C samples are due mainly to the synergistic effects of eddy current losses and resonance losses.

### 3.5 Microwave absorption properties and mechanisms of the samples

On the basis of transmission line theory, the RL values of the (Hf0.215Zr0.215Nb0.215Ta0.215Co0.140)C samples were calculated via a metal-backplane model to evaluate their microwave absorption performance. The RL can be expressed by Eqs. (3) and (4) [48−50]:

(3) RL(dB)=20log|Zin−Z0Zin+Z0|

(4) Zin=Z0μr/εrtanh(j(2πfdc)μrεr)

where * Zin is the input impedance of the absorber,  Z0 denotes the impedance in free space,  f is the microwave frequency,  d is the sample thickness,  c is the speed of light in a vacuum, and  μr and  εr are the relative permeability and permittivity of the material, respectively. The 2D and 3D RL curves of the samples with different powder contents are shown in Fig. 10. For the sample containing 30 wt% filler, the minimum reflection loss (RLmin) reaches −7.53 dB at a thickness of 3.00 mm and a frequency of 16.22 GHz; for the 40 wt% filler, the RLmin improves significantly to −20.79 dB at 2.08 mm thickness and 14.69 GHz, and an effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) of 0.17 GHz is achieved at 2.45 mm; for the sample with 50 wt% content, a notable RLmin of −37.95 dB is achieved at 3.10 mm and a frequency of 14.01 GHz, whereas the maximum EAB of 0.44 GHz is observed at 3.34 mm. These results indicate that increasing the filler content effectively enhances the microwave absorption performance, both in terms of the minimum RL and EAB.

Figure 12 shows the electromagnetic wave absorption mechanism of the high-entropy ceramic powder (Hf0.215Zr0.215Nb0.215Ta0.215Co0.140)C. The irregular morphology and interparticle voids facilitate multiple reflections of incident electromagnetic waves, thereby increasing their attenuation within the material (Fig. 12(a)). The dielectric loss mechanisms primarily include interfacial polarization, dipole polarization, and conduction loss [55]. Interfacial polarization arises from the abundant heterogeneous interfaces formed during synthesis, which serve as charge accumulation sites, enhancing dielectric relaxation (Fig. 12(b)) [56]. Dipole polarization is induced by charge density mismatches at the interface, further intensified by significant lattice distortion and defects in the ceramic matrix (Fig. 12(c)) [57]. The introduction of cobalt increases the number of lattice defects and heterojunction interfaces in the ceramic matrix, thereby further enhancing the interfacial polarization and dipole polarization reactions. Conduction loss results from the directional migration of free electrons and their scattering with lattice ions, transforming electromagnetic energy into heat (Fig. 12(d)) [58]. This type of loss is strongly related to the electrical conductivity of the material; the higher the conductivity is, the greater the degree of conductive loss. However, excessively high conductivity may cause impedance mismatch, increasing the reflection of electromagnetic waves at the material surface and thus diminishing the overall absorption performance. With increasing cobalt content, the permittivity and permeability of the samples gradually decrease, resulting in changes in both the impedance and attenuation capability of the samples (Fig. 12(e)). Moreover, Co incorporation induces magnetic loss mechanisms, including eddy current loss, resonance loss, and hysteresis loss, thereby enhancing the attenuation capability of electromagnetic waves [59]. In conclusion, (Hf0.215Zr0.215Nb0.215Ta0.215Co0.140)C has the highest dielectric constant and permeability together with moderate conductivity, which achieves an optimal balance among polarization relaxation, conduction loss, and magnetic loss. Ultimately, dominated by dielectric loss and assisted by synergistic magnetic loss, the material achieves a balance between impedance matching and a high attenuation constant, thereby realizing optimal microwave absorption performance.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, (Hf(1−*X*)/4Zr(1−*X*)/4Nb(1−*X*)/4Ta(1−*X*)/4Co*X*)C (*X* = 0.14, 0.18, and 0.20) high-entropy ceramic powders were successfully synthesized via a PDC route at 1700—1900 °C. The phase composition analysis, microstructural characterization, and XPS results confirmed the formation of a single-phase rock‒salt structure in all the compositions. The substitution of Co not only stabilized the single-phase structure but also introduced magnetic loss characteristics into the ceramic system. Among the compositions studied, the (Hf0.215Zr0.215Nb0.215Ta0.215Co0.140)C ceramic prepared at 1700 °C exhibited the best microwave absorption performance, with an RLmin of −37.95 dB at a thickness of 3.10 mm. The enhanced absorption was attributed to the synergistic contribution of multiple mechanisms: interfacial polarization, dipole relaxation, conduction loss, and magnetic loss (such as eddy current and natural resonance). This work reveals the application prospects of cobalt-doped high-entropy carbides as lightweight and efficient microwave-absorbing materials. These findings provide key insights into the composition–structure–property relationships and loss mechanisms, offering guidance for the design of high-entropy ceramics for electromagnetic wave attenuation.
