## 1 Introduction

With the development of intelligent sensor manufacturing, the research of high-performance temperature sensing materials, which can operate positively in extreme environments such as aeroengines, nuclear reactors, and hypersonic vehicles, has attracted significant attention [1−3]. Unfortunately, the currently used commercial temperature sensors struggle to be applied in complex extreme environments. For instance, the low measurement temperature of traditional ceramics (4−6]. Therefore, it is urgent to explore new temperature sensors that can withstand the impact of harsh environments.

Polymer-derived ceramics (PDCs) as sensing materials have been considered promising candidates for monitoring temperature signals due to their excellent thermal stability, good corrosion/oxidation resistance, creep resistance, and high-temperature semiconductor characteristics [7−14]. Presently, several temperature sensors equipped with PDCs have been reported. For example, Cui *et al*. [15] prepared a SiCN thin-film temperature sensor, which can work well in temperature from room temperature to 800 ℃. Wu *et al.* [16] fabricated a SiBCN thin-film thermistor and demonstrated its stability in the regions of 25–800 ℃. However, SiCN and SiBCN ceramics have relatively poor oxidation/corrosion resistance in high temperature, resulting in SiCN and SiBCN-based temperature sensors having short lifespans. Subsequently, the temperature sensor fabricated with SiAlCN ceramics, which have excellent high-temperature resistance to oxidation and corrosion environments, was developed in our previous research [17]. The maximum operation temperature of the prepared sensor could reach up to 1000 ℃. Unfortunately, the performance of the SiAlCN-based temperature sensors started to degrade after a period of measurement time, which is possibly attributed to the unsatisfactory structural and functional stability of SiAlCN ceramics.

Consequently, although the above-mentioned temperature sensors based on PDCs have been investigated, those weaknesses still should be overcome for further practical application. First, the maximum monitor temperature still needs to be improved for the demand of extreme environments within aircraft engines. Second, the most of research focused on the sensing performance of SiCN/SiCO ceramics and one element dopped PDCs (SiAlCN, SiBCN, SiAlCO) [18−22], while the properties of multi-dopped PDCs have been barely explored. Third, SiBCN ceramics have poor oxidation/corrosion resistance and SiAlCN ceramics have unsatisfied high-temperature stability. As a result, boron and aluminum co-doping PDCs are proposed to resolve the above problems.

In this study, SiAlBCN ceramics were prepared at different pyrolysis temperatures by polymer conversion method. Their structural evolution was investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman, solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (SSNMR), and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR). Then, a temperature sensor with SiAlBCN ceramics as the sensor body was fabricated and its performance was evaluated including temperature–resistance characteristics, repeatability, sensitivity, and stability. Furthermore, considering the practical application in extreme environments, the corrosion/oxidation resistance of SiAlBCN ceramics was performed and the corresponding kinetics was analyzed.

### 2.1 Materials

Commercially liquid polyborosilazane (PSNB; Institute of Chemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China) and aluminum-tri-sec-butoxide (ASB; 97%, Sigma-Aldrich, China) were brought as precursors. Their chemical structures are shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Phenylbis(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl)phosphine oxide (Irgacure 819, 97%, BASF, Germany) was used as the photoinitiator. All chemical reagents were used without any purification.

### 2.2 Preparation of polymer-derived SiAlBCN ceramics

To prepare the polymer-derived SiAlBCN ceramics, the liquid SiAlBCN precursor was prepared by mixing a certain amount of PSNB (B content is 6 wt%), 10 wt% ASB, and 4.5 wt% Irgacure 819. The mixture solution was stirred for 30 min at 60 ℃ until a homogenous solution was obtained. Afterward, the prepared precursor was filled into a silicone mold with a specific dimension of *ϕ*10 mm × 1.5 mm, which was then crosslinked for 5 min using an ultraviolet light instrument. Finally, the cured samples were pyrolyzed at different temperatures (1000–1500 ℃) for 4 h. The whole procedure was carried out under the ultrahigh purity N2 atmosphere to prevent oxygen contamination.

To investigate the oxidation and corrosion resistance, the SiAlBCN ceramics pyrolyzed at 1300 ℃ were treated for 100 h at 1200 ℃ with the partial pressure of water vapor (50%) and oxygen (50%). The SiCN and SiAlCN ceramics pyrolyzed at 1300 ℃ were selected as the control targets. The schematic of the water-oxygen corrosion experimental setup is shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The XRD patterns of SiAlBCN ceramics were recorded by a diffractometer (SmartLab, Rigaku Instrument Corp., Japan) with Cu Kα radiation with an angular 2*θ* ranging from 10° to 80°. Raman spectroscopy of the samples was obtained by a Raman spectrometer (LabRam HR Evolution, HORIBA, France) with a wavelength of 523 nm. The compositions of the samples were determined by an inductively coupled plasma–atomic emission spectrometer (ICP–AES, Agilent 5110, USA). 27Al and 29Si spectra of all samples were performed via a SSNMR spectrometer (AVANCE III 400WB, Bruker, Germany) at the resonance frequency of 10 kHz using a 7 mm probe. 11B SSNMR spectra were acquired at the resonance frequency of 12 kHz equipped with a 4 mm probe. The morphology and microstructures of SiAlBCN ceramics were observed by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; JSM-7001F, JEOL). EPR spectra were recorded by a spectrometer (EMX-9.5/12, Bruker, Germany) with a frequency of 9.8 GHz and a magnetic field from 290 to 390 mT at room temperature.

### 2.4 Sensor preparation and measurement

To fabricate a high-temperature sensor, two holes with a depth of 0.5 mm and a diameter of 0.3 mm were punched on the surface of the polymer-derived SiAlBCN ceramics. Platinum wires with a diameter of 0.2 mm were then fixed into the holes with a special solder. Finally, the fabricated sensor was treated at 1200 ℃. Furthermore, to evaluate the performance of the SiAlBCN ceramics sensor, a measurement setup was built, and its schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 1(b). This test platform contains a data collection system, a Wheatstone bridge, a K-type thermocouple, and a heating furnace. The repeatability, sensitivity, and stability of the SiAlBCN sensor were performed in an aerobic environment.

### 3.1 Structural evolution of SiAlBCN ceramics

Figure 2(a) shows the XRD patterns of the polymer-derived SiAlBCN ceramics pyrolyzed in the temperature range from 1000 to 1500 ℃. It can be observed that the broad diffraction peaks at around 26° are assigned to graphitic carbon, indicating that SiAlBCN ceramics prepared at 1000–1300 ℃ exhibited a typical amorphous structure. For the SiAlBCN ceramics prepared at 1400 and 1500 ℃, the diffraction peaks at 35°, 43°, and 60° can be assigned to the (111), (200), and (220) planes of β-SiC [23], respectively, displaying worse anti-crystallization properties compared to the SiBCN ceramics [24,25]. Meanwhile, the chemical composition of the SiAlBCN ceramics pyrolyzed at 1000 ℃ was determined to be SiAl0.05B0.04C1.66N1.49O0.55.

*
Fig. 2(a) XRD patterns and (b) Raman spectra of SiAlBCN ceramics prepared at different temperatures. (c) Variations of FWHM and (d) I*D/*I*G as the function of pyrolysis temperature.

The Raman spectra of the SiAlBCN ceramics prepared at different temperatures are shown in Fig. 2(b). A typical D band at 1350 cm−1 and a G band at 1580 cm−1 can be visibly observed for all spectra. The D and G bands are attributed to breathing modes of disordered carbon and in-plane bond of stretching of sp2 carbon atoms [26], respectively. Their full width at half maximum (FWHM) decreased gradually with the increasing pyrolysis temperature (Fig. 2(c)), suggesting the transformation of the free carbon phase from amorphous to nanocrystalline structure. Meanwhile, the *I*D/*I*G value increased from 1.49 to 2.56, indicating that higher temperature is conducive to promoting the order degree of the free carbon phase.

To further observe the microstructure evolution of the SiAlBCN ceramics, SSNMR spectra were employed to determine their atomic bonding structure. Figure 3(a) shows the 29Si SSNMR spectra of the SiAlBCN ceramics prepared at different temperatures. The resonance peak at around −34 ppm corresponded to the SiCN3 unit [27], which was mainly caused by the resonance of the amorphous SiC*x*N4−*x* (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 4) phase. The signals appearing at −19, −50, and −107 ppm were assigned to SiC4, SiN4, and SiO4 units, respectively [28,29]. The generated SiO4 component could be caused by the photoinitiator or aluminum source that contained oxygen. To obtain more details about structural components, SSNMR curves were fitted by Lorentz function. Figure 4(a) shows the relative mole percentages of 29Si structural components and the percentage of SiCN3 decreased gradually with the increasing pyrolysis temperature. (1) 4SiCN3↔3SiN4+SiC4

To confirm this possible Reaction (1), its reaction constant was calculated based on Eq. (2):

(2) K=[SiN4]3[SiC4][SiCN3]4

where [SiN4], [SiC4], and [SiCN3] are mole concentrations of their respective units. According to Van’t Hoff equation [30], the temperature-dependent equilibrium constant can be expressed by Eq. (3):

(3) lnK=−ΔG∘RT=−ΔH∘RT+ΔS∘R

where ΔH°* is the standard enthalpy of the reaction; Δ*S°* is the entropy; *R* is the general gas constant (8.314 J/(mol·K)); *T* is the reaction temperature (K). The relationship between ln([SiN4]3∙[SiC4]/[SiCN3]4) and 1000/*T* is shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. The calculated Δ*H°* and Δ*S°* were 253,200 and 157.4, respectively. Accordingly, Δ*G°* should be positive at the temperature range of 1000–1500 ℃, indicating that the structural evolution of Si components was an endothermal process. The increased temperature can promote remixes of the amorphous SiC*x*N4−*x* phase, generating more ordered structure.

The 11B SSNMR spectra had two signals at around 14 and 26 ppm, corresponding to BN2C and BC3 [31], respectively (Fig. 3(b)). The board peak at about −18 ppm was attributed to the background signal. The transformation from BN2C to BC3 was achieved by increased pyrolysis temperature (Fig. 4(b)). In addition, 27Al SSNMR spectra were also recorded and are displayed in Fig. 3(c). Two resonance signals appeared at approximately 5 and 50 ppm, implying the formation of AlN5 and AlN6 units [32,33]. With the increasing pyrolysis temperature, AlN5 converted to AlN6 gradually, but the relative content of AlN5 was still much higher compared to that of AlN6. Based on the above results, the introduced elements (Al and B) could predispose the release of carbon and nitrogen from the amorphous ceramic structure.

Furthermore, the emergence of defects is inevitable during the conversion process from polymer precursor to ceramics. Hence, EPR was employed to investigate the occurrence of defects. Figure 5(a) presents the EPR spectra of the SiAlBCN ceramics at the frequency of 9.8 GHz, and only a group of resonance signals appeared. *g*-factor, which represents orbital motion and spin of electrons under the paramagnetic center, was determined to be 2.0036±0.0001, suggesting the formation of carbon defects (unpaired electrons) [34]. Additionally, the concentration of the carbon dangling bond was calculated by second-integration, as shown in Fig. 5(b). It can be observed that the concentration of carbon dangling bond decreased at 1100 ℃, which should be attributed to the unresolved hyperfine interactions between the electron spin and the nuclear spin (like 1H). However, the defect concentration increased with the increasing pyrolysis temperature (1200 and 1300 ℃), which could be related to the release of nitrogen and carbon elements from the amorphous ceramic phase, generating more carbon defects. For higher pyrolysis temperatures (1400 and 1500 ℃), β-SiC crystalline and carbon clusters were formed, leading to a more ordered ceramic phase and fewer carbon defects.

### 3.2 Sensing performance of SiAlBCN ceramic temperature sensor

To evaluate the high-temperature sensing performance, the performance of the SiAlBCN ceramic temperature sensor was measured, and its optical image is shown in the inset of Fig. 6(a). The inserted Pt wires connected tightly to the body of the SiAlBCN sensor (Fig. 6(a)), guaranteeing that the voltage signal could be outputted continuously. Figure 6(b) displays the temperature–resistance curve of the SiAlBCN sensor from room temperature to 1100 ℃. It can be seen that the resistance of the SiAlBCN sensor decreased monotonically during the whole temperature range, exhibiting a typical negative temperature coefficient property, which can confirm the application of the SiAlBCN ceramics in the high-temperature sensing field. Furthermore, temperature–resistance characteristics of the SiAlBCN ceramics were analyzed by Eq. (4):

The relationship between the resistance of the SiAlBCN ceramics and the test temperature was fitted separately at 25–500, 500–800, and 800–1100 ℃ (Figs. S4–S6 in the ESM). The corresponding *C*1 values, which symbolize the sensitivity of sensing materials, are −2554, −5405, and −8510. Additionally, the sensitivity coefficient of the thermistor was calculated by Eq. (5) [17]:

(5) αT=(1ρ)(∂ρ∂T)=−BT2

where *B* and *ρ* are the material constant and the resistivity of thermistor, respectively; *T* is the measurement temperature. The sensitivity coefficient of the SiAlBCN ceramics varied from −3.40%·K−1 to −0.16%·K−1 when the temperature increased from 25 to 1100 ℃. Compared to other PDCs-based temperature sensors, the SiAlBCN sensor possesses more excellent sensitivity and higher measured temperatures (Table S1 in the ESM).

To further measure the performance of the SiAlBCN temperature sensor, the resistance was converted to voltage signals, and the voltage–temperature (*V*–*T*) curve is shown in Fig. 6(c). The output voltage signal increased monotonously with the increasing temperature, achieving a positive and stable conversion of *V*–*T*. Meanwhile, the output voltage (815–820.5 ℃) was magnified, as shown in Fig. 6(d). As the temperature changed by 1, 0.5, and 0.1 ℃, the corresponding voltage change rates were 2.18, 1.76, and 2.01 mV/℃, respectively, indicating that the prepared SiAlBCN sensor has competent sensitivity. Subsequently, the SiAlBCN sensor was measured for consecutive 16 cycles from 300 to 900 ℃ (Fig. 6(e)). It can be seen that the response of output voltage exhibited a similar trend to that of thermocouples, displaying excellent repeatability and stability. In addition, the SiAlBCN sensor was preserved at 650 °C for 150 h to evaluate the stability and the variation of the voltage signal is shown in Fig. 6(f). The change of the output voltage was 0.75% during the whole heating period, suggesting its outstanding stability under high-temperature conditions.

### 3.3 Oxidant/corrosion resistance performance of SiAlBCN ceramics

To meet stable working and long lifespan of temperature sensors, the property of the SiAlBCN ceramics has to be guaranteed during high-temperature environments. Therefore, the oxidation/corrosion resistance performance of the SiAlBCN ceramics was investigated at 1200 ℃ for 100 h. Figure 7(a) shows the weight changes of SiCN, SiBCN, and SiAlBCN ceramics. It can be seen that the SiAlBCN ceramics had much less weight loss compared to the other two ceramics, indicating that SiAlBCN ceramics exhibited better oxidation and corrosion resistance. Meanwhile, several cracks can be observed clearly on the surface of the SiCN and SiBCN ceramics (Fig. 7(c)); while the surface of the SiAlBCN ceramics was crack-free and smooth, which is consistent with the results of weight changes. To further analyze the oxidation/corrosion layer, XRD patterns of SiCN, SiBCN, and SiAlBCN ceramics were recorded (Fig. 7(b)). It can be found that the main component of the oxidative layer was cristobalite. The formed cristobalite on the surface of SiAlBCN ceramics had better crystallinity, preventing further erosion of water and oxygen atoms. Subsequently, the cross section of the oxidative layer was analyzed by line scanning of energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), as shown in Fig. 7(d). The thickness of the oxidative layer was 15–20 µm. The elemental analysis results also show that the relative content of Si decreased apparently, and the relative content of C and O increased slightly, which could be attributed to the reaction between oxidized silicon and steam, generating volatile substances (like Si(OH)4) [35]. However, the introduced aluminum could increase the diffusion activation energy [36], attenuating the oxygen permeation rate.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, polymer-derived SiAlBCN ceramics were prepared under different pyrolysis temperatures. Their structural evolution was analyzed systematically using XRD, Raman spectrum, SSNMR, and EPR spectra. The results found that the size of the free carbon phase enlarged with the increasing temperature and the amorphous SiAlBCN phase became more ordered with the structural reassembly. The AlN*x* mixture underwent the transition from AlN5 to AlN6 units with the increasing temperature, but AlN5 was still the predominant unit. Meanwhile, compared to SiCN and SiBCN ceramics, SiAlBCN PDCs exhibited excellent oxidation/corrosion resistance, which was related to their low oxidative rate constant (3.43 mg2/(cm4·h)) and volatilized rate constant (0.57 mg/(cm2·h)), guaranteeing that they could survive well in extreme environments. Furthermore, the temperature sensor fabricated with SiAlBCN ceramics was prepared and possessed outstanding performance, including good temperature–resistance character, repeatability, accuracy, and stability. This work demonstrated that SiAlBCN ceramics as sensing materials could be applied for high-temperature extreme environments.
