## 1 Introduction

Recent advancements in optoelectronic technologies have driven the demand for multifunctional luminescent materials capable of operating across multiple spectral regions to increase device performance and application versatility [1−3]. In particular, materials exhibiting both visible (Vis) and near-infrared (NIR) emissions hold great promise for applications in optical communication, bioimaging, photocatalysis, and photovoltaics [4−6]. While Vis emission is crucial for lighting and displays [7−9], NIR light offers superior tissue penetration, making it highly suitable for biomedical imaging and diagnostics [10−12]. Therefore, dual-emission materials represent a compelling platform for integrated optoelectronic systems.

Manganese (Mn) ions have emerged as versatile luminescent activators because of their abundant oxidation states, cost-effectiveness, and broad emission tunability [13−15]. Mn2+ typically exhibits green-to-red broadband emission [16,17], whereas Mn4+ favors red to far-red emission in octahedral environments [18,19]. However, controlling the valence state of Mn during synthesis remains challenging. Conventional methods rely on reducing atmospheres (e.g., H2, CO) to stabilize Mn2+, which incur higher costs, safety risks, and limited scalability [20−22]. To address these limitations, increasing attention has been given to self-reducing hosts that enable *in situ* Mn4+ → Mn2+ conversion without external reducing agents. This spontaneous reduction is typically driven by intrinsic defects (e.g., interstitial oxygen, cation vacancies) that act as electron donors, altering the local charge environment around the Mn ions [23,24]. Host lattices containing tetrahedral anion groups ([BO4]5−, [PO4]3−, etc.) are particularly conducive to this process, enabling the coexistence of stable heterovalent Mn [25,26].

Several oxide hosts, including silicates [27,28], phosphates [29], gallates [30], and germanates, have demonstrated partial or complete self-reduction of Mn4+ [31]. Among them, spinel-structured materials (e.g., ZnAl2O4, Mg2SnO4, Mg4(Ga/Al)SbO8, and MgGa2O4) are especially attractive because of their robust frameworks, chemical stability, and capacity to host multivalent dopants [32−35]. Rational crystal engineering in such lattices plays a critical role in regulating defect chemistry and enabling dual-valent Mn emission. Previous studies have explored Mn valence regulation via aliovalent codoping or charge compensation strategies. For example, Si *et al*. [30] employed Mg2+/Ge4+ codoping in ZnGa2O4 to tune the Mn2+/Mn4+ site distribution for anticounterfeiting, whereas Xue *et al*. [35] and Chen *et al*. [36] introduced Li+ in MgGa2O4 and ZnAl2O4 to suppress self-reduction and maintain Mn4+-dominant emission. Codoping systems such as ZnGa2O4:Mn2+ and Cr3+ have also demonstrated visible/NIR dual emission via multi-ion interactions [37]. However, these approaches often rely on complex compositions, multiple dopants, or the suppression of reduction behavior. Li2ZnGe3O8 (LZGO), with its interconnected tetrahedral [Li/ZnO4] and octahedral [GeO6] units, offers a stable three-dimensional framework that supports defect formation and Mn doping [38−40]. While earlier reports noted that Mn2+ in LZGO was not completely reduced even in a reducing atmosphere [41], a comprehensive understanding of how intrinsic defects mediate Mn4+/Mn2+ coexistence and site-specific luminescence is still lacking.

In this work, we systematically investigated the defect-driven self-reduction mechanism and site-selective dual emission in Mn-doped LZGO phosphors. As shown schematically in Fig. 1, interstitial oxygen and Zn vacancies promote the partial reduction of Mn4+ to Mn2+, with Mn2+ preferentially occupying tetrahedral Zn sites and Mn4+ substituting octahedral Ge sites. This spatial separation enables selective visible and NIR emission under different excitations. The LZGO:*x*Mn (*x* = 0–0.006) system thus offers a robust and scalable platform for dual-emissive phosphors without the need for hazardous reducing atmospheres, showing strong potential for anticounterfeiting, biomedical imaging, and next-generation optoelectronic applications.

### 2.1 Preparation

To synthesize LZGO:*x*Mn phosphors with Mn doping concentrations ranging from *x* = 0–0.006, a high-temperature solid-state reaction method was employed. The starting materials included lithium carbonate (Li2CO3, AR), zinc oxide (ZnO, AR), germanium dioxide (GeO2, 99.99%), and manganese dioxide (MnO2, 99.99%), which were weighed according to stoichiometric ratios.

The materials were mixed with ethanol in an agate mortar and ground thoroughly for 30 min to achieve homogeneity. The homogenized mixture was then transferred to an electric tube furnace and calcined at 1000 °C for 3 h in an air atmosphere. After the reaction was complete, the samples were allowed to cool to room temperature before being regenerated into the fine powder for further testing.

### 2.2 Characterizations

X-ray diffraction was performed via a diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5418 Å) at 40 kV and 40 mA to determine the phase purity. The Rietveld refinement of the crystal structure was conducted via general structure analysis system (GSAS) software. The scanning range for XRD was from 10° to 130° in 2*θ* with a step size of 0.02 (°)/s. The surface morphology and microstructure of the phosphors were observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM) with a JSM-7610F instrument. Additionally, elemental mapping and distribution analysis were conducted via energy‒dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) to confirm the uniform distribution of Mn and other elements. Ultraviolet‒visible (UV‒vis) diffuse reflectance spectroscopy was performed on a spectrophotometer (SolidSpec-3700, Shimadzu) to analyze the optical absorption properties of the phosphors.

Photoluminescence (PL) and photoluminescence excitation (PLE) spectra were recorded via a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F4700, Hitachi) equipped with a 150 W xenon lamp as the excitation source. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was carried out on an electron spectrometer (ESCALABXi800, Thermo Scientific) to determine the surface chemical states and elemental composition of the Mn in the samples, providing insight into the self-reducing behavior and valence states of the Mn ions. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were performed via an X-band spectrometer (EMXplus, Bruker) operating at a microwave frequency of 9.75 GHz. Raman spectra were obtained via a Nicolet Almega dispersive Raman microscope (Lab-RAM HR Evolution, HORIBA) with a 532 nm excitation wavelength, allowing for the investigation of the molecular structures and vibrational modes within the phosphor materials. Each sample was freshly prepared for individual measurements, ensuring accuracy and consistency across the characterization methods.

### 2.3 Anti-counterfeiting and NIR-display applications

The “LZGO” plate is a 2 mm deep 3D printed object made of PLA light-curing resin with an LZGO:0.003Mn (LZGOM0.003) phosphor in the grooves. The LZGOM0.003 phosphor was integrated onto a commercial 490 nm LED chip to form an NIR pc-LED. Photos taken in daylight and at different wavelengths were taken with a digital camera. NIR imaging images were taken with a Canon EOS M100 instrument equipped with a 720 nm longpass filter.

### 3.1 Crystal structure and micromorphology

The LZGO phosphor crystallizes in a spinel-type structure composed of interconnected tetrahedral and octahedral units (Fig. 2(a)). Specifically, Li and Zn ions occupy the 8c sites, forming [Li/ZnO4] tetrahedra, whereas additional Li+ ions are located at the 4b sites in [LiO6] octahedra. Ge4+ ions reside at the 12d positions, forming [GeO6] octahedra, which connect through shared edges and vertices, constructing a stable three-dimensional framework. This architecture facilitates defect accommodation and supports subsequent doping strategies [42].

Rietveld refinement confirmed that LZGO adopts a cubic phase (space group *P*4332) with unit cell parameters (*a* = *b* = *c* = 8.1924 Å, *α* = *β* = *γ* = 90°, and *V* = 549.836 Å3), as shown in Fig. 2(b). The Raman spectra (Fig. 2(c)) reveal characteristic vibrations of the spinel lattice, including lattice modes (−1), O-cation-O bending (200–350 cm−1), and stretching vibrations of [GeO6], [LiO4/ZnO4], and [LiO6] units, confirming the structural integrity of the samples [43,44].

Upon Mn doping (*x* = 0–0.006), the XRD patterns remain phase pure, with no secondary phases detected (Fig. 2(d)), indicating excellent structural stability. The peak shifts to lower angles with increasing Mn concentration, reflecting lattice expansion due to the substitution of Zn2+ (0.6 Å) by the slightly larger Mn2+ (0.66 Å) at tetrahedral sites. Moreover, Mn4+ is inferred to substitute Ge4+ at octahedral sites on the basis of its identical valence state (+4), which matches the ionic radii in sixfold coordination (both 0.53 Å), and previous reports of Mn4+ favoring octahedral occupancy in spinel-related lattices [45]. The lattice parameter variation as a function of the Mn content is plotted in Fig. 2(e), supporting the proposed site-selective occupation. Rietveld refinement plots for all doped samples are provided in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), with the corresponding structural parameters summarized in Table S1 in the ESM.

The surface morphology of LZGOM0.003, as observed via SEM (Fig. 2(f)), displays irregular particles smaller than 10 μm. Elemental mappings (Fig. 2(g)) confirmed the uniform spatial distribution of Mn, Zn, Ge, and O throughout the lattice, verifying the successful incorporation of the Mn dopants. Li is not visibly detected because of its low characteristic X-ray energy.

### 3.2 Self-reduction and dual-emission properties

The UV‒Vis diffuse reflectance spectra (DRS) of the LZGO:*x*Mn (*x* = 0–0.006) phosphors (Fig. 3(a)) exhibit absorption bands at ~210, 250, 305, 390, and 530 nm. These results are attributed to host lattice absorption, Mn2+–O2− and Mn4+–O2− charge transfer transitions, and the characteristic d–d transitions of Mn4+ (4A2 → 4T1 and 4A2 → 4T2). As the Mn concentration increases, the Mn4+-related features become more pronounced, indicating the effective incorporation of Mn ions and suggesting the occurrence of self-reduction.

*

(a) UV diffuse reflectance spectra of LZGO:x*Mn (*x* = 0–0.006) phosphors; (b) PL and PLE spectra of LZGO, Mn2+ and Mn4+ in LZGOM0.003 phosphors, respectively; (c) Tanabe–Sugano energy-level diagram of Mn2+ ions; (d) Decay curves of LZGOM0.003 phosphor for 245 and 305 nm excitation where *λ*ex denotes the excitation wavelength; (e) Tanabe–Sugano energy-level diagram of Mn4+ ions; (f) PL spectra under 242 nm excitation, showing Mn2+ emission (~525 nm); (g) PL spectra under 305 nm excitation, showing Mn4+ emission (~825 nm); (h) Dependence of Mn2+ and Mn4+ emission intensities on Mn concentration, where green emission corresponds to Mn2+ and NIR emission to Mn4+, respectively.

The photoluminescence excitation (PLE) and emission (PL) spectra (Fig. 3(b)) further confirmed the dual-emission behavior. The LZGO host exhibits broad emission at ~410 nm under 242 nm excitation, originating from donor‒acceptor recombination of intrinsic defects [46,47]. Upon Mn doping, strong green emission at ~525 nm appears in LZGOM0.003, corresponding to the 4T1(4G) → 6A1(6S) transition of Mn2+ in tetrahedral coordination. Its excitation spectrum shows a broad band at 242 nm (CTB) and several weaker peaks at 360–444 nm, arising from d-d transitions to higher excited states.

In contrast, Mn4+ exhibits broad, asymmetric NIR emission centered at ~825 nm under 305 nm excitation, assigned to the spin-forbidden 2E → 4A2 transition. Its PLE spectrum shows distinct bands at ~305, 390, and 530 nm. Although this transition is parity and spin forbidden under ideal octahedral symmetry, it becomes partially allowed owing to spin‒orbit coupling and vibronic relaxation, especially in distorted or strained octahedral environments. According to the classification framework proposed by Adachi [48], such a spectral profile is typical of type-B oxide phosphors. These materials exhibit a broad red emission band without a distinct zero‒phonon line or vibronic structure, resulting from Mn4+ ions occupying multiple octahedral coordination sites in cubic lattices. The diversity in local environments and the presence of crystal field distortions lead to energy level broadening and enhanced phonon sideband features [49]. In the case of LZGO, which possesses a cubic spinel structure with [GeO6] octahedra, Mn4+ likely substitutes for Ge4+ at these sites, resulting in the observed broadband NIR emission, which is consistent with type-B behavior. The spectral differences between Mn2+ and Mn4+ reflect their respective coordination environments and electronic configurations.

To further understand the crystal field environments, the crystal field strength (*D*q) and Racah parameters (*B* and *C*) were calculated for Mn2+ on the basis of the observed transitions 6A1 → 4E(4D), 4A1/4E(4G), and 4T2(4G). The calculated values were *D*q = 818.42 cm−1, *B* = 606.9 cm−1, and *C* = 3492.1 cm−1, with a *D*q/*B* ratio of 1.35, indicating a weak tetrahedral field (Fig. 3(c)). For Mn4+, the *D*q/*B* ratio derived from the excitation transitions ((4A2 → 4T1, 4T2) was 2.87, corresponding to a strong octahedral field (Fig. 3(e)). This result is consistent with the expected crystal field environments. The calculation methods and expressions are detailed in Eqs. S(1)–S(8) in the ESM [50].

The fluorescence decay curves of LZGOM0.003 recorded at 242 and 305 nm excitations reveal different average lifetimes for Mn2+ and Mn4+ emission: 3.63 and 0.32 ms, respectively (Fig. 3(d)). The average lifetime (*τ*avg) was obtained by fitting with a double-exponential function (Eq. (1)):

(1) τavg=A1τ12+A2τ22A1τ1+A2τ2

where *A*1 and *A*2 are amplitude coefficients and *τ*1 and *τ*2 are the fast and slow decay components, respectively. This lifetime difference further supports the coexistence of dual valence states and their site-selective emission properties. The dependence of the luminescence intensity on the Mn concentration is shown in Figs. 3(f)–3(h). The emission intensity of Mn2+ increases with doping and reaches a maximum at *x* = 0.003, beyond which quenching effects are observed due to increased Mn‒Mn interactions. The number of Mn4+ emission peaks gradually increases with further doping. This discrepancy is attributed to differences in their respective site occupations and electronic configurations. The critical distance (*R*c) for energy transfer quenching was calculated as 35.25 Å via the Blasse model (Eq. S(9) in the ESM), suggesting a multipolar interaction mechanism [51].

### 3.3 Self-reduction mechanism of Mn<sup>4+</sup> to Mn<sup>2+</sup>

To elucidate the valence states of Mn in LZGO:*x*Mn (*x* = 0.001–0.006), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on the representative sample LZGOM0.003. The full-spectrum XPS scan (Fig. 4(a)) confirmed the presence of matrix elements (Li, Zn, Ge, O) and the doped Mn species. The high-resolution Mn 2p spectra (Fig. 4(b)) show peaks at 640.9 and 653.9 eV, attributed to Mn2+ 2p3/2 and 2p1/2, respectively, and peaks at 643.3 and 657.1 eV, corresponding to Mn4+ 2p3/2 and 2p1/2, respectively. In addition to the XPS and structural analyses, the EPR spectrum exhibited six hyperfine peaks with a *g* factor of 2.027 (Fig. S2 in the ESM), which was attributed to the interaction between the unpaired electron cloud and the 55Mn nucleus with spin *I* = 5/2 [52]. This provides direct validation of the incorporation of Mn2+ into the lattice. These results confirm the coexistence of Mn2+ and Mn4+, supporting the occurrence of self-reduction during synthesis.

Oxygen vacancies play a crucial role in enabling this redox process [53]. The high-resolution O 1sspectra of the undoped and Mn-doped samples (Figs. 4(c) and 4(d)) reveal two deconvoluted peaks at 530.48 and 531.68 eV, corresponding to lattice oxygen and interstitial oxygen-related defects ( Oi′′), respectively [54,55]. After Mn incorporation, the intensity of the  Oi′′ peak increases significantly, suggesting that Mn doping enhances defect formation. These  Oi′′ defects can act as electron donors, transferring electrons to Mn4+ and promoting its reduction to Mn2+. This is further corroborated by the observed increase in the unit cell volume, which is indicative of charge compensation and lattice distortion associated with defect formation [29].

The XPS results provide compelling evidence that Mn4+ → Mn2+ reduction occurs spontaneously under ambient synthesis conditions. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 4(e), the self-reduction mechanism involves a nonequivalent substitution process, in which one Mn4+ ion replaces two Zn2+ ions in tetrahedral sites. This charge imbalance leads to the formation of dipole-like defect complexes comprising negatively charged cation vacancies ( VZn′′) and positively charged vacancy  (MnZn⋅⋅) defects, as described by the charge compensation reaction in Eq. (2) [56]. The  Oi′′ species serve as additional electron donors, transferring electrons to Mn4+ and stabilizing Mn2+.

Furthermore, the rigid three-dimensional spinel framework of LZGO provides sufficient space to accommodate oxygen-related defects. During high-temperature solid-state synthesis, Zn and Ge volatilization may further contribute to the formation of  VZn′′ and  VGe vacancies. Upon thermal activation, electrons from these donor defects can be transferred to adjacent Mn4+ centers, enabling their reduction [57]. The dynamic interaction of these defects release, migration, and electron trapping facilitates continuous Mn4+ → Mn2+ conversion [58]. The complete charge compensation and reduction mechanism can be summarized by Reactions (2)–(6):

(2) MnO2→ZnOMnZn⋅⋅+VZn′′+2OO×

(3) MnO2→ZnOMnZn⋅⋅+OO×+Oi′′

(4) VZn′′→VZn×+2e

(5) Oi′′→OO×+2e

(6) 2e+MnZn⋅⋅→MnZn×

These results highlight the critical role of oxygen defects and charge imbalance in driving self-reduction, enabling the heterovalent coexistence of Mn2+/Mn4+ without external reducing agents. This mechanism not only validates the dual-emission behavior described above but also provides a design strategy for developing multifunctional phosphors with tunable redox chemistry and structural adaptability.

### 3.4 Anti-counterfeiting and NIR-display applications

The excitation-dependent dual-emission behavior of LZGO:*x*Mn (*x* = 0.001–0.006) phosphors offers a promising strategy for optical anticounterfeiting and NIR display applications. By selectively activating Mn2+ and Mn4+ emission under different excitation wavelengths, multilevel optical responses can be achieved, enabling secure and versatile information encoding.

To demonstrate this potential, a 3D-printed pattern spelling “LZGO” was fabricated using a PLA-based light-curing resin filled with the LZGOM0.003 phosphor. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the pattern appears faintly pink under daylight. Upon irradiation with 254 nm UV light, strong green luminescence from Mn2+ becomes visible (Fig. 5(b)). However, when excited at 310 nm, the pattern is visually undetectable with a standard camera (Fig. 5(c)), whereas an NIR-sensitive camera reveals a clear emission signal from Mn4+ (Fig. 5(d)). This excitation wavelength-dependent visibility provides a multidimensional security feature that is difficult to replicate.

In addition to static anticounterfeiting, LZGOM0.003 was also integrated into a commercial 490 nm LED chip to fabricate an NIR phosphor-converted LED (NIR pc-LED). The constructed device maintains stable NIR emission across a wide operating current range (20–320 mA), with minimal visible light leakage, as shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. When coupled with a 590 nm longpass filter, the visible component is effectively suppressed, yielding an isolated NIR output.

To quantitatively assess its photoluminescence efficiency in device applications, we measured the internal quantum efficiency (IQE) and external quantum efficiency (EQE) of the LZGOM0.003 phosphor under 305 nm excitation. IQE reached ~72.3%, and EQE was ~32.8%. Furthermore, the pc-LED was evaluated in a simulated night vision scenario to demonstrate real-world functionality. Under complete darkness, conventional cameras fail to capture structural details. However, illumination with the NIR pc-LED enabled clear visualization of the hidden patterns and objects via a 720 nm-filtered NIR camera (Figs. 5(e)–5(g)). This underscores the utility of LZGO:x*Mn in low-light surveillance, remote sensing, and biomedical imaging.

Taken together, the integration of self-reducing Mn2+/Mn4+ dual-emissive phosphors into both static and dynamic platforms highlights their multifunctionality. The combination of stable NIR output, high efficiency, and tunable excitation makes LZGO:*x*Mn a promising candidate for next-generation anticounterfeiting technologies and optoelectronic components.

## 4 Conclusions

In conclusion, this study systematically investigated the self-reduction mechanism and dual-emission behavior of Mn-doped Li2ZnGe3O8 (LZGO:*x*Mn, *x* = 0.001–0.006) spinel phosphors synthesized via a high-temperature solid-state route. Structural and spectroscopic analyses confirmed the coexistence of Mn2+ and Mn4+ oxidation states, enabled by oxygen vacancies and lattice defects that drive intrinsic charge compensation and spontaneous Mn4+ → Mn2+ conversion. The materials exhibit distinct visible and near-infrared emissions corresponding to the Mn2+ and Mn4+ centers, respectively, with clearly differentiated decay lifetimes (3.63 vs. 0.32 ms) under selective excitation. This dual-emission functionality, achieved without external reducing agents, demonstrates excellent optical tunability and environmental compatibility. By integrating LZGO:*x*Mn phosphors into anticounterfeiting patterns and NIR pc-LEDs, this study further validates their application potential in secure encoding, night vision, and optoelectronic devices. In addition to performance realization, this research introduces a defect engineering-driven strategy for designing multifunctional redox-active luminescent materials. Taken together, these findings provide both mechanistic insights and design guidance for developing the next generation of energy-efficient, environmentally friendly, and adaptable phosphors.
