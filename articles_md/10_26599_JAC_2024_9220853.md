## 1 Introduction

With the rapid development of near-field communication (NFC) technology, computers, mobile phones, tablets, and other devices can be conveniently connected wirelessly, thereby further realizing data exchange and services [1,2]. With more and more applications of NFC in mobile communication devices, the signal attenuation caused by metal parts cannot be ignored. A layer of soft magnetic materials between the label and the metal is necessary to reduce the eddy current loss caused by the metal part inside and outside the devices [3]. The addition of soft magnetic materials can generate a coupling magnetic field and reduce the attenuation of the metal layer [4−6].

The materials used as near-field antenna substrates mainly include bulk ferrites, nanocrystalline/amorphous, as well as some composite materials of magnetic powders and polymers. The real part of the permeability of nanocrystalline/amorphous can be as high as 2000 and has higher saturation magnetization. However, for the NFC technology applied to 13.56 MHz (according to the ECMA340/ISO18092 standard), when considering the temperature effect, bulk ferrites have better temperature stability and lower loss at a high frequency at low flux density, compared with nanocrystalline [7−9]. The composite materials prepared by mixing high-permeability magnetic powders into a polymer matrix have the advantages of a simple preparation process and stable magnetic properties. However, when magnetic powders are filled into a polymer matrix, there is a big gap between the magnetic particles, which will result in low permeability [10]. At the frequency of 13.56 MHz, the real part of the permeability of most composite materials is generally in the range from 20 to 200, usually corresponding to a large magnetic loss, which is also difficult to apply in NFC antennas [11,12].

On the other hand, among the various soft magnetic ferrites, Ni–Zn ferrites have excellent performance in MHz frequency band due to their high resistivity and appropriate permeability [13]. Thus they have great potential in high-frequency applications, such as inductance cores, various antennas, and coupling transformers. In particular, Ni–Zn ferrites have been widely used as NFC antenna substrates [14−16]. For an NFC antenna system, the main aim is to obtain high magnetic field coupling efficiency, which is positively related to the inductance. Also, the attenuation of inductance mainly depends on the current loss (induced current and displacement current) and magnetic loss of the substrate from the electron exchange interaction between Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions. Therefore, lower *µ''* and *ε''* (the imaginary part of permeability and permittivity, respectively) are required while maintaining high initial permeability [17]. However, the dielectric loss of Ni–Zn ferrites is much higher than that of many dielectric materials, and the saturation magnetization also needs to be further improved [18−20]. Fortunately, in literature [21−28], it has been reported that the dielectric loss of Ni–Zn ferrites can be reduced by doping with some transition metal ions, such as Cr, Mn, Cu, W, and rare earth (RE) metals. Specifically, the doping of RE ions has been widely adopted as an important method for modifying various ferrites. By introducing RE ions into the spinel lattice, the RE–Fe interactions appear (3d–4f coupling), which can lead to the improvement of many properties of ferrites [29−32]. And a small amount of RE ions doping can adjust the properties of ferrites, such as electric, magnetic, and structural properties, thus meeting various applications [33−35]. Reasonably, some scientists have also worked on modifying Ni–Zn ferrites through the doping of RE ions. For example, Kumar *et al.* [36] synthesized a group of Ce3+ ion substituted ferrites with a chemical formula of Ni0.2Zn0.8Ce*x*Fe2−*x*O4 (*x* = 0–0.04) using self-combustion chemistry. No secondary phase was found in their samples, and as the concentration of Ce3+ ions increased, the magnetic moment of the resultant ferrites increased. Therefore, such ferrites may be outstanding core materials for electronic devices like transformers, telecommunications, and high-frequency applications. Also, a literature survey indicated that RE ions doped Ni–Zn ferrites can serve as an excellent electrical insulator, which has the attracted special attention of many researchers in recent years [37]. In other words, the introduction of rare earth metals into Ni–Zn ferrites can significantly improve their dielectric properties.

In the various RE additives, Ho3+ ions have the second largest magnetic moment (10.4*μ*B). Due to its large ionic radius (0.97 Å), Ho3+ ions will occupy the octahedral sites in the ferrites. After the doping of Ho3+ ions, a higher saturation magnetization might be expected for the resultant ferrites. In literature, Ho3+ ions doped Ni–Zn ferrites were first reported by Ghafoor *et al.* [38]. They indicated that the doping of Ho3+ ions could enhance the DC electrical resistivity of Ni0.7Zn0.3Fe2O4 ferrites, which is of much significance for the application of microstrip antennas. However, in the work of Akhter *et al.* [39], it was found that the addition of Ho3+ ions would reduce the saturation magnetization of ferrites, which runs against the performance improvement of antennas. Also, this phenomenon was also confirmed by other literature [40,41]. In our previous work [42], we investigated the doping of Ho3+ ions into Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 ferrites. Our results indicate that the sample with a nominal composition of Ni0.5Zn0.5Ho*x*Fe2−*x*O4 has significantly low dielectric and magnetic loss at *x* = 0.2, while the highest Curie temperature is reached. However, our results also reveal that the saturation magnetization of the samples decreases gradually with increasing substitution levels of Ho3+ ions, which implies that the resultant Ni0.5Zn0.5Ho0.02Fe1.98O4 ferrites would also have a relatively low saturation magnetization.

To counteract the negative effects of Ho3+ ions doping on Ni–Zn ferrites for NFC antennas, extra measures should be adopted. After a comprehensive survey of literature, we found that the doping of the commonly available Co2+ additives could effectively enhance the saturation magnetization of Ni–Zn ferrites [43], and dielectric constant and dielectric loss tangent decrease with the increasing content of Co2+ ions in the doped Ni–Zn ferrites [44]. The doping of Co2+ ions can change the magnetization mechanism and thus enhance the cut-off frequency of Ni–Zn ferrites, because CoFe2O4 is ferrites with high magnetocrystalline anisotropy associated with Co atoms. In addition, Co2+ ions belong to magnetic ions, implying that there is no need to worry that the addition of Co2+ will deteriorate the saturation magnetization, initial permeability, and other magnetic parameters of ferrites. Hence, in this study, novel Ho3+ and Co2+ ions co-doped Ni–Zn ferrites with nominal chemical formula Ni0.5−*x*Zn0.5Ho0.02Co*x*Fe1.98O4 (where *x* = 0–0.2 with a step of 0.05) were designed and fabricated by a molten salt method on the basis of our previous optimal results on Ho3+ ions alone doped Ni–Zn ferrites [42], by applying analytical-grade metal nitrates as raw materials and KOH as the precipitation agent and molten salt precursor. The molten salt method can effectively reduce the synthesis temperature of ferrites and reduce the required reaction time [45−47]. As expected, the dielectric loss of Ni–Zn ferrites was considerably minished through the co-doping of Ho3+ and Co2+ ions, while the high saturation magnetization was almost preserved, in comparison with those of Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 ferrites. In particular, with *x* = 0.1, the Ho3+ and Co2+ co-doped Ni–Zn ferrites possess the highest Curie temperature, lowest dielectric constant, lowest magnetic and dielectric loss, rather high saturation magnetization, relatively low coercivity, and dramatically heightened cut-off frequency in spite of a slightly lowered permeability. Moreover, the acquired Ni–Zn ferrites have been simulated to apply in NFC antennas, revealing that the devices manufactured by the optimal Ni0.4Zn0.5Ho0.02Co0.1Fe1.98O4 ferrite ceramics would have significantly improved performance at a high frequency with lower leakage and longer transmission distance of magnetic field, compared with that fabricated by pure Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 ferrites. Therefore, the Ni0.4Zn0.5Ho0.02Co0.1Fe1.98O4 ferrite ceramics would be a good candidate for NFC antenna substrates.

### 2.1 Specimen fabrication

Figure 1 illustrates a chart for sample preparation. Under the optimal doping level of Ho3+ ions [42], a series of ferrite specimens with chemical formula Ni0.5−*x*Zn0.5Ho0.02Co*x*Fe1.98O4 (*x* = 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, and 0.2) were designed and fabricated by a molten salt method to optimize the doping level of Co2+ ions. The utilized raw materials involved in analytical-grade Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (99.0%), Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (99.0%), Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (99.0%), Ho(NO3)3·4H2O (99.9%), Co(NO3)2·6H2O (99.9%), and KOH (99.0%) powders, which were all supplied by Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. During the processing, all the raw powders were initially blended in a planetary ball mill and milled for 6 h with ethyl alcohol as the dispersion media and high-resistance ZrO2 balls as the grinding media. For the milling, the weight ratio of the raw materials, dispersion media (ethyl alcohol), and grinding media (ZrO2 balls) was 1 : 5 : 4, the volume of the jars was 1 L, and the rotational speed was 250 r·min−1. During the ball milling process, KOH reacts with metal nitrates to form a precipitate, and the reactant KNO3 will participate in the subsequent calcination process as the actual molten salt. After milling, the resultant slurry was dried for 2 h in an open oven at 80 ℃. After drying, the acquired powder chunks were mulled into powders, and then the resultant powders were calcined in a muffle furnace (KL-12, Luoyang Shenjia Kiln. Co., Ltd.) at 300 ℃ for 3 h with a heating rate of 10 ℃·min−1. After calcination, the attained chunks were crushed into powders and milled again under identical conditions for the first milling. The second milled slurry was cleaned with deionized water by centrifuging for at least 3 times to remove K+ ions in the mixture. Afterwards, the centrifuged precipitate was desiccated in the open oven at 120 ℃ for 24 h, and mulled into powders again after desiccating. Subsequently, the attained powders were squeezed into toroidal rings (*ϕ*10 mm in inner, *ϕ*20 mm in outer, and 3 mm in height), *ϕ*6 mm × 1 mm pellets, and *ϕ*12 mm × 1 mm tablets, for different measurements. Finally, in the muffle furnace, the specimens were sintered at 1200 ℃ for 4 h with a heating rate of 2 ℃·min−1. For comparison, Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 was also synthesized by the same method.

### 2.2 Material characterization

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/max-RB, Rigaku, Cu Kα, *λ* = 1.5418 Å) was utilized to examine the phase composition of the specimens in continuous scanning mode at a step of 0.02° and a scanning rate of 8 (°)·min−1. The XRD results were refined and the phase composition was quantitatively analyzed by the Topas software. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU8020, Hitachi) was applied to observe the microstructure on their polished and fracture surfaces, while their element compositions were measured by the attached energy dispersion X-ray (EDX) spectroscope. Then the grain size was statistically evaluated through the software Nano Measurer PC from the acquired SEM images. Also, the crystalline structure of the samples was further examined by a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM 2010F, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The chemical states of the elements were examined by an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCSLAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with non-monochromatic Al Kα radiation and a photon energy of 1486.7 eV. For the XPS characterization, the binding energies were corrected by referencing them to the C1s line (284.8 eV). The relative density was defined as the percentage of the apparent density to the theoretical density of the corresponding specimens. For its calculation, the apparent density of the specimens was measured by Archimedean method according to the international standard (ISO18754), and the theoretical density was estimated by the mixture rule. The surface area was evaluated by mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP; AutoPore IV 9520, Micromeritics). Thermogravimetric-differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC) thermal analysis of the prepared samples was performed on a Linseis thermal analyzer (STA PT1600, Linseis) from room temperature to 1400 ℃ at a heating rate of 2.5 ℃·min−1 in air flow. The Mössbauer spectrum was recorded on a SEE Co W304 Mössbauer spectrometer, which is characteristic of a 57Co/Rh point source with a diameter of 0.5 mm and a relative activity of 50 mCi. The iron absorption thickness was precisely maintained at a minimum of 10 mg·cm−2 to optimize the Mössbauer absorption efficiency for 57Fe. The measurements were carried out at room temperature using a constant acceleration drive system, and the spectrometer calibration was performed using standard iron foil to guarantee the accuracy of the recorded results. Data were analyzed using the sophisticated Moss Win 4.0 software, which is adept at processing and fitting Mössbauer spectrum data. By applying a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM; LakeShore 7307, Quantum Design) under a maximum magnetic field of 796,000 A·m−1, the hysteresis loops and temperature dependence of magnetization were acquired. The saturation magnetization (*M*s) and coercivity (*H*c) were then reckoned from the acquired hysteresis loops, and Curie temperature (*T*C) was estimated from the temperature dependence of magnetization. The complex permeability (*μ = μ' + μ''*) and dielectric constant (*ε = ε' + ε''*) were measured by an Agilent E4991A impedance analyzer in the frequency ranged from 1 to 1000 MHz. All these measurements except *T*C were executed at room temperature.

### 2.3 Modelling of NFC antennas

To explore the application of the obtained Ho3+ and Co2+ co-doped Ni–Zn ferrites in NFC antennas, the designed NFC antenna was simulated by the PC software CST Studio Suite 2019.

A balanced 13.56 MHz NFC coil antenna and its matching circuit are designed, which is shown in Fig. 2. In this balanced structure, currents mutually cancel their effects with net current flowing only on the antenna radiating element instead of the ground plane, so that the performance of the antenna is not compromised [48].

The designed NFC antenna is composed of a dielectric substrate and a rectangular coil. The above-acquired Ni–Zn ferrites were utilized as the substrate of the NFC antenna. The existence of a via hole is required by the balanced structure. The diameter of the via hole is 0.5 mm, and the height is 1.6 mm. During the simulation, the base pin of the chip with an impedance of 27 Ω is selected, and the impedance of the antenna pin is set at 50 Ω, which should be matched with an extra circuit. By designing the circuit in the software Smith Chart V3.10, the values of all circuit components can be read directly. Smith chart can directly obtain resistance and capacitance values that meet the impedance matching without the need for complex mathematical operations. The substrate thickness is 1.6 mm, and the length and width of the antenna are 47 and 34 mm, respectively. The wiring materials are pure copper, the thickness is 1 oz (0.035 µm), the width of the wiring is 1 mm, and the spacing between the turns is 0.25 mm. The simulation boundary is set to open, the background is set to normal, and the frequency range is 10–15 MHz. The solver adopts the frequency domain solver, and a monitor is set at 13.56 MHz to observe the electromagnetic field of the antenna at this frequency.

On the fresh fracture surface as shown by the SEM images in Fig. 5, the elemental compositions of the obtained Ni0.5−*x*Zn0.5Ho0.02Co*x*Fe1.98O4 (*x* = 0–0.2) ceramic samples were measured, and the results are shown in Table 2. On the fresh fracture surface of the typical sample Ni0.3Zn0.5Ho0.02Co0.2Fe1.98O4, the recorded SEM-EDX mapping images are shown in Fig. 5. In addition, the distribution of Fe atoms in the second phase is also significantly different (see Fig. 5(g)), which may be due to the lower percentage of Fe atoms in the foreign phase. Therefore, it is deduced that the foreign phase at the grain boundary may be of rare earth ferrites.

To further investigate the crystalline structure of the prepared samples, a TEM examination was carried out. Figure 6(a) shows a low-magnification TEM image of the typical sample Ni0.3Zn0.5Ho0.02Co0.2Fe1.98O4. Figure 6(b) presents a typical high-resolution image taken from the rectangle area, and the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern of the corresponding region is shown in Fig. 6(c). From Fig. 6(b), it can be clearly seen that the grains of the sample have clear lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of 0.31 nm, which is close to that of the (022) crystal plane of the undoped Ni–Zn ferrite phase (0.30 nm). Therefore, this plane can be indexed to the (022) crystal plane of ferrites, which is also confirmed by the SAED pattern in Fig. 6(c). As for the increase in crystal plane spacing from the undoped N–Zn ferrites, it can be attributed to the doping of Ho3+ and Co2+ with larger ionic radii into the ferrite lattices. Moreover, the present TEM results also confirm that the sample has good crystallinity.

Based on the above-mentioned results, the microstructure of the obtained ceramics can be explained as follows. After the doping of Ho3+ ions, the number of Ho3+ ions with high electronic valance would increase in the grain boundary region. To balance the electrical charges, the number of metallic ion vacancies would increase. Resultantly, the movement speed of the grain boundaries would be promoted [60,61]. Consequently, the grain growth would be expedited during the sintering process. Meanwhile, the mass transportation was accelerated. The higher mass transfer rate will result in faster grain growth and larger grain size. Meanwhile, the densification of the sample increases, and the porosity decreases. However, due to larger bond energy of Co2+–O2− than that of Ni2+–O2−, more energy is required to force Co2+ ions entering into the lattices to form Co2+–O2− bonds, after further co-doping of Co2+ to replace Ni2+ ions. Correspondingly, more energy is expended for the Co2+ ions substituted ferrites to complete the crystallization and growth of the grains. Consequently, the grain size would minify after the co-doping of Co2+ ions. Because the foreign HoFeO3 phase at the grain boundary will hinder the migration of grain boundary due to its pinning effect, the grain growth would be inhibited, finally resulting in continuously decreasing grain size. Moreover, the mass transportation was also impeded because of the co-doping of Co2+ ions, which ran against the densification of ferrite ceramics. As a result, with the increase of the concentration *x*, the co-doping of Co2+ ions leads to the increase of the porosity gradually.

The raising in relative density (densification) after the doping of Ho3+ ions could be attributed to the improved sinterability due to the promoted movement speed of grain boundaries and thus accelerated mass transportation as discussed above [60]. In combination with the formation of the foreign phase (HoFeO3) with higher density compared to pure Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 ferrites, the apparent density of the specimens would definitely be enlarged. On the other hand, the falling in the relative density of the specimens after the co-doping of Co ions could be ascribed to the impeded mass transportation during the sintering process. Accordingly, the apparent density of Ho3+ and Co2+ ions co-doped specimens would decline with enhancing the doping level of Co2+ ions.

### 3.2 Magnetic properties

Mössbauer analysis was performed to investigate the demonstration of the doped ions. The recorded Mössbauer spectra of the typical sample Ni0.5−*x*Zn0.5Ho0.02Co*x*Fe1.98O4 (*x* = 0 and 0.2) are shown in Fig. 10. Comparatively, that of pure Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 is also displayed. In addition, the corresponding Mössbauer parameters are listed in Table 4. The black dots and red lines represent the experimental (ex.) and calculated (cal.) data of the investigated ferrites, respectively. The two sextets observed in Fig. 10 correspond to the A (blue lines) and B sites (green lines) of Fe3+ ions in the ferrite lattice [63]. Table 4 shows that the isomer shift value of the A sites is smaller than that of the B sites. Because the Fe3+–O2− bond stretching at the B sites is higher than that at the A sites, the orbital ion overlap of Fe3+ at the B sites is smaller, which will result in a larger isomer shift value of the B sites than that of the A sites [64]. Also, the isomer shift values of all the samples are less than 0.5, indicating that all the iron ions are present as Fe3+, since the isomer shift value of Fe2+ is greater than 0.5 [65,66]. Moreover, the observed negligible quadruple splitting values suggest the presence of a symmetric environment around iron nuclei in ferrites [67]. In addition, the magnetic moments presented in tetrahedrons and octahedrons of the ferrite sublattice generate the hyperfine magnetic field of the sample [66]. When only Ho3+ is doped, the hyperfine magnetic field at the A sites increases while the hyperfine magnetic field at the B sites decreases. This can be explained that when Ho3+ occupies the octahedral B positions, they will cause some Fe3+ ions that originally occupied the octahedron to rearrange and occupy the A sites. Because the magnetic moment of Ho3+ is very large (10.4*μ*B), the hyperfine magnetic field values of both B and A sites will increase. The calculated relative area confirms this statement. From Table 4, it can be seen that the relative area of Fe3+ ions at the B site decreases after the doping of Ho3+ ions, while the area at the A site increases. This result indicates that Ho3+ occupies the B site and Fe3+ instead occupies the tetrahedral A site. Similarly, when Co2+ entered the sublattice, the hyperfine magnetic field value of the B site increased due to the higher magnetic moment of Co2+ (3*μ*B) than that of Ni2+ (2*μ*B). The relative area of the B site decreases slightly, indicating that a small part of Co2+ occupies the octahedral position where originally Fe3+ ions were located [68]. There are many factors that can affect the line width of the samples, such as occupancy of ions, thermal motion of atoms, relaxation effects, and even thickness of the samples. Here in this work, the addition of Co2+ and Ho3+ ions into the Ni–Zn ferrites caused the dislocation of cations inside the sub-lattices, thereby affecting the hyperfine field distribution over the A and B sites [69], which may be the reason for the change in Mössbauer line widths in Table 4.

Declined *M*s was observed in Ni–Zn ferrites after the doping of Ho3+ ions [38,42]. However, when Co2+ ions joined in, *M*s would boost with the designed, enhancing doping level of *x* from 0 to 0.2. As is well known, *M*s of Ni–Zn ferrites can be expressed by Eq. (1) [70]:

(1) Ms=|MB−MA|

where *M*B is the magnetic moment in octahedral B sites, and *M*A is that in tetrahedral A sites. In Ni–Zn ferrites, Zn2+ ions inhabit the A sites, Ni2+ ions have a preference for the B site, but Fe3+ ions occupy both A and B sites [71]. Therefore, the magnetic moment of the B sites in Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 ferrites is higher than that of A sites, because the magnetic moments of Fe3+, Ni2+, and Zn2+ ions are 5*μ*B, 2*μ*B, and 0, respectively. Because RE Ho3+ ions have a large ionic radius, they prefer to occupy the octahedral B sites. Thus, Fe3+ ions, which originally occupied part of the B sites, would instead occupy the tetrahedral A sites after the doping of Ho3+ ions [19]. Since the original Zn2+ at the A sites is non-magnetic, such a rearrangement of ions would increase the value of *M*A. Moreover, the foreign phase HoFeO3 formed during the sintering process with a feeble saturation magnetic moment of about 0.05*μ*B per formula unit [42]. Eventually, with the addition of Ho3+, *M*s will decrease. However, when Ni2+ ions with a smaller magnetic moment (2*μ*B) in the B sites were superseded by Co2+ ions with a higher magnetic moment (3*μ*B), the magnetic moment of the B sites would be increased while the magnetic moment of the A sites would remain constant. Resultantly, the *M*s value of the Ho3+ and Co2+ ions co-doped ferrite specimens gradually went up with enhancing the doping level of Co2+ ions.

*H*c of the acquired ferrites enhanced after the doping of Ho 3+ ions, and maintained rising when Co2+ ions were co-doped. *H*c of crystallites with a size larger than the critical size of single domain depends on the law of 1/*D* as Eq. (2) [72−74]:

(2) Hc=Pc⋅A⋅K1Ms⋅D

where *P*c is the dimensionless factor, *A* is the exchange constant, *K*1 is the magnetocrystalline anisotropy constant, and *D* is the grain size. It is known that there are two mechanisms of magnetization: the domain wall movement and the domain rotation, in which the first one needs less energy [34]. In the acquired ferrites, the existence of the foreign HoFeO3 phase will engender a higher anisotropy field to push the domain walls. Therefore, the doping of Ho3+ ions will lead to the increase of *H*c from 174.133 to 244.197 A·m−1 [38]. Also, the *H*c value would maintain rising after the co-doping of Co2+ ions due to their even higher crystalline anisotropy energy [75]. In addition, the *H*c value of the acquired ferrites also depends on their grain size. The smaller the grain size, the larger the *H*c value, because the reduction in grain size will increase the number of grain boundaries and inhibit the displacement of a domain wall. Thus, the declined grain size of the acquired ferrites after the co-doping of Co2+ ions would also contribute to the enhancement of *H*c.

The *T*C value is a very vital property for ferrites, which defines the highest operating temperature. It can be seen from Table 5 that the *T*C value of the acquired ferrites would be heightened after the doping of Ho3+ ions. However, after Co2+ ions were co-doped into ferrites, the *T*C value enhanced initially, and then declined slightly when additional Co2+ ions joined in. According to Neel theory, there are three exchange interactions (A–A, A–B, and B–B) among the cations in the A and B sublattices, in which the A–B interaction is the predominant one. Also, in the A–B interaction, the magnetic moments are antiparallel to each other [76]. When Ho3+ ions were doped into the Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 ferrites, Fe3+ ions were superseded by Ho3+ ions, while some Fe3+ and Ni2+ ions were driven into the A sites as stated above, enhancing the quantity of magnetic ions in the A sites. Therefore, the A–B exchange effect in ferrites would be increased [77]. Consequently, the *T*C value of the acquired ferrites (Ni0.5Zn0.5Ho0.02Fe1.98O4) would hoist after the doping of Ho3+ ions. With the further doping of Co2+ ions into the Ni0.5Zn0.5Ho0.02Fe1.98O4 ferrites, the quantity of magnetic ions in the B sites was augmented. Resultantly, the A–B exchange effect in the acquired Ho3+ and Co2+ ions co-doped ferrites would be heightened. Consequently, the *T*C value of ferrites went up after the co-doping of Ho3+ and Co2+ ions, and the maximum value reached 305 ℃. However, the *T*C value of the acquired Ho3+ and Co2+ ions co-doped ferrites would decline slightly with additional Co2+ ions. This is because in such cases, some Fe3+ ions would be superseded by the additional Co2+ ions, which will cause a weaker Fe–Co exchange effect compared with the Fe–Fe effect, finally producing decreased *T*C [78].

Consequently, *μ'* continues to decrease with the increasing doping level of Co2+ ions.

As for tan*δ*m, the specimen doped with only Ho3+ ions has a significant reduction in the range of 1–100 MHz compared to pure Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 ferrites. After further doping of Co2+ ions, the tan*δ*m value decreases significantly in the 1–100 MHz band, reaching the minimum when *x* = 0.10. When the frequency range is above 100 MHz, both *x* = 0.10 and 0.15 samples exhibit low tan*δ*m values. The tan*δ*m value of ferrites can be defined by Eqs. (6) and (7) [91]:

(6) tanδm=μ″/μ′

(7) tanδm=tan⁡δe+tan⁡δh+tan⁡δc

where *μ''* and *μ'* respectively represent the imaginary and real parts of the complex permeability; and tan*δ*e*,* tan*δ*h*,* and tan*δ*c are the eddy current loss tangent, the hysteresis loss tangent, and the residual loss tangent, respectively. Eddy current loss is the loss caused by the heating of a device due to the induced current, which is inversely proportional to the resistivity of the materials. Typical soft ferrites such as Ni–Zn ferrites have negligible eddy current loss due to their high electrical resistivity. The hysteresis loss is the loss caused by magnetic hysteresis phenomenon, and the hysteresis loss of magnetized materials per unit volume is equal to the energy corresponding to the area of the hysteresis loop. In this work, because the *H*c value of all the acquired ferrites is less than 875 A·m−1, their hysteresis loss can also be ignored [87]. The residual loss includes all the losses except eddy current loss and hysteresis loss, which is positively correlated with the concentration of Fe2+ ions in ferrites. In the obtained specimens, both Ho3+ and Co2+ ions will replace Fe atoms in the lattices, which will inhibit the behavior of Fe2+ ⇔ Fe3+ in the ferrites [90]. Therefore, with the increasing doping level of Ho3+ and Co2+ ions, the tan*δ*m value shows an obvious downward trend.

As an important parameter, *f*r represents the upper limit of the applicable frequency of soft ferrites. As can be seen in Table 5, the *f*r value of all the acquired specimens in this work is greater than 13.56 MHz, which can meet the requirement of an NFC antenna. Moreover, with the increasing doping level of Co2+ ions, the *f*r value keeps rising, finally reaching 148 MHz for the designed Ni0.3Zn0.5Co0.2Ho0.02Fe1.98O4 ferrites with the highest doping level of Co2+ ions. For the samples only doped with Ho3+, because *f*r is very sensitive to the magnetocrystalline anisotropy, and the doping of Ho3+ can increase the anisotropy constant [92], so the spin magnetization is the main way of magnetization, which will result in increased *f*r. Similarly, because the doping of Co2+ ions will also make the magnetization of the domain walls more difficult, spin magnetization thus becomes the dominant mode for the magnetization of the present ferrites [93]. Also, as is well known, the low-frequency behavior of the permeability for ferrites is controlled by domain-wall motion, while at higher frequencies it is the spin rotation that influences the permeability [94]. Therefore, the *f*r value of the ferrites will increase with the increasing doping level of Ho3+ and Co2+ ions.

### 3.3 Dielectric properties

The dielectric constant (*ε'*) and dielectric loss tangent (tan*δ*) as a function of the frequency for the as-obtained Ni–Zn ferrites doped with different contents of Ho3+ and Co2+ ions are plotted in Fig. 14. The tan*δ* value is a crucial property for soft ferrites and can be represented by *ε''/ε'*. From Fig. 14, it can be seen that as the frequency increases, the dielectric constant (*ε'*) of all the samples decreases, which becomes stable when the frequency falls in the range of high frequency. Such behavior can be explained by Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization theory [95,96]. For many electronic ceramics, the dielectric behavior in low-frequency ranges can be ascribed to the space charge effect. Also, the main contribution comes from the electron hopping between Fe2+ and Fe3+. When the frequency increases, the frequency of electron hopping cannot keep up with the frequency of the applied electric field, resulting in weakened polarization and exhibiting a behavior of high dielectric constant at low frequencies and low dielectric constant at high frequencies. At 13.56 MHz of the NFC application, compared with that of the pure Ni–Zn ferrites, the tan*δ* values of the doped samples are also significantly lower. By examining the changes of *ε'* and tan*δ* with the doping level, it can be seen that the introduction of Ho3+ ions alone could significantly reduce their values. Also, this conclusion was also confirmed by other literature [38,42]. After the co-doping of Co2+ ions, both of them continue to decline somewhat, when the doping level of *x* is no more than 0.1. However, when more Co2+ ions were added, the *ε´* and tan*δ* values of the acquired ferrites would be enlarged.

*
Fig. 14Frequency dependence of (a) dielectric constant (ε´*) and (b) dielectric loss tangent (tan*δ*) for as-obtained Ni0.5−*x*Zn0.5Ho0.02Co*x*Fe1.98O4 (*x* = 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, and 0.2) specimens. Comparatively, those of pure Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 are also displayed.

According to Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization theory, the substitution of Ho3+ ions for Fe3+ ions at the B sites, would cause a lattice expansion as mentioned above. As a result of the substitution, the hopping rate of the electron transfer between Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions will be inhibited owing to the reduced amount of Fe3+ ions, which is responsible for the conduction mechanism of ferrites. Moreover, because the formation of the foreign HoFeO3 phase could also generate an insulating intergranular layer on the grain boundaries of ferrites, a reduced space charge polarization is expected to decrease the dielectric constant. Consequently, the *ε´* and tan*δ* values would be diminished as expected.

Furthermore, because the electrical conductivity of the grain boundary is much higher than that of the grains in soft ferrites, the electrical conductivity of ferrites is primarily determined by the number of grain boundaries. The more the grain boundaries, the higher the electrical conductivity. After Co2+ ions were co-doped into the Ni0.5Zn0.5Ho0.02Fe1.98O4 ferrites, the grain became smaller. Resultantly, the number of grain boundaries will be increased. Therefore, the *ε'* and tan*δ* values of the acquired ferrites would continue to drop down to some extent. However, when the doping level *x* is more than 0.1, the *ε´* and tan*δ* values went up slightly. This can be attributed to the decrease in density. The tan*δ* value of ferrites can be expressed as Eq. (8) [97]:

(8) tan⁡δ=(1−P)⋅tan⁡δ0+C⋅Pn

where tan*δ*0 is the dielectric loss of the densely structured sample, *P* is the sample porosity, and *C* and *n* are a constant. As the porosity increases, the tan*δ* value will increase. In our work, when the doping concentration is greater than 0.1, the density of the sample decreases, resulting in an increase in the tan*δ* value.

### 3.4 Performance of NFC antennas

In combination with the magnetic and dielectric properties of the acquired Ho3+ and Co2+ ions co-doped Ni–Zn ferrites, the Ni0.4Zn0.5Ho0.02Co0.1Fe1.98O4 specimen would possess the optimally comprehensive performance for NFC antennas due to its least dielectric loss, lowest dielectric constant, highest Curie temperature, rather high saturation magnetization, and relatively low coercivity. Therefore, with the designed structure and size in Section 2.3, the transmission of the NFC antenna manufactured by the Ni0.4Zn0.5Ho0.02Co0.1Fe1.98O4 ferrite sample was simulated. For comparison, pure Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 ferrites and FR4 copper clad laminate were also simulated as substrates. In the simulation, the applied structure and magnetoelectric parameters of ferrites are their actual values measured in Sections 3.1–3.3.

Figure 15 shows the Smith chart and S11 parameters (which represent the return loss characteristic) obtained from the simulation based on the three selected substrates. To maximize the power of the antenna at 13.56 MHz, a circuit was designed to match the impedance of the antenna with a capacitor and a resistor connecting in parallel. As can be seen from the obtained Smith chart, the impedance of the antenna is matched with that of the chip by adjusting the capacitance and resistance, which is close to 27 Ω. The minimum value of S11 parameter is obtained near 13.56 MHz, which proves that the antenna and the circuit are well-matched.

*
Fig. 15(a) Smith chart and (b) S11 parameters for three selected substrate materials: FR4, pure Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4, and Ni0.4Zn0.5Ho0.02Co0.1Fe1.98O4.

Figure 16 displays the magnetic field distributions of the antennas with the three selected substrate materials at 13.56 MHz (side view). Obviously, from the magnetic field nephogram loaded with the FR4 substrate (see Fig. 16(a)), it is seen that although the magnetic field is mainly distributed on the top and inside of the substrate, there is also some magnetic field below it, which indicates serious leakage of magnetic flux. As a result, it will lead to insufficient magnetic flux at the receiver to generate induced electromotive force, and make the NFC system unable to obtain enough signals to work. Moreover, in the practical use of an antenna, it is difficult to avoid a metal environment. The leaked magnetic field will be disturbed by the metal to produce an eddy current, which will cause an increase in the device temperature, leading to the unstable performance of the devices and increasing security risk. In addition, the environmentally induced electricity may produce extra magnetic field, further disturbing the magnetic flux at the receiver. However, the magnetic field distribution of the NFC system with a ferrite substrate indicates that the ferrite substrate can effectively shield the leakage of magnetic flux (see Figs. 16(b) and 16(c). Because of the high permeability of ferrites, it will provide a magnetic field path for the space magnetic field of the near-field antenna [98]. Thus, only a very small part of the magnetic field enters the metal environment through the magnetic substrate to generate an eddy current, while most of the magnetic field passes through the middle region of the transceiver antenna, which makes the performance of the antenna greatly improved.

## 4 Conclusions

Novel Ni–Zn ferrite ceramics co-doped with Ho3+ and Co2+ ions in the chemical formula of Ni0.5−*x*Zn0.5Ho0.02Co*x*Fe1.98O4 (*x* = 0–0.2) were fabricated on the basis of molten salt synthesis with metal nitrates as raw materials and KOH as the precipitation agent and molten salt precursor. The magnetic and dielectric properties were optimized, and the specimens were used as an NFC antenna substrate. The conclusions can be summarized as follows.

(i) Both Ho3+ and Co2+ ions could enter into the lattice of ferrites, replacing the Fe3+ and Ni2+ ions in the B sites, respectively. The additional Ho3+ ions might also generate a foreign HoFeO3 phase in the specimens. The addition of Ho3+ ions can improve the sintering densification, but that of Co2+ ions has the opposite effect.

(ii) The addition of Co2+ ions can increase the *M*s value of ferrites to counteract against the adverse effect of Ho3+ doping on *M*s. The secondary phase (HoFeO3) and the addition of Co2+ ions will increase the *H*c value of ferrites. When *x* = 0.10, the Ho3+ and Co2+ co-doped ferrites have the highest Curie temperature (305 ℃). The *ε´* and tan*δ* values of the acquired ferrites decrease obviously with the co-doping of Ho3+ and Co2+ ions, reaching the lowest values when *x* = 0.1. The low loss will be very much helpful to fabricate the ferrite substrate of the NFC antenna with excellent performance. In addition, the cut-off frequency of ferrites could be extended by the co-doping of Ho3+ and Co2+ ions, implying that they which will have great application potential in electronic devices under high frequency.

(iii) With the optimal Ho3+ and Co2+ co-doped Ni–Zn ferrites (Ni0.4Zn0.5Ho0.02Co0.1Fe1.98O4) as the NFC antenna substrate, the magnetic flux is 25% higher than that with the pure Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 ferrite substrate and nearly 200% higher than that with the FR4 substrate, when the distance between the transmitter and the receiver is 1 mm. Under the same conditions, the antennas manufactured by the optimal Ni0.4Zn0.5Ho0.02Co0.1Fe2O4 ferrite substrate can present the longest communication distance. These results reveal that it will be a good candidate for the substrate of the NFC antenna under high frequency.
