## 1 Introduction

The reversible protonic ceramic electrochemical cell (R-PCEC), derived from the protonic ceramic fuel cell (PCFC) technology, is a highly efficient energy conversion technology that can convert chemical and electrical energy at medium and low temperatures with great versatility. Recently, there has been a great amount of research conducted on R-PCECs because of their numerous benefits. These include the ability to directly produce dry hydrogen, operate at lower temperatures, and offer significant cost-cutting opportunities [1−4]. R-PCECs are capable of converting water and carbon dioxide into hydrogen and other valuable carbon-based fuels via the electrolysis mode, providing an attractive means of efficient energy storage. Meanwhile, R-PCECs can generate the electricity on demand in the fuel cell mode supplementary to other intermittent renewable electricity sources, such as solar and wind power [5−7].

The proton-conducting type oxides as the principal component of the electrolyte can not only ensure good proton conductivity for R-PCEC at low temperatures but also improve safety and stability of the R-PCEC system. BaZr0.1Ce0.7Y0.1Yb0.1O3 (BZCYYb) is a BaCeO3−BaZrO3-based electrolyte obtained by co-doping with two aliovalent elements at the B-site. The materials have been extensively studied and found to exhibit favorable proton and oxygen ion conduction characteristics. As a result, it is widely regarded as the preferred electrolyte materials for typical R-PCECs [8−10]. Due to the significant role of oxygen ion and proton conduction in BZCYYb, the simultaneous steam electrolysis from the solid oxide electrolysis cells (SOECs) can be achieved by feeding steam to both sides of the air and fuel electrodes, leading to a notable rise in current density at 1.3 V [11,12].

However, the most important challenge facing the development of R-PCECs is addressing the inadequate performance of the air electrodes during oxygen reduction reaction (ORR; in the fuel cell mode) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER; in the electrolysis mode) at low and medium temperatures. Although mixed oxide-ion/electron-hole conductors (MIECs) exhibit good catalytic activity for ORR in the conventional SOFC with oxygen ion conducting electrolyte, the severe degradation of the electrochemical performance occurs when MIECs are used as the air electrode in PCECs [13,14]. During the fabrication or ORR operation, the decomposition of the electrode and chemical reaction with the electrolyte may arise, which leads to the degradation of the electrochemical performance [15]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore new air electrode materials with high catalytic activity and chemical stability suitable for R-PCEC systems.

To efficiently enlarge active sites of the reaction, air electrode materials for R-PCECs should be the triple conducting oxide to facilitate the transfer of oxygen ions, electrons, and hydrogen ions. Doping protonic conducting oxides with transition metals to increase their oxygen ion and electron conductivity, such as various multivalent element-doped BaCeO3 and BaZrO3, has been suggested as a more promising approach to the creation of the air electrode materials, although low total conductivity still restrains the practical application of these electrodes [16,17]. The triple phase boundaries (TPB) enhancement of ORR/OER can also be accomplished through a mixture of conventional MIECs and pure protonic conducting oxides, and the mechanism underlying the effect of mixed air electrode micromorphology change on electrochemical performance has been clearly recognized [18,19]. Furthermore, some MIEC materials, such as La0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3−*δ* (LSCF), Ba0.5Sr0.5Co0.8Fe0.2O3−*δ* (BSCF), PrBaCo2O5+*δ* (PBCO), and BaCo0.5Fe0.5O3–*δ* (BCF), have electrocatalytic activity for proton reactions because of their high oxygen vacancy content [20,21]. The experimental observation demonstrates that oxygen ions and protons are involved in the electrochemical reaction, as the electrochemical efficiency of these electrode materials is highly dependent on the water pressure. A few cobalt containing perovskites, such as PrBa0.5Sr0.5Co2O5+*δ* (PBSC) [22], NdBa0.5Sr0.5Co1.5Fe0.5O5+*δ* (NBSCF) [23], Ba0.9K0.1Co0.4Fe0.4Zr0.2O3−*δ* (BKCFZ) [24], and BaCo0.4Fe0.4Zr0.1Y0.1O3−*δ* (BCFZY) [5] have recently been identified as triple conducting oxides in proton conducting SOFCs or SOECs at intermediate temperatures (600–700 ℃). However, the thermal expansion coefficient (TEC) of cobalt-based perovskite is relatively high, especially compared to barium-cerate-based electrolytes, making it difficult to optimize the interface between the electrode and the electrolyte [25,26].

As a multivalent element, Sn is an excellent doping choice because it can simultaneously modify thermal expansion coefficient and boost catalytic activity of the doped substance, as shown by numerous investigations [27−29]. Sn doping has been found to have a positive effect on the catalytic activity in certain fuel electrode materials, such as Sn-doped Ni–GDC and Ni–YSZ [27]. When Sn is doped into the B-site of the BaFeO3−*δ* parent oxide, the resulting BaFe0.95Sn0.05O3−*δ* materials exhibit high efficiency for SOFCs because they stabilize a single perovskite lattice structure and promotes oxygen reduction activity [30]. In addition, Sn-doped Bi0.5Sr0.5FeO3−*δ* exhibits expanded lattice parameters, resulting in increased *r*c and larger average binding energy (ABE) values. Therefore, BSFSn has smaller TEC compared to BSF at temperatures between 50 and 800 ℃, with an average value of 12.9×10−6 K−1 [31]. It has been demonstrated that the thermal expansion coefficient modified by Sn doping is more closely matched to that of the electrolyte, thereby promoting chemical and structural compatibility between the electrode and the electrolyte, and lowering interfacial resistance and increasing ORR reactivity.

Herein, we report a perovskite oxide with the potential for proton conduction, Pr0.5Ba0.5Co0.7Fe0.3−*x*Sn*x*O3−*δ* (*x* = 0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.07) named as PBCFS01-07, as an air electrode in R-PCECs with superior ORR activity. Our experimental investigation and density function theory (DFT) calculation demonstrate that the proton defect can be easily generated through hydration reaction when Sn is doped in the B-site of the Pr0.5Ba0.5Co0.7Fe0.3O3−*δ* perovskite. Therefore, the B-site substitution of Sn can effectively lower the energy barrier of proton conduction. By facilitating ORR and OER, the triple conduction ability demonstrated by the materials improves their electrochemical performance, enabling a reversible operation of the electrochemical cell at temperatures between 500 and 700 ℃.

## 2 Experimental

The citric acid–nitrate gel combustion technique was utilized in the preparation of both electrode materials and electrolyte materials. PBCFS01-07, for instance, is synthesized by dissolving Pr(NO3)3·6H2O, BaCO3, Co(NO3)3·6H2O, Fe(NO3)·9H2O, and SnO in nitric acid solution in stoichiometric proportions, followed by the addition of citric acid at a molar ratio of citric acid/metal ions of 1 : 1.5. The solution was heated while stirred to vaporize water until it changed to viscous gel and finally self-combusted. The precursors of BZCYYb and anode functional layer (AFL) composed of NiO–BaZr0.1Ce0.7Y0.1Yb0.1O3 (Ni–BZCYYb) were prepared in the same manner as PBCFS01-07. The final powders of PBCFS01-07, BZCYYb, and AFL were obtained by sintering the precursor powders in a muffle furnace (KSL-1700X-S, Kejing) for 3 h at a temperature of 1000 ℃. The fuel electrode powders of NiO–BZCYYb are produced by mixing NiO, BZCYYb, and maize starch with a ratio of 60 : 40 : 20 in alcohol solution using the ball milling technique for 24 h. Subsequently, the mixture is subjected to dehydration.

A mature “fuel electrode–functional layer–electrolyte” three-layers co-sintering method was applied for the manufacture of fuel electrode-supported half-cells at 1350 ℃ for 5 h. A five-millimeter-radius stainless steel module was used to press anode powders of 0.36 g to obtain the anode layer at 300 MP, followed by the sequential pressing of AFL powders of 0.014 g and electrolyte powders of 0.005 g at 200 MPa, yielding a three-layer half-point cell supported by the anode. Cathode powders of 0.7 g, electrolyte powders of 0.3 g, and ethylcellulose-pineol adhesive of 10 wt% were mixed in an agate mortar and pestle to prepare the air electrode slurry. The electrolyte layer was brushed with air–electrode slurry, and the collector area was regulated to be 0.196 cm2 using a fixed model. To optimize the air electrode’s porosity, the air electrode slurry was coated on a dense BZCYYb electrolyte film and then dried in a vacuum furnace (DZ-IBCIV, Qinsite). The single cell was then sintered in a microwave oven at 900 ℃ for 10 min to ensure a good bond between the cathode and electrolyte layers.

Phase structures were characterized by an X-ray diffractometer (D8 advance, Bruker) using Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 0.15406 nm, *V* = 40 kV, *I* = 40 mA). The surface chemical states of the PBCFS01-07 powders were examined with an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific), while the morphology and crystallinity were examined through a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-2100F, JEOL). A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Sigma 300, ZEISS) was used to examine the cell microstructures.

The electrical conductivity relaxation (ECR) was applied to analyze the ORR activity of PBCFS01-07 by observing the oxygen ion adsorption and diffusion ability. Utilizing a stainless-steel die and a hydraulic press, the PBCFS01-07 powders were uni-axially pressed into a cuboid bar at 250 MPa. The pressed samples were sintered at 1300 ℃ for 5 h in an air atmosphere to reach the theoretical relative density of more than 95%, with a final size of roughly 3.5 mm × 1.65 mm × 7.5 mm. To automatically record the ECR behavior of the sample bars from 350 to 750 ℃, they were heated in a tube furnace (OTF-1200X-S, Kejing) and connected to a digital multimeter (Keithley 2400, Tektronix) through four silver lead wires based on the four-probe approach. To determine the materials’ volume diffusivity and surface exchange coefficient, the gas mixture entering the alumina tube was changed from N2 : O2 = 1 : 1 to air while maintaining the same flow rate of roughly 50 mL∙min−1.

The assessment of hydrogen permeation flux was carried out using a custom-built apparatus. A level and parallel surface was achieved through careful preparation of the sintered PBCFS05 pellets by polishing both surfaces with SiC sandpaper. To obtain a reliable gas sealing, the pellets were securely mounted on an alumina tube using a glass ring sealant and subjected to a sealing temperature of 900 ℃. The gas at the feed side was 20 mL∙min−1 H2 and 80 mL∙min−1 N2, while the sweep gas at the permeate side was high purity Ar at a rate of 20 mL∙min−1.

The proton conductivity of the materials can be obtained by Wagner equation (Eq. (1)):

(1) σH+=JH24F2LRTlnPH2suppPH2perm

where * σH+ is the proton conductivity,  JH2 represents the H2 permeation flux, F* is the Faraday constant which is 96,485.33 C∙mol−1, and *L* represents the thicknesses of PBCFS05 (0.76 mm). *R* denotes the ideal gas constant, which has a value of 8.314 J·(mol∙K)−1. *T* represents the operating temperature in Kelvin. The variables “* PH2supp” and “ PH2perm” denote the hydrogen partial pressure on the hydrogen supply side and the permeate side, respectively.

Using an electrochemical apparatus (Squidstat Plus, Admiral Instruments) with a four-probe configuration, a single cell was examined. The water vapor pressure (0.03 atm) was attained by following H2 through a water bubbler at approximately 25 ℃. For the fuel cell test, 3% H2O-humidified H2 of 20 mL∙min−1 was supplied to the fuel electrode as the fuel, while ambient air was supplied to the air electrode as the oxidant. For the electrolysis test, the fuel electrode was subjected to humidified H2 (3% H2O) of 20 mL∙min−1, while the air electrode was supplied with 100 mL∙min−1 of humidified (3% H2O) air. To acquire the I*–*V*/*I*–*P* curves of the cell, the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) mode was utilized. Additionally, electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were measured under open circuit conditions with a frequency range of 0.1–1 MHz and 10 mV as AC amplitude.

Theoretical calculations using density functional theory (DFT) were conducted on PBCFS01-07. The Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) was utilized with Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) parametrization of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) to incorporate electron exchange and correlation. Additionally, the projector augmented wave (PAW) method was employed to represent electron waves in a pseudo-potential format [32−34]. With a 4 × 4 × 4 Monkhorst–Pack type *k*-space grid for energy accumulation in volume and a single Gamma point for energy estimation in a slab, the kinetic energy cut-off of the plane wave basis set was 500 eV, and force tolerance on each atom was set to 0.02 eV/Å for the purpose of structural relaxation calculations [35]. An illustration of the structure in *Pm*/3*m* is demonstrated in (Fig. S4(a) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)).

The bond length of the transition metal to the oxygen atom is altered when PBCF is doped with various ratios of elemental tin. The amount of oxygen vacancies in the doped materials is drastically affected by this modification. The energy required for vacancy creation ( EOV) is determined by Eq. (2):

(2) EOV=Edefect+12EO2−EABO3

In Eq. (2),  Edefect represents the energy of the bulk with defects induced,  EO2 is the energy of an oxygen molecule, and  EABO3 is the energy of complete bulk without defects.

When the system’s basicity rises and oxygen vacancies are produced to compensate for a charge, the system’s charge becomes more neutral. As a result, the substance becomes more water-reactive, which triggers the subsequent Stotz–Wagner pathway (Eq. (3)):

(3) H2O(g)+OO×+VO··→2OHO·

Hydration ability can be directly anticipated using the formula for estimating hydration energy based on the production of oxygen vacancies (Eq. (4)):

(4) Ehydration=E2OH−EH2O−Edefect

Furthermore, the proton conduction in perovskite oxides is based on two types of paths: rotation and hopping. While remaining linked to the oxygen ion, the proton spins 90° around the B–O–B axis, whereas hopping means the proton leaps from one oxygen ion to another in the same octahedron, or hops from one oxygen ion to another between distinct octahedra. The CI-NEB technique was used to determine the energetic barriers along the proton transfer pathways.

### 3.1 Analysis of composition and structure

Analysis by X-ray diffraction (XRD) shows that as the amount of Sn dopant increases, the XRD peak shifts to a lower diffraction angle (Fig. 1), indicating lattice expansion in Pr0.5Ba0.5Co0.7Fe0.3−*x*Sn*x*O3−*δ* (*x* = 0.01, 0.03, 0.05, and 0.07). The replacement of smaller Fe3+ (*r* = 0.645 Å) or Fe4+ (*r* = 0.585 Å) ions with larger Sn4+ (*r* = 0.69Å) ions leads to the lattice expansion. Goldschmidt tolerance factor (* t=(rA+rO)/[2(rB+rO)]) provides a concise explanation for the durability of ABO3 type perovskite oxides where r*A is the ionic radius of the A-site, *r*B is the ionic radius of the B-site, and *r*O is the ionic radius of the oxygen [36]. Theoretical research reveals that * t can be reduced to less than 1.0 by substituting a larger cation at the B site [37]. Therefore, the creation of Pr0.5Ba0.5Co0.7Fe0.3O3 with a cubic structure is favored by the replacement of smaller Fe ions with larger Sn4+ ions. Rietveld refinement of X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis for Sn doped Pr0.5Ba0.5Co0.7Fe0.3−x*Sn*x*O3−*δ* (*x* = 0.01, 0.03, 0.05, and 0.07) samples reveals a cubic structure with *pm** 3¯m* space group symmetry, as shown in Table S1 and Fig. S1 in the ESM. Since the B-site doping modifies the electron configuration of the B-site cations, the lattice parameter rises in proportion to the amount of Sn doping. Furthermore, there is only one peak (110) located near 32°, indicating that the oxygen ion transport in the BO6 octahedron is a three-dimensional conduit that promotes oxygen reduction and oxidation reactions [38].

### 3.2 Analysis of transport properties

Air electrode materials with higher electrical conductivity typically exhibit improved charge collection efficiency, lower ohmic loss, and faster charge compensation rate during the ORR process. Figure 3(a) shows the electrical conductivity of PBCFS01-07 measured by a DC four-probe method. The conductivity curve of PBCFS01 demonstrates a gradual increase in the conductivity with increasing the temperature from 350 to 450 ℃, followed by a decrease in the conductivity at temperatures above 450 ℃, indicating that the conductivity of PBCFS01 changes from semiconductor-like to metallic properties at a temperature of approximately 450 ℃. Comparing the conductivity curves of PBCFS01-07, the semiconductor-like nature progressively decreases and tends to be metallic between 350 and 450 ℃ as the doping content of Sn rises. Evidently, PBCFS05 almost exhibits metallic conduction between 350 and 750 ℃. It has been reported that electronic conduction in perovskite-related oxides occurs via electron hopping along the B–O–B bonds [39]. The formation of oxygen vacancies as a result of thermal reduction (Co4+/Fe4+ to Co3+/Fe3+ for cobalt–iron-based perovskite) is responsible for the decrease in the electrical conductivity with an increase in the temperature [40], leading to an obstruction of the route along which electrons travel. By analyzing the conductivity–temperature relationship, the activation energy for electrical conduction of the PBCFS05 conductivity is the lowest.

According to the *D* and *k* values for PBCFS01-07, PBCFS05 has the lowest activation energy barrier for oxygen adsorption and dissociation and for bulk conduction, which could represent an advantage for the transport of oxygen ions.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is utilized to describe the O 1s core-level spectra to provide more insight into chemical properties of the synthesized PBCFS01-07 powders. The rate-limiting step of the cathodic oxygen reduction process is critically impacted by the surface oxygen species, which have a significant effect on the fuel cell performance. Therefore, it is crucial to search for the oxygen species present in air electrode materials. The O 1s core-level spectra are decomposed into four sections: the first originated from the lattice oxygen (* OL); the other three contributions are attributed to the chemisorbed oxygen species ( OC) of  O2−,  O−, and  O2−, respectively. The fitted curve of PBCFS01-07 is depicted in Figs. 4(a)–4(d), and the bond energy and the area percentage ratios of  OC/ OL are listed in Table S3 in the ESM. In the presence of oxygen, a sequence of alterations occur on the surface of the oxide catalyst according to the general scheme [42]:  O2↔O2→+e−O2−→+e−O22−↔2O−→+2e−2O2− (lattice). According to the literature, the ORR activity of various air electrode materials can be evaluated using the  OC/ OL ratio as the benchmark [43]. Therefore, we can conclude from Table S3 in the ESM that the value of  OC/ OL increases with increasing the Sn content, indicating that Sn-doping enhances the ORR activity in the air electrode surface. For SOFCs, PBCFS05 may prove to be an effective cathode when x* = 0.05, where its ORR activity is the highest. Sn4+ produces lone pair electrons, which assists the oxygen ions bound to it easily to escape from the crystal lattice forming more oxygen vacancies [44].

### 3.3 Theoretical calculation

It is crucial to reveal the reaction mechanism and identify the basic steps of the electrochemical reaction at the cathode for PCFC to better understand the cathode process. The enthalpy of the reaction can be determined by calculating the energy of the complete lattice, defective lattice, and oxygen molecules in the gas phase. In the complete lattice of PBCFS05, there is an oxygen (* OO×) located between two transition metal ions ( MM×), whereas in the defective lattice, there is an oxygen vacancy ( VO··) accompanied by two adjacent reduced metal sites ( MM′). Defective PBCFS05 lattices are formed when a neutral oxygen atom is removed, resulting in the creation of  VO··, and the degree of non-stoichiometry (δ*) depends on the size of the periodic supercell model. From the description in Kröger*–*Vink reaction, the formation of an oxygen vacancy can be expressed according to Eq. (5):

(5) 2MM×+OO×→2MM'+VO··+12O2(g)

By calculating the oxygen vacancy formation energy of various oxygen sites (summarized in Table S4 in the ESM), it can be concluded that the optimal oxygen vacancy site is located between the Fe and Co atoms on the Co–Fe–Sn–O plane and has energy of −3.6 eV (Fig. S4(b) in the ESM). Upon the removal of an oxygen atom from the complete lattice by breaking two Co–O–Fe bonds, the redistribution of two electrons associated with the neutral O2− ion occurs within the defective crystal. The thermodynamics of the  VO·· formation depends on various factors such as the strength of the M–O–M bond and the electron magnetization of two neighboring M ions that transform from a highly charged to a less charged configuration [45,46]. Calculations indicate that doping Sn weakens adjacent Co–O bond energy and increases electron magnetization in Co–O–Fe.

To further demonstrate the introduction of protons by Sn doping, theoretical studies based on a well-established model are conducted to examine the impact of Sn-doping on the hydration capacity of the materials. After selecting the appropriate oxygen ions, the surrounding proton energy landscape was analyzed so that the protons occupied the positions with the lowest energy. The calculated hydration energy for PBCFS05 is 0.65 eV, lower than that of PBCF [47]. On the basis of Coulombic repulsion alone, one might anticipate that the introduction of Sn into the PBCF system would result in an enhancement in its hydration capacity. Additionally, the importance of basicity in assessing the hydration capacity of such materials has been established [48,49].

The proton transfer typically takes two routes based on the “Grotthuss-type” mechanism, shown in Figs. S5(c) and S6 in the ESM: reorientation and intra-octahedral hopping. The energy barriers of proton reorientation and intra-octahedral hopping were calculated by the NEB method. For reorientations with the lowest energy of −0.0803 eV (O21(a) to O21(b)), the proton is migrating to a location that is either closer to the Sn ion or further from the Fe ion. For the possible intra–octahedral hopping, it has been found that protons transferring across the path of O21–O18 require the lowest energy, only −0.11035 eV. The DFT results indicate that transporting protons is simpler in PBCFS05 than PBCF because the barrier energy for the hopping and rotating processes are lower. To enhance the verification of proton conductivity in PBCFS05, hydrogen permeation tests were conducted on pellets of PBCFS05, as depicted in Fig. S7 in the ESM. The proton conductivity of PBCFS05 in discussion exhibits a level of comparability to that of other related materials [50].

### 3.4 Electrochemical performance in fuel cell mode

Figure 5(a) displays representative cross-sectional SEM images of the single cells composed of Ni–BZCYYb1711 fuel electrode, AFL, dense BZCYYb1711 electrolyte (6–7 μm), and the PBCFS05 air electrode after the electrochemical performance test. To investigate the impact of Sn doping levels on electrochemical behaviour, fuel electrode-supported SOFCs with PBCFS01-07 cathodes are tested at 700 ℃. As shown in Fig. 5(b), the maximum power density (MPD) of 1225, 1332, 1644, and 1416 mW∙cm−2 is achieved by fuel cells with PBCFS01, PBCFS03, PBCFS05, and PBCFS07 air electrodes, respectively. The corresponding polarization resistances are shown in Fig. 5(c) to evaluate the air electrode reaction process. At 700 ℃, PBCFS05 has the lowest polarization resistance of 0.029 Ω∙cm2, while PBCFS01 has the highest polarization resistance of 0.05 Ω∙cm2. Moreover, fuel cells with PBCFS01-07 retain their advantageous electrochemical performance even at the operating temperatures down to 600 ℃. Figure 5(d) shows that PBCFS05 exhibits the highest catalytic activity of ORR at 600 ℃ with MPD of 1122 mW∙cm−2, while MPD of fuel cells with PBCFS01, PBCFS03, and PBCFS07 measured at 600 ℃ is 640, 710, and 864 mW∙cm−2, respectively. The polarization resistance of PBCFS05, PBCFS01, PBCFS03, and PBCFS07 at 600 ℃ is 0.09, 0.23, 0.16, and 0.14 Ω∙cm2, respectively. Distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis of EIS data obtained at 600 ℃ aids in the comprehensive analysis of the kinetics of electrode reactions. The DRT maps can be categorized into peaks that are distributed at low (LF; less than 10 Hz), intermediate (IF; 10–2000 Hz), and high frequencies (HF; more than 2000 Hz). ### 3.5 Performance and stability in electrolysis mode

The electrochemical performance of R-PCEC with PBCFS05 as the air electrode was also evaluated in the electrolysis mode, with the *I*–*V* plots at 550–650 ℃ shown in Fig. 6(a), and 3.56, 1.79, 0.75, and 0.33 A∙cm−2 were the corresponding current density at a cell voltage of 1.3 V for temperatures at 650, 600, 550, and 500 ℃, respectively. Figure 6(b) depicts the cell current as a function of time of R-PCEC using PBCFS05 as the air electrode. The experimental conditions were operating the cell in both fuel cell and electrolysis modes at a temperature of 600 ℃. The cyclic operation consisted of switching the cell voltage from 1.3 to 0.7 V and maintaining each voltage level for duration of 4 h in both modes. The entire cyclic testing lasted for 100 h (for a total of 13 cycles), and a stable operation was observed, indicating excellent reversibility of PCECs.

## 4 Conclusions

This study reports Sn-doping on the B-site, Pr0.5Ba0.5Co0.7Fe0.3−*x*Sn*x*O3−*δ* as the air electrode for R-PCEC applications. At 600 ℃, single cells using PBCFS05 as the air electrode demonstrated the maximum power density of 1.12 W∙cm−2 in the fuel cell mode and current density of 1.79 A∙cm−2 at 1.3 V in the electrolysis mode, outperforming the majority of R-PCEC using other air electrode materials. In addition, fuel cells using PBCFS05 as air electrode cells exhibited reasonable stability during operation at 0.7 V in the fuel cell mode for 100 h and excellent reversible cell performance for a total of 13 cycles in the fuel cell (*V* = 0.7 V) and the electrolysis (*V* = 1.3 V) mode. The rational and effective design of PBCFS05 has been shown to have potential as a promising air electrode for applications in R-PCECs, with high cell performance and good long-term stability.
