## 1 Introduction

Temperature plays a pivotal role in the environment, directly affecting the survival, function, and overall health of organisms by shaping their physiological and behavioral responses. Likewise, pressure is an essential physical parameter, ranging from cosmic phenomena to complex biological processes within the human body. Both factors serve as critical indicators in health assessment, offering valuable data on functions such as breathing patterns, joint dynamics, and other vital activities [1]. As a result, the development of dual-mode sensors capable of detecting both temperature and pressure simultaneously has garnered significant attention. These advanced sensors hold immense potential in areas such as medical care, rehabilitation, human‒computer interaction, and flexible robotics.

Flexible pressure sensors translate applied pressure into electrical signals and are generally divided into three main types: piezoresistive, capacitive, and piezoelectric [2−4]. Among these sensors, piezoresistive sensors are the most widely used and detect pressure through variations in electrical resistance [5]. Accurate monitoring of body temperature is essential for health assessment, but conventional thermometers often lack flexibility and comfort. Flexible temperature sensors, such as thin films and hydrogels, provide a more versatile alternative [6,7]. These sensors are typically classified into resistive, capacitive, and thermoelectric (TE) categories [8]. Resistive sensors operate by measuring resistance changes, whereas capacitive sensors detect pressure via alterations in the capacitance between electrode layers separated by an insulating material. However, capacitive sensors are prone to interference from mechanical factors, such as skin deformation caused by tensile stress, which can affect their measurement accuracy.

TE temperature sensors offer a unique advantage by directly converting heat into electricity via the Seebeck effect [9–11], eliminating the need for an external power source. This makes them ideal for scenarios where continuous power is unavailable [12]. By utilizing the temperature difference between the human body and its environment, these sensors generate measurable electrical signals [13]. However, TE devices, particularly those made from inorganic materials, face significant limitations in flexible sensor applications because of their brittle and rigid nature [14−16]. Even when integrated with flexible substrates such as polyimide (PI), materials such as Bi2Te3 are prone to microcracks under repeated mechanical stress. Additionally, skin stretching during motion can further compromise device durability [17]. To overcome these challenges, the development of high-tensile, flexible TE materials is essential for creating more reliable wearable electronics.

Traditional bimodal temperature‒pressure sensors consist of separate elements for each function, which are made from different materials and either stacked or integrated onto a flexible substrate [18,19]. This complexity reduces efficiency and performance. Simplifying these systems while improving their functionality is a key challenge. For example, Chhetry *et al*. [20] developed a hybrid temperature‒strain sensor that integrates a black phosphorus and graphene heterostructure into a highly stretchable and durable substrate, enabling accurate, interference-free measurement of both parameters. However, their temperature sensors depend on thermal resistance effects and require external batteries, which limits their ability for long-term, unattended monitoring. The development of self-powered sensors that are highly sensitive to both pressure and temperature is another critical challenge.

Designing dual-mode sensors requires consideration of their electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding performance [21,22]. As electromagnetic pollution increases, it disrupts electronic systems and poses risks to human health. In addition, antibacterial performance is essential for wearable electronics [23]. Materials with dense inorganic structures often exhibit low moisture permeability, trapping sweat and moisture to create a warm, humid environment conducive to bacterial growth and skin irritation. Effective moisture management is essential for skin comfort and health, as inefficient perspiration expulsion can cause discomfort and impair skin function. For long-term health monitoring, wearable electronics must integrate breathable and antibacterial materials, which are critical for disease prevention, early detection, and treatment. Thus, developing films with superior EMI shielding, antibacterial properties, and moisture permeability is vital.

Carbon cloth (CC), made from carbon fibers (CFs) derived through the stabilization and carbonization of polyacrylonitrile, is renowned for its high moisture permeability, excellent conductivity, and superior EMI shielding properties. These characteristics make CFs highly suitable for diverse applications, including batteries [24], supercapacitors [25], and EMI shielding [26]. However, their TE performance is limited by a low Seebeck coefficient (*S*) of only −1.8 μV·K−1. Silver sulfide (Ag2S), a transition metal sulfide with a band gap of 1.23 eV, offers a unique combination of properties, including excellent TE performance [27], mechanical flexibility [28], and ductility [29]. Additionally, its silver content may impart antibacterial properties due to the antimicrobial effects of silver ions [30]. Coating CFs with Ag2S could combine the strengths of both materials, potentially enhancing their TE performance, EMI shielding ability, moisture permeability, antibacterial properties, and tensile strength (TS)—making them strong candidates for wearable sensors.

In this work, we developed a multifunctional material that combines pressure sensing and self-powered temperature sensing within a single structure, achieved by electrodepositing Ag2S onto CFs within CC. As shown in Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), while traditional TE materials such as Bi2Te3 exhibit excellent TE properties [31], their rigidity and the toxicity of TE make them unsuitable. Yang *et al*. [32] developed a PI-silver composite film with antibacterial properties, but its application is limited to pressure sensing. Other TE sensing materials, such as MXene-polyurethane and Mo2TiC2 [33,34], exhibit restricted power factors (PFs) and are limited in temperature sensing applications. In contrast, the CC/Ag2S film developed in this work not only demonstrates competitive TE performance but also integrates multifunctional capabilities, including pressure sensing, electromagnetic shielding, moisture permeability, and antibacterial properties, making it a more versatile and practical solution for wearable electronics.

### 2.1 Material synthesis

The aqueous electrolyte of Ag2S was prepared by dissolving silver nitrate (99.99%, AgNO3; Aladdin, China) and thiourea (99%, CH4N2S; Macklin, China) in deionized water. The final electrolyte contained 0.01 M Ag+ and 0.03 M·S2−. The CC (Suzhou Sinero Technology Co., Ltd., China) was ultrasonically cleaned with acetone, ethanol, and deionized water for 10 min in sequence and then used as the working electrode (WE). A platinum sheet (2.5 cm × 2.5 cm) was used as the counter electrode (CE), and saturated KCl was used as the reference electrode (RE). The electrodeposition (ED) of Ag2S was carried out by ED under stirring using an electrochemical workstation (CHI660E, Shanghai Chenhua Instruments, China). The CC/Ag2S was washed with ethanol and deionized water in turn and then dried at 60 °C for 1 h. The four composite films with a size of 8 mm × 20 mm with the best performance were selected as the TE legs and electrically connected with silver paste (Ag 3073, Shenzhen Sinwe New Materials, China).

### 2.2 Characterizations

The composite film was characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; SmartLab, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation. The microstructure was observed via a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Phenom Pharos G1, Phenom-World, the Netherlands) and an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS, Phenom Pharos G1, Phenom-World, the Netherlands). The composition and chemical bonding of the elements were analyzed via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Escalab 250Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA). *S* was tested via a portable Seebeck coefficient tester (PTM-3, Jiayitong Technology, China). The electrical conductivity (*σ*), mobility (*μ*), and carrier concentration (*n*) were measured with a Hall effect measurement system (CH-100, CH-Magnetoelectricity Technology, China) under a 400 mT magnetic field. TS was tested with a tensile testing machine (DR-509A, East Sun, China). A contact angle measurement apparatus (JGW-360A, Chenghui testing machine, China) was used to identify the hydrophobicity of CC/Ag2S. A vector network analyzer (VNA; E5071C, Aligent, USA) was employed to evaluate the electromagnetic shielding performance of a sample of ~22.84 mm × 10.16 mm × 0.405 mm in the frequency range of 8.2–12.4 GHz (X-band). A multimeter (Keithley 6500, USA) was connected to measure the output voltage and output power. The resistance, voltage, and other signals of the sensor were recorded in real-time via a multimeter (Keithley 6500, USA).

### 2.3 Calculations

Spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to investigate the electronic structure of CC, Ag2S, and CC/Ag2S via the Vienna *Ab initio* Simulation Package (VASP) [35], which implements the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method [36,37]. The Kohn–Sham wave functions were used to describe the behavior of valence electrons and were expanded in a plane wave basis set with an energy cutoff of 520 eV [38]. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) [39] was employed to account for electron exchange and correlation interactions, whereas van der Waals (vdW) interactions were included via Grimme’s DFT-D3 method [40] to model dispersive forces. Convergence criteria were set to ensure a total energy tolerance within 0.000001 eV and forces on each atom below 0.05 eV·Å−1. The python materials genomics (Pymatgen) [41] and atomic simulation environment (ASE) [42] libraries were employed for crystal structure manipulation and input file generation. The bulk structure computational parameters were adopted from the Materials Project Database [43]. The work function (*Φ*) and charge density difference are analyzed via the VASPKIT code [44].

### 3.1 Synthesis, phases, and microstructures

As shown in Fig. 1(a), CC serves as the WE. Potentiostatic ED facilitates the growth of Ag2S grains. Figure S2(a) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) shows that the optimal deposition potential is determined by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) at a scan rate of 10 mV·s−1 prior to ED. To minimize concentration polarization, the electrolyte was stirred at 250 r·min−1 during both LSV and ED. The results indicate that the electrochemical reaction starts at about −0.33 V (onset potential). Because Ag readily loses its single valence electron and the nitrate ion remains intact due to its stability, AgNO3 is preferentially ionized to produce Ag+ ions. In a weakly alkaline environment, the combination of deprotonation, increased electron density on S, and the stability of S2− in alkaline environments lead to the easier formation of S2− ions from thiourea. Ag+ and S2− ions migrate to the WE interface layer under the action of the electric field force. In ED, when S2− ions are present, they react with Ag+ to form Ag2S. This can occur because Ag2S has very low solubility in water, driving the precipitation of the compound when both Ag+ and S2− ions are present. The electrochemical reaction involving the formation of Ag2S can be divided into several key steps, as shown by Reactions (1)–(4) [45]:

Two additional peaks at 163.6 and 164.8 eV are attributed to sulfur oxides [49].

### 3.2 Thermoelectric performance

The TE performance of the CC/Ag2S film is illustrated in Figs. 2(a)–2(c). As shown in Fig. 2(a), *n* of pure CC was measured to be 1.19×1020 cm−3. When Ag2S is electrodeposited onto CC, *n* decreases significantly, mainly due to the ultralow intrinsic *n* of Ag2S (~1014 cm−3) [27], which leads to an overall reduction in *n* due to the weighted effect. As the deposition potential is shifted more negatively, *n* continues to decrease slowly. This shift promoted the growth of Ag2S closer to the stoichiometric ratio, reducing the defect density. Figures 2(d) and 2(e), Fig. S6 in the ESM show the electronic band structures of CC, Ag2S, and CC/Ag2S. The band gaps of CC, Ag2S, and CC/Ag2S are 0, 1.23, and 0.1 eV, respectively. After the ED of Ag2S onto CC, the band gap of CC/Ag2S is much lower than that of Ag2S, allowing for easier excitation of valence electrons from the valence band to the conduction band, thus generating more free electrons. This leads to greater *n* of CC/Ag2S than of Ag2S [27]. *n* of the CC/Ag2S film at −0.6 V is 3.59×1018 cm−3, which is two orders of magnitude lower than that of the pure CC, which shows metallic conducting behavior.

As shown in Fig. 2(a), *μ* increases as the deposition potential shifts negatively. This occurred because Ag2S encapsulates the CFs, and the resulting cross-linked core‒shell structure enhances carrier transfer along the CF, promoting carrier movement. *μ* of CC/Ag2S reaches a maximum value of 70.8 cm2·V−1·s−1 at −0.6 V. As shown in Fig. 2(b), *σ* can be calculated as *σ = enμ* [50], where *e* is the elementary charge, *σ* is the electrical conductivity, and *n* is the carrier concentration. After ED of Ag2S onto CC, the diameter of the Ag2S-coated CFs increased, improving the electrical connection between the CFs, reaching a maximum of 41 S·cm−1 at −0.6 V. Figures 2(f)–2(h) depict *Φ* of CC, Ag2S, and CC/Ag2S. Clearly, when CC contacts Ag2S, electrons will flow from CC with smaller *Φ* of 4.25 eV to Ag2S (*Φ* = 5.61 eV), and the derived CC/Ag2S materials will exhibit much lower *Φ* of 4.17 eV, demonstrating more active electron behavior, which exemplifies the experimental observations. This phenomenon can be further proven by the charge distribution at the interface of CC/Ag2S, as depicted in Fig. 2(i), in which the yellow and blue regions denote charge accumulation and depletion, respectively. Obviously, after forming CC/Ag2S, the electrons will transfer from CC to Ag2S. This redistribution reflects enhanced electron accumulation at the carbon/Ag2S interface, corroborating the observed charge transfer phenomena and indicating a synergistic effect that improves carrier transport and increases *μ*. Additionally, this interfacial characteristic increases EMI shielding performance through polarization, as discussed in Section 3.5.

The dependence of *S*, on the deposition potential is shown in Fig. 2(b). According to the Mott (Eq. (5)) [51]:

(5) |S|=8π2kB23eh2m∗T(π3n)23

where *h* is the Planck constant, *k*B is the Bolyzmann constant, *m** is the effective mass of the carriers, and *e* is the elementary charge. The absolute value of *S* is inversely related to *n*. As shown in Figs. 2(d) and 2(e), CC/Ag2S has greater band curvature than Ag2S does, indicating the smaller *m**. This reduction in *m** leads to the lower *S* for CC/Ag2S than for Ag2S. Additionally, the reduced band degeneracy of CC/Ag2S further contributes to the decrease in *S*. Pure CC has the *S* of −1.8 μV·K−1, whereas CC/Ag2S deposited at −0.6 V achieves −22 μV·K−1 (Fig. 2(b)), a 13-fold increase primarily due to the weighted effect. The negative *S* indicates that electron transport is dominant, confirming that CC/Ag2S is an n-type semiconductor across the deposition potential range. Most Ag-based fast-ion conductor TE materials exhibit n-type properties, likely because Ag interstitial ions act as donor impurities. At −0.6 V, PF reaches a peak of 2 μW·m−1·K−2 (Fig. 2(c)), which is 170 times greater than that of pure CC, reflecting the synergistic trends in *S* and *σ*.

Figure S7 in the ESM illustrates the temperature-dependent behavior of *S* and *σ* for CC/Ag2S at −0.6 V. As shown in Fig. S7(a) in the ESM, *σ* of CC/Ag2S is low at 300 K because of the immobility of Ag ions in the monoclinic Ag2S. With increasing temperature, a phase transition occurs, transforming the monoclinic structure into a body-centered cubic structure, enabling fast ion conduction as Ag ions migrate between neighboring positions [52]. Hence, *σ* increases significantly, reaching 90 S·cm−1 at 470 K, which is 2.2 times greater than that at room temperature. Figure S7(b) in the ESM shows that *S* decreases linearly with temperature, reaching −6 μV·K−1 at 470 K.

As illustrated in Figs. S8(a) and S8(b) in the ESM, four CC/Ag2S films with the optimal TE performance are selected to integrate the TE generator (TEG). To demonstrate the power generation capability of TEG under different temperature gradients (∆*T*), one side of TEG is exposed to air, and the other side is placed on a constant-temperature heating table. As shown in Fig. S8(c) in the ESM, when ∆*T* increases from 10 to 30 K, the output voltage increases from 0.16 to 0.58 mV. To adjust the output current for testing the output power, an external adjustable resistor is connected to complete the circuit. The output power (*P*) is then calculated according to Eq. (6) [51]:

(6) P=E2(Rex−Rtotal)2Rex+4Rtotal

where *E* is output voltage of TEG under the ∆*T*, *R*ex and *R*total are the external and internal resistances, respectively. When ∆*T* is fixed, the maximum *P* (*P*max) can be obtained when *R*ex = *R*total. Figure S8(d) in the ESM shows the dependence of the output power on the output current; when ∆*T* is 10, 20, and 30 K, *P*max is 0.13, 0.64, and 1.4 nW, respectively.

### 3.3 Temperature sensor

On the basis of its TE performance, the TEG can be effectively utilized for temperature sensing. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the sensor sensitivity is evaluated at room temperature, demonstrating a minimum detection limit of ∆*T* = 1 K, highlighting its strong sensitivity to temperature changes. The output voltage increases linearly with ∆*T*, as illustrated in Fig. 3(b). The tactile sensor, which is composed of multiple TE legs, generates distinct voltage signals depending on the number of fingers in contact. Figure 3(c) depicts the sensor response to varying numbers of finger touches. Upon contact, the output voltage rises immediately, with the rapid response time of ~0.7 s and a recovery time of 2.1 s, as shown in Fig. 3(d). As shown in Fig. S9(a) in the ESM, the temperature sensor in this work has a significantly higher responsivity than those made from other materials [53−59]. The voltage signal generated by a single finger touch is defined as “A”, whereas the signals from two, three, and four fingers are defined as “P”, “R”, and “Y”, respectively (Fig. 3(e)). These signals can be combined creatively; for example, the word “pray” is formed by sequentially touching a sensor with two, three, one, or four fingers (Fig. 3(f)). In other words, “pay” and “ray” can also be generated through different finger arrangements (Fig. S10 in the ESM). Additionally, the tactile sensor exhibited excellent stability over 100 cycles, as shown in Fig. S11(a) in the ESM. This self-powered tactile sensor holds promising potential for assisting individuals with language barriers in the future.

As shown in Fig. 3(g), the sensor is integrated into a mask for respiration sensing, with one end inside (hot side, contact with exhaled air) and the other outside (cold side, exposure to ambient air). The temperature difference from exhaled air generates a voltage signal. Figure 3(h) shows its ability to monitor respiratory rate (RR) changes during activities such as standing, squatting, and sitting. In the standing state, RR is low at ~16.6 bpm, with the period of T* = 3.62 s. When transitioning to a squatting position, RR increases significantly to 25.8 bpm (*T* = 2.33 s). Upon sitting, RR decreases to 18.4 bpm (*T* = 3.26 s), a value between the standing and active states. These RR variations are influenced by the vertical pressure gradient between the heart and head in different postures [60]. The respiratory sensor maintains a stable amplitude over a 300 s test period during normal breathing (Fig. S11(b) in the ESM), confirming its excellent stability.

### 3.4 Pressure sensors

Owing to the excellent flexibility of the film and its well-formed three-dimensional conductive network, the resistance changes during the bending process. A pressure sensor composed of a single film is designed to monitor these changes. As shown in Fig. 4(a), compression of different pressures along the in-plane direction is applied to the sensor to observe its resistance variation. There are three distinct pressure ranges of 0–41, 41–78, and 78–120 kPa, with corresponding sensitivities of −1.74, −0.98, and −0.29 %·kPa−1, respectively. This high sensitivity is well-suited for stress detection in human joints. As shown in Fig. 4(b), the sensor is attached to the finger joint, demonstrating fast response time of 0.23 s and recovery time of 0.25 s, which is superior to those of pressure sensors made of other materials reported (Fig. S9(b) in the ESM) [51,61−66]. As shown in Fig. 4(c), after over 1000 cycles of compression/release, the amplitude of ∆*R*/*R*0 remains almost unchanged, and the stable signal output ensures long-term reliability. During physical activity, the degree of bending in body joints varies, leading to inconsistent changes in relative resistance. The sensor can detect mechanical deformation of different amplitudes within the pressure range. Figures 4(d)–4(g) show the resistance changes during various joint movements, such as those of the elbow, wrist, knee, and finger, each producing a distinct signal.

To investigate the relationship between structural deformation and the relative resistance change of CC/Ag2S in pressure sensing, a simple resistance model is developed. Figure 4(h) presents a schematic diagram of CC/Ag2S in both relaxed and bent states. In the relaxed state, there is a certain gap between the CFs, allowing the total original resistance (R*total) to be calculated via Eq. (7):

(7) Rtotal=l⋅Rx+m⋅Ry

where *R**x* and *R**y* are the resistances in the *x* and *y* directions, respectively, *l* and *m* are the number of resistors in *x* and *y* directions. In the original state, the total resistance of CC/Ag2S is the sum of the resistances within the circuit. Given that the CC/Ag2S film obtained via ED is relatively uniform, it is assumed that *R**x* = *R**y* = *R*0. Therefore, Eq. (7) simplifies to *R*total = (l + *m*)·*R*0. When CC/Ag2S is bent, the conductive path increases, leading to a reduction in film resistance. The formation of a conductive network decreases the contact resistance. The total resistance (*R'*total) of CC/Ag2S under bending strain can be calculated via Eq. (8):

(8) 1Rtotal′=lRx+mRy+nRc

where *R*c is the contact resistance formed after the CFs contact, and *n* is the number of the contact resistors. Equation (8) can also be simplified to Eq. (9):

(9) Rtotal′=R0⋅Rc(l+m)⋅Rc+n⋅R0

The total resistance of CC/Ag2S in the bent state is significantly lower than that in the original relaxed state. The resistance model reasonably explains the relationship between film bending deformation and the corresponding resistance change.

### 3.5 Electromagnetic shielding performance

The EMI shielding performance of CC and CC/Ag2S films in the frequency range of 8.2–12.4 GHz is shown in Fig. 5(a). The total shielding effectiveness (SET) is the sum of absorption (SEA), reflection (SER), and internal multiple reflection loss (SEM). When SET exceeds 15 dB, SEM can be ignored. Electromagnetic interference shielding effectiveness (EMI SE) of CC/Ag2S at all potentials is greater than that of CC. Notably, the CC/Ag2S deposited at −0.6 V has the highest SET of 50.1 dB, which is more than twice that of the original CC (23.6 dB). Figure 5(b) shows the average values of SER, SEA, and SET of CC/Ag2S at different potentials. SET increases with increasing SER and SEA values. SEA of all samples always exceeds SER, indicating that SEA predominantly contributes to the SET for CC/Ag2S. Since EMI SE is closely related to sample thickness, a comparative analysis with other studies [67−75] (Fig. 5(c)) shows that CC/Ag2S exceeds other materials such as CC/MXene and CC/Bi2Te3.

*
Fig. 5(a) SET and average SET, (b) SEA, and SER of pure CC and CC/Ag2S deposited at −0.54, −0.56, and −0.60 V at 8.2–12.4 GHz. (c) EMI SE vs. thickness of CC/Ag2S in this work and other materials reported. (d) SEA/SET, (e) δ*, and (f) power coefficient of pure CC and CC/Ag2S deposited at −0.54, −0.56, and −0.60 V. (g) Schematic illustration of the mechanism.

To determine the primary shielding mechanism, the SEA/SET ratios of the films deposited at different potentials are compared. As shown in Fig. 5(d), the SEA/SET ratio of CC/Ag2S deposited at −0.60 V reached 73.5%, representing a significant improvement over that of pure CC (56.6%). The skin depth (*δ*) represents the depth at which the incident wave intensity is reduced to 1/e (~36.8%) of the initial value and is closely related to the shielding ability of the materials. *δ* can be calculated via Eq. (10) [76]:

(10) δ=8.686⋅dSEA

where *d* is the film thickness. Figure 5(e) shows the variation in *δ* deposited at different potentials. Compared with that of pure CC, *δ* of CC/Ag2S deposited at −0.60 V is significantly smaller, likely due to the increased *σ* and film thickness. Figure 5(f) also shows the absorptance (*A*), reflectance (*R*), and transmittance (*T*) of the different samples. Notably, the reflection coefficient of CC/Ag2S deposited at −0.60 V is as high as 0.953, the absorption coefficient is as low as 0.047, and the transmission coefficient is almost negligible, demonstrating that the film exhibits excellent shielding performance. The contribution of absorption to the overall SET depends on its ability to attenuate nonreflected power. Since EMI SE is a relative measure and independent of absolute power values, the fact that reflected energy exceeds absorbed energy does not negate the conclusion that absorption is the dominant shielding mechanism.

The EMI shielding mechanism of the CC/Ag2S film is illustrated in Fig. 5(g). The impedance mismatch between free space and the sample causes partial reflection of incident waves, driven by synergistic effects of multiple reflections, transmission losses, and polarization between the CC and Ag2S layers. The multilayer cross-linked core‒shell structure of CC/Ag2S further enhances multiple reflections and scattering of electromagnetic waves (EMWs), extending their propagation path and weakening their energy. As EMWs pass through the fiber columns, interactions with surrounding pores lead to energy absorption. Variations in *σ* and dielectric properties, caused by the distinct phases of CC and Ag2S, induce Maxwell‒Wagner–Siller interface polarization, further enhancing the absorption performance of these materials [77]. Under an EM field, free carriers are trapped by defects during transport, causing space charge accumulation. The uneven distribution of carriers at interfaces and defect sites generates dipole moments and local fields, inducing dipole polarization, as evidenced by the charge‒density difference image of CC/Ag2S obtained via DFT calculations (Fig. 2(i)).

### 3.6 Dual-mode stability and robustness under EMI

Figures 6(a)–6(c) and S12(a) in the ESM illustrate that the sensor maintains stable performance in both temperature and pressure sensing, with no cross-interference. The sensor also exhibited minimal hysteresis across different cycles (Fig. S13 in the ESM). This can be attributed to the material properties and structural design. For temperature sensing, stability is achieved through the TE properties of Ag2S, which generate a consistent voltage proportional to the temperature gradient. The excellent thermal conductivity of CC further aids in the even distribution of heat. For pressure sensing, stability arises from the reversible change in contact resistance between layers when subjected to pressure. The flexibility of the film prevents cracking, whereas the reversible transition between point and surface contact, enabled by the mechanical robustness of the core‒shell structure, ensures repeatable and stable sensor outputs. In combined sensing, the sensor design allows temperature and pressure sensors to operate independently. This separation minimizes interference between the two sensing functions, ensuring stable outputs for both temperature and pressure.

The EMI shielding performance is crucial for the effective application of soft sensors in real-world environments. Figures 6(d)–6(i), Figs. This stability can be attributed to the low frequencies of typical electromagnetic sources [78], which do not generate significant heat or cause temperature fluctuations that affect the TE voltage. Additionally, these waves lack the energy required to excite electrons or holes in the sensor materials, thereby ensuring that the resistance remains stable. As a result, the sensor exhibits consistent performance under routine EMI.

### 3.7 Mechanical performance

One way to assess the flexibility of materials is to attach it to the surface of multiple elbows with decreasing radii and study the variation in *σ* [79]. As shown in Figs. S14(a) and S14(b) in the ESM, at a bending radius of 5 mm, the changes in *σ* and *S* of the CC/Ag2S film remain within 10%. As shown in Fig. S14(c) in the ESM, 90° artificial bending experiments show that the change in *σ* still remains within 10%, indicating excellent stability under this deformation. When a material is thin enough, it can be flexible. Hence, it is crucial to consider thickness when evaluating flexibility. Peng *et al*. [80] proposed a figure of merit for flexibility: *f*FOM = *T*h/2*r*, where *T*h is the material thickness, and *r* is the curvature radius before fracture. The higher *f*FOM indicates superior flexibility. As shown in Fig. 7(a), *f*FOM of CC/Ag2S is as high as 4.08×10−2, significantly surpassing that of commonly used TE materials such as BiSbTe and MXene [75,81−86].

*
Fig. 7(a) Figure of merit for the flexibility of CC/Ag2S films and other materials. (b) Stress‒strain curves of CC and CC/Ag2S. (c) Tensile strength vs.* break elongation of the CC/Ag2S film and other reported materials. (d) One-way repeating unit cell (RUC) of CC/Ag2S. Von-mise stress distributions of (e) CC RUC and (f) CC/Ag2S RUC at a strain of 14.37%. (g) Von Mises stress distribution of CC/Ag2S RUC at a strain of 21.83%. (h) Von Mises stress distribution of CC/Ag2S RUC at a small shear modulus and a strain of 14.37%. (i) Macroscopic mechanical von Mises stress cloud diagram based on the multilayer shell mode.

In real-world applications, wearable electronics often endure tensile stress. A low TS can lead to surface cracks, degrading performance or causing failure. Ag2S, a ductile semiconductor, exhibits metal-like flexibility with 20% bending and 4.2% tensile deformation [29]. This unique mechanical property stems from its crystal structure and slip systems, which include multiple slip mechanisms [87]. Mechanical tests revealed that CC/Ag2S exhibited a relatively stable elastic limit plateau, followed by a rapid increase in stress, culminating in fracture failure (Fig. S15 in the ESM). As shown in Fig. 7(b), TS of pure CC is 5.1 MPa, whereas the TS of CC/Ag2S increases tenfold, reaching 59.3 MPa. As shown in Fig. 7(c), CC/Ag2S has a superior TS, outperforming Bi2Te3/CC, poly (3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene): poly (styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS)/rubber and others [60,75,88−92].

Figures 7(d)–7(h) present the micromechanical analysis results from the simulations. In Fig. 7(d), the composite is simplified as a one-way RUC, where CC and Ag2S form a core‒shell structure. As shown in Fig. 1(d), the interphase layer between CC and Ag2S can be neglected. In this model, the bottom end of the RUC is fixed, whereas the opposite end is subjected to a displacement load. Figure 7(e) shows the von Mises stress distribution in CC RUC under a strain of 14.37%. The maximum stress, which is concentrated at the outer side of the fixed end, is 40.8 MPa. In contrast, Fig. 7(f) shows the von Mises stress distribution of the CC/Ag2S RUC under the same strain. For cylindrical elastic materials under uniaxial tension, stress is typically concentrated on the outer side [93]. Owing to the higher Young’s modulus of Ag2S than that of CC [94], the stress is primarily concentrated in the Ag2S shell, significantly reducing the stress experienced by CC. Under these conditions, the maximum von Mises stress on the CC is only 17.18 MPa. Since the shear modulus significantly affects the stress distribution, Fig. 7(h) shows the von Mises stress distribution when the shear modulus of CC is minimized. Even in this scenario, stress remains concentrated on Ag2S, with the maximum von Mises stress on the CC decreasing to 5.38 MPa. Figure 7(g) depicts the von Mises stress distribution for the CC/Ag2S RUC at a strain of 21.83%, where the maximum stress continues to reside within the Ag2S shell. Figure 7(i) displays the macroscopic mechanical analysis of a multilayer shell module simulated in COMSOL. When the tensile strain reaches 14.37%, the von Mises stress section diagram reveals that the maximum stress occurs at the exterior of the Ag2S shell. The simulation results demonstrate that the Ag2S shell substantially enhances TS of the composite materials. By redistributing and concentrating stress within the Ag2S shell, owing to its higher Young’s modulus, the stress on the CC core is significantly alleviated, thereby improving the overall mechanical performance. Therefore, this core‒shell structure effectively enhances the load-bearing capacity of the composite under tensile strain.

### 3.8 Antibacterial performance

Antibacterial properties are important for the long-term use of wearable electronics. The antibacterial effect of CC/Ag2S against gram-positive *Staphylococcus aureus* (*S. aureus*) was visually demonstrated via the plate colony counting method. As shown in Figs. 8(a) and 8(b), the number of *S. aureus* colonies was greatly reduced after the application of CC/Ag2S. Figure 8(c) presents a comparison of different dilution ratios (10 and 100 times). The bacterial concentration in the control group was 1.21×105 CFU·mL−1, which decreased to 6×103 CFU·mL−1 after inhibition, resulting in an antibacterial rate of 95.04%. As schematically shown in Fig. 8(d), when Ag+ ions interact with the bacterial cell membrane, they disrupt its structure and increase its permeability [32]. This disruption allows intracellular components, including cytoplasmic contents such as ions, proteins, and other molecules, to leak out of the cell. This leakage, or “outflow of cytoplasm”, damages the cell and ultimately leads to its death. This process is a key part of the antibacterial mechanism, which destabilizes the structural integrity of bacterial cells. Additionally, Ag+ ions exhibit a strong affinity for the nitrogen and oxygen atoms in nucleobases. By binding to these sites, Ag+ interferes with the hydrogen bonding between complementary bases, destabilizing the deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) double helix. Furthermore, Ag+ ions can form cross-links between DNA strands or between DNA and proteins, obstructing replication and transcription processes. This disruption compromises DNA functionality, ultimately impairing the reproductive ability of bacterial cells.

*

To ensure comfort, the film must offer high moisture permeability. This minimizes sweat-induced discomfort, keeps the skin dry, and prevents issues such as inflammation. The moisture permeability is measured by the water vapor permeability (WVP). As shown in Fig. 8(e), the intrinsically high porosity of CC endows it with excellent moisture permeability, allowing water vapor to pass through from one side to the other. Figure 8(f) shows the stability of moisture permeability over time, with noticeable changes in CC and CC/Ag2S, whereas PI has no moisture permeability. As illustrated in Fig. 8(g), WVP of CC is as high as 2987 g·m−2·d−1. In CC/Ag2S, Ag2S tightly wraps around the carbon fibers, reducing the surrounding pore spaces. Consequently, the WVP of CC/Ag2S slightly decreases to 2655 g·m−2·d−1. To visually assess the moisture permeability of CC/Ag2S, both CC/Ag2S and PI were placed between two glass bottles. The lower glass bottle was filled with hot water, while the upper bottle was kept dry. After a few minutes, numerous small droplets condense in the bottle above the CC/Ag2S film, whereas the bottle above PI remains dry, demonstrating the superior moisture permeability of CC/Ag2S (Fig. S16 in the ESM). As shown in Fig. S17 in the ESM, when the film is subjected to sonication to simulate vibration and sweating, the results confirm that Ag2S remains securely attached to CC without any signs of detachment, indicating excellent stability.

### 3.9 Abrasive resistance and Joule heating performance

Owing to their high surface area and numerous micropores, carbon-based materials tend to absorb water, which can degrade both their electrical performance and stability. Therefore, in addition to moisture permeability, the contact angles of CC and CC/Ag2S were characterized to assess the potential impact of water on the film. Moreover, the scratch test is an effective method for evaluating the abrasive resistance of a film. In this test, 600-mesh aluminum oxide sandpaper was used as an abrasion surface. As shown in Figs. S18(a) and S18(b) in the ESM, the prepared film underwent multiple cycles of abrasion with sandpaper at a weight of 25 g. Figure S18(c) in the ESM shows that the contact angle of pure CC was 85.7°. After the ED of Ag2S, the contact angle of CC/Ag2S increases to 123.1°. These contact angle measurements indicate that the sample has good hydrophobicity. Hydrophobicity is equally crucial for wearable electronics, as it helps resist interference from liquids such as rain, sweat, and water. As shown in Fig. S19 in the ESM, dual-mode temperature‒pressure tests in normal and underwater environments confirmed its excellent stability. After 30 scratch tests, the water contact angle remains as high as 118.1°, which is only 4% lower than the initial value of CC/Ag2S. These findings demonstrate that the composite film possesses stable mechanical wear resistance.

Figure S20 in the ESM shows the Joule heating properties of the single CC/Ag2S film, along with the corresponding temperature distribution captured by an infrared imager. As shown in Fig. S20(a) in the ESM, the saturation temperature of the device can be modulated by varying the applied voltage. When the applied voltage increases from 0 to 3 V, the saturation temperature increases from 25.0 to 34.9 °C. Figure S20(b) in the ESM demonstrates that the saturation temperature is reached in ~45 s. As shown in Fig. S20(c) in the ESM, the device exhibits a repeatable heating and cooling process when different voltages were applied. In addition, the generator shows consistent temperature changes over six cycles (Fig. S20(d) in the ESM), confirming its excellent heating durability. The moderate voltage used ensures personal safety, allowing the device to be integrated into the garment as a wearable heater, which can be self-powered by a portable battery.

## 4 Conclusions

Traditional dual-sensor designs are complex, and modern wearable electronics require materials that combine high EMI shielding, TS, and antibacterial properties. This work presents a bimodal temperature‒pressure sensor with significant potential for health monitoring, wearable devices, and robotic applications. By employing a simple electrodeposition method, core-shell carbon fiber/Ag2S thin films were developed, enabling high temperature sensitivity (response time: 0.7 s) and self-powered sensing through a thermoelectric mechanism, effectively detecting finger touches and respiratory states. For pressure sensing, the shell-to-shell contact mechanism delivers exceptional sensitivity with ultrafast response time of 0.2 s, allowing precise detection of human behaviors, including microexpressions and body movements. In addition to its sensing capabilities, the film exhibits doubled EMI shielding, a 10-fold improvement in TS, over 95% antibacterial efficacy, and excellent stability. These combined features position the sensor as a promising solution for self-powered electronic skin systems in health monitoring, wearable, artificial intelligence (AI), and other advanced technologies.
