## 1 Introduction

As a kind of ultra-high temperature ceramics (UHTCs), transition metal carbide ceramics (such as ZrC, TiC, and NbC) have high melting points, Young’s moduli, high-temperature stabilities, and other excellent mechanical and thermal properties and are widely used in the aerospace industry, nuclear industry, machinery, metallurgy field, etc. [1,2]. With the development of the aerospace and nuclear industries, the performance requirements for ultrahigh-temperature materials have become increasingly stringent.

After the report of the high-entropy alloy concept [3], high-entropy materials have received extensive attention due to their special properties resulting from their complex compositions [4,5]. Multiplecomponents provide unexpected properties and broad composition design space for high-entropy materials [6,7]. In recent years, research on high-entropy carbide ceramics (i.e., multicomponent carbide ceramics) has made great progress since the first report of multicomponent (ZrHfNbTa)C carbide ceramics by Castle *et al*. [8]. Compared with those of single-component carbide ceramics, the comprehensive properties of multicomponent ceramics have improved [9]. The room temperature mechanical properties of multicomponent carbide ceramics are greater than those of monocarbides or binary carbides due to the formation of solid solutions [10,11]. Moreover, the oxidation resistance in air or water vapor, creep resistance, and high-temperature mechanical properties of multicomponent carbide ceramics are improved due to the complex composition, electronic structure, and lattice distortion, which have already been verified by experimental results in previous reports [12−14]. With more thorough studies on multicomponent carbide ceramics, many efforts have been made to investigate quaternary, quinary, senary, and even nonary system carbides [15−17]. However, most of the research on multicomponent carbide ceramics has focused on single-phase ceramics, for which it is difficult to achieve further significant advances in comprehensive performance, especially in terms of mechanical properties, according to existing reports.

To the best of our knowledge, multiphase ceramics usually possess relatively excellent mechanical properties compared to single-phase ceramics because of their high relative densities, fine grain sizes, and the occurrence of the second phase [18]. In addition, phase decomposition can directly form multiphase ceramics without the addition of a second phase, and the microstructure from *in situ* phase decomposition usually has a certain orientation relationship between the two obtained phases, which may be likely to result in novel mechanical properties. A great amount of research on the phase decomposition of (Ti,Zr)C binary carbides has indicated that multiphase carbide ceramics can improve the mechanical properties effectively because of the grain refinement derived from spinodal decomposition without the addition of sintering aids [19−21]. Borgh *et al*. [22] reported that the (Ti,Zr)C powders synthesized at 2200 °C by carbothermal reduction decomposed into two phases (a Zr-rich phase and a Ti-rich phase) with lamellar nanostructures after 1300 °C annealing, which was beneficial for hardening and strengthening.

Notably, the ratio of metal to carbon in transition metal carbides is usually nonstoichiometric, and the percentage of carbon vacancies can reach up to 50% [23]. The results show that a change in carbon content can significantly affect the phase, structure, sintering activity, mechanical properties, and thermal properties of multicomponent carbide ceramics [24−26]. Liu *et al*. [27] reported that the presence of carbon vacancies could even increase the lattice rigidity in multicomponent carbide ceramics. In addition, Razumovskiy *et al*. [28] calculated the miscibility gaps of (Ti,Zr)C by the first-principles method, which proved that the carbon content could significantly affect the decomposition temperature of the miscibility gaps. Nevertheless, there are few experimental reports about phase decomposition caused by a decrease in the carbon content, which is composed of two carbide phases with FCC structure in the form of alternating lamellar nanostructures. Hence, the study of phase decomposition induced by variations in carbon content has extensive potential for improving mechanical properties.

In this work, to achieve phase decomposition of two carbides with FCC structures and improve their comprehensive mechanical properties, a series of nonstoichiometric (TiZrVNb)C*x* ceramics with different carbon contents were fabricated by spark plasma sintering (SPS) at different temperatures. The phase composition, microstructure evolution, and mechanical properties derived from variations in the carbon content and sintering temperatures were investigated in detail.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

Commercially available TiC (particle size 1.5–2 μm, purity ≥ 98.0%, Zhuzhou Haokun Hard Materials Co., Ltd., China), ZrC (particle size 1.0–1.5 μm, purity ≥ 98.0%, Zhuzhou Haokun Hard Materials Co., Ltd., China), VC (particle size 1.0–1.5 μm, purity ≥ 98.0%, Zhuzhou Haokun Hard Materials Co., Ltd., China), NbC (particle size 1.0–1.5 μm, purity ≥ 98.0%, Zhuzhou Haokun Hard Materials Co., Ltd., China), TiH2 (particle size 1.0–1.5 μm, purity ≥ 99.8%, Jinzhou Haixin Metal Materials Co., Ltd., China), and ZrH2 (particle size 1.0–1.5 μm, purity ≥ 99.8%, Jinzhou Haixin Metal Materials Co., Ltd., China) were used as the starting raw materials. Therein, equimolar amounts of TiH2 and ZrH2 were introduced to adjust the carbon content. For the (TiZrVNb)C*x* ceramics, the content of metal atoms is equimolar, and the carbon content (*x*) is theoretically 1.0, 0.9, 0.8, and 0.75. There is an inevitable difference in the carbon content between the theoretical and experimental values. To avoid ambiguity and simplify the description, the carbon contents are named C1, C2, C3, and C4, corresponding to 1.0, 0.9, 0.8, and 0.75, respectively.

After weighing the corresponding qualities of carbide and hydride power, the powder mixtures were ball-milled homogeneously by a high-energy planetary mill (P4, Fritsch, Germany) in a cemented carbide jar at 600 r/min for 20 h with cemented carbide balls as the medium. The ball-to-powder ratio was selected to be 20 : 1 in weight. After milling, the powder mixtures were passed through an 80-mesh sieve in an argon atmosphere. Then, the monolithic samples were fabricated by SPS furnace (HPD25 SI, FCT, Germany) in graphite dies lined with graphite foil (~0.4 mm in thickness). All of the samples were heated to the desired sintering temperature (2200–2400 °C) at 50 °C/min for a holding time of 10 min under argon atmosphere protection. Afterwards, the compacts were cooled to room temperature at 100 °C/min. The bulk samples were designated in accordance with their carbon contents and sintering temperatures. For example, C1-24 indicates that this sample was sintered at 2400 °C with a carbon content of C1. In addition, a ~1 mm thick layer on both surfaces of all bulk samples was removed by grinding for later microstructure and property characterization. Figure 1 shows a schematic illustration of the preparation of multicomponent carbide ceramics with different carbon contents. In addition, heat treatment was conducted at 2100 °C for 10 h at a heating rate of 10 °C/min in a pressureless sintering furnace (WS0404, Ningxia Sincere Co., Ltd., China).

### 2.2 Characterization

The phase composition of the different samples was analyzed by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Panaytical Corp., Empyrean, the Netherlands). The diffraction data were collected using Cu-Kα radiation at 40 kV and 100 mA with a 2*θ* range of 20°–90° for phase composition analysis and Le-Bail refinement. The FullProf suite software package was used to calculate the lattice parameters of all the solid solution phases. Field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; SU5000, Hitachi, Japan) with an X-ray spectrometer (AZTEC, Oxford Instrument, UK) was used to observe the microstructure and measure the elemental content of the polished surface and fracture surface of the samples. Wavelength dispersive spectroscopy (WDS) was performed with a microanalyzer (JXA-8230, JEOL, Japan) to perform electron probe microanalysis (EPMA) of the element distribution. A transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200X G2, FEI, USA) equipped with a Super-X energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) detector was also used for high-resolution TEM (HRTEM), selected area electron diffraction (SAED), and other analyses. In addition, the relative densities of all the samples were measured by image analysis software (Photoshop CS6, Adobe, USA) according to the area fraction of the pores in the images of the polished surface by counting several positions. A carbon/sulfur analyzer (CS744, LECO, USA) and an oxygen/nitrogen analyzer (ON736, LECO, USA) were used to determine the carbon and oxygen contents of the ceramics, respectively. After the bound carbon was dissolved in inorganic acid and the test solution was filtrated, the free carbon content was determined subsequently. After weighing a certain mass of the sample, the sample was dissolved in hydrofluoric acid and nitric acid by heating in a water bath at 90 °C for 30 min. The dissolved sample was filtered through filter paper and then washed with distilled water to neutralize it. After burning the filter paper and filter residue at 450 °C for 30 min, the free carbon content could be determined according to the mass of the ash and the mass of the filter paper and filter residue.

Under the test condition of 4.9 N loading for 15 s dwell, the hardness test was performed by Vickers indentation (HVS-30). Three-point flexural strength (*σ*) tests were performed on 3 mm × 4 mm × 20 mm bars on a mechanical testing system (Instron-5569) with a 16 mm span and a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min at room temperature. According to Niihara’s equation [29], the indentation fracture toughness, *K*IC, was calculated by the crack lengths of the indents. The Young’s modulus (*E*) was measured on ultrasonic equipment (RFDA-HTVP1750C, IMCE, Belgium).

### 2.3 Computational methods

The Vienna *Ab initio* Simulation Package (VASP) [30,31] was used to carry out the first-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Structural optimizations were performed by the projector-augmented wave (PAW) [32,33] coupled with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional [34]. The structural optimizations for individual metal carbides were completed using unit cells in our previous study [35]. Since transition metal carbides possess a typical rock-salt structure, the metal atoms and carbon atoms occupy one face-centered cubic (FCC) sublattice. The metal atoms were assumed to be randomly arranged in the cation sublattice, similar to the carbon atoms and carbon vacancies in the anion sublattice. Here, the “MCSQS” code of the Alloy Theoretic Automated Toolkit (ATAT) [36] was adopted to generate special quasirandom structures (SQSs) [37] with 40 atoms. To calculate the lattice constants and total energies of the SQS supercells, a *Γ*-centered 6 × 6 × 6 *k*-point grid and a kinetic energy cutoff of 500 eV were selected. In addition, to ensure full relaxation for all atoms, the geometric optimization process was terminated when the force components were smaller than 0.02 eV/Å. The formation energy Δ*H*form can be expressed as Eq. (1):

(1) ΔHform=EHEC−∑inciEi

where *E**i* is the DFT ground-state energy of metal and carbon elements and *c**i* is the molar fraction of the *i*-th component.

*Ω*A–B can be described as a function of the A–A, B–B, and A–B interaction energies in terms of nearest neighbor interactions (Eq. (2)) [38]:

(2) ΩA−B=zL[uA−B−12(uA−A+uB−B)]

where *z* is the coordination number; *L* is Avogadro’s number; *u*A–A, *u*B–B, and *u*A–B are the energies of the A–A, B–B, and A–B interactions, respectively.

The XRD patterns of the (TiZrVNb)C*x* samples with different carbon contents sintered at different temperatures are shown in Fig. 2(a). Enlarged views of the patterns within a certain range can be observed in Figs. 2(b)–2(d) for a distinct comparison. A single-phase multicomponent carbide ceramic with a rock-salt structure is basically formed for the C1-24 sample. Herein, the FCC multicomponent carbide phase with equimolar metal atoms is called the “homogenetic phase” in the later description to distinguish other carbide phases in this work. With decreasing carbon content, two other FCC structure diffraction peaks appear at the two sides of the diffraction peaks of the homogenetic phase in the patterns, namely, the Zr-poor phase and Zr-rich phase, which are determined by the following EDS results. This case is similar to the phase decomposition of (Ti,Zr)C [22] and indicates that the reduction in carbon content promotes phase decomposition in the (TiZrVNb)C*x* system rather than the formation of a single phase. With decreasing the sintering temperature, the shoulder peak of these samples widens, indicating that the formation of a solid solution was not complete for the C1-22 and C2-22 samples. However, the diffraction peaks do not widen and completely form two phases for the C3-22 sample, which indicates that the phase separation of the system caused by the reduction in carbon content may be thermodynamically spontaneous. The formation energies of (TiZrVNb)C*x* with different carbon contents were calculated via DFT calculations (Fig. 3), which decrease first with the carbon content decreasing from 1.0 to 0.85 and then increase with the carbon content decreasing from 0.85 to 0.5. This means that (TiZrVNb)C*x* with a carbon content of 0.85 is the most stable in this system. Hence, in the carbon content range of 0.73–0.87 in this work, the thermodynamic stability decreases with decreasing carbon content, which may lead to phase decomposition.

Moreover, it is apparent that the diffraction peak position of the homogenetic phase shifts to high angles with decreasing the carbon content (Fig. 2(c)), which means that the lattice parameters decrease with decreasing the carbon content. The relationships between the lattice parameters and carbon content in the (TiZrVNb)C*x* ceramics were calculated via DFT calculations (Fig. 3), which reveals that the lattice parameters increase within a small range and then decrease with decreasing the carbon content. In the carbon content range of these samples (0.73–0.86), the variation trend of the lattice parameters from the Le-Bail refinement (Table 2) is in agreement with that from the DFT calculations. The same trend was observed for the (TiZrHfVNbTa)C*x* and (TiZrNbTaMo)C*x* ceramics in Refs. [25,26]. In addition, the diffraction peak positions of the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase in C2-23 sample are approximately the same as those in C2-24 sample, which demonstrates that the lattice parameters of the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase change little. The situation is the same for the C3-23 and C3-24 samples. Nevertheless, the diffraction peak positions of the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase in C3-22 sample apparently shift to low angles and high angles, respectively. This means that the lattice parameters of the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase undergo some changes. The shift in the positions of the diffraction peaks represents the variation in the corresponding lattice parameters. The variation in the lattice parameter is confirmed by the results of Le-Bail refinement, as listed in Table 2, which can be attributed to the difference in the content of metal atoms. The metal element contents of the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase in the C3 and C4 samples are shown in Table 3 and were determined by EDS. The Ti, Zr, V, and Nb contents in the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase for the C2 samples sintered at 2300 and 2400 °C change little, and the variation in the content of each metal element is no more than 1 at%. The results are the same for the C3 samples sintered at 2300 and 2400 °C. As a consequence, the lattice parameter rarely changes as well. However, the Zr content of the Zr-rich phase in the C3-22 sample increases significantly in contrast with that in the C3-22 sample, corresponding to its decrease in the Zr-poor phase. Since the radius of the Zr atom is larger than that of other metal atoms, the addition of Zr in the Zr-rich phase results in an increase in the lattice parameter. Moreover, the decrease of the Zr content in the Zr-poor phase leads to a decrease in the lattice parameter. With the sintering temperature increasing from 2200 to 2300 °C, the distribution of metal elements becomes more uniform, which indicates that the temperature increase promotes the diffusion of metal atoms. However, there is nearly no change in the metal content in the Zr-rich phase and Zr-poor phase for the C2 samples, even though the sintering temperature increases to 2400 °C.

### 3.2 Microstructure evolution and phase decomposition

SEM images of the (TiZrVNb)C*x* samples with different carbon contents sintered at different temperatures are shown in Fig. 4. The Zr-poor phase and Zr-rich phase can be clearly observed in the SEM images. Taking the C2-24 sample as an example, the dark and bright phases marked by the arrows in Fig. 4(b) correspond to the Zr-poor phase and Zr-rich phase, respectively. Little free carbon can be found in the images of the C1 samples, as shown in Fig. 4(a), which is consistent with the results of the carbon content analysis. However, there are no diffraction peaks for carbon in the XRD patterns because of the low carbon content. An incomplete solid solution can be observed in the C1-22, C1-23, and C2-22 samples, which is in good agreement with the XRD results. With decreasing the carbon content, there is only a Zr-poor phase and Zr-rich phase in C3-22 sample, even with no homogenetic phase and incomplete solid solution. The phase separation with the reduction in carbon content in this system may be spontaneous in thermodynamics. This phenomenon indicates that a decrease in the carbon content can promote the diffusion of metal atoms rather than the formation of single-phase multicomponent ceramics, which have some distinctions compared with Refs. [25,39]. With increasing the sintering temperature, the relative volume fraction of the homogenetic phase also increases, corresponding to an increase in the diffraction peak intensity. Notably, there is an alternate lamellar nanostructure composed of a Zr-poor phase and a Zr-rich phase in the C2 and C3 samples sintered at 2300 and 2400 °C, respectively. This microstructure is similar to that of phase decomposition in (Ti,Zr)C ceramics after heat treatment [19,22], forming nodular structures with a width of several hundred nanometers and a coherent or semicoherent orientation relationship between two phases. To confirm the phase decomposition behavior, the C2-24 sample was heat-treated at 2100 °C for 10 h, as shown in Fig. 5. According to the comparison of the SEM micrographs, apparent phase decomposition occurs in the C2-24 sample after heat treatment, which effectively supports the occurrence of phase decomposition. Thermodynamics show that phase decomposition is spontaneous.

Afterwards, the metal element distributions of the C2-23 and C3-23 samples were determined by EPMA (Fig. 6). It is apparent that Zr and V are distributed in two different phases with the most significant differences in content. In addition, Ti is also abundant in the Zr-poor phase, and the Nb distribution is more uniform in the two phases, with a slightly high content in the Zr-poor phase. This result coincides with the EDS analysis (Table 3). To reveal the reason for the metal element distribution, the interaction coefficients among all the metal elements in the transition metal carbides were calculated via DFT calculations. The values of the interaction coefficients Ω*Ti–Zr, *Ω*Ti–V, *Ω*Ti–Nb, *Ω*Zr–V, *Ω*Zr–Nb, and *Ω*V–Nb are 55.178, 1.566, −9.708, 124.301, 3.927, and 38.134 kJ/mol, respectively. The value is lower, and the ability to form a solid solution is stronger. As a consequence, the Zr makes it difficult to form a solid solution with Ti and V due to such high interaction coefficients, which results in the formation of a Zr-poor phase and a Zr-rich phase. The enrichment of Ti in the Zr-poor phase can be attributed to both the high value of *Ω*Ti−Zr and the low value of *Ω*Ti−V. Meanwhile, since all the values of *Ω*Ti−Nb, *Ω*Zr−Nb, and *Ω*V−Nb are relatively low, the Nb element distribution is relatively uniform in the two phases as well. It has been reported that the formation energy of Nb vacancies is lower than those of other metal atoms in this case [40,41]. Hence, the diffusion of Nb is easier, which can also explain its uniform distribution. It is worth noting that the distribution of carbon atoms is also different in the Zr-poor phase and Zr-rich phase, in addition to the metal atoms, which have not been reported in open-reported studies about the phase decomposition of carbide ceramics. The content of carbon in the Zr-poor phase is slightly greater than that in the Zr-rich phase, which might be attributed to the different valence states and M−C bonding. This phenomenon reveals that not only metal atoms but also carbon atoms will be distributed anew during the process of phase decomposition in multicomponent carbides.

Then, TEM characterization was performed to further study the orientation relationship between the Zr-poor phase and Zr-rich phase. Figure 7(b) shows the selected area electron diffraction (SEAD) pattern of the interface between the Zr-poor phase and Zr-rich phase in the C2-24 sample (Fig. 7(a)) with doublet diffraction spots, which suggests the occurrence of a semicoherent orientation relationship of the {1 1¯0}Zr-poor phase/{1 1¯0}Zr-rich phase. A continuous lattice image of the semicoherent interface is shown in Fig. 7(c), and the interplanar spacings of the (111) planes of the Zr-poor and Zr-rich phases are determined to be 0.2375 and 0.2481 nm, respectively. Coherent or semicoherent interfaces can usually be observed in phase decomposition structures as effective evidence of phase decomposition in high-entropy alloys and (Ti,Zr)C [22,42,43]. Moreover, there is a discrete dark contrast at the interface between the two phases because of the lattice mismatch of both sides, which can be clearly observed in Fig. 7(c). The fast Fourier transform (FFT) of the HRTEM image of the semicoherent interface is shown in the inset of Fig. 7(c). Figures 7(e) and 7(f) are the inverted FFTs (IFFTs) of the (11 1¯) and (111) planes, respectively. There are several edge dislocations at the semicoherent interface (Figs. 7(e) and 7(f)), which results in a strain concentration at the interface. The lattice mismatch is adjusted by these edge dislocations. It has been reported that the lattice mismatch between TiC-rich and ZrC-rich phases in the phase decomposition structure of (Ti,Zr)C leads to a strain field with a periodic contrast pattern [19,21]. In addition, a semicoherent interface between the Zr-poor and Zr-rich phases can also be found in the C3-24 sample (Fig. 8) with the orientation relationship of the {01 1¯}Zr-poor phase/{01 1¯}Zr-rich phase. In these cases, the nanometer nodular structures of the Zr-poor and Zr-rich phases with a semicoherent relationship and the same crystal structure are similar to typical spinodal decomposition rather than nucleation and growth.

### 3.3 Mechanical properties

The mechanical properties of the (TiZrVNb)C*x* samples with different carbon contents sintered at different temperatures are listed in Table 4. The samples sintered at 2300 °C possess greater hardness than those sintered at other temperatures, and the Vickers hardness of the C1-23 and C2-23 samples reach approximately 28 GPa. The relatively high Vickers hardness can be attributed to the alternating lamellar nanostructures of phase decomposition, especially the high dislocation density and high strain concentration in the intragranular region and at the interface induced by phase decomposition (Figs. 7(a), 7(e), and 7(f)). However, with decreasing the carbon content, the Vickers hardness also decreases, even though the volume fraction of phase decomposition increases for the C3 and C4 samples. This is caused by the decrease in the covalent bond strength due to the decrease in the carbon content. As reported, the covalent bond strength of nonstoichiometric carbides weakens because of the existence of anion vacancies, which leads to a decrease in hardness [25,44].

The negative effect of carbon vacancies on the mechanical properties also affects the flexural strength, fracture toughness, and Young’s modulus, the values of which decrease with decreasing the carbon content. According to the micrographs of the fracture surfaces, the fracture surfaces become much smoother with decreasing carbon content, as shown in Figs. 9(e) and 9(f), which indicates that the intragranular bond strength decreases as well. However, it is worth noting that the strength of C2-23 sample is much greater than that of C1-23 sample, reaching 473 MPa with a lower carbon content. On one hand, the existence of a high dislocation density and stress concentration can prevent the sliding of dislocations, which can improve the strength [45,46]. Yang *et al*. [47] reported that semicoherent interfaces, as a barrier to dislocation penetration, changed the deformation behavior from dislocation slipping to amorphization, which can result in effective strengthening. On the other hand, the grain refinement caused by phase decomposition can also increase the strength based on the Hall‒Petch rule. For the C2-24 and C2-22 samples, the relatively low strength is due to the large grains with greater volume fractions and the lack of semicoherent interfaces induced by phase decomposition, respectively.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, a series of equimolar (TiZrVNb)C*x* ceramics with different carbon contents were fabricated by SPS at different temperatures. The phase composition, microstructural evolution, and mechanical properties derived from variations in the carbon content and sintering temperature were investigated in detail. Phase decomposition in the form of spinodal-like decomposition was discovered. An equimolar single-phase salt-rock solid solution decomposes into two different salt-rock solid solutions with alternating lamellar nanostructures during the decomposition process, namely, the Zr-poor phase and Zr-rich phase. The interfaces between the Zr-poor phase and the Zr-rich phase possess a semicoherent orientation relationship. The high dislocation density and strain concentration at the semicoherent interfaces can effectively prevent the slipping and growth of dislocations and play an important role in hardening and strengthening under the synergistic effect of grain refinement. The best comprehensive mechanical properties, such as a Vickers hardness of 27.9 GPa, a flexural strength of 473 MPa, and a fracture toughness of 3.1 MPa·m1/2, were achieved in the C2-23 sample. Compared to single-phase high-entropy carbide ceramics, the multiphase multicomponent carbides achieved by phase decomposition have a significant advantage in terms of hardness and strength. This work provides important insight into the microstructure evolution and optimization of the mechanical properties of multicomponent carbide ceramics.
