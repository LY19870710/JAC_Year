## 1 Introduction

High-entropy oxides (HEOs) are solid solutions consisting of five or more elements on the same sublattice and have garnered much attention because of their high-entropy effects [1−3]. Among HEOs, high-entropy fluorite oxides (HEFOs) have wide application prospects in the field of thermal protection due to their low thermal conductivity, high Young’s modulus, and desirable performance at high temperatures [2−4]. Most previous research has focused primarily on the synthesis, phase composition, and properties of new HEFOs [5−11]. For instance, Gild *et* *al*. [10] synthesized eight single-phase HEFOs, which exhibited thermal conductivities ranging from 1.1 to 1.81 W·m−1·K−1, lower than that of 8 mol% yttria-stabilized zirconia (8YSZ, 2.02±0.17 W·m−1·K−1). They attributed the lower thermal conductivity to the solid solution of heavy elements and the scattering of phonons by point defects. Ping *et* *al*. [11] prepared four HEFOs with the general formula (Y0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Ce0.2X0.2)O2−*δ* (X = La, Nd, Gd, and Dy) and reported that the thermal conductivity of these HEFOs ranged from 1.68 to 2.18 W·m−1·K−1 at temperatures between 25 and 900 °C. Notably, (Y0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Ce0.2Gd0.2)O2−*δ* exhibited a superior thermal expansion coefficient (CTE) and oxygen barrier property compared with those of yttria-stabilized zirconia. Some HEFOs exhibit higher CTEs and calcia–magnesia–alumina–silicates (CMAS) corrosion resistance than 8YSZ [12]. Hu *et* *al*. [8] investigated the high-temperature stability of typical (CeHfZrGdLa)O*x* HEFOs and provided significant practical implications for the potential utilization of HEFOs in high-temperature applications.

In addition to the synthesis and properties of new HEFOs, some researchers have studied the factors affecting the formability of HEFOs. Spiridigliozzi *et* *al*. [13] conducted a systematic analysis of eighteen rare-earth-based oxides with equimolar compositions and suggested that single-phase HEFOs could be formed when the standard deviation of the cationic radii of the involved elements was greater than 0.095. This criterion may not be universally applicable to all HEFOs [14,15] but may be more suitable for distinguishing between the formation of fluorite and bixbyite structures [13,16]. In the study by Kumbhakar *et* *al*. [17], a series of materials with nearly equimolar concentrations of 4, 5, 6, or 7 different cations (Ce, La, Sm, Pr, Tb, Y, and Zr) were synthesized, and it was observed that a high configuration entropy (1.7*R*) was beneficial for stabilizing the fluorite structure. Recently, in an investigation of entropy-stabilized fluorite oxides by Kumar *et* *al*. [18], X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of a (Ce0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Sn0.2Ti0.2)O2 bulk sample showed a single-phase fluorite structure, whereas XRD pattern of powder sample corresponded to a multiphase sample. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) results showed that the intensities of the oxygen vacancy and chemisorbed oxygen (OC) peaks in the bulk sample were much greater than those in the powder sample. Kumar *et* *al*. [18] reported that the difference in the O 1s core-level spectra of the bulk and powder samples was the reason for such phase composition differences between the bulk and powder samples. Thus, they suggested that the formation of the fluorite structure could be influenced by oxygen vacancies and the oxidation states of the cations. Kumar *et* *al*. [18] also emphasized the significance of maintaining Ce4+ for the successful synthesis of HEFOs. However, a change in the valence of Ce was still observed in some HEFOs [19,20]. Emergence of Ce3+ increases the ionic radius and generates oxygen vacancies. Overall, research on the influencing factors of HEFO formation has been neither comprehensive nor systematic, and no effective design method for HEFOs has been proposed.

Furthermore, the range of elements used in these materials is relatively narrow, although a variety of HEFOs have been produced. A comprehensive analysis of the literature revealed that most synthesized HEFOs contain at least one of four elements, Zr, Ce, Hf, or Pr, while the remaining elements are typically rare earth elements, Al, Si, Mn, Fe, Sn, or Ti. For instance, in the studies conducted by Cheng *et* *al*. [9], Ce, Hf, Y, Pr, and Gd occupied the cation site of the fluorite structure. For another example, in the studies conducted by Gild *et* *al*. [10] and Wright *et* *al*. [21], the primary elements in the five principal-element HEFOs were Ce, Zr, and Hf, with the remaining elements coming from Y, Yb, Ca, Mg, La, Gd, and Ti. Djenadic *et* *al*. [14] prepared three equimolar HEFOs using rare earth nitrates as raw materials, incorporating five to seven principal elements. Overall, the cations used in the synthesized HEFOs are primarily +3 and +4, which restricts the potential to fully exploit the advantages of high-entropy materials and their vast compositional space.

In this study, (Ce0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Sn0.2Ti0.2)O2 was selected as one of the main materials, and CaO was used to introduce oxygen vacancies to the samples. We first investigated the effects of the CaO content on the formability of single-phase (Ca*x*(Ce0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Sn0.2Ti0.2)1−*x*)O2−*δ* (*x* = 0–0.3). Then, a grid search was performed near the two single-phase compositions (Ca*x*(Ce0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Sn0.2Ti0.2)1−*x*)O2−*δ* (*x* = 0.2 and 0.22)) to systematically study the influence of the mean cation radius (* r¯) and oxygen vacancy concentration (Ovac) on the formability of single-phase (Cax*Ce*y*1Zr*y*2Hf*z*Sn*z*Ti*z*)O2−*δ*. The above research findings were further validated in three new designed HEFOs based on the valence combination strategy [22], which were composed of elements with different valences ranging from +3 to +6. The room temperature thermal conductivity of the as-prepared single-phase HEFOs was measured, and the corrected thermal conductivity of the dense materials was calculated. The effects of oxygen vacancies and disorder on the corrected thermal conductivity were investigated. Finally, a novel approach for the compositional design of HEFOs with low thermal conductivity was developed, which will contribute to expanding the composition range and advancing performance research on HEFOs.

### 2.1 Compositional design

To investigate the effects of oxygen vacancies and * r¯ on the formability of HEFOs, we designed the composition of the samples using different methods. First, considering the mechanism of doped fully stabilized zirconia and the role of oxygen vacancies in the formation of HEFOs, Ca2+ was introduced into (Ce0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Sn0.2Ti0.2)O2 to obtain oxygen vacancies. Then, based on the above results, (Cax*Ce*y*1Zr*y*2Hf*z*Sn*z*Ti*z*)O2−*δ* was designed to obtain samples with different  r¯ and oxygen vacancy concentration (Ovac). After that, three new compositions were designed to verify the effects of  r¯ and Ovac. The detailed composition design process is as follows.

#### 2.1.1 Designing with respect to mechanism of doped fully stabilized zirconia

In general, pure ZrO2 exhibits a monoclinic structure at room temperature. However, the addition of suitable amounts of low-valent oxides such as CaO and Y2O3 to pure ZrO2 allows it to maintain a stable cubic structure at room temperature. When one Zr4+ (0.84 Å) is substituted by one Ca2+ (1.12 Å), one oxygen vacancy will be generated due to charge conservation. This nonequivalent substitution solid solution typically results in larger cation and oxygen vacancies within the ZrO2 lattice, which is believed to be the reason for the stability of cubic ZrO2 [23]. Additionally, Kumar *et* *al*. [18] proposed a potential connection between the formation of the fluorite structure and the presence of oxygen vacancies. In our previous study, we discovered that substituting certain cations in (Ce0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Sn0.2Ti0.2)O2 with CaO resulted in the formation of single-phase HEFOs that contained oxygen vacancies [6,15]. Herein, we explored the effects of adding CaO on the formability of (Ca,Ce,Zr,Hf,Sn,Ti)O2−*δ* HEFOs by considering * r¯ and Ovac.  r¯ was calculated using Eq. (1):

(1) r¯=∑i=1n′ciri

where  n′, c**i*, and *r**i* represent the number of cation-site elements, the mole fraction of the *i*th cation, and the ionic radius of the *i*th cation, respectively.

The nonstoichiometry *c**i* must satisfy the condition of * ∑i=16ci=1. #### 2.1.3 Element extension and experimental verification

To expand the selection range of elements, a valence combination strategy was used. The valence combination strategy [22] is a two-step method in which possible valence combinations are determined first according to electrical balance, and cations are then selected based on geometrical requirements. This strategy has been successfully applied to the compositional design of high-entropy perovskite oxides [22] and high-entropy pyrochlore oxides [24]. According to this strategy, elements with valences exceeding +3/+4 can be selected for the design of HEFOs. For fluorite oxide, the average valence of the selected cation should be +4. By considering the cation radius, ion stability, and cost of the raw materials, three equimolar high-entropy compositions can be achieved. The molar ratio of the chosen cations can be adjusted to easily modify * r¯ and Ovac of the designed combinations. ### 2.2 Raw materials and sample preparation

Commercially available CaCO3 (99.5%), La2O3 (99.99%), Y2O3 (99.9%), Yb2O3 (99.99%), CeO2 (99.95%), ZrO2 (99.99%), HfO2 (99.9%), SnO2 (99.5%), TiO2 (99%), Nb2O5 (99.99%), Ta2O5 (99.5%), and WO3 (99.99%) were used as raw materials to synthesize the designed samples.

The samples were synthesized using the solid-state reaction synthesis method, and we adopted a one-time *in* *situ* sintering process to save energy. We first weighed the raw materials according to the designed chemical formula and then mixed the powders with ammonium polyacrylate (dispersant) and anhydrous ethanol by ball mixing at 100 r·min−1 for 24 h. The mixed slurries were then dried, sieved through a 200-mesh sieve, and uniaxially pressed into disc-shaped green bodies. Finally, the green bodies were placed on a Pt plate, calcined at 330 °C for 2 h to remove the organic materials, and sintered at different temperatures for 5 h to synthesize the samples.

During the sintering process, samples containing CaCO3 were calcined at 750 °C for an additional 1.5 h to ensure complete decomposition. The heating rate of the sintering process was 2 °C/min, and after sintering, the samples were cooled in the furnace.

### 2.3 Characterizations

Sintered samples were ground into powders, and phase composition of powder samples was analyzed by an X-ray diffractometer (D8ADVANCE A25, Bruker, Germany). Elemental state of powder samples was analyzed using an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (ESCALB Xi+, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Czech Republic). XPS measurements utilized monochromatic Al Kα radiation (*hν* = 1486.68 eV), and spectra were corrected using Avantage software (version 5.9922) based on C 1s (284.8 eV). Surface morphology of as-sintered bulk samples was observed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Merlin Compact, Zeiss, Germany). Elemental distribution was investigated using an electron energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS) on an instrument equipped with FE-SEM. Grain size was measured from SEM images of at least 100 grains using ImageJ software (version 1.51j8).

Theoretical density of single-phase samples can be calculated using the unit cell mass and the lattice parameter. Bulk density (*ρ*b) was determined by the Archimedes method using Eq. (4):

(4) ρb=m1m3−m2×ρl

where *m*1 is the mass of the dried samples; *m*2 is the mass of the saturated samples in deionized water; *m*3 is the mass of the saturated samples; *ρ*l is the density of water (0.9982 g·cm−3). Total porosity can be obtained by comparing the theoretical density and *ρ*b.

Room temperature thermal conductivity of the samples was measured using a thermal constant analyzer (TPS 2500S, Hot Disk, Sweden) on disk-shaped samples with a diameter of 15 mm and a height of 4 mm. To investigate the important factors affecting the intrinsic thermal conductivity, all thermal conductivities listed in this study are the values of dense samples corrected based on Eq. (5) [25]:

(5) k=(kRT−kgP1/144)/k=(kRT−kgP1/144)(1−P)3/322(1−P)3/322

where *k* is the corrected thermal conductivity of dense materials; *k*RT is the measured thermal conductivity of samples; *k*g is the thermal conductivity of air (0.0259 W·m−1·K−1); *P* is the total porosity.

#### 3.1.1 Effect of CaO content on phase composition of (Ca,Ce,Zr,Hf,Sn,Ti)O<sub>2−δ</sub>

All of the (Ca*x*(Ce0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Sn0.2Ti0.2)1−*x*)O2−*δ* (*x* = 0–0.3) samples were prepared by *in* *situ* sintering using identical process parameters. Samples were sintered at 1550 °C for 5 h, and XRD patterns of the powder samples are shown in Fig. 1. It is evident that in the absence of CaO, sample F1-0 is composed of various phases, including a fluorite phase, a tetragonal phase (PDF#38-1436), a monoclinic phase (PDF#88-2396), and SnO2 (PDF#77-0452). However, as *x* increases to 0.06, the diffraction peaks of SnO2 and the monoclinic phase disappear, and the sample is mainly composed of the tetragonal phase, Zr(Sn,Ti)O4 (orthorhombic phase, PDF#80-1784), and the fluorite phase. As *x* continues to increase, the amount of the fluorite phase gradually increases. When *x* reaches 0.20, all the peaks in the diffraction pattern of the sample coincide with those of the fluorite phase, indicating that the sample is composed of a pure fluorite phase. However, as *x* increases to 0.24 or higher, new diffraction peaks of the perovskite phase (PDF#80-1784) emerge, and their intensity significantly increases with increasing *x*. In summary, both too low and too high of a CaO content are unfavorable for the formation of a single fluorite phase. Additionally, the main secondary phase observed varies with the CaO content, which aligns with the results of the CaO–ZrO2 phase diagram [26].

#### 3.2.3 Factors influencing thermal conductivity of HEFOs

It is well known that thermal energy is transported through phonons in these electrical insulating materials, and phonon movement is mainly restricted by various phonon-scattering processes, including phonon–phonon scattering, grain boundary scattering, and defect scattering [33−36]. Xu *et* *al*. [33] reported that the low thermal conductivity of HEFOs could be attributed to the following two factors: HEFOs possess a large amount of oxygen vacancies, which serve as phonon scattering sites, and the chemical complexity of HEFOs creates mass fluctuations and strain field fluctuations, which can reduce the rate of phonon propagation and induce phonon scattering. To investigate the effects of oxygen vacancies and fluctuations on the thermal conductivity of HEFOs separately, the results for the samples in Tables 4 and 5 were analyzed in depth. For samples with the same Ovac of 10% (Table 4), the phonon scattering caused by the anion sublattice remains constant. Fluctuations caused by cationic substitution mainly include mass disorder (*D*m) and size disorder (*D*δ), which can be calculated through Eqs. (7) and (8) [37−39]:

(7) Dm=∑i=1n′ci(1−mim¯)2

(8) Dδ=∑i=1n′ci(1−rir¯)2

where *c**i*, *m**i*, and *r**i* represent mole fraction, atomic mass, and ionic radius of the *i*th cation, respectively; * n′,  m¯, and  r¯ are the number of cation-site elements, mean cation mass, and mean cation radius, respectively. To gain a better understanding of the relationship between thermal conductivity and disorder, the calculated data of the samples in Table 4 are presented in Fig. 13. It is evident that there is an opposite trend between thermal conductivity and mass/size disorder as the  r¯ increases. Linear fitting results shown in Figs. 13(b) and 13(c) reveal a strong negative correlation between thermal conductivity and mass/size disorder. This suggests that as D*m and *D*δ increase, thermal conductivity should decrease, which is consistent with the studies by Wang *et* *al*. [40] on the low thermal conductivity of high-entropy rare-earth tantalates via multiscale defect analysis. Among the five samples, the sample F1-10-2 exhibits the highest *D*m and *D*δ, resulting in the lowest thermal conductivity of 1.24 W·m−1·K−1. In contrast, the sample F1-10-5 has the lowest values for both *D*m and *D*δ, and it demonstrates the highest thermal conductivity of 1.51 W·m−1·K−1.

Figure 14 illustrates the relationship between the thermal conductivity, Ovac, mass disorder, and size disorder for samples with equal  r¯ (Table 5). In contrast to the pattern observed in Fig. 13(a), where the thermal conductivity decreases as the mass/size disorder increases, Fig. 14(a) shows that the thermal conductivity initially increases and then decreases as the mass/size disorder increases. Furthermore, Figs. 14(b) and 14(c) indicate a weak correlation between the thermal conductivity and fluctuations (D*m and *D*δ). Based on the prediction of the point defect scattering model, it was expected that the sample F1-11-4, which has the highest disorder and highest Ovac, would have the lowest thermal conductivity among the four samples. However, in reality, the sample F1-8-4, which has the lowest disorder and lowest Ovac, actually has the lowest thermal conductivity of 1.36 W·m−1·K−1. This anomalous variation in thermal conductivity could be attributed to the aggregation of oxygen vacancies as Ovac increases [20,41−43]. The presence of point defects can lead to phonon scattering, but when these defects aggregate, they can weaken the scattering effect and cause the thermal conductivity to deviate from what is predicted by the point defect phonon scattering model. Jia *et* *al*. [44] found that the balance between lattice distortion and oxygen vacancies is very important for the properties of perovskite-type HEOs. Therefore, when designing materials with the aim of low thermal conductivity, attention needs to be given to the balance between oxygen vacancies and disorder.

Despite the relatively low disorder (D*m ranging from 0.0568 to 0.0637 and *D*δ ranging from 0.1012 to 0.1472), thermal conductivities of the three verification samples F2-10, F3-10, and F4-10 are even lower than that of the sample F1-10-5, which has relatively greater disorder (*D*m = 0.2194, *D*δ = 0.1403). This decrease in the thermal conductivity of these samples could be attributed to the introduction of large mass elements (Yb, Ta, and W) into the new samples, which results in a lower sound speed and, consequently lower thermal conductivity, even at lower disorder levels. The addition of heavy elements is beneficial for decreasing the thermal conductivity but also increases the theoretical density of the material.

### 3.3 Proposing a new design approach

In general, an appropriate increase in * r¯ and the introduction of Ovac contribute to the formability of HEFOs. For multiprincipal solid solutions such as (Cax*Ce*y*1Zr*y*2Hf*z*Sn*z*Ti*z*)O2−*δ*, thermal conductivity is primarily influenced by oxygen vacancies and disorder. It is important to note that low thermal conductivity requires greater disorder, but Ovac should be kept within a reasonable range to avoid aggregation. In addition, thermal conductivity could be reduced by adding heavy elements.

Based on the findings of our study, we propose a new design approach for HEFOs with low thermal conductivity. This approach involves two steps: first, determining the element combinations based on the valence combination and element properties; second, within the range of  r¯ and Ovac capable of forming HEFOs, mass disorder and size disorder should be increased as much as possible, and Ovac should be adjusted to a level that prevents agglomeration. This approach emphasizes the careful consideration of the content of high-valence cations in the composition. This is because high-valence cations typically have relatively small radii, and their introduction can lead to a reduction in Ovac.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, a design methodology is proposed based on a systematic study of the effects of  r¯ and Ovac on the formation of HEFOs, as well as the effects of oxygen vacancies and disorder on thermal conductivity. The following conclusions are obtained: An appropriate increase in  r¯ and the introduction of Ovac contribute to the formability of HEFOs. For samples with the same Ovac, a negative correlation exists between thermal conductivity and size disorder, as well as mass disorder. Aggregation of oxygen vacancies resulting from high Ovac levels can weaken the phonon scattering effect. A series of single-phase HEFOs with low thermal conductivities ranging from 1.24 to 1.51 W·m−1·K−1 have been successfully synthesized. Considering these findings, along with the valence combination strategy, we propose a two-step compositional design approach: first, determine the element combinations based on the valence combination strategy and element properties; second, within the range of  r¯ and Ovac capable of forming HEFOs, increase the mass disorder and size disorder as much as possible, and adjust the Ovac to a level that prevents agglomeration.
