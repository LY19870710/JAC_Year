## 1 Introduction

Flash synthesis (FSyn) is a novel, electric field-assisted solid-state reaction method, which broadens the application of flash sintering techniques [1,2]. When the sample is heated, applying an electric field of a certain strength will cause a sudden surge in current, accompanied by a rapid solid-state reaction of the sample. FSyn retains the advantages of the low processing temperature and short processing time, characteristics of flash sintering [3−5] while simultaneously enabling the synthesis and sintering of ceramics in a single step [6−8]. The low-temperature and short-duration characteristics of FSyn effectively suppress volatile element loss, enabling the preparation of single-phase oxide ceramics that are challenging to synthesize via conventional methods. For instance, Gil-González *et al.* [9] achieved single-phase BiFeO3 ceramics with uniform electrical properties and high insulation within seconds at 625 °C. Similarly, flash-synthesized Pb(Zr,Ti)O3 [10] and Na0.5K0.5NbO3 [11] ceramics within 30 s at 564 and 583 °C, respectively, exhibit negligible Pb, K, and Na loss alongside the pure phase, enhanced microstructural homogeneity, higher density, and superior performance compared with conventionally prepared counterparts. High-entropy oxides typically require prolonged high-temperature calcination because sluggish solid-state diffusion inherent to their multicomponent systems has also been successfully synthesized via FSyn [12−20]. Notably, Liu *et al.* [13] obtained single-phase (Mg,Co,Ni,Cu,Zn)O at room temperature within seconds using FSyn. These studies demonstrate that the critical electric field can significantly promote mass transfer, making FSyn a significant advantage in the preparation of high-entropy oxides. Owing to these benefits, FSyn has garnered widespread attention. To date, a variety of material systems, including dielectric ceramics [6,10,11,21−26], ionic conductors [27−31], semiconductors [32,33], insulators [8,9,34−36], high-entropy oxides [12−20], composites [37,38], and eutectic ceramics [39,40], have been successfully prepared via FSyn.

Although FSyn shows significant promise for ceramic preparation, predicting the final products remains challenging. Several reports have indicated that FSyn can effectively suppress the formation of secondary phases and precisely synthesize target products. For instance, Zhang *et al.* [39] demonstrated that flash treatment could rapidly transform and eliminate byproducts, enabling the accurate synthesis of Al2O3–Y3Al5O12 eutectic ceramics. Similarly, Avila *et al.* [29] found that FSyn could inhibit the formation of intermediates and was able to successfully prepare Li0.5La0.5TiO3. However, in certain material systems, FSyn may promote the formation of intermediate phases. Avila *et al.* [31] reported that during FSyn of Li6.25Al0.25La3Zr2O12, the generation of intermediate phases was enhanced, and their dissolution was delayed. In some cases, a pure phase can be obtained under specific FSyn conditions. For example, pure-phase BiFeO3 can be obtained only through FSyn within a narrow parameter range, which allows the formation of byproducts to be avoided [9]. The uncertainty in product outcomes during FSyn arises from the limited understanding of the underlying mechanisms. Exploring these mechanisms is crucial for achieving precise synthesis of materials via FSyn, minimizing byproduct formation, and enhancing the quality and performance of the products.

In addition to electrical parameters, which are crucial for determining the reaction pathway and the final products during FSyn [7,37], the external environment plays a crucial role in the flash process. In Ren’s study [41], flash sintering of Na3Zr2(SiO4)2(PO4) under a direct-current electric field resulted in the directional migration and segregation of sodium ions, leading to the generation of byproducts. This issue can be mitigated by embedding the anode in NaNO3, which allows for timely replenishment of sodium. Dai *et al.* [42] also provided a comprehensive summary of the ion migration behavior and the potential interactions between the sample and the environment. Additionally, Li *et al.* [43] reported that an applied electric field could be used to reversibly inject hydrogen ions into a sample, inducing a reversible insulator–metal transition. This demonstrates that an electric field can promote substance exchange between the sample and the environment, thereby altering the composition and properties of the involved materials. However, the influence of the environment on FSyn has not been thoroughly investigated and thus remains being not fully understood.

Herein, FSyn represents an emerging low-temperature, rapid synthesis technique with distinct advantages for fabricating materials containing volatile elements, such as lithium-ion battery cathode materials [15,27,44]. Driven by the exponential growth of portable electronics and electric vehicles, the demand for high-performance lithium-ion battery cathodes has surged. LiCoO2 (LCO), as a representative cathode material, is widely used in 3C electronic products. Compared with multicomponent cathode materials, it has a relatively simple chemical composition and crystal structure. These characteristics make it an ideal candidate for initial investigations into FSyn mechanisms, providing a foundation for further extending the mechanism and applications to other cathode materials. Using CoO precursors, we investigated the reaction pathways and environmental impacts, revealing that temperature and atmosphere govern phase reactions, while electric fields enhance reaction kinetics. Based on these findings, process improvement strategies have been proposed. These strategies can serve as a reference for the precise FSyn of other materials.

## 2 Experimental

CoO (99.5%) and Li2CO3 (99.99%) were sourced from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China, to prepare LCO. These materials were stoichiometrically mixed and ground in an agate mortar for 1 h to create a homogeneous blend, with a polyvinyl alcohol (Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) aqueous solution used as the binder. The mixture was then pressed into dog-bone-shaped samples measuring approximately 3.3 mm × 1.7 mm × 20 mm under a uniaxial pressure of 10 MPa via a mold. Silver paste was used as the conductive agent, while nichrome-alloy wires were utilized as the electrodes to connect the sample to a programmable alternating-current (AC) power source (6700 series; Extech Electronics Co., Ltd., China), and the samples were suspended in a furnace. The electrical parameters during the experiment were automatically recorded by the power supply.

A preset electric field intensity of 40 V·cm−1 and a furnace temperature of 200 °C were determined through preliminary temperature-ramped flash experiments [45]. A 50-Hz AC electric field was employed to mitigate the potential segregation of lithium ions [41,42]. The FSyn process can also be divided into three stages, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Upon application of the electric field, the current density increases slowly in stage I with minimal reactions. After a brief incubation, it surges nonlinearly, marking the onset of a flash event in stage II, during which intense reactions and compositional changes occur. In stage III, FSyn progresses under constant current, with continuous changes in the phase composition and conductivity, leading to significant variations in the electric field strength. This behavior contrasts with that of conventional flash sintering, during which changes in the electric field strength during stage III are often minimal.

To explore the phase evolution during different stages in the FSyn process, initially, the samples that underwent only stages I and II were prepared at specific current density thresholds: 5 mA·mm−2 (J5), 10 mA·mm−2 (J10), 20 mA·mm−2 (J20), 60 mA·mm−2 (J60), and 140 mA·mm−2 (J140). The power supply was turned off immediately upon reaching these current limits. These samples did not undergo stage III. These samples were named I-J5-t0, I-J10-t0, I-J20-t0, II-J60-t0, and II-J140-t0. Samples that underwent Stage III were also prepared. The current density was maintained at preset values of 60 mA·mm−2 (J60) and 140 mA·mm−2 (J140) for durations of 30, 60, and 600 s, respectively, before the power supply was turned off. Subsequently, the samples were cooled in the furnace. These samples were named III-J60-t30, III-J60-t60, III-J60-t600, III-J140-t30, III-J140-t60, and III-J140-t600. FSyn experiments were also carried out in Ar and O2 atmospheres at 140 mA·mm−2 and kept for 30 s to investigate the effect of the atmosphere on FSyn. In practice, the samples were immediately removed from the furnace chamber after the power was turned off, creating a quenching effect that ensured preservation of the sample’s transient state during FSy.

In addition, to explore the generation of intermediates, using LCO and CoO as raw materials, an FSyn experiment was carried out at 230 °C, with an electric field strength of 40 V·cm−1 and a current density of 140 mA·mm−2 in an Ar atmosphere, which was maintained for 30 s. Control experiments were also conducted to prepare LCO via conventional heating (CH) method, using the same sample and furnace as in the FSyn experiments. The samples in the furnace were heated to temperatures of 400, 500, 600, 700, and 800 °C, respectively, at a rate of 5 °C·min−1. After each temperature was held for 1 h, the samples were cooled within the furnace.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out to determine the phase composition of the samples. A D8 Advance diffractometer (Bruker, Germany) was used, employing Co Kα radiation at operating conditions of 35 kV and 40 mA. The XRD patterns were acquired over the 2θ* range from 10° to 90° with a step size of 0.02° at a scanning speed of 2 (°)·min−1. Rietveld refinement of the XRD data was performed via GSAS software [46].

The emissivity of the sample was determined to be 0.86 via averaged multipoint measurements using an IR-2 dual-band emissivity meter (Wanyi, China). During the FSyn process, the temperature profiles of the samples were monitored *in situ* via a high-resolution thermal infrared camera (VarioCAM® HDx inspect 600, InfraTec GmbH, Germany).

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were conducted at room temperature via a Bruker EMX Plus X-band spectrometer (Germany) to determine the presence of oxygen vacancies in the flash-synthesized samples.

## 3 Results and discussion

During the flash event, peaks corresponding to the high-temperature LCO (HT-LCO) phase appeared rapidly and intensified over time (Fig. 2), indicating that HT-LCO can be synthesized directly under an electric field, with contents of 11.49 wt% in II-J60-t0 and 19.88 wt% in II-J140-t0 (Fig. 3). This is in contrast to CH methods, which typically involve a slow transition from the low-temperature LCO (LT-LCO) phase to HT-LCO [47,48]. Additionally, a significant amount of the intermediate phase Lix*Co1−*x*O was observed as long as the flash event happened, with a content of 18.15 wt%, which coincides with the disappearance of the CoO peaks, as shown in the magnified pattern from 48° to 52° in Fig. 2, particularly at a current density of 140 mA·mm−2. As the duration of stage III increases, the Li*x*Co1−*x*O peak shifts to higher angles, which signifies an increase in the lithium content within the compound. Notably, the Li*x*Co1−*x*O phase is absent at a current density of 60 mA·mm−2, and the intensity of the CoO peaks gradually diminishes until they completely vanish.

The presence of the Li*x*Co1−*x*O intermediate phase during the FSyn process is significantly influenced by the current density. The current generates Joule heat, which increases the sample temperature far above that of the furnace [49]. The temperature profiles of the samples during the FSyn processes were recorded via a thermal infrared camera and are shown in Fig. 4. At a current density of 60 mA·mm−2, the overall sample temperature is below 600 °C, with a recorded maximum of 559 °C (Figs. 4(a) and 4(b)). In contrast, at a current density of 140 mA·mm−2, the sample temperature increases to 600 °C, with a peak at 633 °C (Figs. 4(c) and 4(d)). According to the phase diagram of Li*x*Co1−*x*O shown in Fig. 5 [50], the generation of Li*x*Co1−*x*O requires a temperature of approximately 600 °C or higher. Thus, it can be concluded that the formation of the intermediate phase Li*x*Co1−*x*O during the FSyn process is thermodynamically determined by the current-induced temperature. Furthermore, Fig. 5 indicates that under an electric field, Li*x*Co1−*x*O with a higher Li concentration can be formed at lower temperatures than those employed in conventional synthesis methods. This suggests that the applied electric field can dynamically promote the synthesis process. This is further supported by the FSyn process triggered at 140 mA·mm−2, and all unoxidized CoO transforms into Li*x*Co1−*x*O once the flash event occurs (Figs. 2 and 3). In addition to generating Joule heat during the FSyn process, the current also introduces oxygen vacancies in the sample [51−53], which facilitates mass transfer and increase the synthesis efficiency [54,55]. Figure 6 reveals the EPR spectra of the flash-synthesized samples at different current densities, where *g* = 2.003 is attributed to oxygen vacancies [56]. The enhanced peak intensity at 140 mA·mm−2 indicates a higher concentration of oxygen vacancies in the corresponding sample. In the FSyn process of LCO from CoO and the Li2CO3 mixture, the current-induced oxygen vacancies form diffusion channels for Li+ ions, which facilitate their replacement of Co2+ ions in Li*x*Co1−*x*O; this results in the synthesis of more Li-rich Li*x*Co1−*x*O than that obtained through conventional synthesis methods.

The formation of Lix*Co1−*x*O is thermodynamically favored at temperatures above 600 °C. However, it is rarely observed in CH processes that rely solely on the heat treatment of CoO and Li2CO3 at temperatures above 600 °C, as illustrated in Fig. 7. This difference indicates that the FSyn process follows a distinct reaction pathway compared with the CH processes. Li*x*Co1−*x*O is a mixed-valence compound with the general formula * Lix(Co1−2x2+Cox3+)O [57]. When synthesized from CoO as the cobalt source, the reaction typically involves changes in the valence state of the cobalt ions [50]. In the FSyn process, these valence state changes are influenced not only by the electric field but also by the external environment [41]. To clarify the reaction pathway during the FSyn process of LCO using CoO and Li2CO3, we conducted experiments in two environments other than air, namely, pure O2 and Ar atmospheres, at a current density of 140 mA·mm−2 for 30 s. Figure 8 shows the XRD patterns of the obtained samples in various atmospheres. After flash treatment in an Ar atmosphere, only the peaks corresponding to CoO and Li2CO3 are observed. In contrast, HT-LCO was successfully synthesized in both air and O2 atmospheres. Notably, in the O2 environment, the intensity of the HT-LCO peaks is higher, while the intensity of the Lix*Co1−*x*O peaks is lower than that of the corresponding peaks for the sample synthesized in air. Based on these results, the following conclusions can be drawn: (1) CoO cannot directly react with Li2CO3 to form new compounds without an oxidizing environment; (2) An oxygen-rich environment is more favorable for the FSyn of pure HT-LCO.

Accordingly, CoO must be oxidized to Co2O3 prior to being involved in chemical reactions to obtain HT-LCO, which is expected to be similar to the FSyn process of HT-LCO from Co2O3 and Li2CO3. The FSyn process based on the Co2O3 precursor can produce a limited quantity of Lix*Co1−*x*O phase, which is due to the charge imbalance originating from the introduction of oxygen vacancies by the current during the flash event [41]. However, the abundant generation of Li*x*Co1−*x*O in the FSyn process of LCO using the CoO precursor indicates that the formation mechanism of the intermediate phase in this study is different from that induced by oxygen vacancies. Typically, Li*x*Co1−*x*O can be regarded as a solid solution of HT-LCO in CoO [58−60]. The immediate generation of HT-LCO in oxygen-containing environments during the FSyn process is evident. The reaction between HT-LCO and CoO can account for the abundant generation of Li*x*Co1−*x*O during the FSyn process of LCO from the CoO precursor. To validate this, an FSyn experiment using HT-LCO and CoO as raw materials was conducted in a pure Ar atmosphere. At a current density of 140 mA·mm−2, a substantial amount of Li*x*Co1−*x*O is observed in the samples following the flash treatment (Fig. 9). Conversely, the synthesis of HT-LCO is more favorable in a pure O2 atmosphere; this can be attributed to the higher oxygen partial pressure, which accelerates the oxidation of CoO and facilitates the transformation of Li*x*Co1−*x*O into HT-LCO [44].

Based on the above discussion, we propose the following pathway for the FSyn of HT-LCO using CoO as the precursor:

When the applied current density is low, leading to a sample temperature below 600 °C, CoO is first oxidized to Co2O3. This Co2O3 then reacts with Li2CO3 to form HT-LCO following Reactions (1) and (2):

(1) 4CoO+O2(g)→2Co2O3

(2) Co2O3+Li2CO3→2LiCoO2+CO2(g)

At a high current density, which results in a sample temperature above 600 °C, a portion of CoO is oxidized and reacts with Li2CO3 to form HT-LCO (Reactions (1) and (2)). Simultaneously, a portion of HT-LCO reacts with the remaining CoO that has not been oxidized in time, leading to the formation of Lix*Co1−*x*O, as described by Reaction (3):

(3) xLiCoO2+(1−2x)CoO→LixCo1−xO0x≤0.21

Finally, through lithium doping and oxygen adsorption, Li*x*Co1−*x*O is ultimately converted into HT-LCO according to Reaction (4):

(4) 4LixCo1−xO+(1−2x)/2Li2CO3+(1−2x)/4O2(g)→(1−x)LiCoO2+(1−2x)/2CO2(g)

Pure HT-LCO is essential for achieving optimal electrochemical properties. Therefore, the formation of Li*x*Co1−*x*O should be avoided during the FSyn process. In stage II, with the onset of the flash event, Reactions (1) and (2) occur, Rietveld refined XRD pattern shows that 17.74 wt% CoO remains in the sample at a current density of 60 mA·mm−2 (Fig. 10(b)), and 20.28 wt% Li*x*Co1−*x*O intermediate phase was generated at a current density of 140 mA·mm−2 (Fig. 10(c)). After the samples are kept in stage III for 10 min, pure HT-LCO is synthesized at a current density of 60 mA·mm−2 (Fig. 10(a)), while the sample synthesized at 140 mA·mm−2 still contains approximately 1.88 wt% Li*x*Co1−*x*O (Figs. 10(a) and 10(d)). This suggests that the transition from Li*x*Co1−*x*O to HT-LCO is remarkably slow at high current densities. This delay may be attributed to the introduction of more oxygen vacancies, which hinder the conversion of Li*x*Co1−*x*O into HT-LCO [41]. Thus, reducing the current density and lowering the sample temperature to prevent the formation of intermediates is an effective strategy to enhance the efficiency of the FSyn of HT-LCO. Additionally, increasing the oxygen partial pressure is another highly effective approach. The reactions involving CoO and the conversion of Li*x*Co1−*x*O into HT-LCO require an external source of oxygen; without this source, these reactions cannot proceed. A higher oxygen partial pressure promotes oxygen adsorption on the sample surface, facilitating these oxidation reactions. Finally, extending the holding time is essential to ensure that the reaction is complete.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, this study reveals the phase evolution mechanisms during FSyn of LCO using CoO as the cobalt source, emphasizing the thermodynamic control of intermediate formation and the kinetic enhancement by electric fields. The formation of Li*x*Co1−*x*O is thermodynamically favored at temperatures above 600 °C, which is significantly influenced by Joule heating. The oxygen vacancies enhance the reaction kinetics by promoting mass transfer, facilitating the production of both HT-LCO and Li*x*Co1−*x*O, as well as the transition from Li*x*Co1−*x*O to HT-LCO. Additionally, the external environment plays a crucial role in the FSyn of HT-LCO. CoO must be oxidized to Co2O3 before further reactions can occur, and Li*x*Co1−*x*O requires oxygen to be converted into HT-LCO. Overall, a higher oxygen partial pressure favors the synthesis of pure HT-LCO. These findings provide a new understanding of the FSyn process, establish targeted strategies for controlling phase evolution in ceramic synthesis, and provide a valuable reference for FSyn in other advanced materials.
