## 1 Introduction

Conventional vapor-compression refrigeration technology contributes to greenhouse gas emissions, underscoring the pressing need for the development of environmentally friendly refrigeration alternatives [1−4]. The electrocaloric effect (ECE) has garnered significant attention due to its high Carnot cycle efficiency (60%) and eco-friendly nature, showing tremendous potential for the next generation of solid-state refrigeration technology [5−9]. ECE occurs when an electric field is applied to and removed from a polar material, leading to an entropy change and a consequent temperature variation [8]. The most critical parameters for the ECE are the isothermal entropy change (Δ*S*) and the adiabatic temperature change (Δ*T*), which are described by the Maxwell equations (Eqs. (1) and (2)) [8]:

(1) ΔS=−1ρ∫E2E1(∂P∂T)EdE

(2) ΔT=−Tρ∫E2E11C(∂P∂T)EdE

where *ρ* and *C* represent the density and specific heat capacity of the ceramic samples, respectively. *T* refers to the measurement temperature, and *P* is the maximum polarization in each hysteresis loop under varying temperatures. *E*1 and *E*2 denote the range of the applied electric field. Based on Eqs. (1) and (2), Δ*T* is dependent mainly on the applied electric field and the pyroelectric coefficient * (∂P/∂T)E. Hence, prevalent approaches to achieving a significant ECE involve augmenting both the maximum applied electric field and the derivative of polarization with respect to temperature. However, achieving only a large ECE is insufficient for practical applications, and simultaneously achieving a large ECE and a wide working temperature range near room temperature is crucial for advancing next-generation solid refrigeration technologies.

It is well-known that the Curie temperature (T*C) of ceramic-based materials is high (*T*C > 100 °C), such as BaTiO3 (BT, *T*C ≈ 120 °C) [10−12], Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3 (*T*C ≈ 320 °C) [13,14], and NaNbO3 (*T*C ≈ 480 °C) [15,16]. However, a large ECE is most likely to be achieved near the *T*C. Consequently, simultaneously achieving a large ECE and a wide working temperature range is a challenge. Shifting the *T*C closer to room temperature is therefore vital. Numerous efforts have been made to realize significant ECEs at room temperature. For instance, Zhang *et al*. [17] achieved a substantial Δ*T* of 1.14 K near 90 °C (*T*span ≈ 52 K in 0.86NaNbO3–0.1BaTiO3–0.04BaZrO3 relaxor ferroelectric ceramics. They also reported a satisfying Δ*T* of 3.2 K in 0.94(Bi0.5Na0.5)TiO3–0.06BaTi0.8Zr0.2O3 ceramic doped with 0.2 wt% AlN [18]. Zhang *et al*. [19] introduced 5.7 mol% Li2CO3 to Ba(Zr0.2Ti0.8)O3 lead-free ferroelectric ceramics, achieving a large Δ*T* of 2.26 K near 333 K. Li *et al*. [20] obtained a Δ*T* of 1.21 K at a temperature of 76 °C in 0.05% Hf doped BaTiO3. Liang *et al*. [21] achieved a large Δ*T* of 2.18 K (near room temperature) in BaTiO3 ceramic by doping with 30% SrCO3. Du *et al*. [22] obtained a significant Δ*T* of 1.6 K near room temperature in (Ba0.95Ca0.05)(Ti0.94Sn0.06)O3 bulk ceramics using the tape-casting technique. Qian *et al*. [23] reported an ultrahigh electrocaloric effect of 4.5 K and a 30 K temperature range in BaTi0.8Zr0.2O3 bulk ceramics. Yin *et al*. [24] reported a noteworthy ECE of 0.63 K, coupled with a broad operational temperature range of 57 K, achieved at an ultralow electric field of 20 kV/cm in laminated composite ceramics. Consequently, the simultaneous attainment of both a substantial ECE and an expansive operational temperature range remains an unresolved challenge.

In this work, we proposed an effective strategy to simultaneously achieve a large ECE and a wide working temperature range in BT lead-free ceramics. As displayed in Fig. 1(a), the second component of CaZrO3 was introduced into the BT-based lead-free ferroelectric ceramics. The *T*C shifts to a low temperature, and a large dielectric dispersion region is also present, indicating that the long-range ferroelectric order is broken to form microdomains or polar nanoregions (PNRs, Fig. 1(b)). As presented in Fig. 1(c), more importantly, the maximum applied electric field of 9% CaZrO3-doped BT is 50 kV/cm, while that of 15% CaZrO3-doped BT significantly increases to 140 kV/cm. Interestingly, the Δ*T* of the samples increases from 0.97 to 1.52 K, and the working temperature range (*T*span) expands from 56 to 103 K (Fig. 1(d)). As a result, both a large ECE and an ultrawide working temperature range were simultaneously achieved near room temperature.

## 2 Experimental

All ceramic samples were prepared using a conventional solid-state reaction technique. The raw materials included BaCO3 (99.95%, Aladdin), CaCO3 (99.99%, Aladdin), TiO2 (99.5%, anatase, Aladdin), and ZrO2 (99.5%, Aladdin). The materials at stoichiometric ratios corresponding to (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*CaZrO3 (where *x* = 0.09, 0.11, 0.13, 0.15, and 0.17) were weighed and ball-milled with ethyl alcohol using zirconia balls in a nylon jar for 10 h. The ball-milled mixtures were dried and subsequently calcined for 4 h at 900 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min. A second round of ball milling was performed under the same conditions as the first round. Subsequently, the resulting powder was pressed into pellets (10 mm in diameter and 1 mm in thickness). These pellets underwent isostatic pressing (250 MPa pressure, held for 8 min). They were then sintered at 1290–1330 °C for 2 h in an oxygen atmosphere. Finally, the sintered samples were polished and coated with silver electrodes on both sides.

The crystalline structure of the ceramic samples was characterized using an X-ray diffractiometer (XRD; PANalytical, the Netherlands) in the 2*θ* range of 10°–70°. Furthermore, Rietveld refinement was applied to the XRD data to determine the phase structure and lattice parameters. The surface morphology of the thermally etched ceramic samples was examined using a scanning electron microscope (S4800, Hitachi, Japan). The dielectric properties were analyzed using a Partulab instrument (DMS-2000, Partulab, China). Temperature-dependent polarization versus electric field (*P*–*E*) loops were measured using a ferroelectric tester (TF Analyzer 2000, aixACCT, Germany). Direct ECE data were obtained via modified differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). The polarization as a function of the electric field at various temperatures was recorded using the same ferroelectric tester. Piezoresponse force microscopy (NanoWizard 4 XP NanoScience, Bruker, Germany) was employed to investigate the evolution of domain morphology.

## 3 Results and discussion

The XRD patterns of (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*CaZrO3 (*x* = 0.09, 0.11, 0.13, 0.15, and 0.17) ceramic samples are shown in Fig. 2(a). All the samples exhibit a single phase, indicating that with the addition of the secondary component, CaZrO3 forms a homogeneous solid solution with BT. The enlarged XRD peaks near 45° are presented in Fig. 2(b). When the CaZrO3 content is less than 11 mol%, there is a distinct splitting of the (002) reflection, which gradually weakens with increasing doping content. As *x* increases, the (002) and (200) reflections gradually merge into a single peak, and the splitting of the (002) peak disappears when the doping content exceeds 13 mol%. The splitting observed in the (002) reflection is attributed to an increase in the tetragonal phase structure, characterized by the coexistence of the *P*4*mm* and *Amm*2 phases [25−27]. Therefore, the tetragonal phase is dominant when the doping content is within 11 mol%, while the cubic phase (*Pm** 3¯m*) becomes more prevalent than *P*4*mm* when the doping content is beyond 13 mol%. Additionally, the (200) peak shifts to a lower angle as *x* increases. This result is ascribed to the larger ionic radius of Zr4+ (0.72 Å) compared to that of Ti4+ (0.605 Å) [28,29]. The introduction of Zr4+ in the presence of Ti4+ results in lattice expansion.

The ferroelectric domain configuration plays a very important role in the ECE property, and the PFMs for the *x* = 0.11, 0.13, and 0.15 compositions are displayed in Fig. 6. The amplitude and phase images for *x* = 0.11, 0.13, and 0.15 are shown in Figs. 6(a)–6(c) and 6(d)–6(f), respectively. At *x* = 0.11 (Fig. 6(a)), macrodomains are distinctly observed, which are not favorable for obtaining a wide working temperature range for the ECE. However, as the doping content increases to *x* = 0.13 and 0.15, the macrodomains are fragmented into microdomains and small domains, thereby increasing the number of domains. The presence of microdomains and small domains is advantageous for achieving a small remnant polarization. That is, a significant increase in entropy can be achieved when the electric field is removed, thereby facilitating the attainment of a large ECE.

To evaluate the ECE performance, temperature-dependent polarization–electric field (*P*–*E*) loops were constructed, as shown in Fig. 7(a). All *P‒E* loops were measured from 303 to 423 K at 10 Hz. The maximum applied electric field increases from 50 kV/cm at *x* = 0.09 to 150 kV/cm at *x* = 0.17, which correlates with the increase in the *x* content. This suggests that the incorporation of CaZrO3 significantly improves the maximum applied electric field of ferroelectric ceramics and is conducive to achieving a large ECE. To quantitatively describe the ECE performance, * (∂P/∂T)E was calculated using Eq. (1), as presented in Fig. 7(b).  (∂P/∂T)E decreases with increasing x* content. Notably, although the peak of the pyroelectric coefficient gradually weakens with increasing *x* content, an ultrawide peak is observed at *x* = 0.13 and 0.15, which could enable the realization of an ultrawide working temperature range. The change in temperature (Δ*T*) was determined using Eq. (2), with the specific heat capacities for *x* = 0.09 to 0.17 provided in Figs. S1(a)*‒*S1(e) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), as depicted in Fig. 7(c). At *x* = 0.09, a maximum Δ*T* of 1.13 K is obtained under a 50 kV/cm electric field, but the temperature span (*T*span) is only 56 K. The limited working temperature range can be attributed to the weak diffusion phase transition. However, the phase transition becomes more diffused as the CaZrO3 content increases. For *x* = 0.11, 0.13, 0.15, and 0.17, wider *T*span values of 85, 101, 103, and 91 K are obtained, respectively, which can be ascribed to the formation of complex heterogeneous compositions. Furthermore, with increasing CaZrO3 doping, the maximum Δ*T* increases from 1.13 K at *x* = 0.09 to 1.97 K at *x* = 0.17. An optimal ECE performance is achieved at *x* = 0.15, where an ultrawide *T*span of 103 K and a large Δ*T* of 1.52 K are simultaneously achieved.

It is evident that the reduction in oxygen vacancies with increasing doping of CaZrO3 can be attributed to the capture of electrons by Ti4+ ions, leading to their reduction to Ti3+. This process contributes to the generation of oxygen vacancies. Conversely, Zr4+ ions maintain a constant valence, implying the suppression of oxygen vacancies. This suggests that appropriate doping with CaZrO3 effectively reduces lattice defects in BaTiO3 lead-free ferroelectric ceramics, which is beneficial for achieving a large maximum applied electric field. The UV‒Vis spectra shown in Fig. 7(d) reveal a typical absorption peak near 380 nm [38,39]. The plots of * (αhv)2vs.hv are exhibited in Fig. 9(e), and the band gaps for x* = 0.09 and 0.15 are determined through linear fitting of these curves, as shown in Fig. 9(f). Notably, the band gap increases from 3.17 eV for *x* = 0.09 to 3.23 eV for *x* = 0.15 with increasing doping content. Furthermore, the increase in the band gap with increasing doping of CaTiO3 components can be attributed to the wider band of CaTiO3 (3.48 eV) compared to that of BaTiO3 (3.15 eV) [40,41]. Ultimately, the increase in band gap and reduction in lattice defects of BaTiO3, achieved through doping with CaZrO3 contribute to the increase in the maximum applied electric field as well as the ECE.

## 4 Conclusions

An effective strategy was proposed to simultaneously achieve a large ECE and an ultrawide working temperature range in BaTiO3 lead-free ceramics through the appropriate introduction of CaZrO3. The optimized composition, particularly at *x* = 0.15, exhibited a significant ECE of 1.52 K and an extensive working temperature range of 103 K. This achievement was verified using direct methods, specifically through corroborative measurements obtained with DSC. The modified Curie‒Weiss law and PFM suggest that the presence of numerous microdomains and small domains is advantageous for obtaining a broad working temperature range. Moreover, the XPS and UV‒Vis results revealed that minimal lattice defects and a high band gap synergistically contribute to achieving a large maximum applied electric field. Ultimately, this approach leads to the realization of a high-performance ECE. Collectively, these findings underscore the successful realization of a high-performance ECE in BaTiO3-based ceramics, marking a significant advancement in the development of environmentally friendly ferroelectric materials.
