## 1 Introduction

With the rapid development of fifth-generation mobile communication, the Internet of Things, high-performance computing, and military radar technologies, the spatial electromagnetic environment has become increasingly complex, leading to more serious problems of electromagnetic wave leakage and interference [1−4]. These issues not only severely affect the operational reliability and measurement accuracy of civilian electronic devices but also pose potential threats to information security and national defense [5]. Consequently, the development of new functional materials capable of efficiently absorbing electromagnetic waves (EMWs) has become crucial for mitigating electromagnetic pollution and enhancing electromagnetic compatibility [6−8]. In recent years, two-dimensional (2D) transition metal carbides/nitrides (MXenes) have attracted widespread attention in the field of EMW absorption because of their easily tunable electrical conductivity and rich surface chemistry, which are essential for meeting the demanding performance requirements of thinness, light weight, wide bandwidth, and strong absorption [9]. Among them, Ti3C2T*x* MXene is regarded as a highly promising EMW absorption material owing to its mature preparation process, high electrical conductivity (~104 S∙m−1), and abundant surface functional groups (–OH, –F, and –O), which enable effective dissipation of electromagnetic energy through conduction loss and interfacial polarization mechanisms.

However, the application of Ti3C2T*x* EMW absorbing materials still faces two critical challenges. On one hand, its excessively high electrical conductivity leads to a severe impedance mismatch with free space, resulting in significant reflection of incident EMWs. On the other hand, MXene nanosheets are prone to stacking and agglomeration, which reduces the number of active polarization interfaces and weakens the dielectric loss capability [10−11]. To address these issues, researchers have developed multiple strategies. In structural design, the construction of three-dimensional (3D) porous frameworks or the incorporation of spacers provides an effective solution to mitigate nanosheet restacking [12−15]. In composition design, combining Ti3C2T*x* with magnetic and other dielectric materials can introduce magnetic loss and modulate the complex permittivity, thereby improving impedance matching [16−20]. Recently, several groups have focused on tuning the electromagnetic properties of MXenes via interlayer spacing adjustment, surface functional group modification, and elemental doping. For example, precise control of the interlayer distance in MXenes enables effective regulation of the electrical conductivity, thereby tuning the conduction loss and improving impedance matching [21,22]. The introduction of surface functional groups can enhance the high-frequency polarization response by increasing the number of dipolar sites [23−26]. The elemental doping strategy introduces defects and perturbs the charge distribution within the MXene lattice, enabling synergistic regulation of both the electrical conductivity and polarization dynamics [27−30].

Since dopant elements are diverse and can occupy different sites in MXenes, such as lattice substitution (LS), functional group substitution (FS), and surface absorption (SA) sites, this strategy shows substantial potential for tailoring high-frequency electromagnetic performance [31]. However, current research has focused on the macroscopic effects of doping on material performance. The specific effects of different doping configurations on the electromagnetic response have not been revealed, and methods for controlling these configurations remain to be explored. Furthermore, for multielement co-doping systems, the site preferences of different dopants, as well as their synergistic effects on the complex permittivity, remain unclear. These unresolved fundamental issues impede the rational design and synthesis of high-performance doped MXene electromagnetic wave absorbers.

In this study, we report the fabrication of an S/N co-doped MXene nanoribbon/nanosheet composite structure. An alkali-based chemical scissor strategy was used to tailor MXene nanosheets into nanoribbons, thus constructing a hybrid architecture that effectively mitigates the restacking of pristine MXene nanosheets. Subsequent S/N co-doping was used to synergistically regulate the electrical conductivity and polarization behavior of the composite. Interestingly, the doping temperature can be simply tuned to controllably optimize the configuration of S and N in MXenes among the LS, FS, and SA. Compared with conventional MXene nanosheets, MXene nanoribbons with a greater density of defects and surface functional groups serve as an ideal platform for accommodating dopant elements. Experimental and theoretical studies reveal the specific effects of the LS, FS, and SA doping configurations on the electromagnetic properties, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for the rational design of high-performance doped MXene EMW absorbing materials. The optimized MXene nanoribbon/nanosheet composite significantly enhances the overall EMW absorption performance, achieving strong absorption (−53.77 dB), a broad effective absorption bandwidth (4.51 GHz), low filler loading (10 wt%), and a small matching thickness (1.26 mm).

### 2.1 Materials

The Ti3AlC2 MAX phase (analytical grade) was purchased from XFNANO Materials Technology Co., Ltd. Lithium fluoride (LiF, analytical grade), potassium hydroxide (KOH, analytical grade), and thiourea (CH4N2S, analytical grade) were obtained from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Absolute ethanol (C2H5OH, analytical grade) and hydrochloric acid (HCl, analytical grade) were purchased from Beijing Chemical Works. Deionized water (18.2 MΩ·cm, 25 °C) was produced via a Milli-Q purification system. Argon (Ar, 99.99%) and nitrogen (N2, 99.99%) were supplied by Beijing AP BAIF Gases Industry Co., Ltd.

#### 2.2.1 Preparation of Ti<sub>3</sub>C<sub>2</sub>Tₓ MXene nanoribbon/nanosheet composites

Ti3C2T*x* MXene nanosheets were synthesized by selectively etching Al from Ti3AlC2. In a typical process, 0.8 g of LiF was dissolved in 10 mL of HCl (9 M) with continuous stirring, followed by the addition of 0.5 g of Ti3AlC2 (400 mesh). After stirring for 36 h, the resulting suspension was repeatedly washed with HCl (1 M) and deionized water until the supernatant reached a neutral pH (≈ 7). The sediment was then delaminated via sonication under a N2 atmosphere. The dispersion was centrifuged to remove few-layer and unexfoliated MXene particles. Finally, the black supernatant was collected and freeze-dried to obtain d-Ti3C2T*x* MXene powder (d-MXene).

For the alkaline treatment, 0.5 g of d-MXene was dispersed in 120 mL of KOH solution (6 M) and stirred under an Ar atmosphere. After reaction periods of 72, 120, and 168 h, the resulting Ti3C2T*x* MXene nanoribbon/nanosheet composites were thoroughly washed and freeze-dried to generate a3-MXene, a5-MXene, and a7-MXene, respectively (“a” denotes alkali treatment, numbers indicate duration).

#### 2.2.2 Preparation of S/N co-doped Ti<sub>3</sub>C<sub>2</sub>Tₓ MXene nanoribbon/nanosheet composites

S/N co-doping was performed in a tubular furnace under flowing Ar. In a typical process, 0.2 g of a5-MXene powder and 4 g of thiourea were placed into separate alumina boats in a tubular furnace. Thiourea was positioned upstream of the sample along the gas flow direction. The samples were annealed at 200, 300, 400, and 500 °C for 3 h under Ar, yielding S/N co-doped a5-MXene sheets (denoted as SN*T*@M, where *T* represents the doping temperature). After annealing, the products were repeatedly washed with deionized water to remove residual thiourea. Similarly, S/N co-doping d-MXene nanosheets used as a control sample were annealed via the same method.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The crystal structures were analyzed by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Rigaku D/max-2500, Japan) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15418 nm, 2*θ* = 3°–120°). Morphologies and elemental distributions were examined by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JEOL JSM-7500, Japan) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS; Oxford Instruments, UK). The microstructures were observed via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEOL JEM-2100F, Japan). The surface composition and chemical states were determined via an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA) with Al Kα radiation, and all the spectra were calibrated to C 1s at 284.8 eV. The specific surface areas were measured via nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method, which was performed on a surface area and porosimetry analyzer (Micromeritics ASAP 2460, USA).

### 2.4 Electromagnetic parameter measurement

The complex permittivity and complex permeability of the samples were measured over the 2–18 GHz frequency range using a vector network analyzer (Agilent E5071C, USA) based on the coaxial line method. The powder was uniformly mixed with paraffin wax at a mass ratio of 1 : 9 and then pressed into toroidal rings with an inner diameter of 3.04 mm, an outer diameter of 7.00 mm, and a thickness of 3.00 mm.

The EMW absorption performance was evaluated on the basis of reflection loss (RL) values calculated from the measured complex permittivity and complex permeability parameters according to transmission line theory. The RL values are given by Eqs. (1) and (2) [32−35]:

(1) RL=20lg|(Zin−Z0)/(Zin+Z0)|

(2) Zin=Z0(μr/εr)12tanh(j(2πfd/c)(μrεr)12)

where * εr,  μr, Z*in, and *Z*0 are the complex permittivity, the complex permeability, the input characteristic impedance, and the free space impedance, respectively; *c*, *d*, and *f* are the velocity of the rays, the thickness, and the frequency of these absorbers, respectively.

### 2.5 DFT calculations

To gain deeper insights into the effects of the S- and N-doping configurations on the electronic structure of the Ti3C2T*x* MXene, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted via the Vienna *ab initio* Simulation Package (VASP). The electron exchange–correlation interactions were treated with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA). The projector-augmented wave (PAW) method was utilized to model the ionic cores, with a plane-wave cutoff energy of 350 eV. van der Waals interactions were included via the DFT–D3 method. All calculations used a 3 × 1 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack *k*-point mesh, and geometry optimizations were converged to forces below 0.01 eV·Å−1 and an energy threshold of 1×10−5 eV·atom−1.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1 illustrates the preparation of S/N co-doped Ti3C2T*x* MXene nanoribbon/nanosheet composites. Ti3C2T*x* nanosheets were obtained by etching Al from Ti3AlC2, forming ultrathin MXene nanosheets (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), denoted as d-MXene). These nanosheets subsequently underwent a precisely controlled alkali treatment under an Ar atmosphere, which led to the formation of a composite consisting of one-dimensional (1D) nanoribbons and 2D nanosheets. The ratio of nanosheets to nanoribbons in the composites can be effectively modulated by varying the alkalization time (Figs. 1(b) and 1(c)). After 3 d of treatment (denoted as a3-MXene, Fig. S2(a) in the ESM), only the edges of a small fraction of the MXene sheets were transformed into ribbon structures. When the treatment was extended to 5 d (a5-MXene), the proportion of nanoribbons increased significantly, resulting in an intertwined morphology of nanosheets and nanoribbons. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images also clearly revealed the hybrid structure of the nanosheets and nanoribbons (Fig. 1(c)). The nanoribbons are sandwiched between the nanosheets, which effectively prevents the dense stacking and aggregation of the MXene nanosheets. As shown in Figs. S2(b)–S2(d) in the ESM, nanoholes and lattice defects are formed within the interior and along the edges of the a5-MXene sample. Previous studies have indicated that Ti and C vacancies are readily generated in MXenes during alkaline treatment and HF etching processes [36,37]. After alkalization for 7 d, the a7-MXene sample predominantly exhibited an entangled network of nanoribbons (Fig. 1(d)). Figure S3 in the ESM presents high-resolution XPS spectra of O 1s, Ti 2p, and C 1s for MXenes treated with different alkali durations. The Ti–O peak shifts from 458.9 eV in d-MXene to 458.3 eV in a5- and a7-MXene, indicating an altered chemical environment of Ti. The weakened C–Ti peak suggests partial disruption of the Ti–C bond due to alkalization. The enhanced C–C peak may originate from newly formed C–C bonds induced by Ti dissolution [37]. Figure S4 in the ESM shows the XRD patterns of the samples. A small amount of TiO2 was also formed during the HF etching and alkaline treatment processes due to the inherent susceptibility of Ti3C2T*x* to oxidation (Fig. S4(a) in the ESM). The d-MXene shows a strong (002) characteristic peak at 6.45°, which is indicative of a well-defined layered structure. As the alkali treatment time increased, the intensity of this peak gradually weakened and slightly shifted to a lower angle. This suggests an expansion of the interlayer spacing and the eventual disruption of the layered structure to form 1D nanoribbons. This structural evolution is further supported by a significant increase in specific surface area, which increased from 10.710 m2·g−1 for the d-MXene samples to 17.794, 29.842, and 41.307 m2·g−1 for the a3-, a5-, and a7-MXene samples, respectively (Fig. S4(b) in the ESM). This trend confirms the increase in the number of 1D nanoribbons in the composites. The above results demonstrate that the formation of MXene nanoribbons effectively alleviates the agglomeration and stacking of MXene nanosheets.

Finally, a5-MXene, which has an optimal ratio of nanoribbons to nanosheets, was selected for subsequent S and N co-doping. The selected composite was freeze-dried and subsequently annealed with thiourea in an Ar atmosphere at various temperatures. Upon heating, thiourea decomposes to release NH3 and H2S, which serve as S and N sources for co-doping the MXene (Fig. 1(e)). Figure 1(f) presents the XRD patterns of the samples co-doped with S and N at different temperatures. After high-temperature doping, no additional impurity phases appear in the XRD pattern, demonstrating the stability of the layered structure. As the doping temperature increases from 200  to 300, 400, and 500  °C, the (002) diffraction peak shifts from 6.29° to 6.31°, 6.36°, and 6.40°, respectively, indicating a slight reduction in the interlayer spacing. This change can be attributed to the partial desorption of surface functional groups from the MXene nanoribbons/nanosheets during annealing, which leads to a decrease in the interlayer spacing [38,39]. The elemental mapping results of a representative SN300@a5M sample (Fig. 2(a)) demonstrate the uniform distribution of S and N in the nanosheets. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 2(a) and Fig. S4(c) in the ESM, both the nanosheet and nanoribbon morphologies are well preserved after elemental doping, indicating that high-temperature treatment does not compromise structural integrity.

XPS was used to characterize the chemical composition and elemental states of the SN*T*@a5M samples. As shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM, the fluorine content gradually decreases with increasing doping temperature, whereas the oxygen content remains relatively stable. In contrast, the S and N contents increase gradually, indicating enhanced doping efficiency at higher temperatures (Fig. S4(f) in the ESM). According to previous studies [31], S and N atoms can occupy three possible sites in MXenes, i.e., LS, FS, and SA. However, their site preferences differ significantly. Owing to its smaller atomic radius and greater electronegativity, N tends to bond with surface functional groups such as Ti–O and Ti–F on MXenes, forming stable Ti–N structures at FS sites [40]. In contrast, S, with its larger atomic radius and lower electronegativity, preferentially substitutes for carbon atoms in the lattice, resulting in a Ti–S–C configuration at LS sites [41]. To further investigate the doping configurations of S and N in the a5-MXene composite structure, high-resolution XPS spectra of the N 1s and S 2p were collected for the SN*T*@a5M samples (Figs. 2(b) and 2(c)). As shown in Figs. 2(b) and 2(c), the N 1s peaks at 396.8, 398.9–400.8, and 402.2 eV correspond to LS (N–Ti), FS (Ti–N), and SA (N–O), respectively. Similarly, the S 2p peaks at 160.7, 164.0, and 168.9 eV correspond to FS (S–Ti), LS (S–Ti–C), and SA (S–O), respectively [40−42].

Analysis of N reveals three distinct bonding states. The LS-N state, characterized by N–Ti bonds, originates from N atoms substituting for C or Ti atoms in the lattice or filling vacancies. The FS-N state indicates that N atoms have replaced surface functional groups (–F, –OH, or –O). The SA-N state, identified by N–O bonds, results from N atoms being adsorbed onto surface oxygen terminals. Similarly, for S, LS-S corresponds to S–Ti–C bonds formed by S atoms substituting for C or Ti atoms; FS-S refers to S–Ti bonds where S replaces surface functional groups; and SA-S corresponds to S–O bonds formed by S atoms attached to surface-O terminals. These findings suggest that an increase in defects and surface functional groups within MXenes facilitates the doping of S and N. As shown in Fig. S5(d) in the ESM, compared with d-MXene nanosheets, the total amount of surface functional groups increases with prolonged alkalization time. Previous XRD results confirmed that prolonged alkalization reduces the crystallinity of MXenes. These results indicate that nanoribbons contain more vacancy defects and surface functional groups than nanosheets do, thereby providing more favorable sites for S and N doping. Quantitative XPS analysis revealed that the total doping content of S and N in SN*T*@a5M reached approximately 13.4 at%, which was higher than that in d-MXene nanosheets (~6.8 at%) (Fig. S6 in the ESM).

At a doping temperature of 200 °C, N atoms are primarily incorporated into SN200@a5M as FS, whereas S atoms predominantly occupy LS sites (Figs. 2(b) and 2(c)). With increasing doping temperature, the number of N atoms in both the SA and LS sites gradually increases, whereas the number of FS sites also increases. In contrast, the number of S atoms in the LS sites decreases, whereas those in the SA and FS sites increase. This study demonstrates that constructing a hybrid architecture of MXene nanoribbons and nanosheets can effectively solve the restacking issue of MXene nanosheets. More importantly, the introduction of nanoribbons facilitates the generation of more defects, surface functional groups, and dopant sites, thereby providing an effective pathway for tailoring the dielectric properties of MXenes, as discussed in the following paragraphs.

The high-frequency complex permittivity (*ε*r = * ε′ − i ε″) is a key parameter determining the microwave absorption performance of nonmagnetic materials [43]. The complex permittivity of d-MXene nanosheets was first compared with that of a-MXene nanoribbon/nanosheet composites. The real part ( ε′) of the permittivity reflects the ability of a material to store electromagnetic energy, whereas the imaginary part ( ε″) represents its dissipation ability. Figure S7 in the ESM and Figs. 3(a) and 3(b) show the frequency-dependent  ε′and  ε″ curves of the samples within the 2–18 GHz range. The d-MXene nanosheets exhibit very high  ε′ and  ε″ values. As the alkalization time increases, the  ε′ and  ε″ values of the material significantly decrease to 25.01–4.96 and 10.25–0.77, respectively, and the electrical conductivity of the samples gradually decreases with increasing etching time (Fig. 3(c)). According to Debye relaxation theory, the dielectric loss is a sum of conductive loss ( εc″) and polarization loss ( εp″), as described by Eqs. (3) and (4) [44−46]:

where *ω* is the angular frequency, *τ* is the relaxation time, *σ* is the conductivity, *ε*s is the static permittivity, and *ε*∞ is the relative permittivity at the high-frequency limit.

According to Eq. (4), each polarization relaxation process is represented by a Cole–Cole semicircle in the * ε″– ε′ plot. The Cole–Cole curves of the a-MXene samples in Figs. 3(d) and 3(f) exhibit two characteristic features. On the one hand, the presence of multiple overlapping semicircles indicates the coexistence of several polarization relaxation mechanisms. On the other hand, a distinct linear tail appears at the end of the curves, reflecting the contribution of conductive loss to the dielectric behavior. With prolonged alkaline treatment time, the linear tail gradually decreases, whereas the semicircular features become more pronounced. This suggests that the proportion of polarization loss in the total dielectric loss gradually increases.

Variations in complex permittivity directly influence the interactions of materials with electromagnetic waves [47−50]. Upon incidence on the material surface, the reflection of electromagnetic waves is governed by the impedance matching of a material. Ideally, impedance matching requires that the input impedance of the material (Z*in) is equal to the impedance of free space (*Z*0), i.e., |*Z*in/*Z*0| = 1, which minimizes reflection and allows more waves to penetrate the material for subsequent dissipation [51,52]. As shown in Fig. S7(d) in the ESM, the |*Z*in/*Z*0| ratio of pristine d-MXene is less than 0.6 at 2–18 GHz, failing to reach the optimal range (0.8–1.2) [53], which is due to severe impedance mismatch caused by excessive conductivity. In a-MXene samples, the decrease in * ε′ and  ε″ progressively shifts the |Z*in/*Z*0| values toward 0.8–1.2. Figure S7(d) in the ESM and Figs. 3(g)–3(i) demonstrate that alkaline treatment broadens the impedance matching region, increasing the impedance matching area proportion from 9.22% for d-MXene to 12.06% for a3-MXene. Subsequent alkali treatment further increased this ratio to 18.14% for a5-MXene. However, excessive alkalization in a7-MXene sharply reduces * ε′ and  ε″, decreasing the proportion to 8.97% and weakening the dissipation capability.

Figure S8 in the ESM displays the RL curves of the aforementioned samples within the 2–18 GHz frequency range. The microwave absorption performance of pristine d-MXene is relatively weak, with a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of only −10.95 dB at a matching thickness of 2.09 mm. In contrast, the alkalized a3-MXene sample exhibited significantly improved absorption (Fig. S9(a) in the ESM). Its RLmin reaches −26.11 dB, and it achieves an effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) of 1.98 GHz at a thickness of 3 mm. The a5-MXene sample, which possesses an optimal ratio of nanoribbons to nanosheets, demonstrates better microwave absorption properties. It attains an RLmin of −45.72 dB at a thickness of 4 mm and an EAB of 4.21 GHz at a thickness of 1.5 mm (Fig. S9(h) in the ESM). However, the excessively alkalized a7-MXene shows a decline in performance, with an RLmin of −20.7 dB and an EAB of only 0.64 GHz. Among the four samples, a5-MXene exhibited the best overall microwave absorption performance. As shown in Fig. S9 in the ESM, the thicknesses corresponding to the absorption peaks of the three samples align well with the curves from Eq. (5) [54,55]:

(5) tm=nλ/4=nc/(4fm(|μr||εr|)12)(n=1,3,5,…)

where  tm and  fm represent the matching thickness and the corresponding frequency, respectively. This phenomenon indicates that the samples follow the quarter-wavelength resonance mechanism. A 180° phase difference exists between the incident and reflected waves, resulting in interference cancellation. Therefore, on the basis of this optimal composition, subsequent studies will focus on further enhancing the absorption properties through elemental doping.

The microwave absorption properties of the SNT*@a5M samples were further evaluated. As shown in Fig. 4, with an appropriate S/N co-doping concentration, the microwave absorption performance of SN*T*@a5M is better than that of the pristine a5-MXene. For example, the RLmin of SN200@a5M decreases to −61.58 dB. SN300@a5M, with a thickness of 1.26 mm, simultaneously achieves strong absorption (RLmin = −53.77 dB) and a broadened EAB of 4.51 GHz. The EAB values of SN400@a5M and SN500@a5M are further extended to 4.69 GHz (at 1.73 mm) and 4.80 GHz (at 2.18 mm), respectively. As shown in Figs. 4(m)–4(p), the EMW absorption behavior of the four samples also follows the quarter-wavelength resonance mechanism. In terms of absorption intensity and effective bandwidth, the SN300@a5M sample exhibited the best comprehensive performance. As shown in Table 1, the comprehensive EMW absorption performance of SN300@a5M surpasses those of other reported MXene-based materials. It achieves a lower RLmin and a broader EAB at a reduced thickness, making it suitable for high-demand electromagnetic protection scenarios. We further investigated the underlying mechanism for the enhancement of the microwave absorption performance by analyzing the complex permittivity of the doped samples.

As shown in Figs. 5(a)–5(c), both * ε′ and ε*″ exhibit a nonmonotonic trend with increasing doping temperature. Below 300 °C, they gradually increase with increasing temperature. However, when the temperature further increases to 400 and 500 °C, both * ε′ and  ε″ of SN400@a5M and SN500@a5M decrease gradually. To understand this phenomenon, we focused on the influence of conductivity and polarization behavior on  ε″. Figure 5(d) shows the variations in  εc″ and  εp″ of the samples with respect to the doping temperature. As the temperature increases,  εc″ initially increases and then decreases, which is consistent with the variation in the measured conductivity (Fig. S10(b) in the ESM). In contrast,  εp″ continuously increases.* Their different variations are closely correlated with the change in microstructure caused by doping. On one hand, the interlayer spacing of SN*T*@a5M gradually decreases with increasing doping temperature (Fig. 1(f)). Previous studies have indicated that a reduction in the interlayer spacing leads to decreased conductivity [22,63], thereby weakening the contribution of conductive loss to * ε″ (Fig. 5(d)). On the other hand, increasing the doping temperature increases the concentration of S/N dopant atoms, which simultaneously affects the conductivity and polarization properties of the material. As revealed in the above structural characterization, S and N atoms can occupy three doping sites in MXenes, i.e., the LS, FS, and SA sites. To clarify the influence of different doping configurations on ε*″, we established three types of models (LS, FS, and SA) and performed DFT calculations. The results indicate that all the systems remain metallic, with their density of states (DOS) crossing the Fermi level (Fig. 5(e)). The FS model results in the highest electron density of states near the Fermi level, suggesting the most significant enhancement effect on conductivity [64]. In contrast, in the LS model, although S/N atoms can form strong interactions with Ti/C atoms, excessive substitution introduces lattice distortion and defect centers, thereby increasing electron scattering and reducing conductivity [65]. SA doping primarily occurs through covalent bonding and has a relatively weaker effect on conductivity. Combining the XPS results, it is evident that at 200 °C, N atoms primarily exist in the FS configuration (Figs. 2(b) and 2(c)). When the temperature increases to 300 °C, the proportion of FS-type N increases further, which increases the conductivity of SN200@a5M and SN300@a5M, resulting in a significant increase in * εc″ (Fig. 5(d)). However, as the temperature further increases, the proportion of LS-type N increases, and the increasing total amount of S atoms also results in a greater number of S atoms occupying LS sites. In SN400@a5M and SN500@a5M, the negative impact of LS-type doping on conductivity outweighs the positive contribution from FS-type doping, causing a decrease in  εc″. In addition, the doping of S and N changes the electron cloud distribution of the material, introducing additional dipoles that generate  εp″ under high-frequency electromagnetic fields. As the doping temperature increased, the impurity content in SNT@a5M increased, resulting in an increase in  εp″. In summary, the doping temperature modulates the ε*″ of a material through multiple mechanisms. The reduction in the interlayer spacing decreases * εc″, whereas changes in the concentration of atoms at different doping sites may either increase or decrease  εc″. Moreover, the  εp″ increases with increasing doping concentration. Figure S11 in the ESM shows the frequency-dependent curves of  εc″ and  εp″ for different samples.

The variation in the complex permittivity induced by doping further influences the impedance matching and |*Z*in/*Z*0| of the materials. As shown in Fig. S12 in the ESM, although the enhanced complex permittivity of SN200@a5M and SN300@a5M leads to a slight deterioration in |*Z*in/*Z*0|, their attenuation constant (*α*), which can quantify the sample's ability to attenuate electromagnetic waves, increases significantly (Fig. S12(f) in the ESM). Consequently, SN300@a5M achieves both strong absorption and a broad effective absorption bandwidth. In contrast, SN400@a5M and SN500@a5M exhibit significantly improved |*Z*in/*Z*0|; however, their *α* values decrease markedly. As a result, these two samples show a further expanded effective absorption bandwidth but a noticeably reduced absorption strength. As depicted in Fig. 5(f), the superior microwave absorption performance of SN300@a5M stems from synergistic loss mechanisms induced by material design and element doping. The initial alkali-assisted chemical scissor strategy results in a unique MXene nanoribbon/nanosheet structure, mitigating nanosheet restacking while promoting multiple reflections of electromagnetic waves; this shearing also increases the active site density. Subsequent S/N co-doping, modulated by temperature, enables concerted tuning of conductive and polarization losses, effectively attenuating microwave radiation to yield outstanding absorption efficacy.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, an alkali-assisted chemical scissor strategy was employed to prepare an MXene nanoribbon/nanosheet composite substrate, which was subsequently subjected to S/N co-doping. The introduction of nanoribbons not only effectively alleviated the stacking of nanosheets but also provided abundant active sites for atomic-level doping. This research reveals a distinct evolution of S/N doping configurations with temperature. Below 300 °C, N atoms and S atoms preferentially occupy functional group substitutions and lattice substitution sites, respectively. This configuration synergistically enhances the electrical conductivity and polarization of the material. When the temperature increases above 400 °C, the N- and S-doped configurations exhibit both surface absorption and lattice substitution, which decreases the conductivity and thus reduces the complex permittivity. The SN300@a5M sample exhibited the best comprehensive EMW absorption performance. At a thin thickness of 1.26 mm and a low filler loading of 10 wt%, an RLmin of −53.77 dB and an EAB of 4.51 GHz are achieved. This work provides deep insights into the structure–property relationship between doping configurations and dielectric performance from the perspective of atomic site occupation, offering important guidance for the development of high-performance EMW-absorbing materials.
