## 1 Introduction

The rapid development of wireless communication technology and the widespread deployment of electronic devices have led to an unprecedented surge in electromagnetic pollution, especially in the low-frequency paradigm (S band: 2–4 GHz, C band: 4–8 GHz), which includes critical communication bands such as 5G, Wi-Fi, and radar systems [1−4]. In this frequency range, effective electromagnetic wave (EMW) absorption is critical for mitigating electromagnetic interference and enhancing stealth capabilities in military and civil applications, especially in the n77 (3.3–4.2 GHz), n78 (3.3–3.8 GHz), and n79 (4.4–5.0 GHz) operating bands dedicated to 5G communications [5−8]. On the other hand, in the aerospace field, the key to breakthroughs in radar stealth technology for high-speed vehicles and aeroengines lies in the development of high-temperature (≥ 500 °C) EMW absorption materials [9−11]. However, existing EMW absorption material systems face obvious technical bottlenecks in low-frequency, high-temperature application scenarios. For example, traditional dielectric absorbers (carbon-based materials) are constrained by poor impedance matching and insufficient low-frequency loss mechanisms, whereas magnetic absorbers (ferrite and metal alloys) suffer from inherent defects such as high density, limited Curie temperature, and easy oxidation at high temperatures [12−14]. Therefore, the development of high-temperature resistant EMW absorption materials with low-frequency responses is urgently needed.

Silicon carbide (SiC), a wide-bandgap semiconductor, has emerged as a promising candidate for EMW absorption because of its exceptional thermal stability, chemical inertness, and environmental resistance [15]. However, the intrinsically low electrical conductivity of SiC results in poor impedance matching with free space, which limits its absorption efficiency, particularly in the low-frequency region [16]. To address these challenges, recent research efforts have focused on tailoring the electromagnetic properties of SiC through defect engineering, heterostructure construction, and hybridization with magnetic species [17−19]. Despite some progress, the development of SiC-based composites that simultaneously integrate low-frequency absorption and high-temperature stability remains unrealized, highlighting the need for further exploration of the novel architecture and loss mechanisms of SiC-based absorbers. The incorporation of transition metals (Co, Fe) and rare earth elements (Ce, La) has shown great promise in optimizing impedance matching and introducing multiple loss mechanisms (magnetic resonance, interface polarization, and defect-induced dipole relaxation). Liu *et al*. [20] demonstrated that Ce doping can optimize the impedance matching of SiC and enrich the loss mechanism, thereby improving the EMW absorption performance. However, systematic investigations on the low-frequency and high-temperature response of SiC modified by this strategy have yet to be reported.

Herein, we present a SiC-based composite for EMW absorption achieved by coconfining transition metal Co and rare earth metal Ce into a SiC matrix. Among them, the reflection loss (*R*L) of SiC/CoSi/CeSi at a low frequency of 3.65 GHz is −66.48 dB, which is 14 times greater than that of pure SiC and shows competitive performance with previous work on SiC-based absorbers. Notably, the SiC/CoSi/CeSi composite demonstrated negligible absorption efficiency decay after high-temperature oxidation at 500 °C, with no observable changes in its structure or composition. The experimental results show that the good thermal stability of SiC, CoSi, and CeSi promotes the intended high-temperature stability of the composite. Mechanistic analysis revealed that the excellent EMW absorption performance of the SiC/CoSi/CeSi composite can be attributed to the porous structure promoting multiple reflections and scattering, the 3D conductive network promoting conduction loss, the rich components promoting interface polarization caused by the heterogeneous interface, and the 4f electrons of Ce contributing to the local magnetic moments and the paramagnetic CoSi synergistically promoting magnetic loss.

### 2.1 Chemicals

The raw materials used include tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), cerium(III) nitrate hexahydrate (Ce(NO3)3·6H2O, 98.5%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate (C4H6CoO4·4H2O, 99.5%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), sucrose (C12H22O11, 99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36 wt%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), tetrapropyl ammonium hydroxide (TPAOH; 25 wt%, Anhui Senrise Technologies Co., Ltd.), and magnesium powder (Mg, 99.5%, Tianjin Damao Chemical Reagent Factory).

### 2.2 Preparation of all silicon zeolite (MFI)

MFI-type silicate-1 zeolite was synthesized hydrothermally. In detail, 6.93 g of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), 10.56 g of tetra-propyl-ammonium hydroxide (TPAOH, 25 wt%), and 15.84 g of water were added and stirred at room temperature for 8 h. Then, the mixture was transferred to an autoclave and kept at 180 °C in an oven for 2 d. The obtained powder was subsequently washed with deionized water and absolute ethyl alcohol until the pH value of the filtrate was close to 7 and then dried at 100 °C for 12 h. Afterwards, it was kept at 550 °C for 4 h in a muffle furnace under an air atmosphere.

### 2.3 Preparation of Si/SiO2

The mesoporous zeolite was prepared via a typical magnesium thermal reduction method. Initially, MFI and magnesium powder (mass ratio of 1 : 1) were poured into a mortar and ground for 45 min. Afterwards, the mixed powder was loaded into a porcelain boat, heated to 700 °C under an argon atmosphere, maintained for 5 h, and then cooled naturally to room temperature. The obtained powder was added to a HCl solution (1 mol∙L−1) for 24 h to remove byproducts (MgO and Mg2Si) or impurities. The product was collected by centrifugation and washed several times with deionized water until the pH reached 7. The obtained powder was vacuum dried in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h to obtain Si/SiO2 powder.

### 2.4 Preparation of SiC/CoSi/CeSi and SiC/CoSi/CeSi-500

First, sucrose (0.237 g), cobalt acetate tetrahydrate (0.003 g), and cerium nitrate hexahydrate (0.03 g) were dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water. Then, 0.1 g of Si/SiO2 powder was poured into the above solution, ultrasonically treated for 10 min, and stirred at room temperature for 3 h. Subsequently, the mixture was freeze-dried at −90 °C for 48 h. Finally, the obtained powder was heat-treated under flowing argon in a tube furnace. Briefly, the temperature was first increased to 800 °C at a heating rate of 2 °C∙min−1, held for 2 h, increased to 1400 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C∙min−1 and held for 2 h. To verify the high-temperature resistance of the obtained material, SiC/CoSi/CeSi was treated at 500 °C in a muffle furnace under an air atmosphere to obtain SiC/CoSi/CeSi-500. For comparison, the materials obtained without Co and Ce, without Co, and without Ce are SiC, SiC/CeSi, and SiC/CoSi, respectively.

### 2.5 Materials characterizations

The morphology of the samples was observed via a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; SU-8010, HITACHI, Japan) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-F200, JEOL, Japan). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed on the SEM and TEM devices. The phase compositions of the samples were measured by an X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectrometer (D8-Advance, Bruker, Germany) with a Cu-Kα radiation source. The graphitization degree of carbon was characterized by measuring the Raman spectrum via a Raman spectrometer (LABRAM HR800, HORIBA, Japan). A high-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Escalade 250Xi, Thermo Fisher, USA) was used to analyze the surface electronic states and elemental compositions of the samples. The Brunauer‒Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area and pore size distributions of SiC were obtained via nitrogen adsorption‒desorption isotherm analysis via a micromeritics-specific surface area analyzer (BRT; ASAP2020M, Micromeritics, USA).

### 2.6 Electromagnetic parameter measurement

To prepare the EMW absorber ring, the sample was mixed with paraffin wax evenly and pressed. Its inner diameter is 3.04 mm, outer diameter is 7.00 mm, and thickness is 2.00–3.00 mm. The electromagnetic parameters of the EMW absorber were measured in the frequency range of 2–18 GHz via the coaxial line method with a vector network analyzer (VNA; 3656D, Ceyear, China).

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1 shows a schematic diagram of the synthesis process of the SiC/CoSi/CeSi composites. First, TPAOH was used as a structure-directing agent, and TEOS was used as a silicon source to synthesize an ordered mesoporous MFI zeolite through a mild hydrothermal reaction. Afterwards, the highly active Si in the zeolite was converted into Si/SiO2 hybrid nanoparticles through magnesium thermal reduction treatment, and the porous structure of the zeolite was retained. Finally, sucrose (carbon source), Si/SiO2 (silicon source), cerium salt, and cobalt salt were mixed evenly and freeze-dried. Then, it was converted into a SiC composite rich in Ce and Co substances through high-temperature heat treatment. Notably, the porous Si/SiO2 derived from the zeolite is highly active and can significantly reduce the sintering temperature to 1380 °C, which is significantly lower than the traditional SiC synthesis temperature (usually ≥ 1500 °C). During the pyrolysis process, the formation of the composites is controlled by three concurrent reactions. Initially, the mixed Si and SiO2 nanoparticles react chemically to produce SiO gas (Eq. (1)). SiO gas subsequently reacts with carbon to form SiC nanoparticles (Eq. (2)). Finally, there is a gas‒solid growth mechanism of SiC nanowires, in which the generation of large amounts of SiO and CO gases ensures the continuous growth of SiC crystals (Eq. (3)). In this process, transition metals (Co) and rare earth elements (Ce) have synergistic catalytic effects. Metallic Co promotes the vapor‒liquid‒solid (VLS) growth of SiC nanowires, whereas Ce promotes nucleation by regulating the surface energy [23,24]. This dual catalytic mechanism forms an interconnected 3D conductive network consisting of SiC nanowires and nanoparticles. Under continuous high temperatures, metallic Co and Ce react with highly reactive Si to produce thermodynamically stable CoSi and CeSi phases, which establish multiple heterogeneous interfaces with the SiC matrix, ultimately forming a ternary composite (SiC/CoSi/CeSi).

The phase composition and structural evolution of the synthesized materials were systematically investigated via X-ray diffraction (XRD). As shown in Fig. 2(a), the main diffraction peaks at 2θ* = 35.6°, 60.0°, and 71.8° correspond to the (111), (220), and (311) planes of cubic silicon carbide (β-SiC, PDF#96-0996), confirming the high crystallinity of the SiC matrix [23]. A comparison of the peak intensities of SiC and CoSi/SiC clearly reveals that the incorporation of Co catalyzes the formation of SiC, thereby enabling the generation of numerous SiC nanowires at a relatively low temperature of 1380 °C, as confirmed by the SEM image below. Notably, the absence of metallic cobalt and crystalline cerium oxide phases indicates that Co and Ce mainly react with Si to form silicide phases. The characteristic peaks at 2*θ* = 41.2° and 47.8° are indexed to the (210) and (211) planes of the cobalt silicide (CoSi, PDF#72-1328), whereas the peak at 2*θ* = 32.5° corresponds to the (111) plane of the cerium silicide (CeSi, PDF#04-0795), confirming that Ce has been successfully incorporated into the silicide structure [24,25]. Additionally, the peak at 2*θ* = 22.1° corresponds to silicon dioxide (SiO2, PDF#97-0531), which may be formed by partial oxidation of SiC under high-temperature conditions [26].

Raman spectroscopy revealed differences in the carbon bonding states within the composites. As shown in Fig. 2(b), two prominent peaks are observed at ~1350 cm−1 (D band) and 1580 cm−1 (G band), corresponding to the A1g and E2g vibration modes of sp2-hybridized carbon, respectively [27]. A lower I*D/*I*G ratio typically indicates a high degree of graphitization and low defect density, whereas a higher *I*D/*I*G ratio indicates the presence of numerous defects or a disordered structure [28]. For the SiC/CoSi/CeSi composite, the *I*D/*I*G ratio is 1.59, indicating a relatively high defect density, which may originate from sp3-hybridized carbon atoms, vacancies, edge defects, disordered structures, or structural distortions introduced by dopants such as Co and Ce. In contrast, the *I*D/*I*G ratio of SiC/CoSi/CeSi-500 decreases significantly to 0.87 after oxidation, indicating a significant decrease in the defect density. This decrease can be attributed to the partial repair or elimination of defects during the oxidation process.

As shown in Fig. 2(c), both SiC and SiC/CoSi/CeSi exhibit type IV isotherms, which are characteristic of mesoporous materials (2–50 nm) [29]. The specific surface areas of SiC and SiC/CoSi/CeSi are 85.0 and 57.1 m2∙g−1, respectively. The pore size distribution curves show that the main peaks of SiC and SiC/CoSi/CeSi are both at 3.92 nm, confirming the mesoporous nature of the materials. The high specific surface area of SiC/CoSi/CeSi provides abundant active sites for interfacial polarization, which significantly improves the dielectric loss.

The XPS survey spectrum reveals the presence of Si, Co, Ce, N, C, and O (Fig. 2(d)), with atomic percentages of 22.3%, 0.3%, 1.5%, 1.7%, 48.5%, and 25.8%, respectively. The high-resolution Si 2p spectrum exhibited two distinct peaks at binding energies of 101.5 and 103.2 eV (Fig. 2(e)), corresponding to Si–C and Si–O bonds, respectively [30]. The presence of Si‒C bonds confirms the formation of SiC, whereas the presence of Si‒O bonds indicates the presence of residual SiO2 in the precursor. The XPS spectrum of Ce 3d (Fig. 2(g)) displays characteristic peaks at binding energies of 882.5, 886.5, 901.0, and 905.0 eV, which are attributed to Ce3+ and Ce2+ [31]. The XPS spectrum of C 1s(Fig. 2(f)) can be deconvoluted into three main peaks at 283.4 eV, 284.8 eV and 285.6 eV, which are assigned to Si–C, C–C, and C–O bonds, respectively [32]. The Si−C bonds confirmed that SiC was successfully formed, whereas the C−C bonds indicated the presence of residual carbon or graphitic domains within the composite [33]. The XPS spectrum of N 1s (Fig. 2(h) and Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) reveals that nitrogen exists in the form of pyridinic N (398.5 eV) and pyrrolic N (400.2 eV) [34]. The XPS spectrum of O 1s (Fig. 2(i) and Fig. S2 in the ESM) exhibits a peak at 532.5 eV, corresponding to the O–Si bond, which is consistent with the formation of SiO2 observed in the XPS spectrum of Si 2p [35]. Owing to the low Co content, no distinct peak is observed in the high-resolution XPS spectrum of Co 2p (Fig. S3 in the ESM), which indicates that Co is mainly incorporated into the SiC matrix as CoSi.

The microstructures and morphologies of the SiC/CoSi/CeSi composites were systematically investigated via SEM, as shown in Fig. 3. The Si/SiO2 precursor (Fig. 3(a) and Fig. S4 in the ESM) still exhibited the regular morphology of the zeolite template, but the surface was rougher. After high-temperature treatment, the precursor is converted into SiC nanoparticles (Fig. 3(b) and Fig. S5 in the ESM). Owing to the strong interaction between particles, SiC nanoparticles tend to aggregate and form a densely packed structure. The introduction of Co significantly changes the morphology of SiC, allowing it to form abundant SiC nanowires at a low calcination temperature of 1380 °C (Figs. 3(c) and 3(d) and Fig. S6 in the ESM). These nanowires are ~50 nm in diameter and several micrometers in length and are interlaced to create a 3D network with SiC nanoparticles interspersed. This transformation is attributed to the catalytic effect of metallic Co, which facilitates the VLS growth mechanism of SiC nanowires. The addition of Ce did not significantly change the overall morphology (Figs. 3(e) and 3(f) and Fig. S7 in the ESM), as the composite retained the 3D network structure of interlaced SiC nanowires and nanoparticles. The interlaced SiC nanowires and CoSi, CeSi, and SiC nanoparticles generate numerous heterointerfaces, which enhance the interface polarization. This porous structure constructed from these nanoparticles and nanowires promotes multiple reflections and scattering of EMW, thereby improving the EMW dissipation efficiency (Fig. 3(k)). The elemental mapping images (Fig. 3(g) and Fig. S8 in the ESM) clearly demonstrate the homogeneous distribution of Co and Ce in the SiC matrix, confirming their successful incorporation. SiC nanowires exhibit exceptional thermal stability and mechanical strength, as evidenced by the intact structure of the SiC/CoSi/CeSi composite after high-temperature oxidation at 500 °C (Figs. 3(h)–3(j) and Fig. S9 in the ESM). This stability is attributed to the inherent structural stability of SiC, CoSi, and CeSi, making SiC/CoSi/CeSi composites promising candidates for high-performance EMW absorption and high-temperature applications (Fig. 3(l)).

The microstructure of SiC/CoSi/CeSi was further investigated via TEM. As shown in Figs. 4(a)–4(d) and Fig. S10(a) in the ESM, SiC/CoSi/CeSi successfully generated a mixture of SiC nanowires and nanoparticles, forming many heterogeneous interfaces. On the other hand, Fig. 4(d) shows that the lattice spacings of the nanoparticles are 0.25 and 0.20 nm, corresponding to the (200) plane of the SiC phase and the (211) plane of the CoSi phase, respectively, confirming the generation of the CoSi phase [37]. The elemental mapping image in Fig. 4(e) shows that Si and C are highly densely dispersed in the nanowires and nanoparticles and that Co and Ce are also present. After high-temperature oxidation, SiC/CoSi/CeSi still retains the coexistence of nanowires and nanoparticles (Fig. 4(f) and Fig. S11(a) in the ESM), which also confirms the good high-temperature antioxidant properties of the composite. Numerous nanoparticles can be seen in the HRTEM images in Figs. 4(g)–4(i). The interplanar spacings are 0.25, 0.20, and 0.26 nm, which are consistent with those of SiC and CoSi [37]. Figures 5(a)–5(h) present the 2D and 3D R*L curves of the samples. Owing to the limited dielectric loss capability of intrinsic SiC, its EMW absorption performance is greatly limited. As shown in Figs. 5(a) and 5(e), the *R*L value of pure SiC in the frequency range of 2–18 GHz is always greater than −10 dB, which is not enough to meet the needs of practical applications. However, after the introduction of transition metal Co, Co reacts with Si to form a SiC/CoSi heterostructure, the EMW absorption performance is improved, and the *R*L value can reach −15.18 dB at 9.68 GHz (Figs. 5(b) and 5(f)). Notably, after the rare earth element Ce is fermented, it has a certain synergistic effect with Co to form a SiC/CoSi/CeSi ternary composite, resulting in a significant increase in EMW absorption. As shown in Figs. 5(c) and 5(g), this composite demonstrates excellent absorption performance, with an *R*L value of −66.48 dB at 3.65 GHz in the low-frequency range. The effective absorption bandwidth (EAB; *R*L ≤ −10 dB) is 2.46 GHz (12.10–14.56 GHz). Obviously, space charge regions formed at the heterointerfaces, inducing interface polarization [38]. The paramagnetic CoSi phase generates substantial magnetic loss through natural resonance (the resonance frequency is 3–6 GHz). Importantly, after oxidation at 500 °C, SiC/CoSi/CeSi-500 retains 94.5% of its *R*L performance, with an *R*L value of −63.34 dB at 4.31 GHz (Figs. 5(d) and 5(h)). SiC/CoSi/CeSi also exhibited good EMW absorption performance after oxidation at 550 °C (Fig. S12 in the ESM), which further confirmed the excellent high-temperature EMW absorption characteristics of the SiC/CoSi/CeSi composite. In contrast, the *R*L value of SiC/CeSi reaches only −18.25 dB at 16.2 GHz. (Figs. S13 and S14 in the ESM). As indicated in Figs. 5(a)–5(d), under the combined effects of Co and Ce, SiC/CoSi/CeSi and SiC/CoSi/CeSi-500 composites with thicknesses ranging from 1–5 mm cover the entire C, X, and Ku bands and most of the S band (Fig. S15 in the ESM), offering a wide EAB. The frequency response analysis (Fig. 5(i)) further confirmed that the material exhibited significant absorption advantages in the low-frequency range (2–6 GHz). Figure 5(j) shows the full frequency strong absorption in the range of 3–6 GHz, achieving full coverage of the dedicated working frequency bands for 5G communication (n77 (3.3–4.2 GHz), n78 (3.3–3.8 GHz), and n79 (4.4–5.0 GHz)). Comparative studies show that the optimal *R*L value of SiC/CoSi/CeSi is 14 times greater than that of intrinsic SiC (Fig. 5(k)), confirming the effectiveness of the synergistic modification of Co and Ce. Theoretical analysis shows that when the material thickness (*t*m) and incident wave frequency (*f*m) satisfy the quarter-wavelength matching condition (*t*m = (2*n*+1)*λ*/4, *n* = 0, 1, 2, ...), the phase-eliminating interference effect makes the *R*L reach an extreme value [39]. Figure 5(l) and Fig. S16 in the ESM show that the experimental data points (solid circles) are highly consistent with the *λ*/4 theoretical curve (purple), further confirming that the electromagnetic loss behavior of SiC/CoSi/CeSi follows interference matching theory.

To further explore the intrinsic reasons for the exceptional EMW absorption performance of SiC/CoSi/CeSi, the complex permittivity (*ε*r = *ε*′ − j*ε*″) and complex permeability (*μ*r = *μ*′ − j*μ*″) were investigated [40]. The real part of the permittivity (*ε*′) reflects the polarization capability of the material in the alternating electromagnetic field, whereas the imaginary part (*ε*″) represents the dielectric loss induced by the rearrangement of the dipole moments [41]. As shown in Figs. 6(a)–6(c), the intrinsic permittivity of SiC is very low, which limits its wave absorption ability. However, the introduction of the transition metal Co significantly increases the permittivity of CoSi/SiC. According to the theory of electronic degrees of freedom (*ε*″ = *σ*/(2π*ε*0*f*), where *ε*0 is the vacuum permittivity), a larger *ε*″ corresponds to a larger permittivity, resulting in strong EMW reflection, thus hindering impedance matching and resulting in poor absorption performance of CoSi/SiC [42]. Interestingly, we found that doping rare earth element Ce into SiC can effectively modulate the permittivity. The introduction of Ce leads to a moderate increase in the permittivity of SiC/CeSi compared with that of SiC and SiC/CoSi. Therefore, adding Ce to SiC/CoSi can offset its excessively high permittivity and achieve optimal impedance matching with *ε*′ and *ε*″ values in the range of 15–20 and 4–5, respectively, thereby enabling strong EMW absorption. Importantly, after oxidation at 500 °C, the permittivity changes very little, allowing the material to maintain excellent performance. Like the permittivity, *μ*′ represents the magnetic energy storage capacity, whereas *μ*″ reflects the magnetic energy loss. SiC/CoSi/CeSi-500 has the highest *μ*′ and *μ*″ values in the low- and medium-frequency ranges, followed by SiC/CoSi/CeSi, SiC/CoSi, and SiC, the latter of which has the smallest values (Figs. S17(a) and S17(b) in the ESM). This can be attributed to the fact that the 3d electrons of Co dominate the natural resonance, whereas the 4f electrons of Ce contribute to the local magnetic moment. These two effects synergistically enhance low-frequency magnetic losses. The *μ*″ spectra show a distinct resonance peak at low frequency, which is consistent with previous studies indicating that the low-frequency resonance peak is related to natural resonance [43]. Moreover, tan*δ**μ* = *μ*″/*μ*′ and tan*δ**ε* = *ε*″/*ε*′ (*δ*μ resperents the magnetic loss angle, and *δ*ε resperents the dielectric loss angle) are typically considered to be the magnetic loss factor and dielectric loss factor, respectively [44]. As shown in Fig. 6(c), SiC exhibited very low dielectric loss, resulting in poor performance. SiC/CoSi has the highest dielectric loss, but its strong reflection prevents most of the incident EMW from entering the material, leading to poor performance. After rare earth modification, SiC/CoSi/CeSi and SiC/CoSi/CeSi-500 achieve a synergistic balance of dielectric and magnetic losses, resulting in extremely strong absorption performance. The tan*δ**μ* curve is similar to the *μ*″ curve, with resonance peaks appearing in the low- and medium-frequency ranges (Fig. S17(c) in the ESM).

According to Debye theory, the relationship between *ε*′ and *ε*″ can be expressed as (*ε*′ − *ε*∞)2 + (*ε*″)2 = (*ε*s − *ε*∞)2 [45]. As shown in Fig. 6(d), SiC/CoSi has both linear and semicircular features, and the linear tail indicates the existence of conduction loss in SiC/CoSi. This indicates the presence of both conduction loss and polarization loss, but the number of semicircles is small, indicating low polarization loss [46]. In contrast, SiC/CoSi/CeSi and SiC/CoSi/CeSi-500 exhibit multiple semicircles (Figs. 6(e) and 6(f)), indicating that they have strong dielectric polarization relaxation and large polarization loss, thereby significantly enhancing their EMW absorption capabilities [47]. The Cole‒Cole plots of both SiC and SiC/CeSi exhibit fewer semicircular arcs, indicating their relatively weak polarization loss capability (Fig. S18 in the ESM). As shown in Fig. 6(g), under the combined effect of transition metals and rare earth elements, the *R*L of SiC/CoSi/CeSi and SiC/CoSi/CeSi-500 is significantly enhanced. This is because many heterojunctions are formed between the SiC nanowires and SiC nanoparticles and between SiC, CoSi, and CeSi, which greatly enhances the interface polarization, as shown in Fig. 6(h).

The EMW absorption capability directly depends on the reflection and attenuation of the incident EMW. Reflection is related to impedance matching, whereas attenuation is associated with electromagnetic loss, which can be evaluated by the attenuation constant (*α* = [((2)1/2π*f*)/*c*] {(*μ"ε"* − *μ'ε'*) + [(*μ"ε"* − *μ'ε'*)2 + (*ε'μ"* + *ε"μ'*)2]1/2}1/2) [48]. Theoretically, a higher *α* value indicates better performance, as it indicates that the absorber can effectively convert incident EMW energy into heat [49]. As shown in Fig. 7(a), although SiC/CoSi has the highest *α* value, its performance is hindered by its excessive permittivity, which leads to impedance mismatch and reflects most of the incident EMW, leaving only a small part to enter the absorber. This finding highlights that effective EMW absorption requires excellent synergy between strong loss capability and optimal impedance matching. Impedance matching refers to the effective impedance alignment between the free space and the material (*Z* = (*μ*r/*ε*r)1/2tanh|j2π*fd*(*μ*r*ε*r)1/2/*c*|), and the closer the *Z* value is to 1, the better the impedance matching [50]. As shown in Fig. 7(c), the *Z* values of SiC/CoSi/CeSi and SiC/CoSi/CeSi-500 are very close to 1, indicating optimal impedance matching. These materials also demonstrate the strongest attenuation constants, second only to CoSi/SiC, which is consistent with their superior EMW absorption performance. Figure 7(d) shows the strongest *R*L of SiC, SiC/CoSi, SiC/CeSi, SiC/CoSi/CeSi, and SiC/CoSi/CeSi-500 and their optimal impedance matching. Additionally, eddy current loss is a key parameter for assessing absorption capacity and can be expressed as *C*0 = *μ"*(*μ'*)-2*f*−1 = 2π*μ*0*d*2*σ* [51]. Figure 7(b) shows that the *C*0 values for all the materials fluctuate noticeably in the low-frequency range, indicating the occurrence of magnetic losses caused by natural resonance [52]. However, in the medium- and high-frequency ranges, their *C*0 curves fluctuate slightly but remain relatively stable, indicating that eddy current loss plays a more important role in the frequency range of 6–18 GHz [53]. The EMW absorption capabilities of SiC/CoSi/CeSi are significantly better than those of other SiC-based absorbers, as shown in Fig. 7(e) and Table S1 in the ESM.

To better assess their practical application, the radar cross section (RCS) of the samples was simulated via computer modeling techniques (CST software) [54]. Figure 7(f) and Fig. S19 in the ESM display the RCS curves of the five coatings and perfect electric conductor (PEC) plates in the range of −60°–60°, as well as the 3D radar wave scattering signals of the PEC plates and coated plates. The 3D radar wave scattering signal diagram shows that the RCS signals for the SiC plate and uncoated PEC plate are the strongest, whereas the signals of the PEC plates coated with CeSi/SiC and CoSi/SiC are slightly weaker. The SiC/CoSi/CeSi- and SiC/CoSi/CeSi-500-coated plates show almost no scattering signals from the back, indicating that the incident waves are fully absorbed, which aligns with their excellent EMW absorption properties. The 2D RCS curve further confirms that SiC behaves similarly to the PEC and does not have significant radar stealth capabilities. After the introduction of the transition metal Co, SiC/CoSi exhibited a slight stealth effect. When Co and the rare earth element Ce are incorporated simultaneously, SiC/CoSi/CeSi demonstrates significant radar stealth performance, with the RCS values at all measured angles remaining below −20 dB. At an angle of 10, the RCS reduction of SiC/CoSi/CeSi reached 22.7 dB∙m2. Importantly, after high-temperature oxidation at 500 °C, the material still maintains excellent radar stealth capability, demonstrating the potential of SiC/CoSi/CeSi to function effectively in high-temperature environments. Figure 7(g) presents the RCS reduction values calculated via the differential method at seven detection angles. The RCS attenuation effect of the SiC/CoSi/CeSi composite is the most effective, with values ranging from 4–25 dB∙m2, which is consistent with the conclusions derived from the reflected *R*L values, further confirming that SiC/CoSi/CeSi composites have strong radar stealth potential.

On the basis of the above analysis, we propose an EMW mechanism for SiC/CoSi/CeSi composites, as shown in Fig. 8. The synergistic integration of the transition metal Co and the rare earth element Ce with SiC produces a 3D conductive network characterized by the interlacement of SiC nanowires and nanoparticles, which promotes conduction loss, and this highly porous structure promotes multiple reflections and scattering of the EMW, thereby depleting the EMW energy. The introduction of Co and Ce also promotes the formation of CoSi and CeSi phases, thereby forming a rich heterogeneous interface and inducing the generation of interface polarization [55]. Furthermore, the 4f electrons of Ce contribute to the local magnetic moments, produce natural resonance with the paramagnetic CoSi phase, and synergistically enhance the low-frequency magnetic loss. Additionally, the thermal stability of SiC, CoSi, and CeSi enables the composite to maintain excellent high-temperature resistance. Therefore, the SiC/CoSi/CeSi composite exhibited excellent EMW absorption properties and high-temperature resistance under the synergistic effects of intrinsic stability, interface polarization, dipole polarization, multiple reflections, conduction loss, natural resonance, and eddy current loss. These structural features establish a multiscale polarization network through the intrinsic dipole moment polarization of SiC, the interfacial polarization of the heterogeneous interfaces, and the defect-induced polarization centers, which collectively improve the polarization loss efficiency.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, a ternary SiC/CoSi/CeSi composite with superior EMW absorption properties was successfully fabricated by the synergistic incorporation of transition metal Co and rare earth element Ce into a SiC matrix. On the basis of systematic analysis of electromagnetic parameters and loss mechanisms, the synergistic effect of Co–Ce enables balanced regulation between dielectric and magnetic losses. The introduction of Co and Ce promoted the formation of SiC nanoparticles, SiC nanowires, CoSi, and CeSi species, generating numerous heterogeneous interfaces and significantly enhancing interface polarization. Ce further amplifies the magnetic loss through the local magnetic moment induced by its 4f electrons. Furthermore, the paramagnetic CoSi phase contributes to magnetic loss via natural resonance. As a result, the ternary composite achieves an impressive *R*L value of −66.48 dB at 3.65 GHz while maintaining 94.5% of its absorption performance (−63.34 dB at 4.31 GHz) even after oxidation at 500 °C. This work innovatively proposes a transition metal–rare earth comodification strategy that combines atomic-scale electronic structure regulation with microscale heterointerface engineering, effectively overcoming the bottleneck of the low-frequency and high-temperature response of traditional SiC-based absorbers.
