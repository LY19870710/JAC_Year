## 1 Introduction

Thermoelectrics (TEs) can directly convert a temperature difference or heat flow into electricity and vice versa on the basis of the Seebeck and Peltier effects, thereby showing great potential in alleviating energy stress, mitigating environmental impact, and building a sustainable society [1−4]. In the last century, thermoelectrics have not been widely used in practical applications because of their low conversion efficiency [5,6]. The maximum efficiency of a TE power generator, * ηmax, is expressed as Eq. (1) [7,8]

(1) ηmax=ΔTThot(1+ZTavg−11+ZTavg+TcoldThot)

where  ΔT is the temperature difference between the hot end ( Thot) and the cold end ( Tcold); the ratio of  ΔT/ Thot is the Carnot efficiency;  ZTavg is the average figure of merit  ZT value over the whole temperature range, which is defined by the Seebeck coefficient ( S), electrical conductivity ( σ), thermal conductivity (κ*), and absolute temperature (* T), given as  ZT=S2σT/κ. Generally, the actual efficiency of a TE device is approximately 90% of the calculated  ηmax due to electrical and thermal contact resistances lost from electrical interconnects and other thermal losses [9,10]. The maximum  ZT values obtained for state-of-the-art TE materials are in the range of 1–2.5, leading to an efficiency of ~13% [11,12]. To obtain higher-efficiency thermoelectrics, three main issues have to be addressed, including enhancing  ZT, increasing the operating window of materials to work with higher temperature differences, and developing cost-effective materials to counteract low efficiency [13]. Although improvements in  ZT are limited by three strongly coupled transport parameters ( S,σ, and κ*), significant progress has been made recently because of continuous efforts worldwide [14−16].

Owing to recent strategies and new physical mechanisms, including nanostructuring, band alignment, and multiband convergence, thermoelectrics has the potential to be used in various fields in the present world (Fig. 1), such as waste heat recovery, electricity generation, electronic sensors, and thermoelectric coolers (TECs), because of their versatility, scalability, and reliability without moving parts [13,17−20]. As displayed in Fig. 1, one of the major present applications is the use of thermoelectric generators (TEGs) for electricity production in extreme environments (i.e., in space, on offshore platforms, and in deserts), as they can generate reliable power over a long period with low maintenance. In space exploration, spacecraft such as Voyagers and Orbiters are equipped with radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs) with low mass and extreme reliability to provide electricity for long-distance missions. Another interesting application of TEGs is the design of the PowerPot, a portable electric generator that doubles as a cooking pot. The PowerPot provides campers with the ability to charge their electronic devices by using boiling water. Compared with compact solar panels, a PowerPot is much more reliable since it can generate electricity from a temperature difference. If improved, thermoelectric modules have the potential to be installed in automobiles, aircraft, and ships for exhausted heat recovery and built up in heat pipes to harvest waste heat in industry. Further applications of TEGs include microgeneration for sensors and electronics, such as body heat-powered wristwatches and medical monitoring devices [21,22]. Furthermore, TECs offer the potential for cooling without refrigerant gas, which has attracted much attention because of growing environmental concerns. They can be found in portable and medical refrigerators, wine bottle coolers, and electronic coolers. Eventually, thermoelectrics might be found in our daily medical applications, making life more convenient and comfortable.

Tracing their development history, TE materials have experienced two major leaps motivated by “big ideas” since the middle of the last century [23−25]. In the 1950s, heavily doped semiconductors were found to possess excellent TE properties with reduced thermal conductivity by alloying. Previously, metal junctions were used for thermoelectrics, but such TE devices were bulky and had low conversion efficiency. During the first few years, many heavily doped TE semiconductors, such as PbSe–PbTe and bismuth telluride (Bi2Te3)–Sb2Te3 [7], were developed to efficiently miniaturize devices owing to the theoretical contributions and practical achievements of Vedernikov and Iordanishvili [26] and Goldsmid [27]. Soon afterward, SiGe semiconductor materials were successfully used in RTGs to convert heat from nuclear power into electricity [28,29]. The second leap started in the mid-1990s, spurred by the emergence of novel quantum confinement concepts introduced by Hicks and Dresselhaus [30], a topic that remains under debate to date. The challenge lies in preserving structural integrity at the nanoscale within dense thermoelectric materials, which is crucial for unlocking the full potential of quantum confinement in thermoelectrics. Indeed, significant improvements have been made to address this challenge. For example, low-dimensional TE materials designed by nanostructuring exhibit enhanced thermopower and reduced lattice thermal conductivity resulting from a high density of interfaces with their own properties and composition [31,32]. The ideal TE material has a structure in which the mean free path of phonons is as short as possible for low thermal conductivity and the mean free path of electrons is as long as possible for high electrical conductivity [33]. This concept requires a deeper insight into phonon and electron transport in solids to develop better TE materials. Since then, many candidate materials have been investigated for their TE potential, including half Heuslers, skutterudites, clathrates, oxides, chalcogenides, Zintl phases, porous silicon, etc. [34−41]. As a result, the range of potential thermoelectric materials has significantly broadened from being limited solely to Bi2Te3 for industrial applications. It now encompasses a diverse array of materials, such as metal-based materials, semiconductors, ceramics, and polymers, which exhibit diverse crystalline structures, ranging from single crystals to polycrystals, and are available in various dimensions, including bulk forms, films, and wires [13]. Currently, the focus on TE technology extends beyond enhancing TE performance. It also encompasses the pursuit of cost-effective, noncritical, and environmentally friendly TE materials [42]. Many efforts have been devoted to improving the sustainability of TE materials in the last two decades. On the one hand, researchers are looking for TE materials containing earth-abundant and nontoxic elements and improving their TE performance through doping, nanostructuring, entropy, grain boundary engineering, etc. [43,44]. On the other hand, with the development of sintering technology, many studies are aiming at manufacturing dense TE materials in a green and cost-effective way [45]. The reduction in cost and energy consumption during the manufacturing process offsets the low TE efficiency, which is promising for building sustainable TE modules.

In general, TE materials must be sintered into dense and robust polycrystalline solids at high temperatures under high-pressure conditions. To activate a sufficient ionic diffusion process, the process temperature normally needs to reach 50%–75% of the melting point in conventional sintering (CS) [46,47]. In addition to high energy consumption, CS also requires a long sintering time to obtain homogenous bodies of high density, which play an important role in the TE and mechanical properties. It is difficult to control the chemical composition via high-temperature sintering, especially when volatile elements (i.e., S, Se, etc.) are involved [48,49]. Driven by these challenges, many new sintering techniques, such as pressure-assisted sintering, field-assisted sintering, and microwave heating, which provide more possibilities for the consolidation of TE materials, have been developed since the 20th century [50]. Table 1 summarizes the advantages and disadvantages of different sintering techniques that are currently used in the TE community, including CS, zone melting (ZM), hot pressing (HP), spark plasma sintering (SPS), microwave sintering (MS), flash sintering (FS), additive manufacturing (three-dimensional (3D) printing), and cold sintering process (CSP). As listed in Table 1, each sintering technique has irreplaceable advantages and inevitable disadvantages. Therefore, providing insight into sintering methods based on comprehensive consideration of the input sintering cost and output material properties is highly important.

For example, CS is the simplest densification method but requires a high sintering temperature and a long sintering time. The ZM process is very easy to perform, suitable for mass production, and frequently utilized in the industrial fabrication of Bi2Te3-based thermoelectric materials. However, TE materials fabricated via the ZM process typically have poor mechanical properties due to their highly preferred orientation, resulting in a short service time. In addition, highly dense bulk materials with good mechanical properties and enhanced TE properties can be obtained via pressure-assisted sintering techniques (i.e., HP and SPS). Nevertheless, complex and expensive equipment is needed for these methods. MS and FS methods were developed as rapid sintering techniques to limit grain growth, but they are unsuitable for all TE materials. 3D printing method has advantages in fabricating TE materials with complex and irregular shapes, but the speed and upscaling limitations, as well as the missing precursors in laser melting and problematic organic binders in the screen printing process, reveal porous and impurity results, which ultimately degrade the TE performance. These efficient sintering methods, which use electrical energy and/or pressure, have made significant progress in lowering the sintering temperature and reducing the sintering time; however, the sintering temperature is still relatively high (above 400 °C) because of the slow solid-state diffusion process. CSP is an ultralow-temperature sintering technique developed by Randall’s group in 2016, in which a transient liquid phase is utilized to assist densification with uniaxial pressure (dozens of or hundreds of MPa), and the sintering temperature is lower than 300 °C. Since then, the CSP technique has attracted extensive attention because of its lower sintering temperature, which can save energy in the fabrication process. Ibn-Mohammed et* *al*. [51] compared the energy consumption per cycle of CSP, CS, SPS, MS, FS, HP, and liquid phase sintering (LPS). Taking ZnO as an example, the comparison results show that each cycle of CSP requires 3.60 kWh of electrical energy consumption, whereas CS, SPS, MS, FS, HP, and LPS consume 38.50, 4.17, 5.25, 45.83, 24.00, and 125.00 kW·h, respectively. Therefore, CSP can save 90.65%, 13.67%, 31.43%, 92.14%, 85.00%, and 97.12% of the energy with respect to CS, SPS, MS, FS, HP, and LPS, respectively. Compared with other sintering methods, CSP has become more attractive in the sustainable TE community because of its low energy consumption, simple equipment, and comparable TE performance [45,52]. During the cold sintering process, an aqueous solution is added as a transient solvent to activate densification through a dissolution‒precipitation process, which is inspired by some natural sintering phenomena at room temperature, such as the generation of pearls in aqueous environments, the hard aggregation of table salt and sugar particles, the chromatic formation of sedimentary rocks, and other natural scenarios [53−55]. In these cases, particle rearrangement and dissolution‒precipitation processes facilitate mass transfer due to solvent flow, thereby enabling densification at ultralow temperatures. Importantly, CSP can achieve densities exceeding 95% in materials such as ZnO, CaF2, and SnSe, which are comparable to the densities obtained with SPS [56−58].

## 2 Cold sintering

The cold sintering process, also called ultralow energy sintering (ULES), is a consolidation technique in the presence of a transient liquid phase at low temperatures (typically not exceeding 300 °C) and uniaxial pressures (dozens of or hundreds of MPa) [80]. Compared with other alternative sintering techniques, the cold sintering process is more attractive and competitive for transforming ceramic or ceramic-based powders into highly dense monoliths, as it is a sustainable fabrication route with low cost, low energy consumption, and limited environmental impact [78,81,82]. This technique has been expanded to include more than one hundred different materials, mostly functional ceramics, involving various fields, i.e., ferroelectrics, piezoelectrics, thermoelectrics, microwave dielectrics, varistors, flexible electronics, solid-state batteries, electronic packaging, multilayer ceramic capacitors, etc. [77,83−88]. This section reviews the development of the CSP technique from a historical perspective, followed by an elaboration of the densification mechanism and the effect of sintering parameters on densification. Finally, the use of CSP in the fabrication of functional ceramics and ceramic-based materials is comprehensively summarized.

### 2.1 Historical perspective

The story of sintering dates back to 25,000 years ago (Paleolithic Age), when a pottery statuette (the Venus of Vestonice) was produced by shaping mineral powders and then sintering them into a ceramic body [80]. Since then, sintering has paved the way for the advancement of materials technology; however, noteworthy breakthroughs remained elusive until the 19th century. The existence of particle bonding was verified by William Hyde Wollaston in 1820, providing the possibility of compacting particles together [89]. After that, he performed the first experiment on sintering high-density platinum blocks with pressure assistance in 1829, and 99% dense materials suitable for fabricating filaments for light bulbs were finally obtained after heating in a furnace [90]. Sintering technology has flourished since the beginning of the 20th century when field-assisted sintering and microwave heating techniques were developed successively [91,92]. However, all the sintering techniques mentioned above require high temperatures to achieve high densification, which is energy-intensive from a sustainability perspective. Hydrothermal reaction sintering (HRS) was first proposed in 1964 for the low-temperature consolidation of metal powders [93]. Sintering under wet conditions and compacting pressure can initiate the formation of interparticle bonding, resulting in increased cohesion strength. Cold sintering under dry conditions at room temperature and high uniaxial pressure (0.5–4 GPa) was developed by Gutmanas’s group in 1979 to achieve consolidation under a plastic deformation regime [94]. This technique also involves ULES but is only suitable for densifying ductile metallic powders and some refractory metals. In 1984, Yamasaki *et al*. [95] reported a more versatile method, named hydrothermal hot pressing (HHP), for the consolidation of ceramic powders. During the HHP process, consolidation was achieved by continuous compression of samples in a sealed hydrothermal chamber with space for water removal, where the solubility and reactivity of the samples increased under hydrothermal conditions. Then, reactive hydrothermal liquid phase sintering (rHLPS), in which samples need to be precompacted prior to sintering and a long sintering time is required to fully complete the hydrothermal reaction, was developed by Riman’s group in 2007 [96]. In practice, the above techniques, including HRS, HHP, and rHLPS, are not suitable for large-scale fabrication because these experimental setups are very complex, and the formed samples are confined to a small size. Therefore, novel, straightforward, scalable, and low-energy sintering methods are urgently needed. CSP developed by Randall’s group in 2016 [78] basically meets the above requirements, which is why research on CSP has attracted extensive interest and attention since 2016.

### 2.2 Densification mechanism

CSP is more like a combination of LPS and HP, requiring a transient solvent to aid in sintering and simultaneously applying external pressure and temperature, but the temperature is generally lower than 300 °C. Therefore, fundamental studies on liquid-phase sintering and HP make it easier to understand the mechanism of CSP densification [97,98]. In addition to the usual consolidation driving forces, temperature, and pressure in CS, transient solvents serve as a novel driving source to lower the activation temperature for densification. As displayed in Fig. 3, the first step of CSP is to introduce a transient solvent into a powder sample by mixing it in a mortar, and sometimes, the transient solvent is introduced via a vapor transport process to ensure homogeneity [99,100]. Generally, the volume fraction of solvent is in the range of 1–20 wt%, which is sufficient to wet the powder and possibly evaporate from the system. The wetted powder is then transferred into a stainless-steel mold and pressurized to the maximum applied load at room temperature. The main process occurring at this stage is the rearrangement of particles, in which the solvent plays an important role in reducing friction between particles, providing transport channels to redistribute particles, and generating strong capillary pressure [101], as visualized in Fig. 3. In addition, early dissolution of particles into the solvent can occur depending on the chemical nature of the liquid‒solid interface, which explains the importance of solvent selection [102]. Subsequently, high powder compaction can be obtained in this stage because of the capillary forces in combination with external pressure.

In the second stage, the green body is heated to the maximum temperature under constant applied pressure and held at such conditions for a certain time. Further densification starts with a mass transport process from high-stress areas to low-stress areas in this stage [78,103,104]. First, large stress gradients in interparticle contact regions caused by local stress concentrations can trigger dissolution from small particles and sharp edges. In addition, a chemical potential gradient is also generated at the stress concentration zone due to the limited solvency of the liquid phase, which promotes matter diffusion through thin liquid layers at grain boundaries. In addition, precipitation occurs due to stress gradients and liquid phase supersaturation. The stress-enhanced dissolution‒precipitation process at contact areas contributes to the mass transport process, leading to physical shrinkage of the system and deformation at grain boundaries, which is recognized as the main densification mechanism for CSP. In addition, other mechanisms, such as grain boundary sliding, lattice and grain boundary diffusion, viscous flow, plastic deformation, and grain growth, are likely concurrently active because of the contemporary application of heat and pressure [98,105]. In particular, for ionic crystal materials such as NaCl, plastic deformation is the dominant mechanism for densification under wet conditions [106]. In some cases, extraordinary grain growth can be observed during solvent evaporation even at low temperatures, i.e., the ZnO/acetic/water system [107], and the reason for the growth is determined to be the presence of a transient liquid phase, which can enhance the surface diffusion of ions.

### 2.3 Sintering parameters

As discussed above, the densification of CSP can be activated at low temperatures in the presence of a transient liquid and external pressure. The bulk density obtained via CSP is closely related to various sintering parameters, including the solvent type, solvent content, powder size, temperature, pressure, and dwell time [108−111]. Many studies have experimentally investigated the effects of these parameters on densification, which is also helpful in understanding the densification mechanism. The introduced solvent is critical for promoting particle rearrangement and activating densification at low temperatures. One of the most important features of the solvent in CSP is the low boiling point, which enables full evaporation during heating, which is why the introduced solvent is called a transient liquid phase. In addition, the solvents commonly used in CSP, such as distilled water, acetic acid, NaOH solutions, and other organic solvents, are typically characterized by high abundance, easy access, safety, and environmental protection [112]. The type and amount of solvent play a decisive role in the degree of densification because the liquid phase is not only used as a transient solvent but also has different degrees of solubility in inorganic particles. For example, NaOH solution was proven to be more effective than tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) in the sintering of SiO2 because of its higher solubility [113,114]. A substantial decrease in densification was observed in ZnO as the acidity of the acetic acid solvent increased from 2 to 9 M because of the negative effect of decreased precipitation on the sintering mechanism [107]. Powder size is also a very important parameter for the final densification of cold-sintered samples, especially for inorganic particles with low solubility [115−117]. The dissolution‒precipitation process can be enhanced when nanoparticles are used because of the large surface-to-volume ratio, leading to high densification, which has been investigated in the sintering of ZrO2-based ceramics [118−121]. However, the final mechanical and electrical properties can also be affected by the particle size, and more comprehensive studies need to focus on this topic in the future. Typically, the sintering temperature for CSP does not exceed 300 °C but needs to be slightly higher than the melting point of the solvent. It is generally believed that heat treatment provides energy for mass transfer and promotes chemical reactions, so densification normally increases with increasing sintering temperature [122,123]. From the perspective of energy savings, the sintering temperature should be as low as possible to ensure densification. In addition, special attention should be given to the sintering temperature in regard to systems with phase transitions or the integration of dissimilar materials such as ceramics and polymers [78,124]. Uniaxial applied pressure is one of the driving sources for powder consolidation in CSP. Liu *et al*. [84] reported the microstructure evolution of cold sintered Li1.3Al0.3Ti1.7(PO4)3 (LATP), a popular solid electrolyte, at different pressures (280, 350, and 420 MPa). The agglomerates of nano-LATP particles and a large fraction of porosity can be observed on the sample pressed with low stress, leading to low densification. The densification was obviously improved by simply increasing the applied pressure by increasing both the degree of particle rearrangement and the dissolution‒precipitation process in the sintering stage. In addition to improving densification, the applied pressure can also be used as a good strategy to inhibit grain growth. Shi *et al*. [125] obtained fine-grained ZnO ceramics via high-pressure cold sintering. When the pressure is greater than the critical value (~1 GPa), the very high-stress gradient can induce homogeneous nucleation in the liquid phase, and grain growth is inhibited by the fast exhaustion of the Zn2+ source. CSP is a non-equilibrium process; therefore, the dwell time should be enough to complete mass transport and recrystallization fully. In a two-step CSP, the density of cold sintered ZnO with 1 M acetic acid at 150 °C under 200 MPa was determined to increase from 85.61% to 97.36% as the holding time increased from 0 to 30 min [126]. The process of crystallization and grain growth mainly continued during extended dwelling time, eventually leading to the densification of the ZnO ceramics. Furthermore, the relative density of the (K,Na)NbO3 ceramic can be increased to 94% by prolonging the holding time of CSP to 4 h, where the molten transient alkaline flux acts as a high-temperature solvent to promote the dissolution‒precipitation process [127]. The increased density was due to sufficient mass transport with extended dwelling time.

### 2.4 CSP application

The novel CSP technique provides a simple, effective, and energy-saving strategy for the densification of ceramic and ceramic-based materials at low temperatures. Almost half of the energy can be saved by CSP, even with post-heating treatment, compared with CS and SPS [128]. Therefore, many researchers have investigated the potential applications of CSP in different fields, such as electronic packaging, microwave devices, solid electrolytes and electrodes, printable electronics, etc. [84−87,129,130]. Guo *et al*. [77] cold-sintered three classical ferroelectric oxides and provided a comprehensive tutorial on CSP that is of great help in material selection and process optimization. Guo *et al*. [85] also demonstrated the feasibility of CSP in microwave and packaging substrate applications by successfully fabricating a series of dielectric ceramics, composites, and multilayer co-fired ceramic structures. The microwave properties of the samples obtained via CSP at 120 °C are comparable to those of the samples prepared via CS at approximately 500 °C because of the relatively high density of CSP. Gao *et al*. [131,132] utilized CSP to densify translucent ceramics such as aluminosilicate and γ-Al2O3 and concluded that the addition of an alkaline solution significantly accelerated the dissolution‒precipitation process, leading to a remarkably low activation energy. Wang *et al*. [133] successfully prepared a bioceramic, dicalcium phosphate anhydrous (CaHPO4) using CSP with added water, achieving a high relative density of 95%. Liu *et al*. [134] designed a microstrip patch antenna by using cold-sintered LiF ceramics as the substrate, and nearly full densification (relative density of 97.6%) was achieved by CSP when the external pressure increased to 500 MPa. However, the relative density of LiF ceramics can reach only ~90% under CS because of their inferior surface free energy [135]. Furthermore, the quality factor of the sample prepared by CSP was almost twice that of the sample obtained by CS. CSP has also been introduced to fabricate solid electrolytes and electrodes where a low sintering temperature is a good way to reduce lithium loss and avoid side reactions and barrier layer formation [136]. We must mention that cold-sintered samples typically have a small amount of amorphous phase at the grain boundaries, which is unfavorable for their electrochemical properties. Lee *et al*. [129] proposed an approach to mitigate inherent high-grain boundary resistance by introducing the concept of ceramic–salt composite electrolytes. Accordingly, bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide lithium (LiTFSI) salt in aqueous solutions was used as a transient solvent for cold sintering of Li1.5Al0.5Ge1.5(PO4)3 (LAGP) at 130 °C, and the conductivity and activation energy of the resultant samples were comparable to those of LAGPs obtained via CS above 800 °C. In addition, the low sintering temperature of CSP makes it possible to bridge the processing temperature gap between ceramic and polymer materials, which opens up a variety of possibilities for the design of printable and flexible electronics [78]. The use of CSP in the fabrication of piezoelectric ceramics, such as Pb(Zr,Ti)O3 (PZT), was able to reduce lead loss and point defects and well-developed polarization switching with high permanent polarizations and strong piezoelectric coefficients was obtained after a post-annealing process [137]. CSP has been extended to more material systems because it offers the opportunity for ultralow-temperature sintering. The potential for the fabrication of thermoelectric materials by CSP has gradually increased, which will be summarized and discussed in Section 3.

### 3.1 Nonoxide semiconductors by CSP

Bi0.5Sb1.5Te3-based compounds, as the most mature p-type TE materials near room temperature for industrial applications, are usually prepared by ZM, HP, hot extrusion, and SPS at 400–500 °C [138−141]. Many efforts and studies have focused on decreasing the grain size to reduce the lattice thermal conductivity by enhancing low-frequency phonon scattering at the grain boundaries [142−144]. Considering the above prospects, CSP was employed to fabricate p-type Bi0.5Sb1.5Te3 bulk materials for the first time at ultralow temperatures (below 150 °C) [52]. The slurry was first prepared by mixing ~15 wt% of deionized water with milled powders and then transferred to a heating mold for the cold sintering process. The introduced deionized water, as a kind of liquid sintering aid, played an important role in the process of dissolution‒precipitation mass transfer and was thus capable of promoting the consolidation of wetted particles. The density of Bi0.5Sb1.5Te3 obtained by CSP at 110–150 °C under a uniaxial pressure of 240 MPa is more than 97% of the theoretical value, 6.82 g·cm–3, which guarantees good mechanical and transport properties. The as-sintered samples exhibit negligible preferred orientation, resulting in homogeneous transport properties parallel and perpendicular to the pressing direction. Figure 4 presents the grain texture and microstructure of the cold-sintered sample sintered at 130 °C. A small grain size of less than 300 nm was observed from the electron backscatter diffraction results, leading to a high density of grain boundaries. Furthermore, the twin boundaries marked with red lines in Fig. 4(b), also observed at the atomic level in Fig. 4(d), serve as phonon scattering centers, which can largely reduce the thermal conductivity but have a minimal impact on the electrical transport properties. The cold sintering process is not only a cost-effective method for thermoelectric fabrication but also a promising approach for decoupling electrical and thermal transport. The highest *ZT* value of 0.56 was achieved for the cold-sintered Bi0.5Sb1.5Te3 samples, which is comparable to that of the samples prepared by high-temperature SPS [145].

A novel defect engineering approach was introduced to enhance the performance of undoped polycrystalline SnSe via a two-step process, including CSP and postannealing [58]. The high sintering pressure in CSP produces not only highly oriented grains but also a high density of lattice dislocations and stacking faults. The low sintering temperature enables precise control of Se vacancies through a postannealing process, resulting in record-low lattice thermal conductivity in polycrystalline SnSe. Moreover, postannealing can break the highly resistive SnOx* layers at the grain boundaries via grain growth, leading to increased charge carrier concentration and largely improved carrier mobility. The average *ZT* achieved in the temperature range of 300–890 K reaches 0.69, surpassing the performance of most reported polycrystalline SnSe materials.

Piyasin *et al*. [148] used the CSP technique with a thiol-amine (TA) solution as the liquid medium to prepare high-performance Cu2Se at 200 °C. The low sintering temperature inhibited grain growth and preserved the microstructure of the Cu2Se precursor powder, leading to significantly reduced lattice thermal conductivity. A remarkable *ZT* of 2.13 was achieved in the cold-sintered sample at 800 K, which is approximately two times greater than *ZT* of 0.65 observed in the hot-pressed sample. Pinitsoontorn’s group [149,150] also explored the feasibility of fabricating bulk Ag2Se using CSP with aqua regia as the liquid medium, as well as a modified CSP approach (mCSP) employing TA as the liquid medium. In mCSPs, TA does not dissolve Ag2Se, which allows TA solution to flow freely between the particles. As the sintering temperature exceeds the boiling point of TA, the liquid evaporates, leaving behind bulk Ag2Se with a tube-like porous structure. The induced defects and formed micropores optimized the charge carrier concentration and effectively suppressed the thermal conductivity, leading to an average *ZT* approaching 1 in the 300–400 K range. Conversely, the aqua regia used during CSP can facilitate the localized movement of Ag atoms near intergranular interfaces, resulting in densely packed grains within the bulk samples, along with nanopores and nanoscale defects. The resulting average *ZT* of 1.06 in the 307−390 K range stands among the highest values reported in the literature.

Another attempt at obtaining thermoelectric semiconductor alloys via CSP typically involves n-type PbTe-based materials. Lu *et al*. [45] compared the TE properties of n-type PbTe0.94Se0.06 prepared via SPS, cold sintering, and warm pressing (WP), as displayed in Fig. 5. CSP was finally proven to be a green and cost-effective technology for the fabrication of advanced TE materials. In this study, PbTe0.94Se0.06 powder was synthesized via the solvothermal method. First, 10 wt% of the solvothermal precursors were added as liquid sintering aids for CSP, which was the only difference between the cold sintering process and the WP process. As shown in the schematic in Fig. 5, the added solvothermal precursors facilitate mass transfer via the dissolution‒precipitation process and restrain the coarsening of precipitates. The CSP for PbTe0.94Se0.06 was performed at 300 °C under a uniaxial pressure of 160 MPa, and the relative density of the resultant sample was as high as 95%. Compared with those of the SPS-prepared samples, smaller grain sizes, and narrower grain size distributions were observed in the cold-sintered samples, thereby resulting in reduced lattice thermal conductivity through strong phonon scattering at the grain boundaries. Most importantly, the low-temperature sintering technique was proven to be effective in suppressing the precipitation of Pb from the PbTe matrix, and CSP was also able to suppress the growth of Pb precipitates and confine them to the nanosized range. Nanosized Pb precipitates resulted in a high surface area, and more electron transfer occurred at the Pb/matrix interfaces, leading to a high charge carrier concentration. The highest *ZT* value of 0.96 was achieved in the cold-sintered sample, which is even higher than that of the SPS-prepared sample.

### 3.2 Oxides by CSP

Thermoelectric oxides, such as Ca3Co4O9, CaMnO3, and SrTiO3, have also attracted intensive attention because of their sustainability (resource-efficient synthesis, nontoxicity, and high abundance of elements) and high oxidation resistance [152−156]. The conventional method for preparing oxide ceramics is usually pressure-less sintering, which is time-consuming and leads to high porosity in the final products. In the past two years, CSP has been successfully employed in the fabrication of highly dense Ca3Co4O9 ceramics, and the effects of sintering parameters on the thermoelectric properties of cold-sintered samples have been investigated in depth [157]. Ca3Co4O9 ceramics have a layered structure consisting of alternating highly electrically conductive CoO2 layers and thermally insulating Ca2CoO3 layers. Moreover, the Ca3Co4O9 phase decomposes into secondary phases, such as Ca2Co2O5, Ca3Co2O6, Co3O4, etc., when heat treated at high temperatures [158] or held for a long time [159]. This also explains why the thermoelectric properties of Ca3Co4O9 depend largely on the sintering process. Santos *et al*. [157] compared the microstructures and TE performances of Ca3Co4O9 prepared via the CS method and CSP. The cold-sintered Ca3Co4O9 samples were prepared using 64 wt% acetic acid solution as a liquid sintering aid under a uniaxial pressure of 350 MPa at 150 °C. Furthermore, two sets of cold-sintered samples were annealed at 900 °C for 6 and 24 h, respectively. The results revealed that all the cold-sintered samples had a greater density and smaller grain size than those fabricated via CS. The high densification of cold-sintered samples contributes to high electrical conductivity, whereas the high density of grain boundaries resulting from small grain sizes reduces the thermal conductivity. Therefore, the *ZT* values of the cold-sintered Ca3Co4O9 ceramics improved throughout the entire measured temperature range. Yu *et al*. [88] obtained highly dense calcium cobaltite ceramics (> 90% theoretical) via CSP in combination with heat treatment on the basis of the reported optimal chemical position Ca2.7Bi0.3Co3.92O9+*δ*. In their study, Ca2.7Bi0.3Co3.92O9+*δ* powder was first calcined in air at 930 °C for 12 h, once or twice, before the cold sintering process. The formation of secondary phases was well controlled after the double calcination process. The low volume fraction of secondary phases increased both the charge carrier concentration and carrier mobility, leading to a substantial increase in the electrical conductivity. In addition, the thermal conductivity was reduced because phonon scattering was intensified by the existence of pores. Therefore, CSP was proven to be a green route to densify thermoelectric Ca3Co4O9 ceramics with low energy consumption and effectively improve the TE performance.

ZnO is often used as a model ceramic for fundamental research in the field of cold sintering [160,161], but it is also a good candidate for thermoelectrics due to its semiconducting properties. Recently, many studies have investigated the densification and grain growth of ZnO ceramics during the cold sintering process and the effect of sintering temperature/pressure on electrical and thermal conductivity [123,126,162−164]. Acetic acid is commonly used as a liquid sintering aid in ZnO ceramic systems. Funahashi *et al*. [123] fabricated monolithic ZnO products with densities greater than 90% of the theoretical value via CSP at temperatures less than 100 °C. The study indicated that the activation energy of grain growth for CSP is dramatically reduced due to the introduction of liquid sintering aids. Notably, the electrical conductivity of cold-sintered ZnO ceramics at ~300 °C reached an equivalent value to that of samples conventionally sintered at 1400 °C, implying that more than half of the manufacturing energy can be saved via CSP. Jing *et al*. [163] reported that postheating treatment in an Ar atmosphere after CSP can significantly enhance the electrical conductivity of ZnO ceramics. Specifically, more than four orders of increase in electrical conductivity were observed after heat treatment at 500 °C. After heat treatment, the grain size of the resultant sample did not obviously change, but the crystallinity was greatly improved, which is beneficial for increasing the electrical conductivity and reducing the thermal conductivity simultaneously.

Liang *et al*. [165] studied the grain growth of ZnO prepared via a hybrid CSP–SPS process. The grain size of the CSP–SPS-prepared samples (205–680 nm) was much smaller than that of the SPS-prepared samples (1–10 μm), as displayed in Fig. 6. The results showed that the densification of the ZnO sample in the CSP–SPS process was initially activated at 85 °C due to the dissolution‒precipitation process in the presence of acetic acid solution, whereas a higher temperature (> 700 °C) was required for SPS. Moreover, grain coarsening was largely limited under low sintering temperatures in the CSP–SPS process, offering more phonon scattering centers thereby reducing the thermal conductivity. Overall, combining CSP and SPS is a promising method for fabricating fine ceramics at low temperatures. Furthermore, the CSP technique makes it possible to densify thermodynamically unstable phases at low temperatures. For example, tin monoxide (SnO) was successfully prepared without the formation of secondary phases by CSP, and the relative density reached 89% when the sintering temperature was below 300 °C [166]. Pure SnO materials exhibit p-type semiconducting behavior with great potential for thermoelectric applications. CSP opens opportunities for developing new material systems suitable for thermoelectrics and providing new designs in traditional material systems to enhance TE performance.

### 3.3 TE composites by CSP

There is a growing interest in TE composites because they can integrate the advantages of individual components and provide much space for improving TE performance [167−169]. For example, introducing inorganic TE particles into a conductive polymer matrix can form an interconnected network to increase the electrical conductivity, and the polymer/inorganic interfaces may act as energy filters to improve the thermopower [170]. In addition, polymer-based composites with good environmental stability, lightweight, versatile processability, and mechanical flexibility are suitable for portable and wearable electronics. In an attempt to investigate the TE properties of 2D MXene/ZnO composites, low-temperature CSP was implemented to obtain a highly dense bulk with the assistance of liquid sintering aids by Guo *et al*. [171]. Interestingly, the pristine structure and chemical functionalization of 2D MXenes can be maintained at low sintering temperatures without any oxidation. As shown in Fig. 7, 2D MXene nanosheets were distributed homogeneously along with the ZnO grains, providing an efficient pathway for electron transport. The electrical conductivity was enhanced by 1–2 orders of magnitude when 5 wt% MXene was introduced into the ZnO ceramics. Furthermore, the grain growth of ZnO was inhibited by 2D MXene nanosheets located at the grain boundaries of ZnO, leading to strong phonon scattering at the grain boundaries. After the introduction of 2D MXenes, the mechanical properties of the cold-sintered MXene/ZnO composites also improved significantly. Specifically, the hardness was more than doubled with 5 wt% 2D MXene. Notably, this work was the first attempt to densify 2D materials into ceramics by CSP at temperatures as low as 300 °C, which addresses the challenges of interdiffusion and chemical reactions at high temperatures. TE properties of V2O5/poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)-poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) composites with high-volume fractions of ceramics were also reported by the same group [172]. The grain growth of V2O5 was limited because of the fast sintering process (~20 min) at an extremely low temperature (120 °C). A small amount of the amorphous phase was observed at the grain boundaries due to the introduction of deionized water as a liquid sintering aid. Similarly, the induced polymer (PEDOT:PSS) was detected around V2O5 grains. They also reported that postheating treatment at 500 °C can decrease the amount of intergranular amorphous phases, thereby increasing the electrical conductivity. The results showed that the transport properties of V2O5/PEDOT:PSS composites were dominated by small polaron hopping, and the electrical conductivity increased by 1–2 orders of magnitude at 50 °C with the addition of 1–2 vol% PEDOT:PSS. Therefore, CPS offers the possibility of preparing inorganic/organic composites at low temperatures, expanding the field of TE materials.

### 3.4 Multilayer devices by CSP

Multilayer electroceramic components have undergone rapid development in various functional devices, such as piezoelectric transformers, actuators, transducers, varistors, capacitors, oxygen sensors, solid-state barriers, solid oxide fuel cells, microwave filters, antennas, electrocaloric cooling systems, and thermoelectrics [173−180]. A major challenge in the multilayer co-firing process is the different shrinkage rates of different layers, which inevitably cause some defects in the multilayers, such as warpages, cracking, and delamination. CSP shows outstanding advantages in integrating different layers, even from ceramics to polymers, because the extremely low sintering temperature can largely suppress shrinkage. Guo *et al*. [85] introduced a novel technology for multilayered structures called “cold-sintered co-fired ceramic (CSCC)”, and Funahashi *et al*. [100] demonstrated the feasibility of using CSCCs to fabricate a multilayer TEG.

The multilayer TEG is a relatively new concept in which electrode connections and gaps between n-type and p-type semiconductors are removed, but an insulating layer is needed between the n-type and p-type sheets, as shown in Fig. 8(a). Unlike conventional π-type structures, multilayer TEGs not only have a low manufacturing cost but also have a high packing density, small size, and high reliability. In the work of Funahashi *et al*. [100], ZnO was selected as an n-type material, and layered Ca3Co4O9 (C349 in Figs. 8(c) and 8(d)) was selected as a p-type material. Yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ in Figs. 8(c) and 8(d)), Li2MoO4 (LMO), and polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) were explored as insulating layers because these materials can be densified at similar temperatures (300–350 °C) with acetic acid as a liquid sintering aid. The acetic acid was not simply mixed with raw materials but was instead transported via evaporation to ensure homogeneity. The multilayer TEG was composed of n-type, p-type, and insulator layers stacked alternatively, as shown in the cross-sectional electron dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mappings (Figs. 8(c) and 8(d)). They demonstrated that it is possible to produce a device with up to 200 layers without any cracks or delamination via this CSCC technique. A multilayer TEG containing 5 n‒p junction pairs (20 layers in total) can output a voltage greater than 15 mV at a temperature difference of less than 100 °C (Fig. 8(b)). Therefore, cold-sintered co-fired ceramic multilayered composites for TEGs can be designed in a single-step process with the assistance of CSP, which offers an alternative to conventional TE modules.

In other cases, some metals commonly used in electroceramic multilayers, such as Fe, Cu, and Al, are easily oxidized in the debinding or/and sintering process, which inevitably results in delamination and poor interface contact of the products. Therefore, one of the critical issues in the metallization process is preventing oxidation in different product-service environments. de Beauvoir et al*. [181] investigated the possibility of metallization integration in co-fired electroceramic multilayers via CSP, and a multilayer process was developed in their work on a ZnO ceramic via the use of Cu, Fe, Al, and mixed Fe/Cu as metal layers at temperatures below 300 °C. ## 4 Conclusions and outlook

In the present review, we report various TE materials that can be cold sintered with the assistance of a transient liquid phase, from semiconductor alloys, oxides, and composites to multilayer devices. The cold sintering process is a relatively low-temperature (~300 °C) and short-term sintering method (~60 min), which not only saves energy and reduces CO2 emissions in the fabrication process but also offers the possibility of limiting grain growth compared with most sintering techniques, such as conventional pressureless sintering, SPS, and HP. Furthermore, the microstructures and morphologies of the resulting samples can be designed to form adjustable interfaces, and thereby, the performance of products can be tailored via the cold sintering process. In addition, the low sintering temperature of CSP offers the possibility to consolidate metastable materials and suppress secondary phase transitions, which opens a new pathway for exploring new materials in TE field. Therefore, CSP simplifies the manufacturing process of TE materials and provides new design approaches for the preparation of high-performance TE materials. However, the CSP technique is currently in the early stage, and its application in the fabrication of thermoelectrics also faces the following challenges:

(1) Research on cold-sintered TE materials is still limited. Considering the difficulties in solvent selection, more extensive investigations are needed to identify the appropriate solvents and sintering conditions for different types of TE materials. These studies are essential for optimizing TE properties and improving efficiency in practical applications.

(2) The use of solvents can lead to the formation of a small amount of amorphous phase at the grain boundaries in cold-sintered TE materials, which can negatively affect the electrical conductivity. Consequently, additional heat treatment may be necessary after cold sintering to remove the amorphous phase and enhance densification. However, this posttreatment can increase energy consumption and diminish the overall energy-saving advantages of the process.

(3) The ability to fabricate large components and scale up production is currently constrained by existing equipment and technology. In large parts, uneven pressure distribution and solvent evaporation often lead to inhomogeneous densities and microstructures. Although current experimental setups are relatively straightforward and affordable, there is a pressing need to develop advanced equipment for industrial automation and large-scale manufacturing.
