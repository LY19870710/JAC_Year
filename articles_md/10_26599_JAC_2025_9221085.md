## 1 Introduction

With the development of rocket propulsion, hypersonic vehicles, and other aerospace industries, the demand for ultra-high-temperature materials with superior mechanical and wear resistance properties has increased dramatically [1]. Specifically, the ultra-high-temperature bearing components of high-end equipment must possess exceptional lubricating and wear resistance properties to minimize friction and wear in power systems during operation. Self-lubricating materials, known for their excellent thermal stability in high-temperature environments, are ideal for addressing the friction and wear problems of advanced mechanical equipment [2]. Traditional approaches to achieving self-lubricating properties in ceramics typically involve incorporating solid lubricants into the ceramic matrix [3]. While effective in improving lubricating performance, this method often leads to a significant reduction in the material’s mechanical properties (with an 83% decrease in strength and a 32% reduction in hardness [4]). Therefore, breaking through the design limitations of traditional self-lubricating ceramics and developing novel single-phase self-lubricating ceramic systems are essential to promote the application of advanced ceramics in high-temperature fields.

High-entropy borides are a new type of single-phase solid solution ceramics developed in recent years and are composed of a variety of group IV–VI transition metals and B elements [5−8]. Compared with the corresponding binary compounds, the formation of high-entropy ceramics favors improved oxidation resistance, mechanical properties, and creep resistance [9−14]. Gild *et al*. [15] demonstrated that the hardness and oxidation resistance of these high-entropy diborides were usually superior to those of five metal diborides prepared via the same fabrication process. Qin *et al*. [16] reported that the incorporation of softer WB2 and MoB2 increased the hardness of high-entropy diborides, even though the grain size increased. Similar advantages are demonstrated in high-entropy boride coatings, which exhibit exceptional hardness and high-temperature ablative resistance [17]. Numerous studies have shown that, as multi-element single-phase ceramics, high-entropy ceramics have superior comprehensive performance compared with traditional ceramics because of their unique high-entropy effect, cocktail effect, solid solution hardening effect, and lattice distortion [18−21]. Therefore, high-entropy ceramics hold significant potential for application in ultra-high-temperature fields.

High-entropy borides exhibit considerable potential as high-temperature lubricating materials. In general, the realization of high-temperature lubricating performance mainly relies on the solid lubricating film on the surface formed by the high-temperature solid lubricant, but it is difficult to achieve high-temperature extremely low friction [22,23]. The lubrication film is often not completely shielded from oxygen, which leads to continuous oxidation of the ceramic substrate, thus further increasing wear. As a result, it is difficult to achieve both low friction and low wear simultaneously. For example, polycrystalline cubic boron nitride/hexagonal boron nitride (PcBN/hBN) composites present a low friction coefficient (0.3) at 1000 °C but are accompanied by a significantly high wear rate of 10−3 mm3/(N·m) [24]. Similarly, high-entropy silicide ceramics demonstrate a relatively low wear rate of (1.88±0.15)×10−6 mm3/(N·m) at 600 °C, yet with a super-high friction coefficient of 0.64±0.05 [25]. To overcome these limitations, the oxidation product B2O3 of B element in high-entropy borides is expected to be used as a new type of high-temperature liquid lubricant because of its low melting point (melting point is 470 °C [26]), providing strong support for high-temperature hydrodynamic lubrication. Furthermore, the composition of high-entropy borides is highly designable due to the large number of constituent elements, and the high-temperature lubricating performance can be further optimized by introducing several metal lubricating elements. Despite these advantages, there remains a paucity of research on the tribological behaviors of high-entropy borides across a broad temperature range. A further investigation of high-temperature tribological properties, as well as the oxidation and wear mechanisms of high-entropy borides, is essential for advancing their application in lubrication.

Therefore, based on the high-entropy concept and tribological design principle, this work introduces elements in high-entropy borides that are capable of generating lubricating phases under temperature or friction-induced conditions. In addition to the B element, Mo and V elements are deliberately introduced to improve the tribological properties of the high-entropy boride designed in this work. Furthermore, according to the theoretical calculations by Divilov *et al*. [27], the (Ti,V,Nb,Ta,Mo)B₂ system containing V and Mo elements demonstrates high disordered enthalpy–entropy descriptor (DEED) values, indicating excellent structural stability. Accordingly, bulk high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 was successfully prepared by high-energy ball milling and reaction sintering processes. The obtained high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 achieves synergistic enhancement of both mechanical and tribological properties over a wide temperature range (from room temperature to 1200 °C), demonstrating significant potential for applications in high-temperature bearing and anti-wear components. Additionally, the microstructures, phase composition evolution, fracture, and lubrication/wear resistance mechanisms of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 were comprehensively investigated and revealed.

### 2.1 Preparation of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2

Commercially available Ti, V, Nb, Ta, Mo (99.9% purity, ≤ 325 mesh; Beijing Xingrongyuan Technology Co., Ltd., China), and B powders (99.9% purity, 0.5–2 μm; Shanghai Macklin Ltd., China) were used as raw materials for the synthesis of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2. To reduce impurities from the oxidation of the initial metal powders, an additional 10% B powder was added to the formulation. During high-temperature sintering, the B powder reacts with metal oxides to form B2O3, whose volatility at high temperatures effectively reduces the amount of oxide impurities in the sintered samples. Thus, the ratio of Ti, V, Nb, Ta, Mo, and B powders was 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 : 11.

The mixed powders were milled in ethanol at 400 r/min for 10 h in a high-energy ball miller (Pulverisette 7 Planetary, Fritsch, Germany) (5 min milling and 5 min pause). Tungsten carbide (WC) material was used as ball milling jars and grinding balls, and the ratio of tungsten carbide balls to ball milled powder was 5 : 1. Then, the mixed powders were sieved and dry pressed into pellets and sintered in a vacuum hot-press sintering furnace (ZT-63-20Y, Shanghai Chenhua Science Technology Co., Ltd., China) at 1800 °C and 40 MPa for 1 h at a heating rate of 10 °C/min and then cooled down with the furnace to finally obtain high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2.

### 2.2 Phase compositions and microstructures

An X-ray diffractometer (EMPYREAN, PANalytical, the Netherlands) was used to determine the phase composition of the prepared samples. The phase structure and lattice parameters of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 were calculated from the structure files of TiB2 using the GSAS software and the Rietveld refinement method. The density of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 was determined by the Archimedes’ method. A scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM; FEI Talos F200X, USA) equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) was used to observe the lattice structure of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2. The grain size and crystal orientation of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 were observed using an electron backscatter diffractometer (EBSD, Nordly Max3, Oxford, UK) after vibrationally polishing.

### 2.3 Mechanical property testing and characterization

A microhardness tester with a Vickers indenter (MH-5-VM, Shanghai Hengyi Technology Co., Ltd., China) was used for indentation testing of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 at 4.9 N for 10 s. The hardness of the samples was also tested under vacuum at 600, 800, 1000, and 1200 °C under a holding pressure of 4.9 N for 10 s (ZD-HVZHT-30, Shandong Zone-De Electro-Mechanical Equipment Co., Ltd., China). Nano-hardness was evaluated by a nanoindentation tester (Nano Test 600, Micro Materials, UK) at 8 mN for 10 s. The flexural strength and fracture toughness of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 were tested using a universal materials testing machine (AGS-X-20KN, Shimazu, Japan). The flexural strength of the material was determined by three-point bending test with a sample size of 25 mm × 4 mm × 3 mm, a span of 20 mm, and a speed of 0.5 mm/min. The flexural strength is calculated by Eq. (1) [28]:

(1) σ=3FL2Bh2

where *σ*, *F*, *L*, *B*, and *h* represent the strength (MPa), the applied force (N), the span (mm), the sample width (mm), and the height (mm), respectively.

The samples were machined to a size of 25 mm × 4 mm × 2 mm for fracture toughness measurement by the single-edge notched beam method (SENB) with a span of 16 mm and a speed of 0.05 mm/min, and the fracture toughness was calculated by Eq. (2) [29]:

(2) KIC=FLBW32f(aW)

where *a* and *W* represent the notch depth (mm) and the sample thickness (mm), respectively. The notch depth (*a*) is generally machined to (0.4–0.5)*W*. *f* (a/W) is the geometry factor and is calculated by Eq. (3):

### 2.4 Tribological property testing and characterizations

The tribological properties of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 were determined by using a high-temperature reciprocating friction and wear tester (GF-1200, Lanzhou Zhongke Kaihua Technology Development Co., Ltd., China) at room temperature (RT), 600 °C, 800 °C, 1000 °C, and 1200 °C. An Al2O3 ball (Vickers hardness of 16 GPa) with a diameter of 6.0 mm was used as the friction pair, and the samples were tested at a frequency of 5 Hz (to an average sliding speed of 50 mm/s) with an amplitude of 2.5 mm under a normal load of 10 N for 1 h. The friction coefficient curves were derived from the data recorded automatically by the high-temperature reciprocating friction and wear tester. The wear rate of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 was calculated by the equation *W* = *V*/*FL*, where *V* is the wear volume; *F* is the applied load; *L* is the sliding distance. After the friction test, the morphologies and elemental distributions of the worn surfaces of the high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 and the Al2O3 balls were characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Verios, FEI, USA). The wear volume of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 after friction tests was measured by a field 3D surface profilometer (VHX-6000, KEYENCE, Japan). The chemical state of the elements on the worn surface was determined using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA). The mass changes of B2O3 and high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 at RT–1500 °C were measured by a simultaneous thermal analyzer (STA 449 F3, NETZSCH, Germany). Moreover, the viscosity of B2O3 at 600–1200 °C was also tested by a high-temperature viscometer (BCT 1700, Shanghai Huanao Technology Co., Ltd., China).

### 3.1 Phase composition and microstructures

The observed XRD pattern (*Y*obs) is in good agreement with the standard XRD patterns of TiB2, which indicates the successful synthesis of (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 with a hexagonal structure. To obtain detailed information on the structure and space group of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2, the XRD patterns of the ceramic samples were subjected to Rietveld refinement. The Rietveld refinement patterns (*Y*cal) of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 illustrate excellent structural stability and a good fit for all diffraction peaks in Fig. 1. The *R* factors for high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 are less than 10%, indicating a high level of precision in this finding. The lattice parameters of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2, as calculated by Rietveld refinement are *a* = *b* = 3.06357 Å, *c* = 3.22204 Å, *α* = *β* = 90°, and *γ* = 120°, and its theoretical density is 7.32 g/cm3. The bulk density of the sample measured by Archimedes’ method was 6.94 g/cm3; thus, the calculated relative density was 94.8%.

### 3.2 Mechanical properties and hardening and toughening mechanisms

A comparison of the mechanical properties of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 with those of reported high-entropy diborides and some of the corresponding transition metal diborides is given in Table 1. Hardness is usually a function of many variables, and it can often be considered a weighted sum of intrinsic (crystal type, valence electron density, dislocations, etc.) and extrinsic (grain size, porosity, etc.) factors. In high-entropy ceramics, different transition metal elements in single-phase solid solutions can lead to lattice distortions due to atomic size mismatches, thus limiting dislocation motion to increase the hardness [30]. The interdiffusion of elements during the formation of high-entropy solid solutions also enhances the grain boundary strength [31]. In addition, small grains can provide high-volume-fraction grain boundaries in high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2, which also prevents dislocation motion to increase the hardness [30]. It has also been reported that high-entropy diborides doped with softer metal Mo can make the materials harder [16]. Thus, the high hardness of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 can be attributed to mass disorder and lattice distortion due to its multi-cationic sublattice structure and small grains. There are more factors affecting the toughness of the material; herein, the strength of interatomic bonds and grain size are the most important factors. Upon the addition of Mo to high-entropy ceramics, the pseudo-energy gap shifts to a negative value, which may be due to the preferential addition of Mo-induced filler electrons to the antibonding molecular orbital [32]. Therefore, the toughening effect of Mo can be understood in terms of reducing the covalent bond strength. Moreover, the high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 prepared in this work has a relatively superior toughness compared with other high-entropy diborides and the corresponding transition metal diborides, which is also the result of the smaller grain size and larger through-crystal fracture energy (Fig. 4).

### 3.3 Tribological properties and self-lubricating mechanisms of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2

Figure 6(a) shows typical friction coefficient curves of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 at RT, 600 °C, 800 °C, 1000 °C, and 1200 °C. The corresponding average friction coefficients are 0.74±0.04, 0.51±0.07, 0.18±0.02, 0.12±0.01, and 0.31±0.10, respectively. The friction coefficient curves are relatively smooth during sliding at RT, 600 °C, 800 °C, and 1000 °C, whereas they fluctuate relatively slowly at 1200 °C. The friction coefficient appears to decrease and then increase with increasing temperature, with the lowest value obtained at 1000 °C. In addition, the wear rate gradually increases with increasing temperature (Fig. 6(b)). Figure 6(c) shows the friction coefficients of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 and other monolithic ceramics at different temperatures [22,23,25,45–56]. Compared with other high-entropy (HfZrTaVNb)B₂ and (TaZrTiVHf)B₂ systems [55,56], high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 in this study exhibits quite exceptional wear resistance properties at room temperature, despite a somewhat slightly higher friction coefficient. SiC and BaCr2O4 show superior lubricating performance (friction coefficient below 0.5) when the temperature ranges from RT to 800 °C. As the temperature increases, the lubricating performance of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 becomes superior, which is significantly lower than that of MAX-phases (TiSiC, TiAlC, and MoAlB), conventional single-phase ceramics (β-Sialon, Si3N4, ZrO2, Al2O3, SiC, and WC), salt (BaCr2O4 and Na2SiO3), and high-entropy (carbon/silicide) ceramics ((TiVNbMoW)C4.375 and (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Mo0.2W0.2)Si2). Especially at 1000 °C, the friction coefficient of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 is as low as 0.12±0.01. Figure 6(d) demonstrates a comparison on the wear rates of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 ceramics and other monolithic ceramics at different temperatures. When the temperature is lower than 600 °C, WC has the best wear resistance, followed by high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2. With increasing temperature, high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Mo0.2.2W0.2)Si2 and TiAlC show the best wear resistance, followed by high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2. Therefore, in summary, high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 exhibits the most comprehensive and excellent tribological properties over a wide temperature range.

The characteristics of the oxidation products in the surface oxide layer essentially contribute to the tribological properties of the material. Among them, MoO3 and V2O5 have a layered structure, which is a good high-temperature solid lubricant, as explained in detail in our previous articles [25,66]. However, more importantly, B2O3 provides high-temperature liquid-phase lubrication, which is vital. Therefore, the viscosity change of B2O3 with increasing temperature was tested to elucidate the high-temperature liquid lubrication mechanism, as shown in Fig. 9(a). The viscosity of B2O3 at 600 °C is the highest, reaching 219.9±3.3 Pa·s, and gradually decreases with increasing temperature. The viscosities of B2O3 at 800, 1000, and 1200 °C are 26.9±0.1, 8.3±0.1, and 3.7±0.1 Pa·s, respectively. The bearing characteristic number ( ηU/p) (here, η* is the lubricant viscosity; *U* is the sliding speed; *p* is the bearing area load) is introduced here to reflect the relationships among the viscosity, sliding speed, and bearing area load with the friction coefficient [67]. The smaller the bearing characteristic number, the lower the friction coefficient. According to the bearing characteristic number, although 600 °C is already above the melting point of B2O3, the friction coefficient of the sample only decreases slightly relative to that of RT because of the high viscosity of B2O3. When the temperature increases to 800 °C, although the frictional contact area increases significantly relative to that at 600 °C, the viscosity of B2O3 decreases sharply, even to the extent of grease. As a result, the friction coefficient is reduced. As the temperature increases further to 1000 °C, the viscosity of B2O3 decreases further, but because the frictional contact area increases several times, the reduction in the friction coefficient is limited.

The above description is in agreement with the results of Fig. 6(b) at 600–1000 °C, while the friction coefficient increases significantly at 1200 °C. This is because Al2O3 is coated on the worn surface at 1200 °C, which changes the contact between the sample and Al2O3 to that between Al2O3 and Al2O3, resulting in an extremely unstable friction coefficient and a high friction coefficient. Figure 9(b) shows the TG analysis of (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 and B2O3. The initial oxidation temperature of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 is approximately 521 °C. The oxidation product B2O3 is a liquid phase at this temperature, which can block oxygen from entering the interior of the substrate, thus preventing the internal oxidation weight gain. However, since B2O3 starts to volatilize at 1185 °C, the oxygen shielding effect fails, leading to a rapid increase in the oxidation of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 near 1231 °C.

The worn surface micrographs of Al2O3 balls (Fig. 10) and high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 (Fig. 11) were also analyzed. The SEM images of the worn surfaces of Al2O3 balls at RT, 600 °C, 800 °C, 1000 °C, and 1200 °C are shown in Fig. 10. The wear of the Al2O3 balls at RT is more severe, and the wear diameter can reach 928 μm. This is mainly because the initial line–surface contact between the Al2O3 ball and the high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 results in a stress concentration, and the low hardness of the Al2O3 ball causes it to rupture and fall off under stress, thus aggravating severe wear of the Al2O3 ball. As the temperature reaches 600 °C, the worn surface of the Al2O3 ball experiences almost no wear except for a small number of adherents, which is mainly due to the friction reduction and wear resistance produced by the viscous flow state of B2O3 and the laminar structure of MoO3 and V2O5. When the temperature increases to 800 °C, the wear of the Al2O3 balls is low, and there is little surface adhesion, which is mainly due to the lower viscosity of B2O3 at 800 °C, which is not prone to adhesion (Fig. 11(c)). Owing to the high-temperature creep effect, the wear diameter of the Al2O3 balls can reach 920 μm when the temperature reaches 1000 °C. The worn surface morphologies of the Al2O3 balls and the corresponding EDS results at 1200 °C are shown in Figs. 10(e) and 10(e'). The Al2O3 balls show severe wear, and V and Mo elements are relatively uniformly distributed on the worn surface. This is mainly because the generated oxidation products, V2O5 and MoO3, have a typical laminar structure, which facilitates interlayer slip [68]. In addition, the large amount of Al2O3 coats the worn surface of the high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 owing to the large wear of the Al2O3 ball, which makes the wear of the sample difficult to measure (Fig. 6(b)).

Although high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 has high hardness and fracture toughness, it is still brittle, and there is obvious grain pulling out during sliding at RT. As the temperature reaches 600 °C, the surface of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 undergoes oxidation, and a large number of oxides are generated. Among them, B2O3 is in a viscous state at 600 °C, and MoO3 and V2O5 are laminar materials, thus inhibiting grain pullout on the worn surface and reducing sample wear. When the temperature rises to 800 °C, the worn surface is smoother, except for some solid impurities (Al2O3). This is because the friction mode is changed from oxidative wear to hydrodynamic lubrication due to the liquid phase of B2O3, which effectively reduces the friction coefficient. When the temperature rises to 1000 °C, the worn debris increases due to the increased wear of Al2O3, but the friction coefficient is even lower because of the further reduction in the viscosity of B2O3 (Fig. 11). However, when the temperature reaches 1200 °C, a large amount of Al2O3 coats the worn surface (Fig. 11(e)), leading to a change from the original friction between the Al2O3 balls and the sample surface to the friction between the Al2O3 balls and the Al2O3 debris; thus, the friction coefficient is extremely unstable.

Figure 12 presents the high-temperature friction mechanism diagram of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 at 800 °C. As the temperature increases, the surface elements of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 undergo gradual oxidation. When the temperature exceeds 470 °C, B2O3 on the surface starts to liquefy and envelops a large number of metal oxides. Concurrently, the protective effect of liquid-phase B2O3 on the surface slows the internal oxidation of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 as the temperature increases, resulting in minimal changes in the oxide layer thickness. Upon reaching 800 °C, the Al2O3 ball begins to slide, and the liquid-phase B2O3, along with the layered MoO3 and V2O5, gradually forms a lubricating and transfer film on both the worn surface of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 and the surface of the Al2O3 ball. B2O3 behaves similarly to grease, forming a liquid-phase lubricating film, while MoO3 and V2O5 present interlayer sliding under shear, significantly reducing the friction.

In general, the extremely low friction of liquid-phase lubrication can only be achieved with lubricating oils and greases, which are completely incapable of being used in high-temperature environments. Nevertheless, the B2O3 induced by in-situ* friction at high temperatures in this work is similar to “high-temperature liquid phase lubricating grease”. Under the dual action of pressure and temperature, B2O3 forms a similar “oil film” with a certain viscosity and thickness, accompanied by friction adsorption lubrication, thus contributing to the reduction in friction and wear in macroscopic high-temperature environments. Consequently, the low friction coefficient and wear rate of the high-entropy boride at approximately 1000 °C are attributed mainly to the liquid-phase lubrication of *in situ*-induced B2O3, while the layered oxidation products MoO3 and V2O5 also play an auxiliary lubrication role. This solid–liquid synergistic lubrication mechanism and good support of the ceramic substrate endow the high-entropy boride with excellent high-temperature tribological properties.

## 4 Conclusions

To develop the potential of high-entropy diborides for applications as high-temperature sliding components, the present work successfully synthesized a novel high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 system using five transition metal powders and B powders as raw materials by hot-pressing sintering. The phase compositions, microstructures, and mechanical and tribological properties of high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 were investigated. The synergistic enhancement of the mechanical properties and high-temperature tribological properties of the material was realized. The conclusions are as follows:

(1) High-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 presents a relative density and average grain size of 94.8% and 1.5 μm, respectively. Excellent fracture toughness (5.4±0.3 MPa·m¹/²) was obtained, a value higher than those previously reported for most of the corresponding transition metal diborides and high-entropy diborides. This is attributed to the presence of fine grains, and the mixed mode of transgranular and intergranular fracture improves the mechanical properties.

(2) Compared with the traditional single-phase ceramics reported thus far, high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 has the best comprehensive tribological properties at high temperatures, realizing the unification of excellent friction-reducing and wear-resistant properties. In particular, at 1000 °C, the friction coefficient can be as low as 0.12±0.01, and the wear rate is as low as (8.8±0.7)×10−5 mm3/(N·m). During high-temperature sliding, the low-melting-point B2O3 provides sufficient hydrodynamic lubrication and protects the substrate from further oxidation, while the high-entropy (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2 substrate provides good support for lubrication and wear resistance.
