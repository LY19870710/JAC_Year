## 1 Introduction

Thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) were introduced for thermal protection in 1976. TBCs are heat-resistant ceramic coatings applied to superalloy vanes on aeroengines that are beneficial for the operation temperature and service life of the engine[1−4]. TBC materials must fulfill the following requirements: a high melting point above 2000 °C, good phase stability, low thermal conductivity under high-temperature conditions, and a similar thermal expansion coefficient (TEC) to that of the alloy matrix [5,6]. Currently, the commonly used material for TBCs is 6–8 wt% Y2O3 partially stabilized ZrO2 (YSZ). However, the operation temperature of YSZ is generally limited to less than 1200 °C owing to the phase transformation and volume expansion at high temperatures [7−9]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new TBC materials that meet the needs of next-generation turbine engines.

In recent years, extensive research has been performed on advanced ceramic materials as potential candidates for TBCs, including rare-earth cerate [10,11], rare-earth phosphate [12], rare-earth niobate [13,14], and rare-earth zirconate [15]. Among the potential candidates, rare-earth zirconate are the most promising TBCs because of their excellent phase stability at high temperatures, better sintering resistance and significantly lower thermal conductivity than traditional YSZ [16]. Rare-earth zirconates have the chemical formula RE2Zr2O7, where RE represents rare-earth elements. They have two crystal structures: disordered defective fluorite and pyrochlore. The defective fluorite structure has one fewer oxygen vacancy at the fixed position than does the pyrochlore structure. Under atmospheric pressure, the crystal structure of RE2Zr2O7 depends mainly on the average ionic radius ratio of the A and B sites, which can be expressed as *R*(A)/*R*(B). When *R*(A)/*R*(B) 17].

High-entropy RE2Zr2O7 (HE-RE2Zr2O7) with a single-phase defective fluorite structure generally has low thermal conductivity, and doping a variety of rare-earth elements at RE sites is conducive to the expansion of the RE–O bond, which contributes to reducing the bond energy and can improve TEC [18]. Yeh introduced the concept of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) in 2014, and they prepared a CuCoNiCrFe alloy with a face-centered cubic structure [19,20]. The concept behind the design of HEAs is the doping of five or more primary elements, each with a known or similar percentage. In 2017, Rost *et al.* [21] proposed a new concept for the use of HEA design for ceramic materials. Using metal oxides with relatively high melting points as the main elements, they designed and prepared a five-component (Mg0.2Ni0.2Co0.2Cu0.2Zn0.2)O ceramic system with a single face-centered cubic rock salt structure. The concept of high entropy has subsequently been gradually introduced into ceramics. High-entropy ceramics (HECs) are usually composed of a nearly equimolar mixture of five or more components that can form a single-phase solid solution [22]. HECs can enhance the comprehensive properties of materials owing to their thermodynamic high-entropy effect, crystallographic lattice distortion effect, sluggish kinetic diffusion effect, and “cocktail” effect [23−25], and some new TBC candidates have been developed using high-entropy design. Inspired by the hysteresis diffusion effect of HECs, Zhao *et al.* [26] designed and synthesized a novel high-entropy rare-earth zirconate (La0.2Ce0.2Nd0.2Sm0.2Eu0.2)2Zr2O7, which has a room-temperature thermal conductivity of only 0.76 W·m−1·K−1, a slow grain growth rate and good resistance to thermal stress cracking. Liu *et al*. [27] used high-speed orthogonal milling combined with a solid-phase reaction method to prepare single-phase defective fluorite-structured (La0.2Nd0.2Sm0.2Gd0.2Yb0.2)2Zr2O7 ceramics, and the samples exhibited a low thermal conductivity of 1.72 W·m−1·K−1, TEC of 10.9×10−6 K−1 at 1000 °C, and excellent Young's modulus (*E* = 186–257 GPa). Wright *et al*. [28] synthesized 18 medium- to high-entropy pyrochlore oxides and four low-entropy oxide benchmark groups at the B site or co-doped them at the A and B sites of RE2Zr2O7 to investigate the relationship between the thermal conductivity of pyrochlore oxides and the size difference factor and mass difference. They found that the thermal conductivity of high-entropy pyrochlore oxides is correlated with the size difference but not the mass difference. Luo *et al.* [29] used the difference in the average electronegativities to tailor the thermal expansion coefficient, and the difference in the ionic radii and atomic masses was used to determine the thermal conductivity. They synthesized (La0.2Sm0.2Eu0.2Gd0.2Yb0.2)2Zr2O7, (La0.2Nd0.2Eu0.2Gd0.2Yb0.2)2Zr2O7, and (La0.2Nd0.2Sm0.2Gd0.2Yb0.2)2Zr2O7. The comprehensive performance of the synthesized HE-RE2Zr2O7 ceramics was superior to that of the La2Zr2O7 ceramics owing to their excellent thermal conductivity of 1.17–1.37 W·m−1·K−1 at 1500 °C and TEC of (10.20–10.39)×10−6 K−1 at 1500 °C. This work provides new insights into the design of novel rare-earth zirconate ceramics with predetermined thermophysical properties for a new generation of TBCs. Through the above studies, increasing the degree of lattice distortion has been found to be favorable for reducing the thermal conductivity, but theoretical calculations to guide the design of the composition are lacking.

Although many studies on HE-RE2Zr2O7 have been performed, there is still a lack of in-depth research on the preparation methods and theoretical calculations used to guide composition design. In this work, the three-dimensional vectors of the average atomic spacing (*d*), interatomic forces (*ϕ*), and average atomic mass (*m*) were obtained by first-principles calculations using the VASP code. Based on the calculated results, (Sc0.2Y0.2La0.2Ho0.2Yb0.2)2Zr2O7 (SYLHYb), which has the lowest theoretical thermal conductivity, and three other compositions of (Sc0.2Ce0.2Nd0.2Eu0.2Gd0.2)2Zr2O7 (SCNEG), (Sc0.2Y0.2Tm0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)2Zr2O7 (SYTYbLu), and (Sc0.2Er0.2Tm0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)2Zr2O7 (SErTYbLu), were experimentally investigated for comparison. Powder and bulk samples of the four HE-RE2Zr2O7 ceramics with single-phase fluorite structures were synthesized by solution precursor plasma spray (SPPS) and pressureless sintering methods, respectively, and their phase compositions, microstructures, mechanical properties, and thermophysical properties were systematically investigated.

### 2.1 Composition design

The entropy of a system reaches a maximum when multiple atoms occupy a Wyckoff position in approximately equal molar proportions, indicating that the system is in its most disordered state. This disorder indicates lattice distortions, various bonding strengths, and differences in the atomic masses at the lattice sites. The disorder decreases the thermal conductivity of the system because it causes stronger phonon scattering. Considering the number of different atomic species occupying a Wyckoff position, there is no relevant literature on which combination of elements maximizes the lattice distortion, the difference in the bonding strengths of different bonds, or the difference in the atomic masses. To quantitatively describe the disorder level of HEC systems, we developed the following method. RE2Zr2O7 is a cubic crystal system, as shown in Fig. 1. The average atomic spacing (*d*), interatomic force (*ϕ*), and average atomic mass (*m*) of this structure were first used to characterize the RE element. The array consisting of these three quantities was then considered to be a vector, and the Euclidean distance between the vectors was used to represent the difference between the two elements. Finally, to describe the difference between *n* elements, the average of the differences between every two elements was used. These three quantities are obtained by first-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations implemented in the VASP code. Furthermore, the plane-wave basis projector augmented wave method was used, and the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof was employed for the exchange correlation potential. A plane-wave energy cutoff of 500 eV was set. The reciprocal spaces of the crystals used for the calculations of the elastic constants were sampled with a 4 × 4 × 4 mesh. To obtain the optimal structure, elastic constants, and second-order force constants, the total energies were minimized until the energy conversion reached less than 10-8 eV. The *i*th atom of the three-dimensional vector (*d**i*, *ϕ**i*, *m**i*) formed by these three quantities was characterized.

The “distance” between the i*th atom and *j*th atom *ρ*(*i*, *j*) is defined to represent the chaos of a high-entropy system, as shown in Eq. (1):

(1) ρ(i,j)=(di−dj)2+(ϕi−ϕj)2+[0.1×(mi−mj)]2

In other words, the greater the “distance”, the greater the disorder of the high-entropy ceramics, and the lower the thermal conductivity. To ensure that the value of *ρ*(*i, j*) is independent of the choice of the units, here, each element in each vector is divided by the corresponding element of one of the *n* vectors. We calculated the combination of five elements selected from 16 rare-earth elements (excluding Pm) to replace the RE site of RE2Zr2O7 to design HE-RE2Zr2O7 ceramics with low thermal conductivity.

### 2.2 Synthesis of HE-RE2Zr2O7

The raw rear-earth powders of commercially available Yb(NO3)3·5H2O, RE(NO3)3·6H2O (RE = Sc, Y, La, Ce, Nd, Eu, Gd, Ho, Er, Tm, and Lu, ≥ 99.99%, Zibo Chungui Co., Ltd., China) and C8H12O8Zr (≥ 99.99%, Mantoufo Fine Chemical Co., Ltd., China) were weighed at a molar ratio of 5RE0.2 : Zr = 1 : 1. The powders were poured into a beaker, and a 0.5 mol/L solution was prepared using deionized water as the solvent. The prepared solution was filtered with a 325-mesh sieve to remove impurities and then sprayed into deionized water by SPPS (Axial III, Mettech, Canada), followed by centrifugation (6000 r/min, 5 min) and drying (100 °C, 36 h) to obtain the ceramic powder.

To prepare the bulk sample, after pressing the ceramic powder in a mold under a uniaxial pressure of 20 MPa for 5 min, a cold isostatic pressing process was performed at 250 MPa for 30 min, yielding a raw ceramic embryo. The raw ceramic embryo was placed in a muffle furnace (SX2-6-13, Shiyan Electric Furnace, China), buried and burned with ZrO2 powder, kept at 600 °C for 2 h for binder discharge (poly(vinyl alcohol)), and heated at 1650 °C for 10 h to obtain the ceramic bulk. The Gd2Zr2O7 (GZ) ceramic was prepared by the same method.

### 2.3 Characterizations of HE-RE2Zr2O7

The HE-RE2Zr2O7 powder samples were characterized by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) using Cu Kα radiation in the 2*θ* range of 5°–90° with a scanning rate of 5 (°)·min−1. To obtain the main peak strength above 10,000, the ceramic bulk was refined by XRD Rietveld analysis at a test rate of 2 (°)·min−1, and the theoretical density (*ρ*0) of the ceramic bulk was obtained. The measured density (*ρ*) of the ceramic bulk was measured by the Archimedes drainage method. A field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; Quanta 650 FEG, FEI, USA) and energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) were used to analyze the micromorphology, element types, and element distribution of HE-RE2Zr2O7. The detailed microstructure of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk was investigated by a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2 F20, FEI, USA).

The specific heat capacity (*c**p*) of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 ceramics can be calculated according to the Neumann–Kopp law [30]. The thermal diffusion coefficient (*λ*) of the bulk samples (*ϕ*12.5 mm × 1.5 mm) was measured from room temperature to 1400 °C with a laser thermal conductivity meter (LFA-427, Netzsch, Germany) at a ramp rate of 10 °C/min. To avoid the transmittance of the sample at high temperatures, a graphite layer was uniformly sprayed on the front and back surfaces of the sample prior to the test. To reduce the error, the same temperature point was measured three times, and the arithmetic mean was taken. The thermal conductivity (*κ*) can be calculated by Eq. (2):

(2) κ=λ×cp×ρ

### 3.2 Characterizations of HE-RE2Zr2O7 powder samples

The XRD patterns of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 powder samples are shown in Fig. 3. The diffraction peaks of the powder samples are consistent with those of GZ (PDF: 80-0471), and there are no other stray peaks, indicating successful synthesis of HE-RE2Zr2O7 powder samples with a single defective fluorite structure. HE-RE2Zr2O7 has both ordered pyrochlore (cubic, *Fm** 3¯m*) and disordered defective fluorite (cubic, *Fd** 3¯m*) structures, which mainly depend on the average radius ratio of the A and B sites. Considering the effects of the valence and coordination number on the ionic radii, in this study, we adopted the Shannon ionic radii [33]. The calculated values of *R*(A)/*R*(B) for SYLHYb, SCNEG, SYTYbLu, SErTYbLu, and GZ are 1.40, 1.46, 1.35, 1.34, and 1.46, respectively, which are all less than or equal to 1.46. Therefore, the designed compositions theoretically possess a defective fluorite structure, which is consistent with the XRD results. The formation of single phases is due to the high entropy effect, which increases the free energy of complex compound phases and facilitates the solid solution of the elements. Furthermore, during the preparation of ceramic powder by SPPS, the plasma flame has a high temperature, and thus, the precipitated particles become molten during spraying. The molten particles are instantaneously sprayed into cold deionized water, which makes it easier to form highly disordered defective fluorite structures [34,35].

SEM images of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 powder samples are shown in Fig. 4. The powder samples were composed of spherical particles, with some particles having a hollow structure. The particle size of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 powder samples ranged from 0.1 to 5 μm, which is attributed to the SPPS process. The precursor was atomized and injected into the plasma flame at different positions, resulting in different degrees of heating, which led to changes in the microstructure and particle size [36]. The elemental contents of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 powder samples were analyzed by EDS (Fig. 5). The contents of RE elements in SYLHYb, SCNEG, SYTYbLu, SErTYbLu, and GZ were approximately 51.96%, 41.15%, 42.03%, 44.35%, and 45.77%, respectively, which are mainly attributed to the different vapor pressures of rare-earth oxides at high temperatures [37]. Based on the XRD and EDS results, each rare-earth oxide smoothly dissolved into the defective fluorite lattice without composition deviation from the designed compositions.

### 3.3 Phase compositions and microstructures of HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples

The XRD patterns of the sintered SYLHYb, SCNEG, SYTYbLu, SErTYbLu, and GZ bulk samples are shown in Fig. 8. Similar to the XRD results of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 powder samples, the diffraction peaks of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples closely matched those of the GZ bulk sample, suggesting that they had similar defective fluoride structures. The peaks of the (111) crystal plane are magnified in Fig. 8(b). It is noteworthy that all of the peaks of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples shifted toward higher diffraction angles compared with those of the GZ bulk sample. This is because the average ionic radii of the five elements in the A position of the SYLHYb, SCNEG, SYTYbLu, and SErTYbLu bulk samples were 1.010, 1.048, 0.969, and 0.966 Å, respectively, which are smaller than the ionic radius of Gd3+ (1.053 Å), and the radii of the individual rare-earth ions can be expressed by the Shannon ionic radius [33,38]. According to Bragg’s law [39], the dissolution of RE elements with small ionic radii into the defective fluorite lattice leads to a decrease in both the lattice parameter and cell volume of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk. Therefore, the positions of their diffraction peaks shift to higher angles.

### 3.4 Thermophysical properties of HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples

The development of rare-earth zirconate ceramics with low thermal conductivity for use as TBC materials is crucial. The specific heat capacities of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 ceramics calculated according to the Neumann–Kopp law are shown in Fig. 11(a). The increase in *c**p* (0.485–0.547 J·g−1·K−1) of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples from 50 to 1200 °C is due to volume expansion and phonon excitation [41], while the decrease in *c**p* from 1200 to 1400 °C is due to the phase transition of ZrO2 from the tetragonal phase to the cubic phase above 1200 °C, which leads to a decrease in the heat capacity.

*
Fig. 11Thermophysical properties of HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulks: (a) heat capacity, (b) thermal diffusivity, (c) experimental thermal conductivity, (d) linear thermal expansion curve, (e) TEC, and (f) thermal conductivity as a function of TEC.

The curves of the thermal diffusion coefficient versus temperature for the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples are shown in Fig. 11(b). The thermal diffusion coefficients decreased with increasing temperature from 50 to 1400 °C. This shows that the heat transfer of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples is dominated by phonon scattering. The thermal diffusion coefficient of the GZ ceramic slightly increased at 1400 °C, which was due to radiative heat transfer. As the temperature increases, the reduced intermolecular interaction force contributes to enhanced phonon scattering, and the high-entropy design increases the lattice distortion, which facilitates phonon scattering. Their combined effect leads to a decrease in the thermal diffusion coefficient of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk.

The thermal conductivities of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples are shown in Fig. 11(c). The thermal conductivity was inversely proportional to the temperature and decreased with increasing temperature. The thermal conductivities of the SYLHYb, SCNEG, SYTYbLu, and SErTYbLu bulk samples at 1400 °C were 1.30, 1.35, 1.38, and 1.45 W·m−1·K−1, respectively. Remarkably, the experimental results were in line with the theoretical results. To further validate the low thermal conductivities of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples, the thermal conductivities of YSZ, La2Zr2O7, and Yb2Zr2O7 are also shown in Fig. 11(c). The thermal conductivities of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples at 1000 °C were much lower than those of YSZ, La2Zr2O7, and Yb2Zr2O7 [1,42−44].

The low thermal conductivities of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples can be explained in terms of Debye’s phonon model (Eqs. (5) and (6)) [45]:

(5) κp=13cpV0Ip

(6) λ=13V0Ip

where  κp is the thermal conductivity affected by phonon heat transfer, V*0 is the speed of sound, and *I*p is the phonon mean free path. According to the Dulong–Petit rule, *c**p* becomes constant at high temperatures. Furthermore, *V*0 is usually negligible. The effects of *c**p* and *V*0, which extend to the thermal conductivity, are very limited, and they are mainly determined by the phonon mean free path. Therefore, the thermal diffusion coefficient is positively related to the thermal conductivity, and the modulation of the low thermal conductivity is based on the reduction in the phonon mean free path.

The relationship between the phonon mean free path (*I**p*) and the temperature (*T*) is given by Eq. (7) [46]:

(7) 1κp∼1Ip∼(bn1/3CθD)T+(D−C2)

where *b, C*, and *D* are constants, *n* is the number of atoms in the original unit cell, and *θ*D is the Debye temperature. From Eq. (7), the phonon mean free path is negatively correlated with the thermal conductivity, while it is positively correlated with the temperature, so the thermal conductivity decreases with increasing temperature. However, at high temperatures, the heat transfer of the ceramic material is affected by the photonic heat transfer (i.e., radiative heat transfer) via Eq. (8) [47]:

(8) κr=163τ2σT3Ir

where * κr is the thermal conductivity affected by the photonic heat transfer, τ* is the refractive index, *σ* is the Stephen Boltzmann constant, and *I*r is the photon mean free path.

It is known that thermal conductivity is positively correlated with the temperature in the high-temperature range. Notably, the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples designed in this study also exhibited good resistance to thermal radiation.

The phonon mean free path of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples is the main factor affecting the thermal conductivity from 50 to 1400 °C. To further illustrate this change, Eqs. (9) and (10) can be used to calculate the phonon mean free path at different temperatures [48,49]:

(9) V0=AEE(1−φ2/3)1.21ρ0

(10) Ip=3λV0

where *I*p is the phonon mean free path, *V*0 is usually negligible, *λ* is the thermal diffusion coefficient, *A* is a constant approximately equal to 0.87, and *E* is the elastic modulus. The calculated results are given in Table 3. It is clear that the phonon mean free paths of all five ceramic bulks decrease with increasing temperature, which is consistent with the analysis of the phonon heat transfer mechanism.

A good compatibility of the TBC material with the superalloy substrate and bond coating requires large TEC, and higher TEC improves the coating life [50]. The thermal expansion data of the SYLHYb and GZ bulk samples are shown in Fig. 11(d). The size change of the SYLHYb bulk sample showed a stable increasing trend with increasing temperature, indicating that the designed SYLHYb bulk sample had excellent radial size stability during the heating process in this study. TEC of SYLHYb and GZ bulk samples increased with increasing temperature from 200 to 1400 °C (Fig. 11(e)), and the other three HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples showed the same trend (Fig. S1 in the ESM). Furthermore, the average TEC of the SYLHYb bulk sample was 10.19×10−6 K−1 at 200–1400 °C, which was slightly greater than that of the GZ bulk sample, whereas the average TEC of the commercial YSZ sample was ~11×10−6 K−1 [51].

From a microscopic point of view, thermal expansion is due to the enhancement of atomic vibrations caused by heat absorption, resulting in a larger atomic spacing. Therefore, TEC is positively correlated with the atomic spacing and negatively related to the lattice energy, which can be expressed by the Born–Rand formula (Eq. (11)) [52,53]:

(11) U=+138940MZ+Z−r0(1−1ε)

where *U* is the lattice energy; *M* and *ε* are the Madelung constant related to the crystal structure of the ion and the Born constant related to the ionic valence, respectively; *Z**+* and *Z**−* are the anionic and cationic charges in the crystals, respectively; *r*0 is the spacing between neighboring anions and cations. The average interaction distance (*r*0) is related to the strength of the ionic bonds, where the ionic spacing increases with decreasing ionic bond strength. The ionic strength is also related to the difference in the electronegativity of the ions. The ionic strength between the A-site cation and O-site ion (*I*A–O) is represented by Pauling’s electronegativity equation (Eq. (12)) [54]:

(12) IA−O=1−exp(−1/4Δχ2)

where *∆χ* is the average electronegativity of the anion and cation. A lower ionic bond strength tends to correspond to a larger interionic distance, and the ionic bond strength is positively correlated with *∆χ*.

The average electronegativity of the SYLHYb (0.9526) bulk is less than that of the GZ (1.0108) bulk. Thus, the increase in the TEC is caused by the decreases in the ionic bond strength and lattice energy due to the decrease in the electronegativity, as well as the synergistic and cocktail effects of HECs. The thermal conductivity of the SYLHYb bulk plotted against the TEC is shown in Fig. 11(f). Compared with YSZ [51] and other silicate [55], phosphate [56], tantalate [57], and zirconate [18] TBC materials, the SYLHYb bulk has lower thermal conductivity and higher TEC, indicating that the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples designed by theoretical calculations are promising for next-generation TBCs.

### 3.5 Mechanical properties of HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples

The mechanical properties of the SYLHYb, GZ, LZ, and YSZ bulk samples are shown in Fig. 12, and those of the other three HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples are shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. The microhardness is usually affected by the bonding strength of the ionic crystal, which is related to the atomic spacing and thus to the lattice energy [49]. Although the microhardness of the SYLHYb bulk sample (7.96 GPa) was lower than that of the La2Zr2O7 bulk sample [29], it was slightly greater than that of the GZ bulk sample prepared by the same method. The SYLHYb bulk sample had a smaller lattice parameter (5.1942 Å, Table 2) and a greater densification degree (up to 90.52%) than did the GZ bulk sample, suggesting that it could have greater lattice energy and therefore better hardness than the GZ bulk sample, which is in agreement with the abovementioned XRD results. However, the grain size of the SYLHYb bulk sample (3.28 μm) was smaller than that of the GZ bulk sample (3.39 μm). The sluggish diffusion effect of the HE-RE2Zr2O7 bulk samples induces the formation of a large number of grain boundaries from grain refinement, and these boundaries impede dislocation movement, resulting in an increase in hardness. This is in agreement with the SEM results.

*
Fig. 12Mechanical properties of SYLHYb and GZ bulks: (a) microhardness, (b) fracture toughness, and (c) brittleness index.

The fracture toughness values of the SYLHYb and GZ bulk samples were calculated based on their microhardness and crack length. A high fracture toughness is beneficial for stopping crack extension, which is beneficial for improving the thermal cycling lifetime of TBCs. The fracture toughness of the SYLHYb bulk sample reached 1.69±0.28 MPa·m1/2 (Fig. 12(b)), which was ~50% greater than that of the GZ bulk sample and almost twice that of La2Zr2O7.

In addition, the damage tolerance of ceramics can be characterized by the brittleness index (M*) (Eq. (13)) [58]:

(13) M=HVKIC

A high brittleness leads to poor resistance to mechanical impacts with rapid temperature changes, which is unfavorable for the long-term use of ceramics. The brittleness index of the SYLHYb bulk sample was only 3.03 μm−1/2, which was ~56% lower than that of the GZ bulk sample and YSZ bulk [59], indicating that the SYLHYb bulk sample has better damage resistance.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, theoretical calculations were used to design HE-RE2Zr2O7 with low thermal conductivity. According to the theoretical calculation results, (Sc0.2Y0.2La0.2Ho0.2Yb0.2)2Zr2O7, (Sc0.2Ce0.2Nd0.2Eu0.2Gd0.2)2Zr2O7, (Sc0.2Y0.2Tm0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)2Zr2O7, and (Sc0.2Er0.2Tm0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)2Zr2O7 powder samples were prepared by SPPS, followed by a solid-state reaction to form the corresponding bulk samples. Both the powder and bulk samples showed a single-defective fluorite structure without any element segregation, as well as good phase stability. The intrinsic thermal conductivities of the (Sc0.2Er0.2Tm0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)2Zr2O7, (Sc0.2Y0.2Tm0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)2Zr2O7, (Sc0.2Ce0.2Nd0.2Eu0.2Gd0.2)2Zr2O7, and (Sc0.2Y0.2La0.2Ho0.2Yb0.2)2Zr2O7 bulk samples at 1400 °C showed a sequential decrease, which was consistent with the theoretical calculation results. Among the samples, the (Sc0.2Y0.2La0.2Ho0.2Yb0.2)2Zr2O7 bulk sample showed an intrinsic thermal conductivity of 1.30 W·m−1·K−1 (at 1400 °C), indicating remarkable thermal insulation and thermal radiation resistance. In addition, the (Sc0.2Y0.2La0.2Ho0.2Yb0.2)2Zr2O7 bulk sample showed a high TEC (10.19×10−6 K−1, 200–1400 °C), significant fracture toughness (1.69±0.28 MPa·m1/2), and good damage resistance (3.03 μm−1/2). All of the above outstanding properties indicate that (Sc0.2Y0.2La0.2Ho0.2Yb0.2)2Zr2O7 is a promising TBC candidate material.
