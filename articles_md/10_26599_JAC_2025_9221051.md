## 1 Introduction

Owing to the rapid expansion of aircraft, geological exploration, and nuclear power generation, there is a greater need for piezoelectric materials capable of performing at high temperatures [1−3]. Currently, lead-based piezoelectric ceramics dominate piezoelectric materials because of their excellent piezoelectric properties. However, these piezoelectric ceramics include a high lead concentration of 60%–70%, causing significant issues related to human health and environmental contamination throughout manufacturing, processing, and waste management [4−6]. Given the hazards associated with lead, lead-free piezoelectric ceramics such as BaTiO3 (BT), K0.5Na0.5NbO3 (KNN), and Na0.5Bi0.5TiO3 (NBT)-based ceramics would be favorable alternatives for a sustainable society, as they have excellent piezoelectric properties. However, such lead-free perovskite piezoelectric ceramics are still unable to work effectively at higher temperatures because of their relatively low Curie temperature (*T*C), which is typically less than 350 °C [3]. To ensure the effective operation of electronic devices at higher temperatures, piezoelectric ceramics are required to possess a high Curie temperature (normally *T*C > 500 °C). On the basis of the aforementioned concerns and requirements, Aurivillius-type bismuth layer-structured ferroelectrics (BLSFs) would be an attractive choice owing to their extremely high Curie temperature. The general formula of BLSFs is (Bi2O2)2+(A*m*−1B*m*O3*m*+1)2−, which can be described as the regular stacking of pseudoperovskite blocks (A*m*−1B*m*O3*m*+1)2− separated along the *c*-axis by a fluorite-like (Bi2O2)2+ layer. The A-site elements exhibit dodecahedral coordination, the B-site elements exhibit octahedral coordination, and *m* (1–6) denotes the number of oxygen octahedra in the pseudoperovskite layers [7,8].

CaBi2Nb2O9 (CBN, *m* = 2), Bi4Ti3O12 (BIT, *m* = 3), and CaBi4Ti4O15 (CBT, *m* = 4) are considered the most promising candidates for high-temperature applications among BLSFs because of their exceptional *T*C and piezoelectric properties. Recently, CBN-based ceramics have achieved *d*33 values exceeding 30 pC/N and urtra-high *T*C of 950 °C on the basis of Na/Sm modification and the template grain growth method [9,10]. However, CBN-based ceramics typically exhibit relatively low piezoelectric properties through simple conventional solid-state reactions. For BIT-based ceramics, high *d*33 values can be achieved through ion modification, but their *T*C is usually below 680 °C [11]. In contrast, CBT-based ceramics not only have high *T*C of 790 °C but also excellent annealing thermal stability via the conventional solid-state reaction method, which has great potential in practical applications [12,13]. However, pure CBT ceramics exhibit poor piezoelectric performance because of their unique structure and defects. On the one hand, the high strain energy in the pseudo-perovskite blocks between the bismuth layers limits spontaneous polarization (*P*s) in the *a–b* plane, resulting in weak piezoelectric properties along the *c*-axis. On the other hand, numerous defects and a low density of domains further deteriorate the piezoelectric activity, causing pure CBT ceramics to exhibit *d*33 values of only 8 pC/N [14−18]. To improve the piezoelectric properties of CBT ceramics, researchers commonly use ion modification methods. For example, Li/Ce, Na/Ce, and K/Ce were designed for co-doping the A-site of CBT ceramics to optimize the domain structure and grain size, resulting in a *d*33 value of approximately 20 pC/N [19,20]. Additionally, the B-site doping method effectively improves the piezoelectric performance, as demonstrated by the high *d*33 value (21.6 pC/N) obtained for the Nb/Fe co-doped CBT ceramics, and the introduction of Nb/Mg and Ta/Mn at the B-site in the CBT ceramics significantly enhances the piezoelectric performance, resulting in a large *d*33 value of 24 pC/N due to ferroelectricity and defect optimization [21−23]. On the basis of these results, B-site complex ion doping appears to be superior to A-site doping; thus, we attempted to improve the piezoelectric constant of CBT ceramics to 18.1 pC/N by doping with W/Co complex ions at the B-site. However, there is still a certain gap between it and practical applications, and further optimization is needed.

On the other hand, many reports suggest that adding variable valence ions (Ce/Mn ions) can effectively optimize the piezoelectric performance. For example, CeO2 is designed to dope CBN-based ceramics, and the *d*33 value can be increased from 9 to 17.3 pC/N because various valence ions (Ce3+/Ce4+) occupy A/B sites [24]. Furthermore, MnO2 has been investigated for its potential to enhance the piezoelectric properties of Pb(Sb0.5Nb0.5)0.02Zr0.51Ti0.47O3 (PZT) piezoelectric ceramics, which induce lattice distortion and effectively improve the piezoelectric properties owing to its diverse valence states [25]. Similarly, MnO2 has also been used to optimize BLSF CBN-based ceramics, where the *d*33 value significantly increased from 15 to 25 pC/N when 0.25 wt% MnO2 was added [26]. Considering the significant effect of MnO2 modification on improving *d*33 of piezoelectric ceramics, we chose to further optimize the piezoelectric properties of CBT-based ceramics in this work. Moreover, by combining Rietveld refinement and piezoelectric response force microscopy analysis to investigate the transformation of crystal and domain structures, the fundamental mechanism underlying its ultrahigh piezoelectric properties was elucidated.

### 2.1 Sample synthesis

CaBi4Ti3.89(W1/2Co1/2)0.11O15+*x* wt%MnO2 (CBTWC–*x*Mn, *x* = 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25) ceramics were prepared via a solid-state sintering method in which the raw materials CaCO3 (99.0%), Bi2O3 (99.9%), TiO2 (98.0%), Co2O3 (99.0%), WO3 (99.0%), and MnO2 (98.8%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China. The raw materials were accurately weighed after calculation according to the chemical composition and then ball-milled for 24 h using ethanol as the medium and zirconia as the grinding balls. The slurries were subsequently dried and calcined at 800 °C for 2 h to pre-synthesize the CBTWC–*x*Mn powders and then ball-milled for another 24 h. The dried powders were granulated with a polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) solution (5 wt%) as a binder and pressed into green pellets with a diameter of 13 mm and a thickness of approximately 0.7 mm. The green pellets were heated at 650 °C for 3 h to burn off the PVA binders and then sintered at 980–1050 °C for 3 h at a heating rate of 5 °C/min, followed by furnace cooling to ambient temperature.

### 2.2 Material characterizations

The crystalline phase structure of the sintered ceramics was characterized over the 2*θ* range from 5° to 80° via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8-Advance, Bruker, Germany) via CuKα radiation in steps of 0.02° with a counting time of 1.2 s per step, and the data were analyzed via Jade software. Rietveld refinements of the XRD data were performed via the GSAS-Ⅱ software, and the crystal structure was analyzed via the VESTA program. Raman spectroscopy was performed with a Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR800, HORIBA Jobin Yvon, France) at room temperature, and Lorentz peak fitting was used to dissect the Raman spectra. The crystal surface morphology and elemental distribution maps were obtained via a scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM; JEOL JEM-F200, Japan) combined with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS). The microstructures of the ceramics were observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU-8010, Hitachi, Japan). The surface element analysis of the ceramic samples was performed with an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Escalab 250XI, Thermo Scientific, USA) and analyzed with the available Avantage software. The domain structures were measured via PFM equipped with a commercial microscope (MFP-3D, Asylum Research, USA), which was characterized utilizing polished and unpoled ceramic samples by applying voltages ranging from 0 to 30 V in a 2 µm × 2 µm region within a 5 µm × 5 µm scanning area.

### 2.3 Electrical property measurements

For electrical measurements, the sintered CBTWC–*x*Mn ceramics were polished on both sides, painted with silver paste, dried and then fired at 800 °C for 30 min to form a circular center electrode with blank edges. The dielectric permittivity and loss were measured at a frequency of 1 MHz, and the Curie temperature of the ceramic samples was determined by measuring the *ε*r–*T* curve from room temperature to 850 °C via an LCR meter (TH2828S, Changzhou Tonghui Electronic Co., Ltd., China) connected to a temperature-controlled chamber. The electrical resistivity was calculated on the basis of the resistance, which was measured by applying 100 V to the sample from room temperature to 600 °C via an insulating resistor meter (TH2684, Changzhou Tonghui Electronic Co., Ltd., China) connected to a programmable mini-furnace. The *P*–*E* hysteresis loops were measured by a precision workstation (TRek model 609B, Radiant, USA) at 160 °C with an applied electric field of 150 kV/cm. For the piezoelectric measurements, the ceramic samples were poled at 180 °C for 20–30 min in a silicone oil bath with an applied electric field of 120–180 kV/cm. The piezoelectric constant *d*33 was measured with a quasistatic *d*33 meter (ZJ-3A, Institute of Acoustics, Academia Sinica, China), and the polarized samples were annealed to assess their thermal stability up to 800 °C.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of the CBTWC–*x*Mn ceramics in the range of 20° ≤ 2*θ* ≤ 60° at room temperature. All samples possess an Aurivillius-type CaBi4Ti4O15 phase, with the most intense diffraction peak (119) corresponding to the characteristic peak (112*m*+1) of BLSFs [17]. When *x* ≥ 0.2, a weak impurity peak located on the left shoulder of the (119) peak can be detected and identified as Bi1.74Ti2O6.624. This is attributed to the dissolution of excess MnO2 into the crystal lattice and the volatilization of Bi2O3, resulting in a local imbalance in the Bi/Ti ratio and the subsequent formation of Bi1.74Ti2O6.624 [27−29]. To analyze the evolution of the crystal structure with composition, the Rietveld refinement of the crystal structure of the CBTWC–*x*Mn ceramics was performed via GSAS-II software, which is based on the CBT structure model [30,31]. The refined configurations of the ceramics with *x* = 0 and *x* = 0.1 are shown in Figs. 1(c) and 1(d), and all the refinement data are shown in Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). All the reliability factors (*R*wp) are less than 8%, with a goodness of fit (GOF) between 1 and 2, indicating high agreement between the precision calculation values and the experimental values. Figures 1(b) and 1(e) show that MnO2 doping promotes the expansion of the lattice, with an increasing trend in the lattice parameters (*a*, *b*, *c*, and *V*). This phenomenon is likely caused by the following factors. On the one hand, the comparable ionic radii of Mn3+/Mn4+ and Ti4+ suggest that a portion of the Mn3+/Mn4+ ions may occupy the B-site of the perovskite-like layer. The substitution of Ti4+ (*r*i = 0.605 Å) with the larger Mn3+ (*r*i = 0.645 Å) would result in lattice expansion, since Mn4+ predominantly converts to Mn3+ when the sintering temperature reaches 1020 °C [32−34]. On the other hand, Mn3+/Mn4+ with a relatively high *x* content may insert into the lattice gaps of CBT-based ceramics, which also contributes to lattice expansion. Moreover, there is a slight downward trend in the fitted curve of *a/b*, suggesting that the crystal structure changes with increasing *x* [35]. To further investigate the effect of MnO2 doping, the visual crystal structure of representative CBTWC–*x*Mn ceramics (*x* = 0, 0.1, 0.2) was analyzed. A change in the inclination (*α*) of the oxygen octahedron toward the *c*-axis can be observed in Figs. 1(f)–1(h). Notably, the CBTWC–0.1Mn ceramics, whose ∠*α* = 4.24°, have the largest degree of distortion among them. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. S1 in the ESM, the rotation angle (∠* βO6), which also represents structural distortion caused by the O6 atoms within the oxygen octahedron, has the highest value of 7.32° when x* = 0.1. All the crystal structural analysis results indicate that 0.1 wt% MnO2 doping has a significant effect on the distortion of the octahedral layer, particularly the degree of inclination between the octahedrons, which should be favorable for increasing the piezoelectric performance [24,36].

Raman spectroscopy measurements were carried out to verify the crystal structure evolution of the CBTWC–*x*Mn ceramics in the wavenumber range of 100–1000 cm−1. As shown in Fig. 2(a), all the Raman patterns displayed the same configuration, and no additional peaks were observed, indicating that the Mn ions should be properly dissolved into the crystal lattice. Additionally, the Raman modes *v*1 to *v*9 are fitted by the Lorentz function, as seen from the represented sample with *x* = 0.1 in Fig. 2(b), which can be divided into low-frequency modes (−1) and high-frequency modes (> 200 cm−1). Figures 2(c) and 2(d) show the Raman modes as a function of the MnO2 doping amount (*x*)*.* The *v*1 and *v*2 modes are reportedly associated with ion vibrations at the (Bi2O2)2+ and A-sites of the pseudo-perovskite layer, respectively [37,38]. Figure 2(c) shows that the *ν*1 mode remains unchanged, whereas the *v*2 mode slightly shifts, which indicates a minimal impact of Mn ions on the bismuth oxygen layer, but a few Mn3+/Mn4+ ions may enter the bismuth vacancies of the A-site [25]. Moreover, the *v*3−*v*9 modes are derived from the internal vibration of the TiO6 octahedron. The representative *ν*4, *ν*7, and *ν*9 modes correspond to the O−Ti−O bending vibrations, the oxygen atom opposing excursion and the Ti−O symmetric stretch of the titanium‒oxygen octahedron, respectively [39,40]. As shown in Fig. 2(d), the *v*4 and *v*7 modes shift to lower wavenumbers, whereas *v*9 shifts to higher wavenumbers, suggesting that Mn ions primarily occupy the B-site, which is consistent with the results in Figs. 1(b)–1(e) and confirms the previous speculation. In addition, both the half-peak width and peak intensity reflect distortion within the oxygen octahedron. Increasing the half-peak width of the *v*4, *v*7, and *v*9 modes suggests that MnO2 doping leads to an increase in local disorder [41]. The intensities of *v*4, *v*7, and *v*9 increase and then decrease with increasing MnO2 content, indicating that the deformation of the oxygen octahedron first increases but then decreases [42]. Notably, the highest intensity is obtained when *x* = 0.1, which is consistent with previous results of the largest inclination angle (*α*) and rotation angle (*β*).

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) can be used to investigate the chemical states of elements. Figure 4(a) displays the spectra of the x* = 0 and *x* = 0.1 compositions, with the corresponding elements identified. As illustrated in Fig. 4(b), the bismuth element spectra exhibit minimal variation between the *x* = 0 and *x* = 0.1 components, which is consistent with the results of the Raman spectra. This suggests that the introduction of MnO2 does not significantly affect the volatilization of bismuth ions. However, the concentration of oxygen vacancies is reduced, as shown in Figs. 4(c) and 4(d). This reduction may be attributed to a lower transformation rate of Ti4+ into Ti3+ rather than the reduced volatilization of bismuth oxide. As shown in Figs. 4(c) and 4(d), the O element exhibits three principal peaks at approximately 529.4, 531, and 532.2 eV. The peak located at approximately 529.4 eV corresponds to lattice oxygen (OL), and the second peak at approximately 531 eV corresponds to vacancy oxygen (OV). The introduction of MnO2 reduces the concentration of oxygen vacancies, as evidenced by the decrease in the peak area of OV : OL from 0.49 to 0.46 [44]. Regarding the Ti element, two valence states (Ti3+ and Ti4+) are identified, corresponding to 457.4, 457.8, and 463.7, 465.8 eV, respectively. Notably, the peak area of Ti3+ : Ti4+ decreases from 0.72 to 0.6, which is consistent with the observed variations in the oxygen vacancy concentration and better electrical performance [45].

Figure 6(a) shows the electrical resistivity (*ρ*) of the CBTWC–*x*Mn ceramics as a function of temperature, and the inset shows the *ρ* values at 500 °C for the ceramics with different *x* values. For each sample, *ρ* gradually decreases with increasing temperature, which can be attributed to the activation of intrinsic carriers [48]. Among them, CBTWC–0.1Mn ceramics possess the highest resistivity (2.32×106 Ω∙cm@500 °C), which is induced by smaller grains and proper donor doping that reduces the concentration of charge carriers. High resistivity allows a higher applied poling electric field, thereby enhancing the polarization efficiency and leading to improved piezoelectric *d*33. The electrical properties of ceramics in high-temperature regions are crucial for practical applications. To clarify the high-temperature conduction mechanism, Fig. 6(b) shows the Arrhenius relationship between ln*σ* (*σ* = 1/*ρ*) and 1000/*T* above 375 °C, and the calculated activation energy (*E*a) as a function of the MnO2 doping amount is plotted in Fig. 6(c). The CBTWC–0.1Mn ceramics have the corresponding highest *E*a values. In addition, a close match between *E*a and the conductivity activation energy of other BLSF ceramics indicates the intrinsic conduction of charge carriers at high temperatures [49].

Figures 7(a)–7(f) show the *P–E* hysteresis loops and *I–E* current loops of the CBTWC–*x*Mn ceramics measured at 160 °C and 10 Hz. The ceramic samples exhibit a saturated *P‒E* loop with a featured *I‒E* curve, and the remaining polarization (*P*r) slightly increases with increasing MnO2 amount when *x* ≤ 0.1, indicating that the presence of MnO2 softens the CBTWC ceramics at this time. Compared with other components, CBTWC–0.1Mn ceramics have the largest 2*P*r value (20.12 μC/cm2) and the most obvious featured current peaks, which may be attributed to enhanced lattice distortion and easily oriented domain structures [50−52]. However, excessive doping of MnO2 can lead to an increase in leakage current, resulting in maximum polarization obtained at lower electric fields, and the *I‒E* curve is not significant. This indicates that the excess dopant may not fully integrate into the lattice structure and may cause hardening effects at the grain boundaries. Therefore, when *x* exceeds 0.1, the ferroelectric properties deteriorate [16].

To investigate the domain configuration and switching, piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) was performed on representative CBTWC–*x*Mn ceramics with *x* = 0 and 0.1. The out-of-plane PFM height and amplitude as well as phase images of the samples within a selected area of 5 µm × 5 µm are presented in Fig. 8. As shown in the brown frame in Figs. 8(a) and 8(b), microdomains presenting distinct bright/dark contrasts can be observed. Compared with those of the CBTWC ceramics, the domains of the CBTWC–0.1Mn ceramics are more uniform and smaller in size, which is attributed mainly to the disruption of long-range polarization ordering by MnO2 [53]. For the CBTWC ceramics, the domain switching is still incomplete even when a voltage of 30 V is applied (Fig. 8(a)). Nevertheless, for the CBTWC–0.1Mn ceramics, the majority of the domains are switched when a lower voltage of 20 V is applied (see Fig. 8(b)). This phenomenon can be attributed to the enhanced lattice distortion and reduction in the domain switching energy barriers caused by MnO2 doping [54]. Figures 8(c) and 8(d) show the evolution of the domain structure after relaxation at different times. Compared with those of the CBTWC ceramics, more domains of the CBTWC–0.1Mn ceramics maintain the same direction after 10 min. This finding indicates that MnO2 doping can make domains easier to orient and more stable, beneficial for improving piezoelectric properties [55], which is consistent with the observation of saturated *P−E* loops and the featured *I−E* curves in Figs. 7(a)–7(f).

Figure 9(a) shows the piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33) of the CBTWC–*x*Mn ceramics. The *d*33 value first increased and then decreased, and an ultrahigh *d*33 of 27.3 pC/N was achieved at *x* = 0.1. On the basis of the above analysis, the superior piezoelectric properties are attributed to greater distortion of the oxygen octahedron, enhanced ferroelectric properties, and a more easily oriented domain structure. Figure 9(b) shows the thermal stability of the piezoelectric constant, which exhibits remarkable annealing stability up to 500 °C. For the optimized CBTWC–0.1Mn composition, *d*33 (26.3 pC/N) remains at 95% of the initial value after annealing at 500 °C. In addition, Fig. 9(c) compares the diverse electrical properties between the CBTWC and CBTWC–0.1Mn compositions, revealing significant enhancements in the piezoelectric constant, ferroelectric property, and dielectric loss upon the introduction of 0.1 wt% MnO2. Figure 9(d) presents a comparison of the *d*33 values of this work with those of various additive-doped CBT ceramics previously reported [7,19−21,23,35,56−59]. Considering the above comparison, CBTWC–0.1Mn ceramics have the highest piezoelectric constant (*d*33), indicating that adding MnO2 is a very promising way to enhance the performance of CBT-based ceramics.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, a systematic study of CBTWC ceramics based on W/Co co-doped CBT ceramics is presented. Compared with the *x* = 0 component, the CBTWC–0.1Mn ceramics exhibit greater lattice distortion and a more easily oriented domain structure. Most importantly, CBTWC–0.1Mn ceramics achieve an ultrahigh piezoelectric constant of 27.3 pC/N and excellent thermal stability. Furthermore, the introduction of 0.1 wt% MnO2 results in notable enhancements in both the ferroelectric and the dielectric properties. Clearly, CBTWC–0.1Mn ceramics are highly promising materials for high-temperature piezoelectric devices.
