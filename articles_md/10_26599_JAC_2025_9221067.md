## 1 Introduction

Silicon carbide (SiC) materials, which have a lower neutron capture cross section, excellent thermal stability at high temperatures, remarkable anti-irradiation, and resistance to molten salt corrosion, can be candidates for fuel cladding in molten salt reactors [1−7]. Compared with SiC ceramics, SiC fiber-reinforced SiC matrix (SiCf/SiC) composites have better mechanical properties [8−16], such as fracture toughness and ductility, and are more widely used. The pores within SiCf/SiC composites, such as small pores between fibers and large pores between fiber bundles [14−17], can act as diffusion pathways for gases and molten salts, which affect the structural properties of the materials. To ensure the service life of the material, a SiC coating is deposited on the surface of SiCf/SiC composites by chemical vapor deposition (CVD).

He atoms produced by (n, α) transmutation reactions in reactors with low solubility within SiC materials are prone to form He bubbles, affecting the microstructure and macroscopic properties of the material [18−22]. There have been many reports on SiC materials irradiated by He ions [22−29]. It has been shown that the regions where He bubbles exist have localized stresses, which cause additional deformation of the lattice and an increase in size, leading to He-irradiated swelling or cracking of the material [30,31]. In addition, He bubbles tend to accumulate at grain boundaries (GBs), exacerbating the fracture of the material along the grain, known as He embrittlement [26,32]. On the other hand, He bubbles themselves can act as pinning points to increase the hardness of the material through the pinning effect [33,34]. They can also aggregate at high temperatures to form He platelets with supersaturated pressure [35]. In our previous work [33,34], we investigated the changes in the microstructure and mechanical properties of SiC materials at different temperatures and doses.

Notably, grain boundaries, as crucial regulatory units for defect annihilation and helium diffusion, play an important role in the evolution of irradiation damage through their density and structural characteristics [36,37]. In traditional coarse-grained CVD-SiC, the sparse grain boundary network leads to the abundant presence of defects such as He bubbles and dislocation loops in the lattice, resulting in significant irradiation-induced hardening [34]. In sharp contrast, nanocrystalline SiC contains defect-trapping and buffering regions with high-density grain boundaries. This promotes annihilation and accelerates the diffusion of helium, reduces the damage caused by helium to the crystal structure, and may lead to better resistance to irradiation-induced damage [38]. However, some studies suggest that GBs have a negligible impact on the generation of overall defects in SiC [39]. The effects of grain boundaries among the factors influencing irradiation performance are not fully understood in the current research. Moreover, the dynamic interaction mechanism among the grain boundaries, helium bubbles, dislocation loops, and stress in nanocrystalline SiC coatings is still unclear and needs further study. This is precisely the key breakthrough direction of this study.

To simulate an environment with a high He concentration in the reactor, nanocrystalline SiC coatings were irradiated with He ions. In this study, the evolution of microstructure and mechanical properties of SiC coatings, which were irradiated with 500 keV He ions with fluences of 1×1016, 3×1016, and 5×1016 ions/cm2, was investigated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Raman spectroscopy, and nanoindentation. The irradiation temperature is 800 °C, which is close to the service temperature of the materials in the reactor. This study primarily compares the disparities in defect evolution between nanocrystalline SiC coatings and CVD-SiC within the same irradiation environment, analyzes the function of grain boundaries in irradiation damage, and elucidates the improved irradiation damage resistance of nanocrystalline SiC coatings.

## 2 Materials and methods

The nanocrystalline SiC coatings used in this study were provided by the Shanghai Institute of Ceramics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern and surface morphology of SiC coating are displayed in Fig. 1. These samples were cut into 10 mm × 6.5 mm × 1 mm blocks. For irradiation experiments, these samples were polished with diamond sandpaper and ultrasonically cleaned with pure water and anhydrous alcohol. High-temperature He ion irradiation experiments were carried out on the 320 keV multi-disciplinary research platform for highly charged ions at the Institute of Modern Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The irradiation doses used were 1×1016, 3×1016, and 5×1016 ions/cm2, and the irradiation temperature was 800 °C.

TEM samples were prepared using a scanning electron microscope/focused ion beam system (SEM/FIB, FEI Helios NanoLab 600). After the deposition of Pt in the irradiated center region, it was thinned using a focused ion beam. The thinned TEM samples were soldered onto a copper grid. Then, a FEI Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN TEM instrument was used to observe the displacement damage and bubble distribution inside the TEM samples. The thickness of the TEM samples in this experiment was ~60 nm. The grain size is defined as half of the sum of the lengths of the major and minor axes of the grains in the nanocrystalline SiC coating. The sizes of a total of 60 grains in three different regions were statistically analyzed, and the average grain size of the sample was determined to be 104.33 nm.

A Nd-YAG laser with a wavelength of 532 nm within an inVia Qontor instrument was used to study the lattice structure within the SiC coating. The site of interest of the sample was focused under a 50× objective lens with a spot size of 1.5 µm. Each spot was scanned 2 times with exposure time of 20 s. The laser power in this study was 50 mW × 50%.

The hardness profile of the SiC coating as a function of indentation depth was measured using the continuous stiffness measurement (CSM) mode of a G200 nanoindenter (Aglient, USA). Selected areas of interest were tested at room temperature under an optical microscope at least 10 times for each sample.

### 3.1 TEM

TEM is an effective way to observe the evolution of microstructures in materials, such as bubbles, cracks, dislocation loops, stacking faults (SFs), and other defects [16, 41−43]. Figure 3 shows the under-focus mode TEM images of peak irradiation damage regions in the SiC coatings irradiated by He ions with various fluences. The vacancies and less soluble He atoms, which are introduced by He ion irradiation, migrated and aggregated to form He-vacancy clusters at high temperatures. With increasing irradiation dose, He-vacancy clusters grew and gradually evolved into He bubbles, which appeared as white dots in the underfocus TEM images, as shown by the red arrows in Fig. 3. Many He bubbles were observed in the GBs and SFs within the SiC coating irradiated by He ions with a fluence of 1×1016 ions/cm2, as shown by the white boxes and black arrows in Figs. 3(b) and 3(c). Notably, enough He bubbles would have great pressure on adjacent planes, causing cracks in the material, as shown by the white arrows in Fig. 3. Figures 3(d)–3(i) show TEM images of peak irradiation damage regions within SiC coatings irradiated by He ions with fluences of 3×1016 and 5×1016 ions/cm2. As the irradiation dose increased, the He platelets of the continuous gas-filled disc (CGD) type were observed, and the number of cracks increased. The CGD-type He platelets are essentially the same as He bubbles, with both being clusters of He atoms. The difference between them lies in their distinct stress environments, which result in different morphologies, and they can be classified into the same type of defect.

The size of the dislocation loops within the SiC coating was determined via Nano Measure software, as shown in Fig. 6. Notably, when the size of the dislocation loops is small, distinguishing them from the black spots induced by irradiation is difficult. In fact, black spots are the result of a large accumulation of defects such as vacancies and interstitial atoms in the lattice. When these defects are arranged in a certain specific pattern, dislocation loops are formed. That is, black spots are the precursors of dislocation loops. Therefore, the subsequent statistical data of the dislocation loops we mentioned may include black spots. To address this issue, we select an appropriate model during the analysis. The average sizes of the black-spot/dislocation loops in SiC coatings irradiated with 500 keV He ions with fluences of 1×1016, 3×1016, and 5×1016 ions/cm2 were 2.97±0.42, 2.74±0.98, and 3.15±1.19 nm, respectively. The number densities of the dislocation loops in the SiC coatings irradiated with 500 keV He ions with fluences of 1×1016, 3×1016, and 5×1016 ions/cm2 were 3.30×1021, 3.00×1022, and 4.76×1022 m−3, respectively. As the irradiation dose increased, the damage accumulated. Furthermore, no dependence between the degree of damage and irradiation dose was observed, which is in agreement with Harrison et al*. [27]. They suggested that the black spot was formed by a core of vacancies and surrounding interstitial atoms and the growth of black spot was limited because the vacancies hardly migrated at 800 °C.

### 3.2 Raman

The peaks in Raman spectra provide information on the molecular bonds in the material, which can be used to analyze the chemical disorder, lattice strain, SFs, etc. [45−48]. Figure 7 shows the Raman spectra of SiC coatings before and after irradiation with 500 keV He ions with various fluences. In the Raman spectra of unirradiated SiC coatings, the peaks at approximately 796 and 965 cm−1, which are associated with the first-order Raman lines of the Si‒C bonds, are referred to as transverse optical (TO) modes and longitudinal optical (LO) modes [49]. In addition, a shoulder exists to the left of both the TO and LO peaks, related to SFs and GBs within the material [50], respectively, which is consistent with the TEM results (Fig. 3). In addition, the peaks in the range of 300–600 cm−1 are related to the vibration mode of Si‒Si bond. The peaks at 1520 and 1714 cm−1, which are related to the second-order Raman lines of the Si‒C bond vibrations, are referred to as 2TO(X) and 2LO(L) [45,51].

Notably, the LO peak shifted to a greater wavenumber after irradiation with a lower dose of He ions, indicating that the stress within the damage layer is mainly composed of the compressive stress generated by He bubbles squeezing the neighboring lattice atoms. According to Hooke’s law, stress causes elastic distortion in SiC. Therefore, compressive stress led to a decrease in the Si‒C bond length and an increase in the bond force constant. According to the principle of Raman scattering, an increase in the force constant resulted in an increase in the wavenumber of the Raman peak. These results indicate that the abundant grain boundaries in the samples exert a unique stress effect on He accommodation. This not only results in stress imbalance and dynamic equilibrium within the unit cells under varying irradiation doses but also may give rise to strains distinct from those in coarse-grained SiC.

### 4.1 Irradiation defects in nanocrystalline SiC coatings

As shown in Figs. 3 and 9, the nanocrystalline SiC coating was distinguished by a high density of GBs and some SFs, whereas the coarse-grained CVD-SiC [55] with a grain size of approximately 5 μm [56] exhibited only a few SFs. This constituted the primary disparity between the two. This difference endowed the nanocrystalline SiC coating with unique behaviors in the formation and evolution of irradiation-induced defects. These behaviors were manifested in multiple processes, including the accommodation of He atoms, growth of dislocations, and generation of stress and strain. The following is a detailed discussion.

#### 4.1.1 Helium bubbles and dislocations

The differences in the size and density of He bubbles between the coarse-crystalline CVD-SiC and nanocrystalline SiC coatings are shown in Fig. 10. At low irradiation doses, the SiC coating contained a relatively large number of large He bubbles, and the number of He bubbles increased significantly as the irradiation dose rose. This was because GBs and SFs were important trapping sites for He atoms. The high-density GBs and a small number of SFs in the SiC coating formed a trapping network (GB–SF network) for He atoms. The He atoms within the lattice were collected into this “network”, generating a substantial number of nucleation sites for He bubbles. According to first-principles calculations [38], the trapping ability of GBs for He was stronger than that of SFs, and the diffusion rate of He atoms in GBs was higher than that in the lattice and SFs. This made it easier for large He bubbles to form and accumulate at the GBs of the coating. Therefore, at the same He dose, the He bubbles at the GBs of the nanocrystalline SiC coating were larger in size and higher in density. This phenomenon triggered a series of chain effects related to dislocations and stress.

In coarse-crystalline CVD-SiC [55], the collisions between He ions and lattice atoms generated vacancies and self-interstitial atoms in the lattice. He atoms combined with lattice vacancies to form He-vacancy structures, which can further attract He atoms to form He bubbles. As the number of vacancies and interstitial atoms increased, they aggregated to form black spots. When the defects further aggregated and were arranged according to certain rules, dislocation loops formed. However, in the nanocrystalline SiC coating, He atoms in the lattice were captured by the GB–SF network and failed to combine with the lattice vacancies. While the number of nucleation sites for He bubbles in the lattice decreased, vacancies and interstitial atoms could recombine at high temperatures, which inhibited the formation of dislocations in the lattice. Moreover, the influx of a large number of defects into the grain boundaries caused stress concentration, leading to extensive nucleation of dislocations and formation of dislocation loops. However, owing to the large number of nucleation sites for dislocation loops, most of which were confined within the grain boundaries, the dislocation loops were smaller in size and greater in number density, which were visible in the form of dark spots (Fig. 5). Figure 11 shows a comparison of the size and density of dislocation loops between the coarse-crystalline CVD-SiC and nanocrystalline SiC coating. The size of dislocation loops in the coating was significantly smaller than that in coarse-crystalline CVD-SiC. During low-dose He ion irradiation, the limited He ions cannot support the growth of a large number of dislocation nuclei, and many dislocation nuclei failed to form visible defects. Therefore, the number density of dislocation loops was similar to that in coarse-crystalline CVD-SiC. As the irradiation dose increased, the number of dislocation loops in the coating increased significantly. The differences in the defect evolution of the two types of SiC are shown in Fig. 12.

In addition to increasing the number of dislocation nuclei at the grain boundaries and suppressing the generation of dislocations in the lattice, the abundant grain boundaries in the coating also impede the movement of dislocations. As discontinuous planes in the crystal structure, grain boundaries disrupt the continuity of the crystal, increasing the energy required for the movement of dislocation loops. The more stable the grain boundaries are, the more difficult it is for dislocations to move and expand over long distances, thus making it difficult for dislocation loops to grow [57]. Moreover, there may be various stress concentrations and stress gradients at grain boundaries. This complex stress state may cause large dislocation loops to decompose into smaller units or recombine through interactions with other dislocations at grain boundaries. Moreover, some He atoms captured by the grain boundaries may hinder the extension of dislocations along their paths. These phenomena account for the smaller size of dislocation loops in the nanocrystalline SiC coating.

#### 4.1.2 Stress

There were significant differences in the changes in the Raman spectral peaks between CVD-SiC and nanocrystalline SiC coatings after irradiation. The LO peak of CVD-SiC did not shift, and the weakening of the peak intensity approached saturation at an irradiation dose of 3×1016 ions/cm2 [55]. However, in Fig. 7, the LO peak of nanocrystalline SiC shifted to a higher wavenumber at a dose of 1×1016 ions/cm2 and returned to its original position at a dose of 3×1016 ions/cm2, the peak intensity decreased as the irradiation dose increased. As shown in Fig. 13(a), this was due to the stress differences caused by the grain boundaries.

On one hand, during low-dose irradiation, the abundant grain boundary network in the coating preferentially accumulated He atoms, which exerted compressive stress on the lattice. According to Hooke’s law and the principle of Raman scattering, this compressive stress can shorten the bond length and increase the force constant, thereby increasing the wavelength of the Raman peak. The tensile stress generated by defects (such as black spots) in the lattice was insufficient to counteract the compressive stress, so the Raman spectral peak shifted. As the irradiation dose increased, the number of black spots and small dislocation loops in the lattice began to increase, and the interstitial atoms accommodated in the grain boundaries also tended to reach saturation. At this time, the compressive stress and tensile stress reached equilibrium, so the Raman spectral peak returned to its original position. In contrast, CVD-SiC did not have abundant grain boundaries, and the two types of stresses always counteracted each other. On the other hand, although the sizes of black spots and dislocation loops in the SiC coating were relatively small, the gradually increasing density with increasing irradiation dose caused significant damage to the lattice integrity. This reduces the effective lattice vibration modes involved in Raman scattering, so the intensity of the LO peak continuously decreases.

The stress damage in coarse-grained CVD silicon carbide mainly led to the generation of a strain field and long cracks near the stacking faults, while the silicon carbide coating mainly exhibited short cracks, as shown in Fig. 3. The strain field in CVD-SiC was caused by the ultra-high pressure generated by supersaturated He bubbles in stacking faults, which led to the distortion of adjacent close-packed planes. In the coating, most He atoms were captured by the grain boundaries, so this strain field disappeared. However, the accumulation of He atoms at the grain boundaries caused He bubbles to grow continuously. When the size of He bubbles reached a certain critical value, the highly concentrated stress exceeded the fracture strength of SiC, triggering micro-cracks, as shown in Fig. 13(b). The coalescence of adjacent He bubbles or their arrangement along grain boundaries may serve as propagation paths for micro-cracks. This viewpoint is based on the current research findings, and it is still not possible to rule out the possibility of other crack generation mechanisms.

### 4.2 Irradiation hardening of nanocrystalline SiC coating

He bubbles and irradiation defects can act as pinning points to prevent the slip of dislocation lines, which reduces the ability of plastic deformation and increases the hardness of the material. It is an effective way to predict the contribution of He bubbles and other irradiation defects to yield strength increment and hardness increment using dispersion barrier hardening (DBH) model and the Friedel–Kroupa–Hirsch (FKH) model. Among them, the DBH model is aimed at some irradiation defects, which have a significant impediment effect on the dislocation line, while the FKH model is aimed at weak obstacles, such as small bubbles and black spots [58,59]. In this study, the yield strength increment (Δ*σ*) could be predicted by the FKH model (Eq. (1)) [58,59]:

(1) Δσ=18⋅M⋅μ⋅b⋅d⋅N23

where *M* represents the Taylor factor of the material (3.06 for face-centered cubic (FCC) polycrystalline materials) [60]; *μ* represents the shear modulus of the material (217 GPa for 3C-SiC) [61]; *b* represents the Burgers vector of the gliding dislocations (0.308 nm); *N* and *d* represent the number density and size of defects, respectively. Reportedly, the yield strength increment (Δ*σ*) is positively proportional to the hardness increment (Δ*H*) (Eq. (2)):

(2) ΔH≈C⋅Δσ

where *C* represents a constant related to the nature of the material. The contributions of He bubbles (Δ*H*bubbles) and black spots (Δ*H*loops) to the hardening of a material can be predicted by the root sum squares (RSS) model (Eq. (3)):

(3) ΔHtotal=(ΔHbubbles2+ΔHloops2)

As the irradiation dose increased, the yield strength increment of the SiC coating increased, as shown in Fig. 14(a). Equations (2) and (3) clearly indicate that the hardness increment of the SiC coating increased with increasing irradiation dose, which was in agreement with the experimentally obtained results. Figure 14(b) shows the curve of hardness increment experimentally measured for the SiC coating with increasing irradiation dose. According to Liu *et al*. [34], there is a power function equation between the hardness increment (Δ*H*) and the He ion irradiation dose (*Φ*) (Eq. (4)):

where *Φ* is the fluence of He ions, and the coefficient *A* and exponent *m* are constants to be obtained through data fitting. The curve of the hardness increment with increasing irradiation dose was fitted using Eq. (4), and the results are shown as the red dashed line in Fig. 14. The nanocrystalline SiC coating clearly has better resistance to irradiation hardening. This is primarily because nanocrystalline SiC reduces the concentration of He clusters within the lattice, thereby weakening the uniform hindrance exerted by the lattice on dislocations. Second, dislocations need greater stress to bypass or cut through the large-sized dislocation loops in coarse-crystalline CVD-SiC. In contrast, the black spots and small dislocation loops in the nanocrystalline SiC coating impose a relatively minor hindering effect on the dislocations. This is reflected in several aspects. First, the movement of dislocations may directly cut through small dislocation loops instead of bypassing them, which requires less energy. In particular, when the Burgers vector of the dislocation loop matches that of the moving dislocation, the hindrance effect is even lower. Second, small dislocation loops and black spots have low stability. Under the action of stress, they are easily “absorbed” or “pushed away” by moving dislocations or grain boundaries and thus do not act as stable obstacles. Third, the local distortion caused by small dislocation loops is slight, and their long-range stress field is weak, resulting in a limited repulsive effect on moving dislocations. Moreover, the abundant grain boundaries in nanocrystalline SiC can release stress via mechanisms such as grain boundary sliding, facilitating the passage of dislocations.

## 5 Conclusions

In this study, the irradiation damage behavior of nanocrystalline SiC coatings irradiated with 500 keV He ions at 1×1016, 3×1016, and 5×1016 ions/cm2 at 800 °C was investigated by TEM, Raman, and nanoindentation. The key findings are summarized below:

In the nanocrystalline SiC coating, high-density grain boundaries and stacking faults formed a GB–SF network that captured He atoms. This promoted the formation of large He bubbles and CGD-type platelets in the network, reducing the He-atom concentration in the lattice. The decrease in He-vacancy structures in the lattice not only inhibits the nucleation of He bubbles within the lattice but also enables the recombination of lattice vacancies and self-interstitial atoms at high temperatures, suppressing the nucleation and growth of dislocations in the lattice. Moreover, the local aggregation of interstitial atoms at the grain boundaries generates stress, leading to extensive dislocation nucleation at the grain boundaries. However, owing to the obstruction of dislocation movement by the grain boundaries and the lack of dislocation nuclei in the lattice, dislocation growth is restricted, manifested as black spot damage and small dislocation loops. Compared with the large dislocation loops induced by irradiation in coarse-crystalline CVD-SiC, these defects impose less hindrance on dislocation movement, reducing the irradiation-induced hardening of nanocrystalline SiC. This He-trapping network also results in the compressive stress generated by grain-boundary defects being greater than the tensile stress caused by lattice defects during low-dose irradiation. As the dose increases, the two types of stress reach equilibrium. Since converge of most He atoms at the grain boundaries, no strain field is generated near the SFs because of the overpressure of He bubbles. However, stress concentrations at the grain boundaries may lead to micro-cracks.

In summary, the GB–SF network formed by high-density grain boundaries in the nanocrystalline SiC coating gives rise to irradiation defects predominantly comprising large He bubbles and black spots at the grain boundaries. This not only enhances the resistance of the coating to irradiation hardening but also might increase the defect density and induce a small number of micro-cracks. This research significantly contributes to optimizing the in-reactor service performance of SiC via strategies of grain boundary regulation.
