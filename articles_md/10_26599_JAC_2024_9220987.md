## 1 Introduction

The ideal suprowear-resistant materials should be “self-adaptive” in response to changes in working conditions and the service environment to meet the complex and variable service requirements of high-end mechanical equipment [1,2]. Only a few materials have exhibited ultralow wear performance in specific environments, such as molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) and hydrogenated diamond-like carbon films (H-DLCs) in vacuum environments and GaN films in nitrogen-dried environments [1,3−6]. However, these materials do not consistently maintain ultralow wear in diverse environments (temperature, humidity, medium, etc.) or harsh conditions (high load, high speed, long cycle, etc.) [1,3,7].

Humidity is a common and important factor that affects friction and wear under various environmental conditions. Surface water adsorption and interfacial water condensation are natural occurrences that can modify the surface characteristics and chemical reactivity of materials, consequently influencing their friction and wear attributes directly [8]. This leads to the failure of most ultralow wear materials when they are exposed to a water vapor atmosphere. For example, the wear mechanism of GaN changes from adhesive wear to oxidative wear in humid air environments, leading to the formation of a fragile tribo-oxide layer on the surface, resulting in a two-order of magnitude increase in the wear rate [9]. H-DLC and MoS2 films depend on an interlayer slip mechanism to exhibit superlubricating and ultralow wear properties in a vacuum environment. In the case of H-DLC, the increase in hydrogen bonding exacerbates the interaction force at the sliding interface when water molecules replace hydrogen atoms to bond with surface carbon atoms [1,10−12]. For MoS2, water molecules accumulate at active sites around grain boundaries or edges and react with MoS2 molecules driven by friction, causing oxidation and destruction of the original structure [13,14].

Ultralow wear behavior is not an inherent material property but rather a system property that involves inherent material properties, operating conditions, and environmental factors [7]. This is because the physical and chemical properties of wear surfaces are altered by friction forces, frictional heat, and the adsorption of environmental substances during the friction process [1,15]. Therefore, achieving self-adaptive wear-resistant materials under moist conditions requires maintaining the stability of the material’s inherent properties or eliciting a positive friction response. Water molecules positively impact the tribological performance of ceramic materials through tribochemical reactions with the friction surface, leading to alterations in the microstructure and composition of the contact interface. On the one hand, friction induces tribo-chemical reactions between water molecules and the friction surface to form new substances, changing the microstructure and composition of the contact interface. On the other hand, water molecules adsorb and infiltrate the material’s surface, providing van der Waals (vdW) forces that shear at the friction interface. Silicide ceramic (Si3N4 (SiC)), oxide ceramic (Al2O3), and boride ceramic (B4C, h-BN) form oxide tribo-layers and colloidal lubricating films through tribo-chemical reactions in high humidity environments [16−20]. However, constrained by corrosive wear and the mechanical properties of frictional pairs, the wear resistance of traditional ceramic can reach 1×10−7 mm3·N−1·m−1 orders of magnitude [21−24].

Inspired by the formation of a lubricating layer through the tribo-chemical reaction of ceramic in water and the Archard theory of a high hardness matrix phase to inhibit brittle fracture and plastic deformation, we designed and prepared a WB4–B ceramic to achieve excellent wear-resistant properties at high moisture contents [25−31]. WB4 ceramic is a new type of super-hard material with ultrahigh hardness (46 GPa on the Vickers scale) and the highest percentage of boron [23,24]. It not only resists material deformation and cracking due to frictional stresses but also has the greatest potential for lubrication by B, generating large amounts of B2O3 or H3BO3 on the friction surface [18,19]. In this work, a WB4–B super-hard bulk ceramic was prepared via spark plasma sintering, and its wear resistance under harsh conditions, such as high loads and long cycles, was investigated. The microstructural evolution, tribo-chemistry, and self-adaptive wear mechanism of WB4–B ceramic in dry‒wet environments were characterized and explored. The results indicate that the wear rate of the WB4‒B super-hard bulk ceramic is approximately 1×10−8 mm3·N−1·m−1, demonstrating its superiority over traditional ceramic materials.

### 2.1 Preparation of materials

WB4–B samples were created through a powder metallurgy technique with an atomic percentage of 10 at% W–90 at% B. The sample preparation process for WB4–B is split into two stages: mechanical ball milling of the powder and the sintering process of the bulk. The homogeneous powder of elemental tungsten and boron was milled in a planetary high-energy ball (Pulverizette 5-type, Fritsch, Germany) milling for 4 h at 250 r·min−1 and a ball‒powder ratio of 4:1. The mixed powder was then encapsulated in a graphite mold with a diameter of 25 mm, the heating rate of the device was set at 100 °C·min−1, 30 MPa pressure was applied at 1550–1600 °C, and the mixture was held isothermally for 2 min in a spark plasma sintering furnace (LABOX-3010KF, NJS Co., Ltd., Japan). The instrumental parameters for SPS sintering were 16 min of stabilized elevated current up to 3000 A, which was then maintained at this current for 2 min before disconnection. The phases and structures of the sintered samples were characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Empyrean, PANalytical B.V. Netherlands), a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-5600LV, JEOL, Japan), and an electron probe microanalysis (EPMA-1600, Shimadzu, Japan).

The tribological properties are expressed as the friction coefficient recorded by the friction tester and the wear rate. The calculation formula for the wear rate of WB4–B disk and WC–Co ball is given by *k* = *V*/*FS*, where *V* represents the wear volume of the worn surface, *F* represents the applied load during friction experiments, and *S* represents the sliding distance. ### 2.3 Material characterizations

The Vickers microindentation hardness of WB4–B sintered samples was determined via a microhardness tester (MH-5-VM, Everone, China). The elastic modulus of the materials was characterized via a nanoindentation tester (NHT2, Anton Paar, Austria). The fracture toughness of the materials was tested via the single-sided precracked beam method with a sample size of 16 mm × 4 mm × 3 mm and an opening depth of 2 mm. The density of the sintered bulk was tested via a true density meter (Accupyc II) with high-purity helium gas. The prepared WB4–B super-hard ceramic microstructure was characterized via an electron backscatter diffractometer (EBSD; EDAX-TSL, USA). The worn surface morphology and elemental distribution were analyzed by an SEM (JSM-5601LV, JEOL, Japan). The material composition of the worn surface was analyzed via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA). The frictional cross-sectional structure and composition of the wear track were analyzed via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200X, FEI, USA).

### 3.1 Material microstructure and mechanical properties

The image quality (IQ) map depicted in Fig. 1(a) illustrates the visual representation of the microstructure of the WB4–B ceramic, demonstrating the dual-phase composition of the materials. EDS spectra in Fig. S1(a) in the ESM show dispersed boron-enriched and tungsten-enriched regions. In this region, the proportion of WB4 phase is 83.7%, and the proportion of the B phase is 16.3%. The orientations of WB4 and B grains in Fig. 1(b) are randomly distributed. The grain boundaries of WB4 grains are mainly large-angle grain boundaries with an average grain size of 3.20 μm (Fig. 2(d)), whereas the grain boundaries of the B grains are mainly large-angle grain boundaries with an average grain size of 1.14 µm (Fig. 2(e)).

The density, microhardness, and fracture toughness were tested to evaluate the mechanical properties of WB4–B ceramic, and the results are presented in Table 1. The Vickers microhardness of WB4–B sample at a load of 0.5 N is 43.91±4.82 GPa, and at a high load (10 N), the convergence hardness is 27.72±0.65 GPa. This phenomenon of decreasing Vickers hardness values with increasing load is known as the indentation size effect. This is because small indentations caused by low loads are strongly influenced by grain boundaries, dislocations, and other hardness strengthening mechanisms in the materials [32]. This phenomenon is prevalent in high-hardness materials; generally, materials with a 50 g load hardness greater than 40 GPa can be called super-hard materials.

The true density of the composite materials is 6.20±0.15 g·cm−3. The modulus of elasticity of the WB4–B ceramic measured via the nanoindentation method is 599.17±29.46 GPa; the fracture toughness of WB4–B ceramic measured via the one-sided precracked beam method is 3.37±0.87 MPa·m1/2. The currently reported low load hardness of WB4–B ranges from 43.3 to 46.2 GPa, the convergent hardness from 28.1 to 31.8 GPa, and the elastic modulus of 553 GPa [23,24]. In contrast, the hardness and elastic modulus of the experimentally prepared WB4–B are similar to the values reported in the literature. As a result, the as-prepared WB4–B super-hard ceramic has excellent compressive and shear resistance compared with conventional ceramic [33−37]. Owing to the high mechanical properties of WB4–B, we chose WC-based ceramic materials as the counterpart materials, which also have high hardness and high modulus [38].

### 3.2 Tribological properties

To evaluate the tribological properties of WB4–B ceramic under harsh operating conditions, we conducted wear testing of WB4–B disk sliding against WC–Co ball at various environmental relative humidities, contact stresses, sliding speeds, and friction cycles, as depicted in Fig. 2. The friction coefficient of WB4–B ceramic in Fig. 2(a) decreases with increasing relative humidity from 0.42 in a dry environment to 0.14 in a high-humidity environment, which is a decrease of 0.64 in the coefficient of friction. WB4–B ceramic demonstrates a high friction coefficient of 0.42 under dry conditions at 20% RH. The friction coefficients decrease to 0.30 and 0.27 at relative humidities of 40%–60% in the normal air environment. In moist environments with an relative humidities of approximately 100%, the friction coefficient is low at 0.14. The wear rate of WB4–B ceramic decreases with increasing relative humidity and is ultralow under a moist atmosphere. In a relatively dry environment of 20% RH, the wear rate is 1.58×10−7 mm3·N−1·m−1. Under normal air conditions with RH values of 40%–60%, the wear rate of WB4–B ceramic decreases to 1.33×10−7 mm3·N−1·m−1. When the relative humidity increases to 80%–100% in a moist atmosphere, the wear rate of WB4–B ceramic further decreases to 7.68×10−8 mm3·N−1·m−1. These findings indicate that the humidity level in the surroundings greatly impacts the tribological characteristics of WB4–B ceramic, leading to a reduction of over 50% in both friction coefficients and wear rates. The improved tribological performance of WB4‒B ceramic in air and moisture environments compared with that in dry environments indicates that the water molecules undergo friction-friendly transformation on the worn surface. Moreover, the high wear resistance of WB4–B ceramic in dry environments, normal air, and moist environments demonstrates its multienvironmental adaptability.

As shown in Fig. 2(b), the Hertzian stresses of the WB4–B/WC–Co tribo-pair are 1.77, 2.23, and 2.81 GPa for different applied loads of 2.5, 5, and 10 N, respectively. For example, at a Hertzian stress of 1.77 GPa, the friction coefficient is 0.28, and the wear rate is 1.72×10−7 mm3·N−1·m−1. The friction coefficient is 0.15, whereas the wear rate is 7.73×10−8 mm3·N−1·m−1 at a Hertzian contact stress of 2.81 GPa. As the contact stress increases from 1.77 to 2.35 GPa, the friction coefficient decreases remarkably from 0.28 to 0.14. When the friction contact stress increases further from 2.35 to 2.81, the friction coefficient remains relatively stable, only marginally changing from 0.14 to 0.15. The wear rate has the same trend with increasing load, first decreasing and then leveling off. Notably, the Hertz contact stress in this work is considerably greater than that of other ultralow wear materials, suggesting that WB4–B super-hard ceramic has a significantly greater load‒carrying capacity than conventional ultralow wear materials. When the Hertzian contact stress reaches 2.81 GPa, the coefficient of friction decreases to 0.15, accompanied by an increase in the wear rate to 7.73×10−8 mm³·N−1·m−1.

At 100% relative humidity, the friction coefficient increases as the sliding speed increases, whereas the wear rate decreases as the sliding speed increases (Fig. 2(c)). At a sliding speed of 0.1 m·s−1, the friction coefficient is 0.13, and the wear rate is 1.27×10−7 mm3·N−1·m−1; when the sliding speed increases to 0.4 m·s−1, the friction coefficient is 0.17, and the wear rate is 7.97×10−8 mm3·N−1·m−1. Overall, the change in friction speed has little effect on the friction coefficient and wear rate of WB4–B ceramic. Figure 2(d) shows the variation in the friction coefficients and wear rates of WB4–B/WC–Co tribo-pair with the number of friction cycles. The maximum frictional cycle reaches 1×105 since it is a necessary parameter for evaluating ultralow-wear materials. The friction coefficient of WB4–B/WC–Co tribo-pair gradually increases from 0.11 to 0.19 with an increasing number of friction cycles. In the case of 1.25×104 friction cycles, the wear rates of WB4–B/WC–Co tribo-pair are 1.27×10−7 mm3·N−1·m−1 for WB4–B disk and 3.93×10−9 mm3·N−1·m−1 for WC–Co ball. After 5×104 friction cycles, the wear rates are 7.68×10−8 mm3·N−1·m−1 for WB4–B disk and 3.29×10−9 mm3·N−1·m−1 for WC–Co ball. During long-term wear with 1×105 friction cycles, the wear rates of WB4–B/WC–Co tribo-pair are 9.50×10−8 and 3.93×10−9 mm3·N−1·m−1, respectively.

Figure 3 displays the three-dimensional morphology and two-dimensional profile diagram of the worn track of WB4–B ceramic under different relative humidity conditions. As the relative humidity increases, the width and depth of the worn track gradually decrease. At 20% RH, the worn track is at a maximum width of 195 μm and a depth of 370 nm, as shown in Fig. 3(a). At 100% RH, the smallest worn track measures a width of 130 μm and a depth of 250 nm, as shown in Fig. 3(d). The depth of the worn track decreases to two-thirds as the relative humidity increases from 20% to 100%. The worn track becomes relatively smooth and shallow with increasing relative humidity.

Since the use of water as a lubricant is a proficient method to reduce the friction and wear of ceramic, we control the formation of lubricating films by regulating water molecules, thereby attaining customizable tribological characteristics. On the basis of the above tribological results, we conclude that WB4–B exhibits “self-adaptive wear” in complex environments and harsh operating conditions. Under specific conditions of high humidity, high load, high speed, and long cycles, WB4–B/WC–Co tribo-pair has a low friction performance of approximately 0.1 and an ultra-low wear characteristic of 1×10−8 and 1×10−9 mm3·N−1·m−1.

### 3.3 Wear mechanism

Figure 4 shows the microstructure and element distributions of the worn surfaces of WB4–B ceramic sliding against WC–Co counterpart under different relative humidity conditions. SEM images of worn surface of WB4–B ceramic indicate that the morphological structure remains smooth and does not undergo destructive evolution compared with the unworn surface. The carbon and oxygen element distribution maps in Figs. 4(a)–4(d) demonstrate that there is no significant accumulation of oxides or WC–Co debris on the worn surface of WB4–B ceramic. In Fig. 4(e), the oxides accumulate on the edge of the worn track of WB4–B. Localized fragmentation resulting from brittle fracture is intermittently observed on the wear surface depicted in Figs. 4(a)–4(d), but the dimensions are relatively minor. The worn scars of the WC–Co balls in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b) are relatively rough, with a brittle fracture morphology. The worn surfaces of WC–Co balls in Figs. 4(c) and 4(d) are relatively smooth, brittle fracture is reduced, and oxide enrichment occurs around the worn scar. Combined with the data in Fig. S2 in the ESM, WC–Co ball-worn scar becomes smooth and oxide-enriched with increasing humidity. Moreover, the cracked morphology of the oxide indicates that the oxide adsorbs water molecules under high-humidity conditions.

The above results show that the wear mechanism of WB4–B ceramic is abrasive wear in dry environments and oxidative wear in high-humidity environments. With increasing humidity, the amount of oxide with adsorbed water molecules at the wear interface increases. This can explain the decrease in the friction coefficient and wear rate with increasing humidity, and the oxides with adsorbed water molecules form a low-shear lubrication film on the worn surface. The impact of water molecules on the tribological characteristics of WB4–B ceramic involves both physical adsorption lubrication and enhancement of tribo-chemistry. Thus, the in* *situ* lubricating film generated by the tribochemical reaction is a key factor in reducing the wear rate and friction coefficient of WB4–B ceramic by altering the wear mechanism.

XPS survey scan in Fig. 5 shows the XPS spectra of the worn surface of WB4–B ceramic under different relative humidity conditions. The elemental carbon adsorption peak is calibrated to 284.8 eV, as shown in Fig. 5(d), thereby analyzing the element bonding type on each worn surface. First, Fig. 5(a) displays the B 1s spectra at 20% RH and 100% RH, where the observed binding energies (BE) of 186.91 eV are associated with boron clusters [39,40]. BE of 186.65–187.89 eV corresponds to W‒B bond of WB4 [39,41,42]. At 100% RH, the peaks at 188.90 and 192.36 eV are attributed to B–O bonds of B2O3 and B*x*O [25,39]. B–O bond exhibits peaks ranging from 532.40 to 532.78 eV in O 1s spectrum. Furthermore, Fig. 5(b) shows W 4f spectra with two BE states attributed to the peaks of W 4f7/2 and W 4f5/2. For example, the peaks of W 4f5/2 at 37.88 eV and W 4f7/2 at 35.69 eV are attributed to W‒B bond of WB4 [39]. The peaks of W 4f7/2 at 36.37 eV and W 4f5/2 at 38.50 eV are related to W‒O bond in WO3 [43,44]. W 4f7/2 peak of W–O is recorded at 32.74 eV, whereas W 4f5/2 peak is located at 34.92 eV, both of which are attributed to WO2 [44]. B–O bond displays peaks within the range of 532.40 to 532.78 eV in O 1s spectral analysis. In addition, Fig. 5(c) shows that the worn surface has oxygen or hydroxyl adsorption peaks at 531.38–531.63 eV. The high moisture wear surface adsorption peak at 531.63 indicates that there is a large amount of hydroxyl bonding on the wear surface under these conditions. B 1s, W 4f, and O 1s spectra all show that the bonding energies increase with increasing humidity, which is due to hydroxyl group adsorption. The above XPS results clearly indicate that WO3 is present on the worn surface at 20% RH and that B2O3 and WO3 are present on the worn surface at 100% RH. Importantly, the positions of the peaks for W‒O bond and B‒O bond are significantly shifted to the right at high humidity, suggesting that the bonding of WO3 and B2O3 with water molecules occurs on the worn surface at high humidity to form H3BO3 and WO3·H2O.

The diffraction pattern of the FFT image in the tribolayer region shows a continuous circular pattern, suggesting that the tribolayer consists of an amorphous composition. The elemental distribution map shows that the tribolayer region is enriched by W and O. The element B is not significantly enriched, which is partly due to the large detection error of the lighter elements. By integrating the analysis of Fig. 6 with the corresponding data in Fig. 5, it is established that the unoxidized, fragmented, and irregular WB4–B substrate grains on the worn surface under high humidity conditions are expelled from the friction interface. The tribolayer, composed entirely of amorphous oxides, exhibits superior lubrication characteristics and mitigates the issues of stress concentration and abrasive failure induced by irregular hard particles at the friction interface. In summary, the worn surface structure in dry environments is dominated by WB4–B substrate materials, in addition to an oxide layer approximately 10 nm thick on the worn surface. In a humid environment, the worn surface structure consists of a tribo-layer with a WB4–B substrate, and the tribo-layers are composed of H3BO3, WO3, and adsorbed water molecules.

The above tribological results and wear characterization show that WB4–B ceramic exhibits good tribological performance in complex environments and harsh operating conditions, but their wear mechanisms are not the same in different environments, as shown in Fig. 7. In dry environments, there is a tribolayer consisting of WB4/WO3 with a stabilized matrix structure that allows WB4–B ceramic to exhibit wear resistance. The small-scale lattice distortions on the worn surface indicate that strong B–B and W–B bonds maintain lattice stability and inhibit grain deformation. The lattice deformation and nanoscale broken grains at the worn surface promote the oxidation reaction, which leads to the generation of oxides on the surface. The tribolayer, consisting of a small amount of oxide and substrate debris, is only 10 nanometers thick but still provides a certain lubrication and antiwear effect. The dislocations arranged on the subsurface have frictional energy absorption and hardening effects. Consequently, WB4–B maintains a stable microstructure, leading to favorable tribological characteristics in dry environments, as the tribolayer and subsurface dislocations reduce grain fragmentation on a large scale.

Compared with dry environments, a better lubricated tribolayer is formed on the wear surface in moist environments. The friction layer consisting of amorphous H3BO3, WO3, and adsorbed water molecules provides lubrication by means of van der Waals shear. At the same time, a thicker lubrication film better disperses the friction stresses and isolates the tribo-pair from direct contact, which results in lower wear. In dry environments, a friction layer that is too thin (~10 nm) is not effective at dispersing the stresses and isolating the upper and lower samples from direct contact. With increasing humidity, the composition of the friction layer gradually changes from WB4/WO3 to H3BO3/WO3 and adsorbed water molecules, and the thickness of the friction layer increases from ~10 to ~100 nm. Consequently, the better lubrication and increased thickness of the tribo-layer provide WB4–B with superior tribological performance under humid conditions.

The main possible tribo-oxidation reaction equations are shown in Eqs. (1)–(3):

(1) 2WB4+9O2=2WO3+4B2O3

(2) 4B+3O2=2B2O3

(3) B2O3+3H2O=2H3BO3

The WB4–B/WC–Co tribo-pair exhibits ultralow wear performance under high Hertzian contact stress, indicating that the lubricating film at the friction interface and subsurface substrate remains structurally stable under high stress. This is attributed to the high compressive (43.91 GPa) and shear strengths (599 GPa) of WB4–B ceramic, which effectively inhibits the generation and growth of microcracks caused by fatigue wear on worn surfaces and in the subsurface. These characteristics contribute to the high load-bearing capacity of WB4–B, such as the 2.81 GPa contact stress, which is the highest contact stress known for superwear-resistant materials. For example, Pt–Au film/Al2O3 (1.1 GPa) tribo-pair, MoS2 film/440 C steel (1.1 GPa) tribo-pair, and self-matched H-DLC film (0.7 GPa) tribo-pair are subject to this condition [6,45,46]. According to the classical Greenwood and Williamson model, materials with high hardness and high modulus of elasticity can reduce contact areas and demonstrate high wear resistance under the same contact pressure [3]. For example, gradient nanostructured Cu alloys with substantially higher hardness values display low friction coefficients and wear rates [47].

Under the severe experimental conditions characterized by moisture environments, WB4–B ceramic continues to exhibit ultralow wear and self-lubricating behavior, which primarily relies on the boundary lubrication provided by the lubrication film on the worn surface and the load-bearing effect of the super-hard substrate. Moreover, WB4–B ceramic achieves wear resistance through different wear resistance mechanisms in various environments and working conditions, illustrating the self-adaptive wear behavior of WB4–B ceramic. The wear mechanisms of materials mainly include abrasive wear, adhesive wear, corrosive wear, and fatigue wear. The realization of wear-resistant materials aims to prevent the degree of destruction of each wear mechanism. WB4–B ceramic prevents severe abrasive wear and resists adhesive wear under dry conditions through its super-hard properties and strong chemical bonding. It progressively reduces abrasive wear and oxidative wear via water molecules and oxide lubrication in moist environments to form a structurally stable colloidal lubrication film. Additionally, the material’s super-hard properties and stable in* *situ* lubrication film effectively reduce fatigue wear caused by prolonged friction. Consequently, WB4–B ceramic achieves excellent wear resistance in diverse environments and challenging operating conditions through a self-adaptive wear strategy on the basis of its super-hard properties and *in* *situ* lubrication. WB4–B ceramic achieves a wear resistance of 1×10−8 mm3·N−1·m−1, whereas conventional bulk ceramic materials are currently reported to have wear rates of 1×10−7–1×10−5 mm3·N−1·m−1 [33,35,48,49].

## 4 Conclusions

In conclusion, WB4–B ceramic, constructed on the basis of a self-adaptive wear strategy, is an ultralow-wear material characterized by its adaptability to various challenging working conditions. This material has a low friction coefficient of approximately 0.1 and an ultralow wear rate of 1×10−8 mm3·N−1·m−1 under high humidity, high contact stress, and prolonged friction cycle scenarios. These properties are the result of its high hardness and the presence of an *in* *situ* lubrication layer. The super-hard WB4–B ceramic effectively prevents surface fractures caused by friction stress and forms a lubricating layer with water molecules featuring a van der Waals force structure with low friction. The wear resistance remains relatively stable at 1×10−8–1×10−7 mm3·N−1·m−1 under changing environments and harsh operating conditions, making it suitable for high-end machinery and equipment in complex and dynamic settings.
