## 1 Introduction

Boride ceramics, renowned for their exceptional performance, span a spectrum from single-phase to composite and high-entropy formulations, serving as essential components in a diverse array of industries [1−10]. Among these materials, lanthanum hexaboride (LaB6) has been of interest for decades owing to its distinctive properties: a low electron work function, high emission current density, remarkable chemical resistance, and minimal evaporation at high temperatures [11]. These attributes distinguish LaB6 from other binary rare-earth hexaborides. Studies have demonstrated that LaB6 outperforms traditional tungsten filaments as thermionic electron cathodes [12]. As vacuum electronics advance, the need for improved machinability of cathode materials becomes increasingly important, especially with the demand for smaller cathode sizes and larger cathode surface areas. However, the inherent brittleness of LaB6 ceramics, both in single-crystal and coarse-grained forms, significantly impedes their manufacture and application [13−15].

The incorporation of transition metal diborides (TMB2) into the LaB6 matrix to form LaB6–TMB2 composites can enhance the mechanical properties of the cathode materials while preserving their thermal field emission capacities [16−19]. Specifically, a 79 : 21 weight ratio of LaB6 to TMB2 induces eutectic formation, which significantly reduces the sintering temperature and results in ceramics with optimal hardness and fracture toughness. ZrB2 is renowned for its exceptional high-temperature stability and favorable mechanical properties, which include high hardness, superior fracture toughness, and stability under extreme thermal conditions. These attributes make it an ideal material candidate for demanding environments and high-temperature applications. The LaB6–ZrB2 eutectic composite is distinguished by its ideal balance of mechanical properties and thermal field emission characteristics [16,20−23]. A previous study using the floating zone method for synthesizing a LaB6(001)–ZrB2 eutectic composite via directional solidification achieved Vickers hardness and fracture toughness values of 21.7–22.6 GPa and 3.5–11.1 MPa·m1/2, respectively [24]. Despite their superior Vickers hardness and fracture toughness, directionally solidified composites face challenges due to their inherent anisotropy. Polycrystalline materials prepared by powder sintering generally do not exhibit anisotropy. Hot-pressed sintered polycrystalline LaB6–ZrB2 composites made from raw materials with an average grain size of 25 μm have a Vickers hardness of 19.2 GPa, a flexural strength of 330.0 MPa, and a fracture toughness of 3.7 MPa·m1/2 [25]. Reducing the grain size of ceramic materials to the nanometer scale can significantly improve their mechanical performance, representing an efficient strategy for enhancing and modifying material engineering [26−30]. Nanocrystalline LaB6 bulk materials have been shown to combine high emission current density with good comprehensive mechanical properties [31]. Therefore, the development of nano- or ultrafine-grained LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramics and their composites is of significant interest to the scientific community. However, research on the sintering of polycrystalline LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramics with finer grains is lacking.

In this work, ultrafine-grained LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramics were successfully prepared via a high-pressure sintering technique with self-synthesized LaB6 nanopowder and commercial ZrB2 nanopowder as precursors. The high pressure facilitates not only particle rearrangement and bonding but also plastic deformation at the contact points between individual grains. Additionally, high pressure can significantly decrease the sintering temperature and decelerate the grain boundary movement rate, thereby restricting grain growth and enabling the production of nanocrystalline ceramic bulk with small fine grains. To select high-pressure conditions, the influence of the sintering temperature on the microstructure and mechanical properties was studied in detail. The bulks fabricated with optimal mechanical performance were evaluated for their thermal emission performance. The ultrafine-grained LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramics demonstrated remarkable mechanical and thermal emission properties, rendering them suitable for potential applications in thermal emission cathodes.

## 2 Experimental

LaB6 nanopowders were synthesized via the solid-state reduction method. La2O3 powder (99.99% purity, 1–3 μm; Qinhuangdao ENO High-Tech Material Development Co., Ltd., China) served as the metal precursor, whereas NaBH4 powder (99.99% purity; InnoChem Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) acted as both the reductant and the boron source. La2O3 and NaBH4 were mechanically mixed in an agate mortar at a molar ratio of 1 : 27 and then consolidated into cylindrical pellets. These pellets, wrapped in graphite paper, were heated to 1373 K in an argon atmosphere within an alumina crucible. After cooling to room temperature, the product was washed, dried, and then mixed with commercial ZrB2 powder (99% purity, 100 nm; Shanghai Adamas-beta Reagent Co., Ltd., China) in a eutectic weight ratio. The mixture was stirred magnetically to form a LaB6–ZrB2 composite nanopowder. To remove surface B2O3 and other impurities, the LaB6–ZrB2 composite nanopowder was subjected to vacuum heat treatment within a graphite mold. Heating was initiated once the vacuum reached 10−4 Pa, with a treatment temperature of 1073 K. After the setting temperature was reached, a pressure of 200 N was applied and maintained for at least 2 h. The system was then cooled, and the pressure was released before the samples were extracted. The treated powders were compacted into pellets, encased in a hexagonal boron nitride capsule, and subjected to high-pressure sintering in a six-anvil press (Guilin Guiye Machinery Co., Ltd., China) at 4 GPa, followed by cooling to ambient temperature.

Characterization of the LaB6 nanopowder and resulting ultrafine-grained LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramics was performed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; DMAX2500/P, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5406 Å), scanning electron microscope (SEM; Verios G4 UC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) coupled with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy system (EDS; Ultim Extreme, Oxford Instruments, UK), and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200X, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. TEM samples were prepared via a gallium-focused ion beam (FIB) from a Scios Dual Beam instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with an accelerating voltage of 30 kV and milled from bulk samples. The pieces were initially cut from the bulk samples via a 30 nA ion beam current, followed by sequential reduction to 7, 5, 3, 1, 0.5, and finally 0.1 nA to further refine the samples into electron-transparent slices thinner than 100 nm. Vickers hardness measurements were performed on the as-fabricated samples via hardness testing instruments (KB-30-BVZ, KB Prüftechnik GmbH, Germany), with loads ranging from 10 to 1000 g applied for 10 s on the polished surfaces. The fracture toughness (*K*Ic) of the ceramics was calculated via the indentation method and Eq. (1) [32]:

(1) KIc=7.42×10−2Fl1.5

where *F* represents the applied load, and *l* denotes the radial crack length from the indentation center. Nanoindentation tests were conducted via a Berkovich diamond indenter on a nanoindenter tester (Nanoindenter G200, KLA Tencor, USA). The nanohardness and elastic modulus were determined by Oliver–Phar methods [33]. The thermal emission current was ascertained via a custom-engineered apparatus operated under high vacuum conditions exceeding 5×10−5 Pa. The distance between the anode and cathode was maintained at 0.85 mm. The sample with the best mechanical properties was selected to test the thermal electron emission properties under different operating temperatures and voltages. A rectangular sample (3 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm) was polished and cleaned ultrasonically with ethanol, and its pulse emission current was measured at temperatures ranging from 1473 to 1773 K. The thermionic emission current density was derived from a pulse width of 20 s and a repetition frequency set at 120 Hz. Temperature regulation was achieved with a disappearing filament optical pyrometer (WGG2-201 N, SAIL, China), ensuring a precision of ±2%.

We conducted X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements on the LaB6–ZrB2 nanopowder and the samples sintered at 4 GPa and various sintering temperatures, as depicted in Fig. 3(a). The results confirm that all the samples consisted of LaB6 and ZrB2. At these pressures and sintering temperatures, no phase transformations or reactions occur. The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the samples sintered at temperatures between 1273 and 1673 K remains nearly identical to that of the raw material, demonstrating that the presence of high pressure effectively suppresses grain growth. However, upon reaching a sintering temperature of 1873 K, the FWHM decreases, indicating a sudden and significant increase in the grain size. The trend of the relative density as a function of the sintering temperature is illustrated in Fig. S2 in the ESM. With increasing the sintering temperature, the relative density steadily increases, reaching its peak at 1673 K. As the sintering temperature continues to increase, there is no significant increase in density. Figure 3(b) shows fracture images of the LaB6–ZrB2 ceramics prepared at various sintering temperatures. As the sintering temperature increased, the grain size increased. The sample sintered at 1273 K has smooth particle surfaces and clear grain boundaries, indicating weak particle bonding and a poor sintering state. At 1473 K, micropores are evident due to grain pullout, and the proportion of smooth crystal surfaces and grain boundaries decreases, suggesting the beginning of grain boundary fusion. The sample sintered at 1673 K shows little change in grain size and a further decrease in smooth crystal surfaces. At 1873 K, grain growth reaches the microscale, with larger holes from grain pullout than at lower temperatures. Smooth crystal surfaces and well-defined grain boundaries are observed, suggesting that grain growth leads to weaker grain boundary bonding and a poorer sintering state.

The mechanical properties of the LaB6–ZrB2 ceramics prepared at various sintering temperatures were investigated. Figure 4(a) shows the Vickers hardness of the bulk samples as a function of the applied load for different sintering temperatures. As the applied load increased from 0.1 to 1.0 kg, the measured Vickers hardness of the samples decreased, eventually stabilizing at a value that represents the true hardness, which was attributed to overcoming the indentation load size effect. Furthermore, the Vickers hardness initially increases with increasing sintering temperature, peaking at 1673 K, after which it decreases sharply at 1873 K due to abnormal grain growth. Figure 4(b) presents the relationships between the Vickers hardness, fracture toughness, and average grain size in relation to the sintering temperature. Below 1673 K, there is a progressive increase in the grain size, hardness, and fracture toughness, as the benefits of increased sintering outweigh the effects of grain growth. The optimal mechanical properties, with a Vickers hardness of 25.6 GPa and a fracture toughness of 4.1 MPa·m1/2 at 1673 K, are achieved where fine-grain strengthening is most effective. However, at a sintering temperature of 1873 K, grain growth accelerated significantly, resulting in a marked reduction in both the Vickers hardness and fracture toughness. These findings indicate that abnormal grain growth at this temperature undermines the fine-grain strengthening mechanism, which are consistent with the fracture surface analysis depicted in Fig. 3. The ultrafine-grained LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramic with optimal mechanical properties was selected for the nanoindentation test, and the maximum loads ranged from 20 to 80 g. Figure 4(c) shows the standard load‒displacement curve of the as-fabricated bulk in a nanoindentation test, from which mechanical property parameters, including the elastic modulus and hardness, can be derived [35]. The absence of pop-in or pop-out phenomena indicates that no phase transitions or deformations occurred during the compression process. Figure 4(d) shows the changes in the nanohardness and elastic modulus as a function of the applied load. Owing to the size effect, there is a slight decrease in both the hardness and modulus with increasing load. At the maximum load of 80 g, the measured nanohardness and elastic modulus are 28.0 and 338 GPa, respectively. The data from the continuous stiffness measurement (CSM) are presented in Fig. S3 in the ESM, which corroborate the findings essentially with those depicted in Fig. 4(d).

To comprehensively evaluate the mechanical properties of the LaB6–ZrB2 composites, the hardness and fracture toughness data of the LaB6 and LaB6-based composites reported previously are summarized in Table 1. Single-crystal LaB6 has H*v of 22.5 GPa along the (100) crystal plane and 25 GPa along the (001) plane, but its fracture toughness is only 1.8 MPa·m1/2 [38,39]. Compared with single-crystal LaB6, polycrystalline LaB6 ceramics have improved fracture toughness, but their hardness is less than that of single crystals [40,41]. Reducing the particle size of polycrystalline LaB6 ceramics to 150 nm results in a Vickers hardness comparable to that of single-crystal LaB6 [31]. Furthermore, the mechanical properties, especially fracture toughness, of the LaB6 composite ceramics are markedly improved by the introduction of second-phase diborides. Table 1 shows that the fracture toughness of the polycrystalline LaB6 matrix can be increased by 6%–30% upon the addition of 21% TiB2 or ZrB2 as a toughening phase to form a eutectic structure. For single-crystal systems, the improvement is more significant [24,25,42−45]. In this work, we developed ultrafine-grained LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramics, achieving a synergistic enhancement in both hardness and toughness compared with previously reported LaB6–ZrB2 ceramics with larger grain sizes, which is attributed to the combined effects of the second phase and refined grains.

Figure 5(a) shows a typical SEM image of a Vickers indentation initiated by a load of 1 kg with radial cracks in the ultrafine-grained LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramic sintered at 1673 K. As shown in Fig. 5(b), the fracture mode of the ultrafine-grained LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramic is predominantly intergranular fracture. The crack deflection, branching, bridging, and pull-out of the grains are also present, which lead to a magnitude of energy dissipation during crack propagation. As a result, a large amount of energy dissipated during microcrack propagation, and the fracture toughness of the ultrafine-grained LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramic sintered at 1673 K reached 4.1 MPa·m1/2, which was significantly greater than that of the reported LaB6–ZrB2 ceramics with larger grain sizes, as listed in Table 1.

The detailed microstructure of the ultrafine-grained LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramics sintered at 1673 K was further studied via TEM analysis. The STEM image is depicted in Fig. 6(a), which shows that the sample possessed a compact microstructure that involved individual ultrafine crystalline grains. The corresponding EDS compositional maps (Fig. 6(b)) show the elemental distributions of La, Zr, and B, which indicate that the ZrB2 grains are inlaid in the LaB6 matrix. Detailed observations of the microstructures of the nanocrystalline grains and their boundaries were carried out via high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM). As depicted in Fig. 6(e), a compact microstructure comprising ultrafine grains can be clearly observed. A clean grain boundary formed between the grains (Fig. 6(f)), which also demonstrated that the degree of sintering was sufficient. According to the interplanar spacing (Figs. 6(g) and 6(h)), the left grain is LaB6, and the right one is ZrB2. A more detailed local magnification at the grain boundary is shown in Fig. 6(i). The atomically clean grain boundaries and good grain-to-grain bonding can be clearly observed. The high purity of the LaB6 and ZrB2 nanopowders in conjunction with ultrahigh pressure during sintering together led to the formation of this interface between LaB6 and ZrB2, which is one of the main factors for the superior mechanical properties of the LaB6–ZrB2 ceramics.

Ultrafine-grained LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramics sintered at 1673 K with optimal comprehensive mechanical property sintering temperatures were selected to test their field emission properties. The thermionic emission current density and the applied anode voltage (J‒U*) characterization curves were determined for the as-fabricated bulk materials over a temperature range from 1473 to 1773 K, as depicted in Fig. 7(a). The emission current density clearly increases with increasing working temperature or operating voltage. Specifically, at a certain working temperature, a continuous increase in the operating voltage will lead to a greater improvement in the current density. The as-fabricated bulk materials achieved a maximum thermionic emission current density of 11.6 A/cm² at 1473 K, 12.4 A/cm² at 1573 K, 20.5 A/cm² at 1673 K, and 27.5 A/cm² at 1773 K under an applied voltage of 3 kV. The field emission properties of the coarse-grained LaB6 were significantly lower than those of the single crystals. Recent research has shown that reducing the grain size of polycrystalline LaB6 to the nanometer scale results in a qualitative improvement in field emission properties, even comparable to that of the LaB6 single crystal. This indicates an increase in the number of grain boundaries per unit volume also increases the current density, which offers a new avenue for optimizing the field emission performance. When ZrB2 is introduced as a secondary phase into LaB6 substrates via directional solidification synthesis, the field emission performance of the resulting eutectic composite is inherently reduced. In this work, the field emission properties of the ultrafine-grained LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramics surpass those of the coarse-grained LaB6, confirming that grain refinement significantly enhances both the mechanical strength and field emission capabilities. ## 4 Conclusions

The ultrafine-grained LaB6–ZrB2 composite ceramics were produced via a high-pressure sintering technique using self-produced LaB6 nanocrystalline powder and commercial ZrB2 powder. Among the samples obtained at 4 GPa and different sintering temperatures, the as-fabricated bulk sintered at 1673 K exhibited outstanding mechanical properties, with Vickers hardness, fracture toughness and elastic modulus values of 25.6 GPa, 4.1 MPa·m1/2, and 338 GPa, respectively. This is mainly due to the finer-grain strengthening mechanism and secondary phase toughening. The highest emission current density of the as-fabricated bulk material is 27.5 A∙cm−2 at 1773 K and still does not reach saturation, making it a candidate thermal emission cathode with enhanced fracture toughness.
