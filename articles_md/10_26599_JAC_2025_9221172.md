## 1 Introduction

As smart and wireless technologies increase convenience, the demand for microwave-absorbing materials to address the escalating issue of electromagnetic pollution has increased. Currently, the design strategies for microwave absorbing materials can be divided into the following points. One is the design of dielectric polarization through microscopic modulation. Another is the design of the macroscopic gradient structure [1,2]. However, achieving a balance between impedance matching and substantial electromagnetic energy loss remains a significant challenge in designing ultrawideband microwave absorbing materials.

A common research approach is to design efficient microwave absorbing materials by tuning their composition and structure [3,4]. For instance, Li *et al*. [5] developed iron-cobalt/nitrogen-doped carbon aerogels with enhanced microwave absorbing performance by adjusting their components and microscopic morphology. Similarly, Wu *et al*. [6] prepared MXene/C aerogels with a macroscopic gradient structure and arrangement, which is an effective way to achieve broadband microwave absorbing performance [7]. However, these explorations demonstrate that the current adjustment methods for multicomponent and multilayered structures often require numerous time-consuming orthogonal experiments, which results in more demanding requirements for fast electromagnetic design. Consequently, a fast and accurate strategy is needed to address the prevailing challenge of designing and fabricating high-performance microwave absorbing materials [8,9].

Electromagnetic parameters are essential for designing and fabricating high-performance microwave absorbing materials. Precise microwave absorbing materials can be achieved through accurate regulation of these electromagnetic parameters. However, the influences on electromagnetic parameters make up an infinite design space, making filtering and evaluating such an enormous combination manually infeasible. In contrast, machine learning can extract meaningful insights from massive datasets and achieve accurate predictions within this infinite design space [10]. Material design strategies utilizing machine learning have been applied in several fields. Huang *et al*. [11] employed machine learning to accurately predict the phases of high-entropy alloys, thereby establishing a set of parametric phase selection rules. Wei *et al*. [12] utilized machine learning to predict the fatigue strength of steel on the basis of its composition and process conditions, with the resulting predictions demonstrating high reliability. He *et al*. [13] employed machine learning to design electromagnetic shielding materials, thereby reducing the burden of experiments and accelerating the development of new materials. In the field of microwave absorbing materials, Che *et al*. [14] have employed machine learning to investigate the microwave absorbing properties of magnetic materials. This primary work is meaningful for magnetic materials. However, magnetic materials are no longer able to meet the demand for next-generation microwave absorbing materials because of their high density and susceptibility to oxidation and other inherent defects. Moreover, this work focused only on the microwave absorbing performance, which does not address the fundamental issue of electromagnetic parameters. The current research indicates a significant gap in the study of electromagnetic parameters of microwave absorbing materials through machine learning.

In this work, we prepared graphene/SiO2 (GS) and graphene/BaTiO3 (GB) electromagnetic parameter datasets via electrostatic spinning technology and developed a machine learning-based electromagnetic parameter prediction system. The machine learning model was trained and validated by using small sample electromagnetic parameter datasets. The results demonstrated that the predicted and measured values of the electromagnetic parameters were in good agreement, with a maximum accuracy of 97.3%. Using machine learning, we can rapidly generate extensive electromagnetic parameter data by predicting samples under varying process conditions and compositions. These predicted and experimentally obtained parameters were then input into simulation software to design impedance gradient structures. Consequently, a multifunctional microwave absorbing aerogel with ultrabroadband absorbing performance (3.26–17.30 GHz, 20 mm) was swiftly obtained. A series of designed samples were experimentally validated on the basis of the predicted conditions and gradient design schemes. Compared with traditional methods, excellent microwave absorbing materials can be designed and prepared within weeks via machine learning. This research indicates that machine learning strategies can expedite the development of graphene/ceramic fibers and dielectric microwave absorbing materials.

### 2.1 Materials

Tetrabutyl silicate (TEOS) and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China. Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) was obtained from Hefei BASF Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China. Phosphoric acid, acetate, barium acetate, and titanium butoxide were supplied by Aladdin Co., Ltd., China. Ethanol (≥ 99.7%) was purchased from Luoyang Chemical Reagent Factory. All reagents were used as received without further purification.

### 2.2 Synthesis of SiO2 fibers

A 10% mass fraction aqueous solution of PVA was prepared by dissolving PVA powder in deionized water and stirring at 80 °C for 6 h. Tetraethyl orthosilicate, deionized water, and phosphoric acid were mixed at a mass ratio of 1:1: 0.02 and stirred for 4 h at room temperature. The obtained two solutions were mixed at a ratio of 1 : 1 by volume to obtain the precursor solution for electrostatic spinning. The precursor solution was then loaded into a 10 mL syringe for electrostatic spinning at a specific voltage (18 kV), a receiving distance (17 cm) and a continuous advancing rate of 1 mL/h, and the relative humidity was 20% at room temperature [15].

### 2.3 Synthesis of BaTiO3 fibers

In the initial step of the procedure, 1.5 g of PVP was added to 6 mL of anhydrous ethanol. The mixture was stirred at room temperature until the solution became clear and transparent, which took approximately 1 h. Subsequently, 4 mL of anhydrous ethanol was mixed with 4 mL of glacial acetic acid. Then, 2 mL of TEOS was added to the alcohol‒acid mixture while it was stirred for 30 min. Subsequently, 1.5 g of barium acetate powder was combined with 1.5 mL of deionized water, and the resulting mixture was stirred at room temperature. The two solutions were then mixed and stirred until clear. Electrospinning was carried out under the following conditions: a voltage of 15 kV, a receiving distance of 20 cm, and a propulsion rate of 0.8 mL/h. The thickness of the resulting fiber membrane was determined by the spinning time, which was set to 8 h in this study [16].

### 2.4 Synthesis of GS and GB aerogels

The SiO2 and BaTiO3 fibers obtained from the above preparation were first dried under vacuum for 2 h to remove the water and then cut to the size of the abrasive tool for spare parts. The graphene slurry was then applied and impregnated onto the fibers at mass ratios of 1 : 1, 2 : 1, and 4 : 1, with graphene concentrations of 4, 8, and 10 mg/mL, respectively. The impregnated samples were placed in a vacuum chamber under negative pressure for 1 h to ensure full contact between the graphene and fibers. The samples were subsequently freeze-dried and reduced with hydrazine hydrate. The samples with graphene concentrations of 10 mg/mL and 1 : 1, 2 : 1, and 4 : 1 mass ratios with SiO2 fibers were named G10S1, G10S2, and G10S4, respectively. The mass ratio of graphene to SiO2 fibers was 4 : 1, and the samples with graphene concentrations of 4 and 8 mg/mL were named G4S4 and G8S4, respectively. Similarly, the samples with graphene concentrations of 10 mg/mL and 1:1, 2:1, and 4:1 mass ratios with BaTiO3 fibers were named G10B1, G10B2, and G10B4, respectively. Additionally, the samples were subjected to annealing at elevated temperatures under a N2 atmosphere. The nomenclature for the annealed samples is as follows: for example, the annealed G10S4 sample is designated G10S4-*X*, where *X* represents the annealing temperature. For example, G10S4-500, G10S4-550, G10S4-600, G10S4-700, and G10S4-800 denote the G10S4 samples annealed at 500, 550, 600, 700, and 800 °C, respectively. Similarly, G10B4-550 and G10B4-700 denote the G10B4 samples annealed at 550 and 700 °C, respectively.

### 2.5 Characterizations

The phase compositions of the GS and GB aerogel composites were measured via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Cu Kα; D8-Discover, Bruker, Germany). The microscopic morphology of the samples was obtained by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Sigma 500, ZEISS, Germany) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-2100 UHR, JEOL, Japan). The surface components were studied via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA). The degree of graphitization was analyzed via a Raman nanospectrometer (Raman DXR3, Thermo Scientific, USA). The contact angles of the composites were observed via a contact angle analyzer (JC2000DM, Shanghai Zhongchen, China). The thermal conductivity of the aerogels was tested via a thermal analyzer (TPS2500S, Hotdisk, Sweden). The side temperatures of the composite aerogel when heated were photographed via an infrared thermal camera (Fluke Ti480 Pro, Fluke, USA). The acoustic absorption properties of the samples were tested via a standing wave tube (AWA6290Z, Hangzhou Aihua, China). Paraffin was injected into the aerogel, which was then prepared into coaxial rings (outer diameter of the coaxial ring (*ϕ*o): 7.0 mm, inner diameter of the coaxial ring (*ϕ*i): 3.04 mm) via a mold. The electromagnetic parameters of the samples were tested via a vector network analyzer (Ceyear Technologies 3672C-S, China). The detailed setup of the radar cross section (RCS) simulations has been reported in a previous study [17].

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(a) illustrates the fabrication process of graphene-infused electrostatic spinning membranes. SiO2 and BaTiO3 fiber membranes were initially produced through electrospinning, following the experimental procedure. These membranes were then trimmed to fit the mold dimensions. Subsequently, graphene at various concentrations (4, 8, and 10 mg/mL) was impregnated into the electrostatic spinning membranes at mass ratios of 1:1, 2 : 1, and 4 : 1, respectively. Negative pressure was applied under vacuum for 1 h. The impregnated samples were freeze-dried to obtain the final products. Hydrogen bonds facilitated the attachment of graphene to the electrostatic spinning films. The preparation of the multilayer composite microwave absorbing material was subsequently guided by the simulation design and optimization of the microwave absorbing structure [18].

As shown in Figs. S1(a) and S1(b) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), the SiO2 and BaTiO3 fibers are long and straight, with diameters of approximately 500 and 300 nm, respectively. These fibers exhibit relatively smooth surfaces and no impurity cross-links between the fibers. The diameter of the BaTiO3 fibers is smaller than that of the SiO2 fibers. As shown in Fig. S1(c) in the ESM, the as-prepared graphene aerogel has a porous lattice structure, with pore diameters ranging from several microns to tens of microns. These mesopores increase the porosity, reduce the sample density and regulate the impedance of the samples. Figures 1(b)–1(g) display SEM images of the G10S4 and G10B4 aerogels. The overall structure of the composite material is similar to a sandwich structure, consisting of two outer layers of graphene aerogel and a middle fiber layer. At the interface, the fibers are well connected to the graphene sheet layer, forming a heterogeneous interface that enhances the microwave absorbing properties of the sample.

Figure 2(a) presents the XRD patterns of the SiO2 fibers and GS aerogels. The XRD pattern of the SiO2 fibers shows weak broad peaks at 25°–30°, indicating the amorphous phase of the SiO2 fibers. Upon the preparation of graphene as a composite aerogel with SiO2 fibers, only peaks corresponding to the (002) crystal plane of graphene are observed. With increasing annealing temperature, the peak of the (002) crystalline plane of graphene shifts to the right due to the removal of functional groups on its surface, resulting in a small crystalline plane spacing. Compared with that of G10S4-500, the characteristic peak of G10S4-600 shifted to the left. This shift occurred because the amorphous carbon residue from the decomposed PVA in the SiO2 fiber membrane at 500 °C imposed compressive stress on the reduced graphene oxide layers. When the temperature increased to 600 °C, the PVA fully decomposed, eliminating the amorphous carbon and releasing the previously compressed residual stress. This results in increased interlayer spacing, causing the characteristic peak to shift to the left. The characteristic peaks of BaTiO3 fibers located at 32°, 38°, and 46° correspond to the (110), (111), and (002) crystal planes, respectively (Fig. 2(b)). Similar to those of the GS aerogel, in the XRD pattern of the GB aerogels, only the characteristic peaks of graphene are found and shift to the right with increasing temperature [19]. Figure 2(c) shows the Raman spectra of the G10S4 and G10B4 aerogels before and after high-temperature annealing. The two peaks corresponding to D and G are located at 1300 and 1600 cm−1, respectively. In general, the intensity ratio (I*D/*I*G) of these two peaks represents the degree of carbonation; a low *I*D/*I*G value indicates high graphitization and low defect density. The *I*D/*I*G value decreases with increasing temperature, which indicates high graphitization after high-temperature treatment. After high-temperature annealing, the *I*D/*I*G ratio of G10S4-700 decreased from 1.46 to 1.11 compared with that of G10S4. This reduction is attributed to the decomposition of residual oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface of reduced graphene oxide upon energy absorption, effectively removing these groups. Consequently, the graphitization degree increased, significantly enhancing the electrical conductivity. Moreover, barium titanate fibers undergo crystallization during the 700 °C annealing process, with most of the energy being used for phase transformation. As a result, the graphitization degree of G10B4-700 is lower than that of G10S4-700.

The microwave absorbing ability of a material mainly depends on its electromagnetic parameters [22], as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. Both the real (ɛ'*) and imaginary (*ɛ''*) parts of the dielectric constant clearly decrease with increasing frequency. Specifically, the values of *ɛ'* range from 10.04 to 2.9, and the values of *ɛ''* range from 5.19 to 1.05. As expected, graphene has a high dielectric constant, and both *ɛ'* and *ɛ''* increase with increasing graphene concentration. The fluctuations in the real and imaginary parts can be attributed to dipole polarization relaxation and interface polarization relaxation. The relaxation process can be further analyzed via the Cole–Cole circles in Fig. S3 in the ESM, where each circle represents a relaxation process and the straight line represents the conductivity loss. Therefore, the dielectric loss, dipole polarization and interfacial polarization of the GS and GB aerogels are interrelated [23].

(1) (ε′−εs+ε∞2)2+(ε′′)2=(εs−ε∞2)2

where *ε*s and *ε*∞ represent the static and high-frequency limit dielectric constants, respectively.

Graphene, SiO2 and BaTiO3 fibers are dielectric materials, and their microwave absorption properties can be attributed solely to the dielectric contribution. The tangent values (tan*δε* = *ε** ′′/ε** ′) of the dielectric loss are shown in Fig. S2(c) in the ESM. The tanδε* values are generally high in the X-band, indicating a strong absorption loss capability within the X-band. The electromagnetic wave attenuation constant and impedance matching are critical indices for the reflection loss of a material, with the specific calculation equation shown in Eqs. (2) and (3) [24]:

(2) α=2πfc×(μ′′ε′′−μ′ε′)+(μ′′ε′′−μ′ε′)2+(μ′ε′′+μ′′ε′)2

(3) Z=ZinZ0=μrεrtanh(j(2πfdc)εrμr)

In this context, *f* and *c* denote the frequency of the electromagnetic wave and the speed of light in vacuum, respectively. *Z*in represents the input impedance of the material, *Z*0 denotes the vacuum impedance, and *d* denotes the thickness of the absorber. Impedance matching implies that the impedance of the aerogel (*Z*in) is close to the impedance of the free space (*Z*0) so that as many electromagnetic waves as possible enter the absorber instead of reflecting on the surface of the absorber. Impedance matching is a prerequisite for the excellent absorption performance of microwave absorbing materials. G10B4 exhibits the greatest attenuation ability because it has the largest electromagnetic parameter among these samples. However, a dielectric constant that is too high can result in an impedance mismatch. Figure S2(e) in the ESM shows the impedance values of the samples at a thickness of 2 mm, indicating that G10S4 has superior impedance matching characteristics in the 10–18 GHz range. Figure S2(f) in the ESM displays the corresponding impedance of G10S4 at the thickness of the optimum reflection loss (RL), and it is evident that the impedance is consistently approximately 1 in the effective absorption bandwidth (EAB). Therefore, considering the requirement for impedance matching, the value of *α* should not be excessively high.

Generally, RL is an important factor for evaluating the microwave absorption performance of GS and GB aerogels and can be calculated according to Eq. (4) [25]:

(4) RL(dB)=20log|Zin−Z0Zin+Z0|

The 3D microwave absorption diagrams of samples with different thicknesses are shown in Fig. 3. As shown in the figures, G10S4 has the smallest RL value among these samples, reaching −56.31 dB at 14.17 GHz with a thickness of 2.3 mm (Figs. 3(a)–3(h)). The corresponding two-dimensional RL curve diagram is shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM. The EAB of G10S4 can reach 6.29 GHz (11.71–18 GHz). These results suggest that the microwave absorption performance decreases with increasing mass of SiO2 fibers due to their low dielectric constant. In addition, with a constant SiO2 fiber content, a decrease in the graphene concentration results in diminished microwave absorbing performance. However, when the graphene concentration was 4 mg/mL, the performance improved compared with that at 8 mg/mL. This occurred because the porosity of the aerogel increases with decreasing graphene concentration, resulting in a gradual increase in impedance matching. The porosity of the aerogel leads to multiple reflections of electromagnetic waves, thereby enhancing the microwave absorbing performance of G4S4 in comparison to that of G8S4. When BaTiO3 fibers are utilized, G10B1 has an RL value of −50.49 dB at 12.22 GHz with a thickness of 3.7 mm. Increasing the BaTiO3 fiber content consequently enhances the microwave absorbing performance, primarily due to the improved impedance matching characteristics with increasing BaTiO3 fiber content [26].

Another important index for evaluating microwave absorbing performance is the EAB, and a narrow EAB limits the application of microwave absorbing materials. The EAB of the samples was analyzed via RL mapping, as shown in Figs. 3(a)–3(h). Among them, G8S4 has the best EAB (7.9 GHz, 10.09–18 GHz, 3 mm). The microwave absorption properties of these samples at different thicknesses and their absorption bandwidths were compared extensively. Among them, G10S4 has the smallest reflection loss (−56.31 dB, 2.3 mm) and a larger EAB (6.3 GHz, 2.3 mm). When the thickness is 3 mm, G10S4 can absorb full coverage of the X-band, indicating that it has the potential to be an excellent absorber for absorbing a variety of electromagnetic waves.

The impedance matching characteristics are crucial in assessing the microwave absorption performance. To maximize the entry of the electromagnetic wave into the absorber material, the characteristic impedance of the absorber, Z*in, needs to be matched with the characteristic impedance of free space, *Z*0 (i.e., *|Z| = |Z*in*/Z*0*|* = 1).

Figure S5 in the ESM shows the relationships between RL, *λ*/4, and *Z* for G4S4, G8S4, and G10S4. As the thickness increases, the maximum RL absorption peak of the GS aerogel gradually shifts to a lower frequency, indicating the tunable absorption band property, which can be explained by the quarter-wavelength theory (Eq. (5)). Where *t*m and *f*m are the matching thickness and the corresponding frequency when RLmin is achieved, respectively. When the impedance matching thickness (*t*m) of the absorbing material is equal to a quarter of the wavelength of the incident wave (Eq. (5)), the phase difference between the incident and reflected waves is 180°, resulting in interference cancellation (i.e., half-wave loss) [27].

(5) tm=nλ4=nc4fm|εr||μr|(n=1,3,5,…)

where *λ* represents the wavelength of the incident wave, while *n* can be any odd number such as 1, 3, 5, and so on.

Figure S5(c) in the ESM shows the curves of RL and |*Z*| vs. frequency for various thicknesses of G10S4. Within the EAB, the |*Z*| value is close to 1 (0.8–1.2). Similarly, the *Z* values of G4S4 and G8S4 in terms of the optimal RL peaks are closest to 1. Owing to the rich pore structure of aerogels, their surfaces are less reflective of electromagnetic waves. The low density of the G10S4 aerogel suggests that most of the volume of the G10S4 aerogel is occupied by air, resulting in good impedance matching of the sample. In addition, the high porosity increased the number of reflections of incident waves inside the material, which improved the microwave absorption properties of the GS aerogels. The microwave absorbing mechanism of the GS aerogel is mainly attributed to the synergistic effect of impedance matching and conductivity as well as polarization. Additionally, the multicomponent interface provides substantial interfacial polarization relaxation, which effectively dissipates the electromagnetic wave energy [28].

From the calculation of the reflection loss of a single layer, achieving broadband absorption is challenging despite the high absorption intensity. Therefore, we propose a multilayer gradient structure design to achieve broadband absorption. However, the traditional method requires the preparation of numerous samples, testing to obtain the electromagnetic parameters, and designing through a trial-and-error iterative process, which has a long cycle time. Therefore, in this study, we prepare small sample datasets and make predictions via machine learning to quickly obtain the electromagnetic parameters for multilayer design. Our machine learning process consists of four steps. First, we obtain a series of graphene-based aerogel samples through experimental preparation, creating a small dataset that includes both process and electromagnetic parameters. Second, we associate these feature variables with electromagnetic parameters by training an appropriate machine learning model. Third, on the basis of the machine learning results, we determine the influence of the process parameters on the electromagnetic parameters and predict the electromagnetic parameters corresponding to the given process parameters. Finally, we verify the accuracy of the machine learning predictions through experimental preparation. The predicted and experimentally derived electromagnetic parameters are then used to optimize reflection loss (Fig. 3(i)).

We employ the random forest algorithm in this study, as it has superior predictive ability. Unlike traditional decision trees, the random forest selects the best categorical attributes by randomly choosing *k* attributes for each node among *P* original attributes (Fig. S6(a) in the ESM). This method is widely used and important for machine learning. Random forest algorithms can construct complex nonlinear relationships between input and output parameters. The purpose of machine learning is to establish the relationship between the target attributes and the selected features [29]. The random forest model was optimized via Bayesian techniques with fivefold cross-validation to fine-tune the hyperparameters. Once the optimal hyperparameters were identified, the model was retrained on the complete dataset.

The shapley additive explanation (SHAP) value highlights the significance of a particular feature’s influence on the dielectric constant. The wider the horizontal extension is, the more critical the feature is to the model. The magnitude of the SHAP value in the graph represents the relative importance of the influence on the parameter and does not indicate the meaning of the specific value. Since the effects of the process parameters on the real and imaginary parts of the dielectric constant are similar, the effects of the process parameters on the imaginary part are elaborated in this study. The effect on the imaginary part is shown in Fig. 3(i) and Fig. S9 in the ESM, where the two most influential features are temperature and graphene concentration, with the importance of the features in the order of T > G > F > B > S (Fig. S9(a) in the ESM). The color of the points indicates the magnitude of the features, with darker colors representing larger features (Figs. S9(b) and S9(c) in the ESM) [30].

On the basis of the mapping relationship between the process parameters and the electromagnetic parameters derived from the aforementioned SHAP values, the electromagnetic parameters for the T, G, F, and S process features were predicted. The pretrained machine learning model, which predicts the parameter space via neural networks, expands the experimental data within a few minutes, significantly increasing the efficiency of the experiments. Figure S10 in the ESM presents the predicted electromagnetic parameters corresponding to the process parameter space of the samples. Figures S10(a) and S10(b) in the ESM show the real and imaginary parts of the dielectric constant predicted when the G, S and F features are variable. Figures S10(c) and S10(d) in the ESM show the real and imaginary parts predicted when the G, T, and F features are variables, respectively. The inversion of process parameters can be achieved by determining the corresponding process on the basis of the desired microwave absorption properties. The accuracy and prediction precision of the machine learning model trained with a small sample dataset are enhanced, enabling the predicted values of the dielectric constant under various process parameters through inputting the process parameters [31]. As shown in Fig. S11 in the ESM, the electromagnetic parameters predicted by the machine learning model are plotted against the actual measured electromagnetic parameters. The results demonstrate that the predicted electromagnetic parameters are in good agreement with the measured values after training with a small sample dataset. The average error values for both the real and imaginary parts of the dielectric constant are approximately 20% over the entire 2–18 GHz range. Figure S11(d) in the ESM shows the average value of the error within the whole frequency band, demonstrating that the maximum error for the real part is 19.5%, with a minimum error of 11.1%, and that the maximum error for the imaginary part is 21.3%, with a minimum error of 2.7%. This finding indicates that the model has high predictive accuracy. The discrepancy in the imaginary part can be attributed to the graphene-based composite aerogel employed in this investigation, which is highly sensitive to temperature. At elevated temperatures, the oxygen-containing functional groups and defects on the surface of graphene are eliminated, resulting in enhanced electrical conductivity and consequently influencing the variation in its imaginary part [32]. The machine learning approach employed in this study is specifically designed for predicting the electromagnetic parameters of nonmagnetic materials.

After expanding the electromagnetic (EM) parameter database via machine learning, both the EM parameters obtained via machine learning prediction and those obtained via experimental preparation were imported into the simulation software. Iterative optimization of the impedance gradient structure was carried out, and the reflection losses obtained from the simulation of different gradient combinations were verified manually. By adjusting the electromagnetic parameters and thickness of various impedance layers, a design with a gradually increasing impedance gradient is achieved. The superior impedance matching properties of the surface layer minimize microwave reflection at the surface of the material, while the subsequent high-loss layer dissipates microwave energy within the material. This gradient structure design balances impedance matching with high-loss characteristics, ensuring effective microwave absorption across both low and high frequencies. After a comprehensive evaluation of the two indices (reflection loss below −10 dB in the range of 2–18 GHz for thinner thicknesses), the structures were optimized for thicknesses of 5, 10, and 20 mm, respectively, and the results are shown in Fig. S12 in the ESM. The optimized impedance gradient structure with a thickness of 20 mm has an extraordinarily wide absorption band of 3.7–18 GHz. On the basis of the current demand for the X-band (8–12 GHz) and wide bandwidth, we choose a 3 mm single layer of the G10S4 aerogel and 10 mm (G4S1–2.5 mm–G10B1–1.5 mm–G4B4–650–6 mm) and 20 mm (G10S1–7 mm–G10S4–10 mm–G4B4–500–3 mm) as the optimal solutions. Furthermore, RCS simulations were conducted on these samples, with thickness substitution employed to refer to the combined aerogel in subsequent simulations.

To assess the accuracy of the simulation, aerogel materials with the same structure as the designed structure were prepared and tested via the NRL-arc method. The results show that the experimental curves align well with the simulated curves. The simulation curves indicate that the RL remains below −10 dB across the 3.7–18 GHz frequency range. The actual samples achieved this performance within the 3.26–17.30 GHz frequency range. Compared with the simulated curve, the measured curve exhibits distinct absorption peaks at 9.3 GHz (−19.8 dB) and 14.3 GHz (−20.02 dB). The EAB of the simulation and experimental results differ only slightly. The experimental results reveal a leftward shift of the EAB compared with the simulation results and demonstrate superior reflection loss. This discrepancy arises primarily because the thickness of different impedance layers cannot be precisely controlled during the actual fabrication process. The thickness of each impedance layer in the fabricated samples is slightly greater than that in the simulated design, leading to a shift in the absorption peak and an increase in the RL peak. Additionally, the surface of the simulated sample is completely flat, whereas the actual tested sample surface has a certain degree of roughness, which contributes to scattering and affects the RL. The absorption peak appearing at 4.4 GHz in the experimental curve is attributed primarily to the resonance between the thickness and wavelength, whereas the peaks at 9.3 and 14.3 GHz are attributed mainly to the resonance of the gradient impedance structure (Figs. 4(a) and 4(b)) [33]. The multilayer structure design achieves ultrabroadband absorption in the range of 3.26–17.30 GHz. This excellent performance is attributed mainly to the gradient impedance design, which results in superior impedance matching performance at the surface, attracting more electromagnetic waves into the interior of the material. As the electromagnetic waves penetrate deeper into the material, the impedance gradually increases, enhancing the loss capability [34]. In the high-frequency range, the dielectric coupling between one-dimensional fibers and two-dimensional lamellae also plays a key role in enhancing absorption (Fig. 4(c)) [35]. In addition, the dipole polarization, conductive losses, and multiple reflections between the pores provided by each layer of the impedance gradient structure are also important factors in achieving broadband microwave absorption performance (Fig. S13 in the ESM) [36].

To evaluate the electromagnetic wave absorption performance of the aerogel under real far-field conditions, the RCS of aerogel-coated perfect electric conductor (PEC) panels was simulated via CST software. The RCS values were analyzed for PEC and aerogel-coated materials at 4 GHz (Fig. 4(d)). The azimuth angle (θ*) ranges from −60° to 60°. At a frequency of 4 GHz, the RCS reduction of a single-layer 3 mm aerogel coating is minimal, reaching only 1.3 dB when incident vertically. However, at frequencies of 8, 10, and 12 GHz, the RCS reduction values improve significantly, reaching 8.4, 18.3, and 14.7 dB, respectively. For lower frequencies, a multilayer impedance-designed aerogel coating is more effective. Specifically, the PEC coated with the 20 mm aerogel shows the highest RCS reduction at 4 GHz, reaching 10.85 dB. At 8, 10, and 12 GHz, the RCS reduction values are 9.6, 10.1, and 9.7 dB, respectively. This design effectively balances wide-band stealth performance (Fig. 4(e)) [37]. This is consistent with the previous results of RL, where the 3 mm sample demonstrated superior microwave absorption in the X-band but exhibited limited effectiveness at low frequencies. In contrast, the 20 mm multilayer design demonstrated comprehensive coverage across the 3–18 GHz range and exhibited enhanced microwave absorption at low frequencies [38]. These simulation results demonstrate the potential of aerogel microwave absorbing materials for stealth applications [39].

As shown in Fig. 4(f), the as-prepared G10S4 aerogel has a low density, allowing it to rest on a dandelion while maintaining the shape of the dandelion intact. The mechanical properties of the pure graphene aerogel and the G10S4 aerogel were evaluated. As illustrated in Fig. S15 in the ESM, at 70% compression strain, the compression strength of the pure graphene aerogel was 11.37 kPa, with no residual stress after deformation recovery. After 200 cycles, the compression strength of the pure graphene aerogel decreases to 8.35 kPa. For the G10S4 aerogel, the maximum compressive strength at 70% compressive strain was 31.6 kPa, which decreased to 23.4 kPa after 200 compressive cycles. These findings indicate that the SiO2 fiber membrane acts as a skeleton in the aerogel, increasing its compressive strength. The mechanism by which G10S4 has better mechanical properties can be attributed to two primary factors. From a macroscopic perspective, the SiO2 fiber membrane acts as a supportive framework. When subjected to external compressive stress, the stress is transmitted to the fiber membrane and subsequently distributed across the broader fiber network and interconnected aerogel matrix, thereby preventing stress concentration in localized areas of the aerogel. From a microscopic perspective, the aerogel and fiber membrane are effectively bonded at the interface, facilitating synergistic stress transmission. Under compressive stress, the pore size within the sample decreases; however, the fiber network restricts the deformation of the aerogel under loading. Upon the release of pressure, the aerogel reverts to a disordered pore structure, thereby enhancing overall dimensional stability and resistance to deformation.

The aerogel also demonstrated excellent hydrophobicity, as confirmed by the water contact angle test using a contact angle tester. The water contact angle was greater than 100° on the surface of the aerogel, with the red droplets remaining stable on the surface without penetrating into the aerogel (Fig. 4(g)). The hydrophobic properties of the materials can reduce the adhesion of droplets to the material surface, thereby significantly delaying the onset of ice formation and mitigating associated risks. Furthermore, hydrophobicity promotes self-cleaning, which reduces corrosion of metal surfaces caused by contaminants such as salt spray and dust, consequently lowering maintenance requirements. The obtained GS aerogel possesses effective thermal insulation properties (Figs. 4(h) and 4(i)), as evidenced by real-time temperature measurements on the heating platform. The temperature at the top was much lower than that at the middle and bottom under the same heating conditions. After 30 min, the temperature of the aerogel surface was 57.3 °C, while the temperatures in the middle and at the bottom were 81.2 and 101.2 °C, respectively. The thermal conductivity of the GS aerogel is essentially minimal, and the thermal insulation mechanism can be divided into the following aspects: First, when heat transfer occurs between the fibers and the aerogel skeleton, the contact surface between the fibers facilitates heat conduction along the three-dimensional network of the solid phase, dispersing it into the interior of the aerogel. The transfer of heat from the surface is reduced [40]. Additionally, the GS aerogel contains a substantial number of mesoporous structures that bind air molecules and thereby diminish radiative heat transfer. Furthermore, the pores within the aerogel impede the transfer of heat in the gaseous phase, and the combined effect of these mechanisms results in the aerogel exhibiting excellent thermal insulation and infrared stealth properties. The multiple functions give the material the ability to serve in complex electromagnetic environments.

Graphene-based vibrators are characterized by a lightweight and uniquely ultrathin structure, which affords them significant advantages in terms of sound absorption. Density and thickness are the two principal structural parameters affecting 3D sound absorbers. Notably, the sound absorption coefficients of the GS and GB aerogels increased with increasing frequency in the range of 100–1000 Hz. As the bulk density increases, the acoustic absorption coefficient of G10B4 reaches 0.96 at 2695 Hz, which is close to the ideal acoustic performance. However, G10S4 has a constant coefficient of 0.98 above 3000 Hz, demonstrating superior acoustic performance (Fig. 4(j)). This is due to the presence of numerous graphene-based oscillators and the pore structure formed by nanosheets and fibers, which facilitates the transmission and dissipation of high-frequency sound waves [41].

To further assess its practical application performance in sound absorption, a fully enclosed sound absorber tube device was prepared to accurately measure its noise absorption capabilities (Fig. 4(k)) [42]. The rightmost horn of the absorber tube emitted the specified noise, whereas a sound wave meter monitored the decibel value of the sound source and the residual noise after absorption by the sample. The noise level of the prepared sample was reduced from 91.4 to 76.8 dB. The implementation of noise reduction measures can decrease the likelihood of stealth aircraft being detected by sonar, thereby improving the overall stealth performance of the aircraft. This serves to further prove the powerful application of the sample not only in aerospace and transport but also in industry to ensure the normal operation of precision instruments.

## 4 Conclusions

Overall, we developed a machine learning prediction system for the electromagnetic parameters of dielectric materials, which provides guidance for the preparation process of high-performance microwave absorbing materials. By utilizing a small-sample dataset obtained from experiments, our prediction results exhibit high accuracy, with errors within 20% of the measured results. By employing the random forest algorithm, we significantly reduced the design space and improved the prediction process compared with manual selection and optimization of small sample datasets. Additionally, simulations have been used to optimize the design of multilayer structures, resulting in excellent microwave absorption performance. The optimized structure achieves ultrawideband absorption of 14.04 GHz (3.26–17.30 GHz) at a thickness of 20 mm, reducing the entire research cycle to just a few weeks. In addition, the aerogel material utilized in this work has favorable thermal and acoustic insulation properties. Therefore, we believe that this study provides inspiration for the rapid design and preparation of multifunctional high-performance microwave absorbing materials.
