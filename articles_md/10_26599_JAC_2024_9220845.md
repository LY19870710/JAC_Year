## 1 Introduction

It is well known that the sunlight plays a crucial role in the ecological cycle. In this system, the plants absorb the light energy and then convert the carbon dioxide and water into organic matter with the release of oxygen via photosynthesis. Their light absorption ability derives from the complex and well-defined photoreceptors on plant leaves [1,2]. These photoreceptors have different responses to sunlight, that is, they selectively absorb blue (400−500 nm), red (600−690 nm), and far red (700−740 nm) light regions for phototropic processes, photosynthesis, and photomorphogenesis, respectively [3−5]. Phytochromes are red (*P*R)/far-red (*P*FR) light photoreceptors, which play significant roles in plant growth and development. The phytochrome *P*R is biologically inactive upon absorption of red photons into the biological-active *P*FR state [6]. This is a reversible process, viz., *P*FR can be also converted back to *P*R by absorbing far-red photons. Under far-red light irradiation, the plants would yield shade-avoidance response as if they were blocked from sunlight [7]. Therefore, it is feasible to increase the productivity or adjust the traits of various crops by varying the spectral components (e.g., *P*R/*P*FR ratio) in phytotron chambers or greenhouses [8]. The Mn4+ or Cr3+ activated luminescent materials can emit far-red light overlapped with the absorption of the phytochrome *P*FR and thus are frequently used as potential artificial light sources for indoor plant cultivation. A phosphor, if emits multiple beneficial fluorescence signals, may realize the functional diversification for plant cultivation.

Apart from the spectral component, the phosphor materials designed as artificial light sources for indoor plant cultivation should also have high luminescence efficiency, good luminescent thermal stability, excellent chemical and physical stability, and low cost. The phosphor-converted light-emitting diodes (pc-LEDs) have advantages over conventional light sources in terms of high luminous efficiency, good durability, low power consumption, compact size, and friendliness to the environment, which make this illuminating system ideal for plant cultivation [9−13]. A ceramic phosphor is superior to the powder counterpart, since the bulk materials avoid using the organic polymer as the packaging materials which have poor thermal dispersion (thermal conductivity: 0.1−0.4 W·m−1·K−1) to readily cause the degradation of luminous intensity, acceleration of aging, and changes in emission color upon long-term operation [14]. Besides, the difference in refractive indices between the powder phosphor and the organic resin would induce light scattering. Hence, the development of transparent ceramic phosphors against using organic resin not only effectively solves these problems but also possesses merits of more excellent thermal conductivity, optical transmission, chemical stability, luminescent thermal stability, and mechanical properties [15].

Body-centered cubic Ln3Al5O12 (LnAG) aluminates (space group: *Ia** 3¯d*, No. 230) comprise 160 atoms per unit cell, where the Ln atoms reside in the dodecahedral interstices formed by the corner-sharing arrangement of the AlO4 and AlO6 polyhedra [16,17]. In this chemical formula, Lu3+, Y3+, and Gd3+ cations are three representative elements for Ln3+. They are attractive host materials owing to their low phonon energy, adjustable bandgap energy, good biocompatibility, and excellent chemical and physical properties. After being doped with rare-earth and/or transition-metal activator(s), these luminescent aluminate materials are extensively applied in the systems of lighting, imaging, laser, optical thermometry, biological sensor, photodynamic therapy, and optical amplifier [18−23]. The Eu3+ doped fluorescent materials are one of efficient red emission sources arising from 4f–4f intra-configurational 5D0→7F1,2 transitions [24−28]. However, a Eu3+ activated phosphor with strong far-red emission from the 5D0→7F4 transition has relatively rare reports. GdAG:Eu is considered better compared to YAG:Eu and LuAG:Eu, since a 4f energy-level overlap between the 6P*J* state of Gd3+ and the 5H*J* state of Eu3+ makes the energy transfer from Gd3+ to Eu3+ possible. However, pure GdAG would suffer from the issue of low thermal stability, namely, GdAG easily decomposes into the GdAlO3 perovskite and Al2O3 above 1300 ℃. Previous reports indicate either substituting Gd3+ with smaller Lu3+/Y3+ to decrease the average Ln3+ radii or replacing Al3+ with larger Ga3+ to extend the dodecahedral interstices can stabilize the cubic garnet lattice structure [29−31].

The optical loss caused by micro defects in ceramic bodies always exists, leading to opaque status or low transparency relative to the corresponding perfect single-crystal materials. How to achieve ceramic materials with zero optical loss is still a challenge. In the present work, optical grade (Gd0.95**−***x*Lu*x*Eu0.05)3Al5O12 ceramics were successfully fabricated by cost-effective vacuum sintering. The compositional effects on structure feature, sintering behavior, and optical performance were investigated in detail. Especially, our developed ceramic phosphors exhibit strong far-red and yellow dual emissions for potential applications to plant cultivation.

### 2.1 Preparation

In a typical synthetic procedure, Gd2O3 (~99.999% purity, Haoke Technology Co., Ltd., China) and Lu2O3 (~99.99% purity, Haoke Technology Co., Ltd., China) raw materials are dissolved in excessive hot nitric acid upon heating till dryness to separately prepare the two nitrates. Mother liquor was obtained by mixing the as-prepared two nitrates, commercial Eu(NO3)3·6H2O (~99.99% purity, Diyang Chemical Co., Ltd., China) and NH4Al(SO4)2·12H2O (~99.999% purity, Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) together according to the chemical formula of (Gd0.95−*x*Lu*x*Eu0.05)3Al5O12 (*x* = 0‒0.95). The molar ratio of Eu3+ to total Ln3+ cations was fixed at 5 at%, since above which luminescence quenching would occur [32]. The mother liquor was dripped into a 1.5 M ammonium hydrogen carbonate (AHC; 99.995% purity, Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China) solution at a rate of ~4 mL/min under magnetic stirring at room temperature, and the molar ratio of AHC to total cations was kept at 2.5 : 1. After being aged for 48 h, the suspension was repeatedly washed with distilled water and absolute ethanol via centrifugal separation, followed by drying at 90 ℃ for more than 12 h in an oven. The dried precursor was lightly crushed using an agate mortar, and then calcined in a tube furnace (NBD-T1700-60IT-110F, Henan Nobody Materials Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China) under flowing oxygen (~100 mL/min) at 1200 ℃ for 4 h to yield the garnet particle. Any additive was not added during the whole particle synthesis process. The powders were pre-compressed in a stainless-steel mold, followed by cold isostatically pressed at 240 MPa. The green body was sintered in a tungsten-heater furnace (ZW-25-20, Shanghai Chenhua Science Technology Co., Ltd., China) at 1680‒1750 ℃ for 4 h under 10−4‒10−5 Pa vacuum. The sintered body was annealed at 1550 ℃ for 7 h in air and was finally polished on both sides to improve surface finish. It should be noted that we selected different temperatures for sintering ceramic samples according to the varying chemical compositions, and the specified parameters along with some information on the final sintered products can refer to Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

### 2.2 Characterizations

The as-prepared powders were characterized by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Model D8 Advance Davinci, Bruker, Germany) using nickel-filtered Cu Kα as incident radiation, a Fourier transform infrared spectroscope (FTIR; Model Nicolet 6700, Thermo, USA), a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Model S-4800, Hitachi, Japan), and a laser diffraction particle size analyzer (Model Nano-ZS90, Malvern Instruments, UK). The in-line transmittances of polished ceramics were measured on a UV/VIS/NIR spectrophotometer (Model Lambda 950, Perkin-Elmer, USA) from 200 to 2100 nm. The microstructures of the bulks were observed on a desktop scanning electron microscope (D-SEM; Model EM-30plus, COXEM, Repblic of Korea). The photoluminescence (PL), photoluminescence excitation (PLE), and fluorescence decay kinetics of phosphors and ceramics were recorded on two different steady-state fluorescence spectrometers, that is, the Model FS5 equipment (Edinburgh Instruments, UK) for spectral tests and the Model FLS 980 one (Edinburgh Instruments) for decay behavior measurements. The quantum efficiency was determined by a professional quantum efficiency measurement system (Model QE-2100, Otsuka Electronics, Japan). The electroluminescence (EL) spectra of the assembled LED device at diverse forward bias currents were measured using a multi-channel spectroradiometer (Model SPEC 3000A, Measurefine Instrument, China).

### 2.3 Computational methodology

First-principles calculations are performed in the framework of the density functional theory (DFT) with the projector augmented plane-wave method, as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package. The generalized gradient approximation proposed by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof is selected for the exchange-correlation potential [32]. The calculations are done on the two chemical compositions of Lu3Al5O12 and Gd3Al5O12. The cut-off energy for the plane wave is set to 450 eV. The energy criterion is set to 10−5 eV in iterative solution of the Kohn–Sham equation. The Brillouin zone integration is performed using a 6 × 6 × 6 *k*-mesh. All the structures are relaxed until the residual forces on the atoms have declined to less than 0.01 eV/Å.

### 3.2 Structural features of synthetic products

Our co-precipitation method leads to rounded precipitation precursors with frequently empty interiors upon pyrolysis into relatively discrete rounded garnet powders without observed hard agglomeration (Fig. S1 in the ESM). Those precursors are primarily in amorphous states (Fig. S2 in the ESM). The chemical compositions of precipitation precursors are qualitatively analyzed by FTIR spectra, and the results reveal that the precursors possess the structure of hydrated basic carbonate sulfate (Fig. S3 in the ESM).

Figure 1(a) exhibits XRD patterns of target particle products for (Gd0.95−*x*Lu*x*Eu0.05)3Al5O12 (*x* = 0.1−0.95) obtained by calcining the precursors at 1200 ℃ for 4 h. Their XRD peaks can be well indexed into the cubic Lu3Al5O12 standard card (JCPDS No. 73-1368) without observed other impure phase, indicating that the precipitation precursors have completely transformed into pure garnet phase via thermal decomposition. TG analysis further reveals that the pyrolytic processes include dehydroxylation, dehydroxylization, decarbonation, desulfuration, and solid-phase reaction (Fig. S4 in the ESM), while a higher Gd3+/Lu3+ ratio leads to a higher decomposition temperature on each stage due to higher alkalinity of Gd3+ than that of Lu3+. The crystallite size (*D*XRD) can be calculated from the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the (420) diffraction band using Scherrer’s equation: *D*XRD *= Kλ/(**β*cos*θ**)*, where *K* is the shape factor (*K* = 0.89), *λ* is the wavelength of X-ray (*λ* = 0.15406 nm), *β* is the FWHM, and *θ* is the Bragg angle [34−37]. The determined crystallite sizes are ~135, 123, 122, 116, and 97 nm for the specimens with *x* = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.95, respectively. The reduced crystallite size with more Lu3+ doping is associated with thermodynamics. The whole calcination processes include thermal decomposition and solid-phase reaction. A high-temperature solid-phase reaction primarily comprises chemical reaction and diffusion process, where the chemical reaction is generally faster than the diffusion process for most of solid-phase reactions. On the other hand, the crystallite formation mainly includes nucleation and growth. Therefore, the chemical reaction that is more prone to occur will provide more nuclei, and vice versa. The Δ*G* value of Eq. (2) is smaller than that of Eq. (1), suggesting that Lu3Al5O12 is formed in priority during high-temperature solid-phase reaction, and thus a higher Lu3+/Gd3+ ratio would create more crystal nuclei to reduce the average crystallite size.

Therefore, more than 10 at% Lu3+ substitution for Gd3+ has been proven to be effective enough for stabilizing the cubic GdAG phase structure by reducing the average Ln3+ radius. The XRD patterns of ceramics become much sharper relative to those of powders. This is because the decreasing average ionic radius of Ln3+ induces the continuous shrinkage of the unit cell with the increase in the amount of Lu3+ substitution for Gd3+, which also confirms that the Lu3+ and Eu3+ dopants have been substantially dissolved into the GdAG lattice.

### 3.3 Transmittances and microstructures of (Gd,Lu)<sub>3</sub>Al<sub>5</sub>O<sub>12</sub>:Eu ceramics

The appearances and in-line transmittances of vacuum sintered (Gd0.95−*x*Lu*x*Eu0.05)3Al5O12 (*x* = 0.1−0.95) ceramics are shown in Fig. 3. The letters under the five ceramics are able to be well read-through upon 365 nm ultraviolet lamp irradiation into characteristic orange emission of Eu3+ (Fig. 3(a)). Nevertheless, the orange emission seems not strong to the naked eye. This is because the most intense excitation band of Eu3+ is located at 394 nm rather than 365 nm (Fig. 5(c)). Another reason is that the dominant 5D0→7F4 emission of Eu3+ is located at ~710 nm (Fig. 5(d)), whilst the human eye sensitivity greatly decreases at 650 nm and approaches almost nil at 700 nm. In natural light, the two specimens with *x* = 0.3 and 0.7 exhibit a yellowish hue, whereas the other three samples are normally colorless. The color is closely associated with the transmission behaviors. As indicated in Fig. 3(b), the absorption peaks in the transmittance curves are caused by intra −4f 6 transitions of Eu3+, but the samples of *x* = 0.3 and 0.7 have stronger absorption extent around 455 nm blue region and thus appear the optical compensatory yellow color. Such phenomenon may be related to more oxygen defects. Under vacuum sintering at elevated temperatures, the oxygen vacancy is easy to form while an extra defect energy level may be introduced. This new energy level may be around 5D2 state of Eu3+ to enhance 7F0,1→5D2 absorption. It should be noted that these oxygen vacancies induced under oxygen-deficient conditions are unstable, and also difficult to be precisely controlled. Even if the ceramics are treated by post-annealing, the oxygen in air is not always able to fully fill up in the vacancy site via diffusion in the dense sintered body.

Even more importantly, the sample of *x* = 0.1 exhibits an optical grade with near-zero optical loss in accordance with the defect-free single crystal. Such an almost perfect optical quality has a scarce report for any transparent ceramic materials, further revealing the advancement of our ceramic technology.

The thermal etching microstructure of the best sample of *x* = 0.1 is shown in the inset of Fig. 3(b). Its statistical average grain size via the WinRoof image analysis software is only around 7.5 μm. The quite fine grain size contributes to improved mechanical properties. Additionally, the grain size is relatively uniform, and no abnormal grain growth or residual pores can be observed. The relative density of the ceramics is determined to be ~100% by Archimedes method, in agreement with the pore-free dense structure and the excellent optical quality.

Figure 4 compares surface microstructures of (Gd0.95−*x*Lu*x*Eu0.05)3Al5O12 ceramic specimens with *x* = 0.1 and 0.95, which are sintered at an identical temperature of 1680 ℃. Their average grain sizes are counted to be ~9.8 μm for *x* = 0.1 and ~2.5 μm for *x* = 0.95. Apparently, the former has a larger grain size than the latter. Point mass diffusion, such as grain-boundary diffusion and volume diffusion, mainly determines the grain size. The higher Gd3+/Lu3+ ratio in the garnet system leads to faster point mass diffusion, which is somewhat similar to our previously reported (Y,Gd)2O3-based sesquioxide [49]. Considering that the particle crystallite size originally decreases with increasing Lu3+ addition (Fig. 1), these phenomena imply that the crystallites in powders may undergo nearly synchronous growth upon subsequent sintering densification. It should be noted that the same bulk sample (e.g., *x* = 0.1) has discrepant statistical average grain sizes from the thermal etching polished surface (~7.5 μm in the inset of Fig. 3(b)) and untreated surface (~9.8 μm in Fig. 4(a)). Apparently, the latter is larger than the former because of the preferential sintering on the surface. In practice, the statistical value on the thermal etching polished surface has better representativeness.

### 3.4 Spectral behaviors of phosphor particles and ceramics

Figure 5(a) presents PLE spectra of (Gd0.95−*x*Lu*x*Eu0.05)3Al5O12 (*x* = 0.1−0.95) powders as a function of the Lu3+ concentration. The broad band at ~230–267 nm is caused by charge transfer (CT) from the 2p orbital of O2− to the 4f orbital of Eu3+, and the neighboring peak at ~275 nm is attributed to the 8S7/2→6I*J* transition of Gd3+ [49,50]. The other weak excitation peaks beyond 300 nm are assignable to the intra −4f6 transition of Eu3+ as indicated in Fig. 5(a), among which the strongest one is located at ~394 nm arising from the 7F0→5L6 transition of Eu3+. Under the 394 nm excitation, the corresponding PL spectra exhibit characteristic Eu3+ emissions deriving form 5D0→7F*J* (*J* = 1−4) transitions, where the 5D0→7F1 transition at ~592−596 nm belongs to the magnetic–dipole transfer, the 5D0→7F2,4 transitions (~610−630 nm for 5D0→7F2 transition and ~696−710 nm for 5D0→7F4 transition) ascribe to the electric-dipole transfer, and the 5D0→7F3 transition at ~650−656 nm is forbidden [25]. Herein, the 5D0→7F4 emission dominates in each case. The 5D0→7F0 transition of Eu3+ (generally at ~580 nm) is absent because it only exists in the point symmetric Cs, C*n*, and Cnv (*n* = 1, 2, 3, 4, 6) sites [51]. However, a cubic garnet lattice provides a crystallographic D2 position for the Eu3+ substitution. The *D*2 lattice site has a high degree of symmetry, but Eu3+ ions sometimes reside at the deviation site of dodecahedral interstices. As a result, either the magnetic-dipole 5D0→7F1 transition or the electric-dipole 5D0→7F4 transfer likely dominates in the emission spectra of Eu3+ doped garnet systems, while their ratio depends on not only the surrounding environment but also the cationic average electronegativity [52]. In this work, the electric-dipole 5D0→7F4 transition presents the strongest intensity due to the geometric distortion.

The PLE and PL spectra of (Gd0.95−*x*Lu*x*Eu0.05)3Al5O12 ceramics are shown in Figs. 5(c) and 5(d), respectively. The band positions of ceramics basically have no change relative to those of powders, but the PLE and PL intensity significantly increase. By taking the sample of *x* = 0.1 as an example, the ceramic phosphor exhibits ~20.5-fold higher PL intensity compared to the particle counterpart. Additionally, the PLE/PL intensity of (Gd0.95−*x*Lu*x*Eu0.05)3Al5O12 powders and ceramics gradually rises at a higher Gd3+/Lu3+ ratio, where the specimen with *x* = 0.1 has the strongest intensity (~3.8 times that of the counterpart with *x* = 0.95). The enhanced luminescence cannot be ascribed to the energy transfer from Gd3+ to Eu3+, because the 394 nm excitation wavelength (~3.1 eV) is not enough to excite the electrons of the ground state onto the 6P*J* level of Gd3+ (~4.0−4.1 eV) as illustrated in Fig. S6 in the ESM. The reason can be considered the average electronegativity for Ln3+. That is, Gd3+ is less electronegative than Lu3+ (1.20 for Gd3+ and 1.27 for Lu3+), and thus a higher Gd3+/Lu3+ ratio facilitates the electron transfer. By comprehensively considering the spectral behaviors as well as the optical transmittances, the optimum ceramic composition is (Gd0.85Lu0.1Eu0.05)3Al5O12.

The Commission International de I’Eclairage (CIE) chromaticity diagrams of (Gd0.95−*x*Lu*x*Eu0.05)3Al5O12 phosphors and ceramics under 394 nm excitation are drawn in Figs. 5(e) and 5(f), respectively. The CIE chromaticity coordinates of (Gd0.95−*x*Lu*x*Eu0.05)3Al5O12 powders are (0.56, 0.34), (0.58, 0.36), (0.58, 0.36), (0.55, 0.34), and (0.56, 0.34) for the specimens with *x* = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.95, respectively, which all lie in the yellowish pink region. The corresponding CIE chromaticity coordinates of the ceramic samples are generally consistent at (0.61, 0.38), which all fall into the reddish−orange scope.

For the purpose of exploring the applicability for plant culture, we compare the PL spectrum of the best (Gd0.85Lu0.1Eu0.05)3Al5O12 ceramic composition with the absorption spectra of phytochrome *P*R and *P*FR as shown in Fig. 6. The dominate far-red emission peak at ~710 nm from the 5D0→7F4 transition of Eu3+ has a significant overlap with the absorption of phytochrome *P*FR. This 5D0→7F4 emission peak position is quite analogous to those of previously reported Mn4+ activated CaGdAlO4, La(MgTi)1/2O3, and Ca3La2W2O12 phosphor particles developed for plant cultivation [12,53,54]. On the other hand, the contributions of the 5D0→7F1,2,3 emissions are relatively small to phytochrome *P*R, because either their band intensity is weak or their peak positions are far away from the maximal absorption wavelength of phytochrome *P*R. However, the second strongest luminescence band from the 5D0→7F1 transition of Eu3+ appears yellow emission. Yang *et al*. [55] found that the yellow light was optimal for the growth and accumulation of bioactive flavonoids in some plants compared with white, red, or blue light. In consequence, our developed ceramic phosphor could sufficiently provide both far-red and yellow emissions, implying the application potential to modulate plant growth as an artificial light source.

### 3.5 Thermal stability of luminescence for transparent ceramic phosphor

The luminescent thermal stability is a significant parameter for the realized functionalization of fluorescent materials in solid-state lighting. The temperature-dependent luminescence measurement for the best fluorescent ceramic composition (Gd0.85Lu0.1Eu0.05)3Al5O12 is performed in the temperature range of 118−833 K, and the results are shown in Figs. 9(a) and 9(b). With the increasing heating temperature, the PL intensity follows a trend of decline due to the thermal quenching effect derived from electron–phonon interaction. Moreover, the change in the emission wavelength, if any, is only ~1 nm within the allowable system error (Fig. S7 in the ESM). If we assume the fluorescence intensity at room temperature to be 100%, the maximum value is achieved at 118 K (Fig. 9(c)), which is ~1.33 times that at room temperature. The thermal quenching temperature, defined as the temperature at which the PL intensity is 50% of the value at room temperature, is a little higher than 533 K for our sample. Such a high thermal quenching temperature indicates that our developed materials have high thermal stability of luminescence. The thermal stability of the sample is further evaluated by the activation energy of the thermal quenching process based on Arrhenius equation (Eq. (9)):

(9) IT=I01+Aexp(−ΔE/(kT))

where *I*0 is the luminescence intensity at room temperature, *I*T is the emission intensity at a certain temperature, *k* is the Boltzmann constant (8.6×10−5 eV/K), *A* is a constant, and Δ*E* is the activation energy of thermal quenching [24,63,64]. The Δ*E* value can be estimated by taking the logarithm on both sides of this equation, viz., the plot of ln(*I**0*/*I**T*−1) against (*kT*)−1 yields a straight line whose slope is equal to −Δ*E*. As shown in Fig. 9(d), the resulting Δ*E* value is ~0.17 eV with a high degree of fitting (*R*2 = 99.4%), which is equivalent to the commercially available Y2O3:Eu3+ red phosphor (0.17 eV) [65]. The probability of a nonradiative transition per unit time (*α*) is closely associated with the activation energy according to Eq. (10):

(10) α=sexp(−ΔEkT)

where *s* is the frequency factor [66]. Apparently, the high activation energy would effectively suppress the probability of nonradiative transition.

### 3.6 EL properties of designed LED lamp

The best fluorescent ceramic specimen is directly covered on a near UV (390−395 nm) chip to package the LED device without any organic polymers, and its appearance can be seen in Fig. S8 in the ESM. Under an injection current of 50 mA, the assembled LED lamp emits weak red emission as shown in Fig. 10(a). Applying a higher working current of more than 150 mA yields the strong glaring red emission. As the current increases, the junction temperature of the packaged red-emitting LED shows an increasing tendency up to a maximum of ~120.8 ℃ at 435 mA, and then decreases to ~106.9 ℃ at 500 mA. Compared with the particle phosphor-converted LED [67], our ceramic phosphor-converted LED exhibits a lower junction temperature and more uniform thermal radiation at each current due to its higher conductivity by avoiding using the organic polymer and its own feature of pure substance. As presented in Fig. 10(c), the EL emission spectra also display the characteristic 5D0→7F*J* (*J* = 1−4) transition of Eu3+, and their intensity rises with enhanced injection current up to 435 mA, and then decrease at a higher current. That is, the 435 mA current seems to be the saturation point. Such phenomenon may be attributed to the induced thermal effect referred to Fig. 10(b). From the normalized curve (Fig. 10(d)), the EL integral intensity at 435 mA is ~11-fold higher than that at 50 mA. At this saturation current, the corresponding color coordinates, color rendering index (CRI), and correlated color temperature (CCT) are (0.61, 0.39), 81.6, and 1745 K, respectively. The elevated working current increases the CRI parameter, but has little effect on CCT and color coordinates (Table S3 in the ESM). The phenomenon of saturated injection current for electroluminescence has seldom been studied for comparison. However, we believe that the pore-free ceramics may have a higher saturated value for high-power LED applications relative to the powder forms and the translucent ceramics due to the higher thermal conductivity.

## 4 Conclusions

A series of (Gd0.95−*x*Lu*x*Eu0.05)3Al5O12 (*x* = 0.1−0.95) particles and ceramic phosphors were prepared to study the compositional effects on structure features, sintering behaviors, and luminescence properties. All the phosphors exhibit main far-red emission at ~710 nm arising from the 5D0→7F4 transition, which overlaps with the absorption of phytochrome *P*FR. The Lu3+ dopant increases the theoretical density, suppresses the crystallite/grain growth, reduces the luminescence intensity, and delays the fluorescence lifetime. The optimum ceramic composition of *x* = 0.1 exhibits an optical grade with near-zero optical loss, a high quantum efficiency of ~42%, and excellent thermal stability with activation energy of ~0.17 eV. The designed ceramic phosphor-converted LED possesses a saturation injection current of 435 mA, a high color rendering index of 81.6, and a low correlated color temperature of 1745 K. Our findings suggest that the developed red-emitting LED has potential applications for plant cultivation.
