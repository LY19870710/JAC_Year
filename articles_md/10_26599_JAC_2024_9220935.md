## 1 Introduction

Carbon materials offer excellent physical–chemical stability and mechanical properties, making them a viable alternative for thermal structural applications for propulsion systems, re-entry vehicles, furnace elements, and engine components [1,2]. However, a critical challenge arises when carbon materials are exposed to temperatures above 500 °C in air due to oxidation [3], requiring thermal oxidation protection coatings for structural integrity. Materials in extreme environments are susceptible to various forms of degradation, including oxidation, ablation, spallation, and melting, due to high temperature, heat flux, and/or chemical corrosion [4]. Therefore, to improve the service life of carbon-based materials and thermal barriers, ceramic coating systems as thermal protection have been employed in oxygen-containing environments [5−9].

Ultra high-temperature ceramics (UHTCs), including carbides and diborides, are classified as materials with melting temperatures > 2500 °C, chemical stability and retained mechanical strength at temperatures above 1650 °C, and oxidation and ablation resistance [3,10]. The strong bonding nature of transition metal diborides (TMB2) comes from a combination of covalent (B–B), ionic (TM–B), and metallic (TM–TM) bonds. UHTCs have emerged as promising solutions for protecting materials from degradation under extreme thermal conditions, such as nozzles, turbine blades, and engines [11].

To overcome the challenges of thermal degradation of carbon in oxygen-containing environments, the use of UHTCs as protective coatings has been investigated. For instance, SiC [12], MoSi2 [13], and diborides or carbides such as ZrB2–SiC [11,14], ZrB2–SiC–Si [15], ZrB2–SiC–HfB2–WC [9], and ZrB2–SiC–WC [16] coatings have been evaluated. The development of UHTC coatings involves fulfilling various requirements, including chemical compatibility and matching expansion coefficients with the substrate, strong interfacial bonding, thermal diffusivity, and low oxygen activity [10]. Moreover, the coatings must exhibit mechanical compatibility to resist spallation caused by thermal expansion mismatch during extreme thermal heating. Traditional fabrication methods such as plasma spraying or pack cementation can result in cracks and micropores in the coatings, which provide diffusion pathways for oxygen and act as stress concentrators [10,17]. In contrast, the spark plasma sintering (SPS) allows the production of dense and homogeneous coatings with tailored microstructures in a short time [16] and can rapidly assemble and join complex thermal protection material systems [18].

The introduction of multicomponent UHTCs in high-temperature applications has been explored to create oxide products with different properties (thermal conductivity, melting points, etc.) during oxidation to offer enhanced temperature-range coverage [19]. A significant advancement in this field is the development of high-entropy ceramics, which are characterized by multiprincipal component compositions with four or more principal cations (5–35 at%). This approach increases the system’s configurational entropy, potentially developing novel ceramics [1]. Compared to conventional UHTCs, high-entropy UHTCs usually exhibit superior mechanical properties, lower thermal conductivity, and improved oxidation resistance attributed to lattice distortion and sluggish diffusion effects. The entropy-stabilization concept has been applied to several multicomponent diborides [19−26].

Bulk high-entropy UHTCs were previously evaluated for their ablation resistance. Guo *et al.* [27] tested (TiZrHfNbTa)C and concluded that the formed single-phase lattice-distorted structure effectively prevented oxygen diffusion into the ceramic. Wang *et al*. [28] produced (HfTaZrNb)C with 98% density and concluded that complex oxide formation and phase distribution acted as barriers to ablation. However, few high-entropy UHTCs have been reported to be manufactured as coatings for ablation resistance. Zhang *et al*. [19] prepared (Hf0.25Zr0.25Ti0.25Cr0.25)B2 incorporated with Si–SiC as a coating on carbon‒carbon composites by slurry painting and reported that selective oxidation of the elements was the key to improving the heat resistance of the coating against ablation at 2127 °C. A (Hf0.25Zr0.25Ta0.25Ti0.25)B2 coating was deposited onto carbon‒carbon composites via atmospheric plasma spraying and tested against ablation [29]. It was reported that the high-entropy effect contributed to better performance compared to other UHTC coatings, combined with the self-healing effect of the Ti-dominant liquid oxides, while Hf and Zr oxides contributed to the thermal stability. Therefore, the broader applicability of high-entropy diboride coatings still requires an in-depth understanding of their thermal, chemical, and mechanical responses under harsh conditions.

Hafnium diboride (HfB2) stands out as an UHTC diboride due to its excellent resistance to oxidation and ablation, with a melting point of 3250 °C [17]. On the other hand, zirconium diboride (ZrB2) is of significant interest as a potential leading-edge material for re-entry vehicles [30] because of its lower density (6.1 g·cm−3) compared to HfB2 (10.5 g·cm−3), a crucial property considered for propulsion applications. We have previously described the manufacturing of high-entropy (Ti0.25V0.25Zr0.25Hf0.25)B2 [25] with a density of 6.7 g·cm−3 and reported its oxidation properties at 1000 and 1500 °C [31]. In this work, high-entropy diboride (HEB) (Ti0.25V0.25Zr0.25Hf0.25)B2 containing Hf and Zr, which ensures high-temperature resistance and Ti and V to lower the density, was manufactured by SPS as a coating on graphite. Experimental and computational approaches were combined to better understand the complex mechanisms of high-temperature joining with a graphite substrate and high-entropy forming *in situ*. To elucidate the behavior of the coating during ablation, thermal properties were obtained by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations driven by a machine learning force field (MLFF) trained on density functional theory (DFT) calculations. These properties were then used to model the temperature distribution using the finite element method (FEM) to understand the time-dependent mechanical stresses at the coating and graphite substrate interface during high-temperature exposure. The ablation process and mechanisms along with the distribution of the oxides were experimentally described, and the combination of the obtained results provided a comprehensive understanding of the thermal stability of the coating under high-temperature and oxygen-rich environments.

### 2.1 Preparation of the HEB coating on graphite

Metal diboride powders (Table 1) were weighed in an equiatomic proportion and dry mixed in a propylene jar with Si3N4 balls (*d* = 8 mm) for 3 h. The ball-to-powder ratio was 3 : 1. The graphite substrate (~1.79 g·cm−3, *d* = 11.5 mm, ~5 mm in thickness) was polished using 600 grit abrasive SiC paper, cleaned with ethanol, and dried at 100 °C for 2 h. The inner surface of a 12 mm diameter graphite die was protected by 0.2 mm graphite foil; the graphite substrate was inserted into a graphite die, and the powder mixture was poured onto the graphite substrate. The graphite die was placed into the furnace chamber of an SPS apparatus (Dr. Sinter Lab Jr., model 211Lx, Fuji Electronic Industrial Co., Ltd., Japan) for sintering at an air pressure of ~1.4 Pa with a uniaxial pressure of 50 MPa and a heating rate of 100 °C·min−1 from room temperature up to 1600 °C and 50 °C·min−1 up to 2000 °C, with holding time of 30 min. The cooling rate was 30 °C·min−1 until the temperature reached 600 °C. Subsequently, the material was left to cool inside the SPS furnace, and the uniaxial pressure was slowly released when the temperature was lower than 1600 °C. Finally, the sintered specimens were ground and polished until they had a mirror-like surface before the characterization and ablation tests.

### 2.2 Oxyacetylene torch test

The ablation tests were performed on the polished specimens using an oxyacetylene torch (with a flow rate and pressure of 0.17 L·s−1 and ~0.1 MPa for C2H2 and 0.24 L·s−1 and 0.4 MPa for O2) equipped with a plasma flame gun. The distance between the plasma gun and the front surface of the specimens was approximately 10 mm. The inner diameter of the gun tip was 0.93 mm. HEB coated graphite specimens were exposed to the plasma flame for 30 and 60 s (reported as HEB-30 and HEB-60, respectively). During testing, the surface temperature of the center of the specimens was measured using an infrared pyrometer (IR-HAQ, Chino Co., Japan), and the peak surface temperatures were 2234 °C for HEB-30 and 2227 °C for HEB-60.

The mass ablation rate (MAR) and linear ablation rate (LAR) were calculated using Eqs. (1) and (2), respectively.

### 2.3 Machine learning force field

A neuroevolutionary potential (NEP) [32] for Hf–Zr–V–Ti–B was developed utilizing the Graphics Processing Units Molecular Dynamics (GPUMD) software package, version 3.8 [33]. The generation of training data followed a two-step approach where an initial dataset was created through random displacement of atoms within relaxed supercells with 81 atoms of HfB2, ZrB2, VB2, TiB2, and (Ti0.25V0.25Zr0.25Hf0.25)B2. The initial dataset served as the basis for training an ensemble of NEPs, which were used to extend the training data. Here, deviation in the force predictions of the NEP ensemble (model deviation) was used to identify structures that deviated significantly from the training data. These structures were then extracted and labeled using DFT calculations, and a new NEP ensemble was trained on the extended dataset. This active learning approach [34,35] was applied to the constant pressure, constant temperature (NpT) ensemble MD simulations of HfB2, ZrB2, VB2, TiB2, and (Ti0.25V0.25Zr0.25Hf0.25)B2 supercells with 108 atoms, spanning temperatures from 300 to 3000 K at 101.325 kPa pressure. Labeling of structures with energies, forces, and virials was performed using DFT calculations at the meta-generalized gradient approximation (meta-GGA) level using the strongly constrained and appropriately normed (r2SCAN) functional [36] as implemented in the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) [37−39]. All DFT calculations were non-spin polarized (ISPIN = 1) using a plane-wave basis set and the projector-augmented wave method [40,41]. Here, a plane wave cutoff energy of 400 eV (ENCUT = 400) was used with disabled symmetry constraints (ISYM = 0). The convergence criterion for the electronic self-consistent loop was set to 10−6 eV (EDIFF = 1E−6), with Gaussian smearing (ISMEAR = 0) applied together with a smear width of 0.05 eV (SIGMA = 0.05). The following VASP pseudopotentials were used: Hf (2003 Jan 20), Zr_sv (2005 Jan 04), V (2002 Apr 08), Ti (2002 Apr 08), and B (2000 Sep 06). All NEP models employed a radial cutoff of 6.0 Å and an angular cutoff of 4.8 Å, with 9 radial and 45 angular descriptors, each with 9 basis functions. For the angular descriptors, the order of the three-, four-, and five-body terms was set to 4, 2, and 0, respectively. The neural network, which maps the descriptors to atomic energies, consists of a single hidden layer with 54 neurons. Training employs the separable natural evolution strategy [42] with a population size of 64 and equal weighting of energy, force, and virial components in the loss function,  λe=λf=λv=1.0. All training runs were performed for 140,000 generations with a batch size of 5000 images. After two iterations of active learning, a satisfactory level of accuracy was reached during the active learning MD simulations (average model deviation of approximately 150 meV·Å−1). The root mean square errors (RMSEs) for energy, force, and virials, evaluated on the test set for the final NEP used for all MD simulations, are shown in Fig. 1. Here, the RMSEs are on par with those of other published MLFFs [32,43].

### 2.4 MD simulations

MD simulations were performed using the GPUMD software package, version 3.8 [33], with a timestep of 1.0 fs for all simulations. To determine the phonon density of states (DOS) for (Ti0.25V0.25Zr0.25Hf0.25)B2 at 300 K, an initial equilibration phase was performed. This involved equilibrating a supercell with 116,622 atoms under the NpT ensemble conditions (300 K and 101.325 kPa pressure) for 100 ps using the Berendsen barostat [44]. Subsequently, the mass-weighted velocity autocorrelation function was derived from a 1 ns NVE ensemble simulation from which the phonon DIS was determined. In a separate set of simulations, the temperature-dependent lattice parameters, density, and specific heat were determined for HfB2, ZrB2, VB2, TiB2, and (Ti0.25V0.25Zr0.25Hf0.25)B2. Here, supercells with 116,622 atoms were equilibrated in the NpT ensemble using a Berendsen barostat for a 50 ps period at 101.325 kPa across a temperature range from 200 to 3000 K, increasing in 100 K intervals. After equilibrium was reached, sampling of thermodynamic properties, specifically potential energy, kinetic energy and lattice parameters, was performed at each temperature for 50 ps in the NpT ensemble using a stochastic cell rescaling barostat [45].

### 2.5 Non-equilibrium molecular dynamics

The temperature-dependent thermal conductivities of HfB2, ZrB2, VB2, TiB2, and (Ti0.25V0.25Zr0.25Hf0.25)B2 were determined using non-equilibrium molecular dynamics (NEMD) as implemented in the GPUMD software package, version 3.8 [33,46]. For all the systems, 227,088 atom (in-plane) and 223,200 atom (out-of-plane) supercells were used in the NEMD simulations to determine the thermal conductivity in the in-plane and out-of-plane directions, respectively. Due to the larger system sizes needed to accurately determine thermal conductivity, only 5 temperatures were studied:  TNEMD=300, 975, 1650, 2325, and 3000 K. For each temperature, equilibration was first performed under the NpT ensemble (300 K and 101.325 kPa pressure) for 500 ps using the Berendsen barostat [44]. NEMD was subsequently performed using a heat source and sink region to simultaneously heat and cool the system. The temperature in each region was set to  Tsource=TNEMD+ΔT and  Tsink=TNEMD−ΔT with  ΔT set to 33% of the target temperature  TNEMD.The source region and sink regions were controlled by separate Langevin thermostats [44], and the region between the source and sink had no temperature control, allowing for the development of a natural temperature gradient. The NEMD simulations were performed for  tNEMD= 5 ns, during which the total energy transferred by the thermostats was  Esource and  Esink, respectively, and the temperatures for the regions between the source and sink were sampled every 5.0 fs and averaged over 200 samples before being written to the file. The thermal conductivity was then calculated as  k=−Q˙×L/[A×(Tsink−Tsource)], where  L is the distance between the source and sink;  A is the cross-sectional area; and  Q˙=(Esource−Esink)/(2tNEMD) is the flow of energy per unit time.

### 2.6 Finite element model (FEM)

An FEM for HEB graphite during the ablation process was developed. The steady-state heat transfer in solids coupled with solid mechanics modules, working together with the thermal expansion multiphysics module, was conducted in COMSOL Multiphysics v.6.0. The model is three-dimensional (3D) time dependent, represented by local heat-up at the center of the coating simulating the heat generated by the oxyacetylene flame. The mesh used consists of 848,373 elements with sizes ranging from 0.0024 to 0.24 cm (schematically shown in Fig. 2(a)).

### 2.7 Thermal stress calculations

The CTE was experimentally determined for comparison using a Gleeble 3800 thermal-mechanical tester (Dynamic Systems Inc., USA). The graphite specimen (*d* = 6 mm and length = 20 mm) and the HEB specimen (3 mm × 3 mm × 10 mm) were subjected to temperature monitoring at 400–1300 °C at a heating rate of 50 °C·min−1. Simultaneously, the displacement during the temperature increase was recorded. The selection of a high heating rate aimed to emulate conditions resembling ablation.

The generation of all the stresses was attributed only to the temperature increase. The thermal strain, *ε*, can be described by Eq. (11):

(11) ϵ=α(T)×(T−Tref)

where *α* is the coefficient of thermal expansion of the material; *T* is the temperature of the material; and *T*ref is the reference temperature, 293 K. The normal stress (*σ*) in the coating is related to the elastic strain by Eq. (12):

(12) σ=E×ϵ1−2ν

where *ν* (Poisson’s ratio) and *E* (elastic modulus) were previously determined for HEB [25] to be 0.149 and 466 GPa, respectively. For graphite, the values were temperature dependent and were also obtained from the MPDB database [47].

To calculate the stress variation along the *x*-, *y*-, and *z*-axes, the total stress change, called the von Mises stress (Eq. (13)), was considered to evaluate the results.

(13) σmises=32(σx−σy)2+(σy−σz)2+(σz−σx)2

Hardness indentations were performed with 1.96, 2.94, and 4.90 N loads at a distance of 10 µm from the coating to evaluate the stresses close to the HEB/graphite interface. Using the same load, reference indents were made 500 µm from the interface. The area of these indents was employed to estimate the stress necessary to cause damage at the interface.

### 2.8 Characterization

The crystal structure of the powder mixture and HEB coating before and after the ablation test was identified using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; PANalytical Empyrean, Malvern, UK) with 40 mA and 45 kV and Cu Kα radiation at room temperature. The microstructure and elemental distribution of the materials were examined using the scanning field emission scanning electron microscopes (SEM; JSM-IT300, JEOL, Japan; Magellan 400 Extreme High-Resolution, FEI Company, the Netherlands) and the energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS; X-Max 80, Oxford Instruments, UK). Vickers microindentation was carried out with a hardness tester (MXT-a1, Matsuzawa Seiki Co., Ltd., Japan) equipped with a microindentation hardness testing system (OmniMet MHT, Buehler, USA). Thermodynamic calculations for the reactions between the diborides and oxygen were performed using FactSage 8.1 software.

### 3.1 Thermal properties

Key parameters, including thermal conductivity, heat capacity, oxidation characteristics, and CTE, influence the thermal response of UHTCs [10]. Specifically, the rate at which the surface temperature of UHTCs increases during the initial heating stage and temperature gradient largely evolve depends on these parameters [9,49]. For the application of thermal barrier coatings, lower conductivities ensure a significant temperature drop from the coating to the substrate [50]. Therefore, lower thermal conductivities with low masses and linear ablation rates are essential for thermal barrier coatings.

The introduction of configurational entropy engineering into solid ceramic solutions, achieved by populating a sublattice with distinct cations, has recently opened paths to leverage strategies to fabricate materials with low thermal conductivities [51]. The disorder in the atomic force constants and the strong phonon scattering caused by severe lattice distortion cause the thermal conductivity of high-entropy UHTCs to be lower than that of their related individual metal diborides and carbides [52−54]. However, according to Wright *et al*. [54], the ideal configurational entropy is not the best descriptor for thermal conductivity. For example, size disorder has been proposed to be a more effective descriptor for describing the thermal conductivity of medium- and high-entropy pyrochlore oxides [55]. The systems used for thermal conductivity calculations and the phonon DOS for (Hf0.25Zr0.25Ti0.25V0.25)B2 are shown in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b), respectively. The low thermal conductivity stems from the broad peaks, indicating highly disordered force constants [51]. This disorder is mainly attributed to the high-entropy effect and lattice distortions present in the HEB.

The *k* of (Hf0.25Zr0.25Ti0.25V0.25)B2 reported here was smaller than that of previously reported HEBs, such as (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2, (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)B2, and (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Ta0.2Cr0.2)B2, which were also found to be much lower than their parent diborides [23]. No information on the thermal conductivity of VB2 was available in the literature, but for the (Zr,V)B2 solid solution, the thermal conductivity is 42 W·m−1·K−1 at 25 °C, while that of pure ZrB2 is 84 W·m−1·K−1 [56]. This indicates that in addition to the high-entropy effect and size disorder, the presence of V might have caused the *k*HEB to be lower than that previously described for other HEBs.

The discrepancy in thermal conductivity between studies of diborides is probably due to the dependence of thermal diffusivity, which is the most important parameter in calculating thermal conductivity indirectly on factors such as composition, grain size, and contact resistance between grains, porosity, and purity [57]. Additionally, the discrepancy between the thermal conductivity values from atomistic MD simulations for TMB2 is due to the limited size of the model system used, which, when less than or comparable to the phonon mean free path, will result in an underestimation of the thermal conductivity since the effective length of ballistic transport is too short [58]. However, for the HEB, phonon scattering is large (Fig. 3(b)), which results in short phonon mean free paths; thus, the calculated values for the thermal conductivity of the HEB should considered to be accurate [66].

#### 3.1.1 Predicted thermal profile and interfacial stresses

Previously, in the work of Carney *et al*. [67], 300–400 °C differences were experimentally observed from the center to the edge regions of 15 mm diameter HfB2-based ceramics during ablation experiments. Similarly, during ablation caused by a laser applied to carbon fiber/SiHfC composites, a temperature difference of more than 1500 °C was expected from the center to the edge region of the 15 mm specimens [68]. Therefore, a simulation of the profile temperature of the coating was performed to analyze the expected heat dissipation on the HEB surface and at the HEB/graphite pair interface (Figs. 4(a) and 4(b)). The temperature at the center of the coating deviates from the experimentally measured values by approximately 200 °C, mainly because oxide formation and evaporation are not considered in the model and would consume energy, further causing a difference in the observed profile temperature along the coating surface.

The temperature profile at 240 s is additionally presented for comparative analysis. Compared with a material possessing a thermal conductivity 10 times greater, after 60 s (shown as 60 s* in Figs. 4(b) and 4(c)), the surface temperature decreases. The maximum stress generated by the temperature change after 60 s is expected to be approximately 2 GPa at the interface and peaks at approximately 8 GPa close to the coating surface (Fig. 4(c)). This maximum stress is expected to occur when the maximum temperature is reached. More discussion on the mechanical properties of the coating is given in Section 3.3.

### 3.2 Coating synthesis and characterization

Figure 5(a) shows the *in-situ* sintered HEB coating on graphite. The coating was homogeneous along the surface (cross-section in Fig. 5(b)) and possessed a single-phase hexagonal structure (Fig. 5(c)). The cross-section of the coating/substrate is shown in Fig. 5(d), and the average coating thickness was 1000 µm, with the metallic elements equally distributed. No cracks along the surface were observed after sintering.

No chemical reaction between the carbon and the transition diborides is expected to occur under sintering conditions, as previously observed for graphite-metal diborides joined at 2200 °C [70]. Diborides are thermodynamically stable structures at this temperature, and although there is no phase transformation, carbon interstitial diffusion into the diboride lattice could also increase the joint strength due to the small amount of carbon and its high diffusion rate at high temperatures [71]. However, no further investigations have shown carbon diffusion. Additionally, EDS analysis (Figs. 5(e) and 5(f)) did not reveal significant diffusion at the interface. In previous investigations by Burykina and Evtushok [70], ZrB2 powder was sintered onto bulk graphite, which was linked to ZrB2 sintering at 2000 °C, and the interaction with the graphite surface was primarily facilitated by the surface roughness. The joining mechanism was wetting from the liquid phase on the graphite substrate, which is hypothesized to be the same as that for the HEB. Moreover, during sintering, direct current flow passes through the conductive materials and causes current-based effects that may have assisted joining, such as electron migration and localized heating at areas of decreased material contact [72].

Since the CTE of HEB is higher than that of graphite at high temperatures (Table 2 and Fig. S2 in the ESM), the coating shrinks more than the substrate at high temperatures due to tensile stresses, while there are compressive stresses in graphite [66]. However, these tensile stresses did not seem to cause excessive relaxation to create cracks or to induce delamination of the coating. This was associated with the relatively low temperature difference at the coating interface with the substrate.

While tensile stresses were not directly measured, it was found that stresses greater than 36.9 GPa were needed to cause localized delamination of the coating from the substrate by microindentation within 10 µm at the interface (Fig. S3 in the ESM). Therefore, the stresses caused by the difference in the coefficient of thermal expansion along the length of the specimen show that the stress present at the interface is not high enough for delamination to occur for the HEB coating. The hardness of the coating, measured in the middle of its thickness (~500 µm), is 31.2±2.1 GPa, similar to that obtained for the bulk ceramic with the same composition [25].

#### 3.3.1 Ablation oxidation products

After the ablation test, the surface of the specimens exhibited increased roughness compared with the initially polished surface, which was attributed to the erosive action of the oxyacetylene plasma flame and oxide formation. The thickness of the oxide layer at the edges (~63 µm pile-up of oxides) of the specimens was greater than that in the central area (~19 µm pile-up of oxides) (Figs. 6(a) and 6(b)). The non-uniform distribution of oxide thickness suggests that the directional flow of the flame during ablation caused the oxides to be blown away from the center to the edges. This behavior indicates that the surface of the specimens reached high temperatures locally, exceeding the melting temperature of some or all the oxides present [28]. In Fig. 7, the equilibrium diagrams of the diborides in an oxygen environment are described. It is possible to observe that, some of the oxides to be formed (TiO2, VO2, V2O3, and V2O4), are expected to be liquid at 2200 °C.

In the central region, a porous oxide layer remained on the surface after 60 s of exposure to the flame at 2200 °C. Hf- and Zr-oxides are uniformly distributed throughout this region, while the V- and Ti-oxide amounts are lower, possibly due to their liquid state. Toward the edge region, it becomes apparent that V-oxides, most likely in a molten state, act as a binding agent, retaining other oxide particles formed in the central region, preserving the oxides on the coating surface and preventing their loss by the aerodynamic forces generated by the oxyacetylene flame. Lv et al*. [29] have shown that the liquid oxide phase formed in multicomponent diborides is beneficial and can promote the densification of oxide grains and increase the protection of the oxide layer.

The oxides formed on the surface were identified by XRD (Fig. 8(a)). However, the surface of the ablated specimens was divided into three regions: edge, transition, and center regions (Fig. 8(b)). This division is based on the morphological characteristics of the oxides present and the expected temperature distribution across the coating surface when the coating is subjected to a scouring flame at a temperature of 2200 °C. Notably, the center region experiences the most severe ablation due to its exposure to the highest temperatures and harsher aerodynamic forces caused by the flame, which is similar to other UHTCs. Consequently, oxides in either molten or solid-states are transported toward the transition region by the intense scouring forces caused by the erosion exerted by the gas flow. This dynamic process leads to the exposure of new ceramic surfaces within the central area, which is the most affected zone during ablation.

The oxide products can become gaseous and evaporate at critical temperatures and oxygen partial pressures, losing the protective oxidation effect. With increasing temperature beyond the melting point of the oxides, their viscosity progressively decreases, and the product is more prone to loss by evaporation and flame erosion. This susceptibility leads to varying ablation ratios for different temperature and time exposures [19]. Therefore, the observed ablation morphology can be attributed to the synergistic effect of aggressive oxidation and mechanical scouring induced by the aerodynamic heating and erosion effects of the harsh gas flow from the oxyacetylene flame [10,73].

Ceramic coating failure usually occurs via spallation, which is the crack growth along the lamellar interface, followed by coalescence of the crack. Remarkably, despite these harsh conditions, no spalling or cracking was observed in the coating, indicating a reliable resistance to erosion in a high-temperature scouring environment and to mechanical stresses caused by the increase in the temperature. The adhesion between the coating and the substrate and their elastic properties play a crucial role in the resistance of the material at high temperatures. It was shown that the coating had good compatibility with the substrate. Furthermore, the diffusion of atoms as well as the motion of dislocations can also contribute to the relaxation of stresses [74].

For HEB-60, both the center and transition regions display minimal morphological differences, characterized by the presence of fine grains in the center region that gradually increase in size with increasing distance from the center of the sample (Fig. 8(c)). The transition region displays a layer with more complex oxides (i.e., two or more cations on the sublattice), which experiences reduced exposure to oxidizing gases and less severe mechanical erosion caused by heat flux compared to the center region.

Distinct variations in the elemental composition can be observed across different ablation regions (detailed information in Fig. S5 and Table S1 in the ESM). In the center region of HEB-30, the morphology of the phase is consistent with solidification from a melt, and the phase appears as a glassy structure. This liquid phase is uniformly distributed in the center region of HEB-30, while in the case of HEB-60, its quantity is relatively reduced. It is possible that the scouring effect of the oxyacetylene flame potentially contributes to the further displacement of the liquid phase over time, decreasing its amount in the center region. Additionally, this region exhibits fine-grained structures primarily composed of Zr and Hf oxides (as also seen before in the cross section in Fig. 6). The solid oxides are also susceptible to mechanical scouring by the flame [14], but in HEB-30, it is possible to observe that they were retained within the liquid phase. Conversely, for HEB-60, larger agglomerates composed mainly of Hf are formed, with Zr appearing to be mixed within the liquid phase. This suggests that prolonged exposure time at high temperatures causes Zr-oxides to melt and merge with the liquid phase previously containing mainly Ti and V. Similar oxide products are observed in the transition region for both ablation time, with a slightly higher oxygen content in the edge region due to the temperature gradient [27].

In the edge region, distinct oxide characteristics are observed for HEB-30 and HEB-60. For HEB-30, the oxides appear to be segregated between Ti–V oxides and Zr–Hf oxides, indicating that they were blown away separately from the center and transition regions by mechanical scouring. In contrast, for HEB-60, it is apparent that a pile-up of oxides from the center and transition regions occurred, resulting in a rougher surface with larger and round agglomerates, including oxides from the four randomly distributed metallic elements.

In addition to mechanical denudation, chemical erosion can occur during the ablation process [75], and oxide phase transformation can occur over time. As seen by the different products observed by XRD (Fig. 8(a)), the monoclinic phase is the predominant phase formed during the ablation process. HfO2 and ZrO2 are expected to be tetragonal at 2200 °C (Fig. 7), but when the material cools down, the tetragonal phase, which is usually not stable under ambient conditions, transforms into a monoclinic phase [76]. Ye et al*. [77] also identified the monoclinic phase after ablation of Hf0.5Zr0.3Ti0.2C at 2347 °C. Furthermore, Hf and Zr are usually mixed in the same lattice in different configurations [78], indicating that the monoclinic oxide phase present is more likely (Hf,Zr)O2 with some Ti and V distributed in the lattice, as seen in our previous work [31]. In addition to ZrO2 and HfO2, only B2O3 is expected to be an oxidation product from ZrB2 and HfB2 oxidation. However, no B2O3 is detected via XRD of the ablated specimens, most likely due to its rapid evaporation at high temperatures [79]. B2O3 is a liquid at temperatures above 1000 °C and evaporates at temperatures higher than 2000 °C. Therefore, it is expected that at these temperatures, very little or no boria is present on the scale [80].

Ti and V oxides were expected to be in a liquid state at the maximum temperature (Table S1 in the ESM) experienced during ablation, i.e., in the center region, which is corroborated by the liquid phase observed in Fig. 8(c). The free Gibbs energy of oxidation of the four metals decreased in the order Hf > Zr > Ti > V (Fig. 9), which supports that the formation of Ti- and V-oxides was less preferred, resulting in their retention in the central region. In contrast, Hf- and Zr-oxides were preferentially formed and tended to disperse away from the center region. Dispersed oxide particles are observed in the transition and edge regions where the temperature is lower than that at the center. These oxide particles are a result of oxidation at lower temperatures, and/or oxides are initially formed in the center region and subsequently blown away by the flame. Similar amounts of Ti and V were observed, and Hf and Zr were distributed on the surface of the ablated samples, even though some evaporation of the liquid oxide phases was expected. The quantification of evaporated oxides *versus* blown oxides remains a challenge and was not performed in this work.

#### 3.3.2 Ablation resistance

After the ablation process, the samples experienced oxidation and mechanical scouring, which changed the thickness and mass of the specimens. The most favorable reactions at ~2200 °C are shown in Fig. 9, and all the reactions form B2O3, which is expected to evaporate at this temperature. The mass ablation rates after 30 s and after 60 s were −0.51 and −1.12 mg·s−1, respectively, and the linear ablation rates were −0.64 and −1.38 µm·s−1 after 30 and 60 s, respectively.

The mass ablation rate obtained is negative due to the combination of B2O3 and Ti–V-oxide evaporation and scouring of other liquid and solid oxides by the oxyacetylene torch, causing a decrease in the HEB mass. When exposed to high temperatures during the ablation process, in addition to protecting the graphite from intense heat flux and temperature variation, the coating proved to be not only oxidation-resistant but also a thermal barrier. A comparison between this work and existing studies is challenging due to the common introduction of a secondary phase, usually SiC, in other works. This addition is primarily attributed to the self-sealing characteristics exhibited by SiO2 at temperatures lower than 2000 °C. However, the increased SiO2 vapor pressure at higher temperatures can disrupt the integrity of the formed glass layer and cause different problems [10]. Consequently, the ablation resistance of the produced coating will primarily rely on the presence of transition metal oxides. The oxidation of the diboride coating will generate B2O3 that is immediately evaporated, resulting in a decrease in the mass of the diboride system. Other studied systems that contain Si will gain mass due to SiO2 formation and CO evaporation (in most cases), resulting in mass gain, as in the case of Hf0.8Ti0.2B2–SiC–Si [17], (Hf0.25Zr0.25Ti0.25Cr0.25)B2–SiC–Si [19], and ZrB2–SiC [14]. Compared with HfB2 under a similar heat flux (2400 kW·m−2) for 30 s, the HEB coating lost more mass during ablation, mainly because the two products were HfO2(s) and B2O3(g) [79]. However, as previously indicated, the utilization of pure HfB2 is highly unlikely due to its high density and cost considerations.

The simulated temperature profile is in good agreement with the observed distribution of oxides in the ablation regions, revealing a notable distinction between the liquid phases at the center and the solid phases toward the edge regions. The simulation suggests a potential temperature difference of approximately 1600 °C within the surface after 60 s under ablation. However, the turbulent nature of the flame in close contact with the coating generated variations between the model and experiment. Consequently, temperatures at the edge region were likely higher than those predicted. Moreover, the creation of oxides on the ablated surface further complicates the comparison. As a result, there is an emphasis on the examination of the temperature distribution between the HEB and graphite, particularly in the central region, where the highest temperature was experimentally determined for comparison with the model, which was also used for the stress analysis.

An increase in high melting point oxides can effectively improve the ablation resistance of the coating by acting as a limited protective barrier and reducing the consumption of the inner coating, which is recommended for future work. Moreover, as pointed out by Tang et al*. [81], the oxidation resistance of HEB depends on its composition rather than its entropy. Although the oxide layer formed on the HEB cannot completely prevent the inward diffusion of oxidizing gas to the inner coating, it can reduce the temperature and mechanical erosion strength of the oxyacetylene flame, thereby weakening ablation damage to the coated sample [17]. The outer refractory oxide layer and inner diboride coating cooperate to resist the ablation conditions before the coating is seriously damaged and the substrate is exposed.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, an ultrahigh-temperature HEB (Ti0.25V0.25Zr0.25Hf0.25)B2 coating of 1000 µm on a graphite substrate was successfully fabricated by the SPS technique. The thermal properties, including thermal conductivity, specific heat capacity, and coefficient of thermal expansion, of the HEB were successfully obtained by MD simulations driven by a MLFF trained on DFT calculations and entered into a time-dependent FEM to describe the behavior of HEB coated graphite under high-temperature ablation conditions. Ablation tests were performed at 2200 °C for 30 and 60 s for model validation, and the mechanical stability of the coating at high temperatures was attributed to the efficient heat dissipation of the HEB–graphite pair, as well as the similar coefficient of thermal expansion between the coating and graphite substrate. An irregular oxide layer was observed on the surface of the coating, with distinct central, transition, and edge regions. The central region primarily consisted of molten Ti and V oxides, while Hf and Zr oxides were more prevalent in the edge region. Mechanical denudation and evaporation of B2O3 and light V–Ti oxides were identified as the primary ablation mechanisms, resulting in a decrease in the mass and thickness of the coating. After 60 s of exposure to the oxyacetylene torch, the coating exhibited a mass ablation rate of −0.51 mg·s−1 and a linear ablation rate of −1.38 µm·s−1. In addition to protecting carbon materials from exposure to oxidizing environments, the coating was proven to also act as a thermal barrier. These findings contribute to the growing body of knowledge surrounding high-entropy materials and their potential applications in engineering components for extreme environments. The novel coating production technique by SPS can be further explored for various applications, such as enhancing wear and irradiation resistance. However, significant efforts are needed to achieve technological breakthroughs, including cost-effective preparation, enhanced interface quality, and complex geometries, for aero-thermal-chemical environments.
