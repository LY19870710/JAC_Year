## 1 Introduction

C/SiC composites are widely utilized in high-temperature thermal protection systems for aerospace applications [1−3], including rocket nozzles [4], combustion chambers [5], gas rudders, and nose cones of intercontinental missile warheads [6,7]. Their adoption in these critical environments is attributed to their outstanding combination of properties, namely, high mechanical strength, thermal resistance, ablation durability, and chemical stability [8,9]. Presently, the main fabrication techniques for C/SiC composites include chemical vapor infiltration (CVI) [10,11], reactive melt infiltration (RMI) [12,13], and polymer infiltration and pyrolysis (PIP) [14,15]. While each of these approaches offers distinct advantages and has enabled the development of various C/SiC composite structures, they also present inherent limitations. These constraints have made it challenging to achieve fast production, cost-effectiveness, and high-performance characteristics simultaneously in C/SiC composites. Specifically, the CVI process is characterized by a long fabrication period and high cost [11,16]. The PIP method suffers from prolonged fabrication cycles, high porosity (> 15%), and a matrix with poor thermal stability that contains free carbon and SiC*x*O*y* phases [17−19]. Moreover, RMI processes involve elevated infiltration temperatures, residual silicon contents, and thermal residual stresses [13,20,21]. Thus, developing rapid and efficient methods for fabricating C/SiC composites while maintaining their excellent comprehensive performance has become an important research direction.

In general, the incorporation of the SiC phase into fiber fabrics can be classified on the basis of the physical state of the precursor, namely, solid, gas, or liquid routes [22,23]. These approaches, whether used independently or in combination, provide viable pathways for fabricating C/SiC composites. Among them, the solid-phase method offers several advantages, including procedural simplicity, reduced equipment requirements, and lower production costs. Additionally, SiC powders typically exhibit high crystallinity and purity, which contribute significantly to the thermal stability of the resulting ceramic matrix. To produce a C/SiC green body with a high SiC powder content, two main approaches have been explored. The first is to adjust the fiber preform structure to facilitate the introduction of powder. Single layer fiber fabrics have been extensively impregnated with slurries via efficient methods such as ultrasonic impregnation, vacuum infiltration, and electrophoretic deposition (EPD) to achieve high and uniform SiC particle loading [24−28]. The impregnated fabrics were subsequently stacked and pressed to ensure homogeneous SiC particle distribution within the preforms. Traditional methods such as fabric stacking and pressing often suffer from inadequate interlaminar bonding, which constrains both the performance and advancement of composite materials. A more refined approach involves adjusting the pressure conditions and parameters during SiC slurry impregnation. When infiltration is carried out under atmospheric pressure, the driving forces for slurry penetration into the fiber fabric are primarily gravitational and capillary in nature. By fine-tuning the SiC powder particle size and optimizing the slurry concentration, it is possible to incorporate up to 20 vol% of SiC particles effectively [29,30]. Furthermore, empirical studies have indicated a strong correlation between the applied external pressure and the SiC content successfully loaded into the preform [31,32]. For example, Zhang *et al*. [32] demonstrated that increasing the impregnation pressure to 8 MPa allowed for a SiC loading of 24.5 vol%, corresponding to a fiber volume fraction of 25 vol%. This pressure-induced gradient enables efficient slurry transport from the outer layers into the core of the preform, effectively occupying internal voids. Interestingly, the process mimics a filtration mechanism, wherein the fiber architecture functions as a filter, facilitating the entrapment and distribution of ceramic particles within the fabric matrix [33,34]. However, both the atmospheric pressure infiltration and pressure infiltration approaches struggle to continuously promote the dynamic rearrangement of ceramic particles, restricting their mobility within fiber networks [29]. In nature, continuous wind blowing in deserts promotes the tight accumulation of sand grains over time. Analogously, ensuring continuous and vigorous movement of ceramic particles is crucial for optimizing particle packing and achieving uniform, high-density introduction within preforms. Furthermore, to consolidate the introduced ceramic particles into integrated structures, hot-pressing, and PIP processes are typically employed [30,34,35]. Extensive studies have shown that the introduction of high-content SiC powders as a filler combined with continuous SiC phases formed by the PIP process as a binder is advantageous for the fabrication of high-performance C/SiC composites. This approach enhances both the densification rate and extent while reducing the sintering temperature [29,30].

This study presents a scalable fabrication strategy that integrates multistep slurry impregnation (MSI) with liquid polycarbosilane (LPCS) PIP to produce high-performance C/SiC composites. The MSI technique enables the continuous and uniform distribution of SiC ceramic powders within the fiber fabric and achieves dense packing, resulting in a green body with a relative density of up to 64.7 vol%. Experimental findings reveal that this combined approach markedly enhances the mechanical properties of the C/SiC composites within a relatively short processing duration. Furthermore, the MSI + PIP-derived composites demonstrate significantly greater ablation resistance at temperatures between 1600 and 2000 °C than those fabricated solely by the PIP method. These results highlight the potential of the proposed method as a promising route for manufacturing C/SiC composites suitable for high-temperature aerospace thermal protection applications.

### 2.1 Raw materials

In this study, the T700 needle-punched carbon fiber fabric used was supplied by Jiangsu Tianniao High Technology Co., Ltd., China. This fabric has a density of 0.54 g/cm3 and a fiber volume fraction of 30.7 vol%. Micron-sized SiC powders with particle sizes ranging from 1.5 to 10 μm was purchased from Weifang Kaihua Silicon Carbide Micro-Powder Co., Ltd., located in Shandong, China. Each type of SiC powder was designated according to its median particle size (*D*50): 1.5, 3, 5, 7, and 10 μm-SiC. To distinguish finished SiC powders from pyrolyzed SiC powder, the former is labeled standard SiC powder. Furthermore, LPCS with a ceramic yield greater than 55 wt% at 1350 °C was acquired from the National University of Defense Technology (NUDT), China.

### 2.2 Preparation of C/SiC composites

Micron-sized SiC powders, absolute ethanol, and dispersant polyethyleneimine were combined at a mass ratio of 100 : 60 : 1 to obtain a ceramic slurry. The mixture was ball milled at 240 r/min for 3 h via a high-energy ball mill to ensure homogeneous dispersion of the SiC powder. The needle-punched carbon fiber fabric is placed into a custom-designed vacuum chamber, which is gradually evacuated to a vacuum level of 100 Pa. Next, a precisely measured volume of SiC ceramic slurry is introduced into the chamber through the pressure difference, and the negative pressure is maintained for 10 min. The negative pressure and atmospheric pressure process is subsequently repeated 2 to 3 times. After this process, the mold was removed from the vacuum chamber. An external mechanical force is applied to the mold via mechanical vibration for 30 min, promoting the transition of the SiC particle arrangement from static packing to dynamic diffusion within the fiber fabric. After the mold was removed from the mechanical-assisted platform, the vacuum-assisted slurry impregnation process and mechanical-assisted process were repeated for a total of two cycles. This cyclic process is referred to as MSI. The resulting wet C/SiC green body, prepared via the MSI process, was then dried at 80 °C for 12 h in an oven to produce a C/SiC green body.

The C/SiC green body is further densified via the PIP process, with infiltration pressures ranging from 10 to 200 MPa. The LPCS were subsequently cured at 150–200 °C for 10 h. Finally, the samples were pyrolyzed in a graphite furnace at 1350 °C with a heating rate of 3 °C/min and held at this temperature for 1 h. Multiple PIP cycles are performed to achieve the final C/SiC composites, which are named MSI + PIP C/SiC.

For comparison, C/SiC composites fabricated solely via the PIP process, which are named PIP C/SiC, are also prepared.

### 2.3 Characterization and testing methods

The bulk density of the C/SiC composites was measured via Archimedes’ method. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; AMBER, TESCAN, Czech Republic) was used to characterize the microstructural morphologies of the composites. The crystalline structures of both the C/SiC composites and the SiC powder particles employed in this study were determined via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X’Pert Pro MPD, PANalytical, the Netherlands) with a Cu-Kα radiation source. The XRD scans were conducted within a 2*θ* range of 10–90° at a scanning speed of 0.2 (°)/s. Raman spectroscopy (inVia Reflex, Renishaw, UK) was used to investigate the chemical composition and structural characteristics of the composites. Spectral data were collected within a spectral range of 100–4000 cm−1. An X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used for elemental analysis, semiquantitative analysis, and determination of Si bonding structures in the SiC powder, scanning within an energy range of 0–5000 eV.

The mechanical properties at room temperature, including flexural strength and fracture toughness, were measured via an Instron 5569 universal testing machine (Instron, USA). The flexural strength was determined via three-point bending tests on samples with dimensions of 3 mm × 4 mm × 36 mm, a span of 30 mm, and a loading rate of 0.5 mm/min. The fracture toughness was measured via single-edge notched beam (SENB) samples (2 mm × 4 mm × 22 mm), with notch depths and widths of 2 and 0.2 mm, respectively. Load‒displacement curves are obtained at a loading rate of 0.05 mm/min. The fracture toughness (*K*IC, MPa·m1/2) and work of fracture (*W*, J/m2) were calculated following established procedures in previous studies [34].

The oxidation and ablation resistance of the C/SiC composites were evaluated via an oxyacetylene torch. The samples with a diameter of 20 mm and a thickness of 10 mm are fixed within a graphite holder positioned 15 mm away from the torch nozzle (diameter: 2 mm). The pressures of O2 and C2H2 are maintained at 0.4 and 0.095 MPa, respectively. The detailed parameters of the gas flow rates under different testing conditions are presented in Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Surface temperatures during ablation are measured by a bichromatic thermometer. Linear ablation rate (LAR, mm/s) and mass ablation rate (MAR, g/s) are calculated by Eqs. (1) and (2), respectively:

(1) lr=(lo−l)/t

(2) mr=(mo−m)/t

where  lo and  l represent sample thicknesses (mm) before and after ablation,  mo and  m denote sample masses (g) before and after ablation, and  t is the ablation duration (s).

### 3.1 Microstructure and mechanical properties of the C/SiC composites

Figure 1(a) shows a schematic diagram of the C/SiC composite green body fabricated via the MSI process. SiC powders with different particle sizes were used for the slurry impregnation process (Fig. 1(b)). The experiments revealed that as the particle size increased, the volume fraction of SiC powders within the preform first increased but then decreased (Fig. 1(c)). When the particle size of the ceramic powders approaches or exceeds the size of the interstitial spaces formed by the fiber architecture, penetration through the slurry channels into the inner regions of the fiber fabric becomes significantly hindered. On the other hand, excessively fine particles tend to form stable suspensions, which impedes rapid sedimentation and dense packing within the preform. Furthermore, smaller SiC particles are subject to stronger Van Der Waals forces, promoting agglomeration and thereby reducing the uniformity of dispersion while obstructing infiltration into the preform interior. An optimal compromise was achieved via the use of SiC powders with a median particle size (*D*50) of 5 μm, resulting in a powder volume fraction of 34 vol% and a green body relative density of 64.7 vol%. This represents an approximately 50% increase in SiC powder loading compared with conventional slurry impregnation methods [32]. This is primarily attributed to the MSI process promoting the dynamic spatial rearrangement of SiC particles inside the preform, thereby enabling a more uniform distribution of the powders throughout the porous network (Fig. S1 in the ESM).

To further investigate the spatial distribution of powders and fibers within the green body and to evaluate both process stability and parameter optimization, computed tomography (CT) analysis was conducted on the 5 μm-C/SiC green body. Micro-CT imaging confirmed a uniform distribution of SiC powders throughout the entire preform (Fig. 1(d)). Phase-segmented images clearly revealed the arrangement of SiC powders and carbon fibers, revealing that within a 25 mm × 25 mm × 25 mm volume, the powders were densely packed and effectively filled the interfiber voids in both the XY* and *XZ* planes (Fig. 1(e)). This homogenous ceramic distribution in the green body facilitates improved sintering behavior, ultimately enhancing both the relative density and mechanical performance of the final composite. Moreover, the efficient and uniform incorporation of standard SiC powders into the fiber fabric contributes to cost reduction. Given that the market price of standard SiC powders is less than 30 yuan/kg, this approach provides a cost-effective foundation for the large-scale production of C/SiC composites and structural components.

The SiC phase formed through LPCS pyrolysis acted as a bonding medium, effectively contributing to the densification of the C/SiC green bodies (Fig. 2(a)), with a ceramic conversion yield of up to 58.00 wt%. The use of extremely high impregnation pressure (~200 MPa), coupled with the relatively low viscosity of the LPCS (≤ 100 mPa·s), ensured excellent infiltration and wetting performance during the process. The structural characteristics of the SiC derived from LPCS pyrolysis are shown in Figs. 2(b) and 2(c). As indicated by the XRD patterns (Fig. 2(b)), the broad diffraction peaks at approximately 35.6°, 60°, and 71.8° correspond to the (111), (220), and (311) planes of β-SiC, respectively. In comparison, the standard SiC powders incorporated via MSI displayed much sharper peaks, indicative of greater crystallinity and larger grain domains. Raman analysis revealed distinct D (1346 cm−1) and G (1604 cm−1) bands (Fig. 2(c)), reflecting considerable carbon residue within the pyrolyzed SiC—a common attribute of polymer-derived ceramics [18]. This excess carbon is attributed primarily to the decomposition and carbonization of residual organic functional groups such as –CH3 and –CH2– in the LPCS. The presence of free carbon within the SiC matrix tends to compromise the resistance of the material to oxidation. Additionally, owing to the microcrystalline and partially amorphous structure of the pyrolyzed SiC, the characteristic Raman signals of β-SiC at 788 cm−1 (transverse optical mode, TO) and 955 cm−1 (longitudinal optical mode, LO) were not detected [36]. Owing to its microstructure and elevated carbon content, the pyrolyzed SiC appeared intensely black, leading to a noticeable transformation of the C/SiC green bodies from a grayish-white to a glossy black appearance after multiple PIP cycles (Fig. 2(e)).

The relationship between the density of C/SiC composites prepared with different particle sizes and the number of PIP cycles is presented in Fig. 2(d). The high density of the green body significantly affects the final composite density. Therefore, optimizing the standard SiC particle size and processing parameters can significantly increase the densification efficiency. This not only shortens the manufacturing cycle and reduces costs but also effectively improves the mechanical performance, which is especially vital in aerospace fields where superior performance is critical.

Figures 2(f)–2(i) present the composite microstructures before and after densification via the PIP process. The images clearly reveal that SiC ceramic particles are densely situated between adjacent fiber bundles. This configuration forms a physical bridging network that increases the overall specific surface area, extends and narrows diffusion channels, and enhances contact between the polymer and the pore surfaces, thereby reinforcing molecular interactions [37]. These structural advantages facilitate more rapid diffusion of LPCS molecules within the green body, significantly improving both infiltration efficiency and pore-filling behavior. Furthermore, the ceramic powders within the preform acts as a structural filler, effectively minimizing the volumetric shrinkage that typically accompanies polymer pyrolysis. This leads to a more consistent and homogeneously distributed ceramic phase in the final composite [21,30]. From the microscopic morphology, standard SiC powders can be observed to be uniformly encapsulated by the pyrolyzed nano SiC phase, thus achieving a dual-continuous structure at the microscopic scale between the continuous fiber phase and a continuous “SiC powders and pyrolysis SiC phase” combined matrix. This observation is further validated by the 3D CT reconstruction of the composite structure shown in Fig. 3. In parallel, the carbon fibers are uniformly coated with a PyC interfacial layer (Fig. 2(i)). This interfacial layer is essential for establishing strong yet functional bonds between the fibers and the ceramic matrix. This enables the fiber pull-out mechanism, which plays a pivotal role in altering the fracture mode of C/SiC composites from inherently brittle to more damage-tolerant and toughened [38,39].

For comparative analysis, C/SiC composites were also prepared via the conventional PIP method. A systematic evaluation of their macroscopic and microscopic structures, thermal behavior, and mechanical properties was conducted to delineate the differences between PIP and MSI + PIP composites. XRD analysis revealed that the primary distinction between the two systems lies in the matrix crystallinity, which is governed by the presence of either pyrolyzed SiC or standard SiC particles (Fig. 4(a)). As illustrated in Fig. 4(b), the evolution of the composite density across PIP cycles shows that the PIP-derived C/SiC composites required 11 infiltration cycles to reach a density of 1.92 g/cm3. In contrast, the MSI + PIP composites achieved the same density within only three cycles, underscoring the advantages of the hybrid method in terms of fabrication speed and process efficiency. Owing to highly efficient pore filling, the densification efficiency of MSI + PIP C/SiC plateaued after 6 cycles. Thus, MSI + PIP C/SiC (PIP-7) and PIP C/SiC (PIP-11) were selected for detailed comparative analysis.

The pore size distributions for both composites are shown in Fig. 4(c). The PIP-based C/SiC composites displayed a bimodal distribution in terms of pore size. Small pores (1–5 μm), formed within fiber bundles, were primarily the result of shrinkage during polymer pyrolysis. In contrast, larger pores (30–120 μm) located between fiber bundles became sealed as closed pores after multiple infiltration cycles. These larger pores became more prominent after machining, as shown in Figs. 4(d)–4(f), contributing to an open porosity of 10.56%–approximately three times greater than that observed in the MSI + PIP composites. In comparison, the MSI + PIP C/SiC composites predominantly featured pores within the 0.1–10 μm range, with only rare instances of larger pores (~100 μm). This reflects a reduction in pore size by one to two orders of magnitude relative to conventional PIP composites. The substantial decrease in porosity and pore size in the MSI + PIP composites is largely due to the introduction of standard SiC powders during the MSI process. This powder preferentially occupies interbundle and interlayer voids within the fiber architecture, significantly limiting pore formation. Moreover, the narrow gaps formed by tightly packed particles promote efficient LPCS infiltration, allowing pyrolyzed products to swiftly encapsulate and bind ceramic particles. This process minimizes closed pore formation and significantly enhances the densification of the final composite, as depicted in Fig. 4(g).

The evolution of flexural strength with different infiltration cycles for the MSI + PIP and PIP C/SiC composites is presented in Fig. 5(a). The flexural strength of the MSI + PIP C/SiC sample rapidly increases, reaching 244±31 MPa after only 3 PIP cycles. In contrast, PIP C/SiC requires more than 7 PIP cycles to achieve comparable strength levels. The improvement in the flexural strength of MSI + PIP C/SiC is attributed primarily to increased composite density, reduced porosity, a larger effective load-bearing area, and a decreased defect size. PIP C/SiC contains many large-scale defects, resulting in stress concentration and crack propagation, which significantly reduce the interface bonding strength of composites (Figs. S2 and S3 in the ESM). As a result, although both types of composites exhibit similar flexural strengths, the MSI + PIP C/SiC (PIP-7) composites demonstrate nearly 2 to 3 times higher the fracture toughness and work of fracture compared with those of their PIP C/SiC (PIP-11) counterparts (Figs. 5(c) and S4 in the ESM). Specifically, the MSI + PIP composites achieve values of 16.33±1.70 MPa·m1/2 for fracture toughness and 5931±560 J/m2 for work of fracture. These findings indicate that highly dense MSI + PIP C/SiC effectively integrate high strength and toughness.

In this stage, the stress of the PIP C/SiC sample nonlinearly increases until the maximum stress value is reached. When the applied stress exceeds the load-bearing capacity of the matrix material, defects such as thermal microcracks and pores that are generated during processing serve as crack initiation sites (Figs. 4(d) and S2 in the ESM). This process is accompanied by matrix cracking, interfacial debonding, and sliding between the fibers and the matrix [40]. As a result, the resistance of the matrix to deformation gradually decreases, leading to a progressive reduction in the slope of the stress–strain curve, which ultimately results in nonlinear behavior and greater strain values. In contrast, the MSI + PIP C/SiC composites incorporate uniformly distributed reinforcement phases, such as fibers and ceramic particles, which enhance load transfer and stress dispersion. ### 3.2 Oxidation and ablation resistance of C/SiC composites

A fundamental step in understanding the ablation behavior of C/SiC composites under dynamic oxidation conditions is to first examine the structural evolution of ceramic phases during high-temperature static oxidation. In general, polymer-derived SiC ceramics possess carbon-rich microstructures characterized by fine grain sizes and low crystallinity, factors that critically affect the high-temperature oxidation resistance of the resulting C/SiC composites [53]. Figure 6(a) shows the thermogravimetric analysis‒differential scanning calorimetry (TG‒DSC) curves of different types of SiC heated to 1500 °C in air. Compared with standard SiC, pyrolyzed SiC results in 0.85 wt% weight loss between 300 and 750 °C, which is attributed primarily to the oxidation of free carbon. Under sufficient oxygen partial pressure, pyrolyzed SiC subsequently undergoes passive oxidation at temperatures above 750 °C, accompanied by the formation of a SiO2 layer and mass gain (Eqs. (3)–(5)). The passive oxidation onset temperature for pyrolysis of SiC is nearly 300 °C lower than that of standard SiC, indicating its higher oxidation activity in air. XPS analysis was employed to reveal differences in the composition and bonding structures of both types of SiC. The full-spectrum survey scans revealed significantly higher C/Si and O/Si peak intensity ratios in pyrolyzed SiC than in standard SiC (Fig. 6(b)), indicating its oxygen-rich and carbon-rich nature. This observation is confirmed by semiquantitative XPS analysis (Fig. 6(c)), where the oxygen content in pyrolyzed SiC reaches 22 at%, and the atomic ratio of carbon to silicon (C/Si) is as high as 2.25. These compositional characteristics substantially impact its high-temperature thermal stability. The Si 2p spectrum reveals that the Si in pyrolyzed SiC predominantly exists as a mixture of Si–C and Si–O–C bonds (SiC*x*O*y*). This bonding indicates that SiC*x*O*y*, microcrystalline β-SiC, and free carbon constitute the primary phases, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 6(e). SiC*x*O*y* exists mainly in the form of Si–O–C, which suppresses the growth of SiC grains [54], whereas free carbon forms a complex carbon network between phases, resulting in extensive grain boundaries [53].

The full XPS spectrum of the oxidized pyrolyzed SiC indicates a significant reduction in the carbon peak intensity, confirming a greater loss of carbon and thus higher oxidation activity and poorer thermal stability at elevated temperatures. Si 2p spectral analysis of pyrolyzed SiC after oxidation revealed the complete transformation of the mixed phases into Si‒O bonding structures. Coupled with the XRD analysis (Fig. 6(g)), this newly formed structure predominantly corresponds to quartz (SiO2). These reactions include the decomposition of amorphous SiCx*O*y* phases [55,56] (Eq. (3)), the oxidation of free carbon (Eq. (4)), and the passive oxidation of SiC (Eq. (5)), collectively leading to carbon and oxygen depletion and resulting in poor high-temperature stability of the pyrolyzed SiC. Similarly, the Si 2p XPS spectra and XRD patterns (Figs. 6(d) and 6(g)) revealed that after oxidation at 1600 °C for 1 h, standard SiC generated only minor amounts of SiO2. The oxidation-induced SiO2 layer formed on the SiC surface has low oxygen permeability, passivating surface defects and impeding further oxygen access to the underlying SiC, significantly reducing the oxidation reaction rate (Figs. 6(f) and 6(h)). The superior oxidation resistance of standard SiC originates entirely from its high crystallinity and purity. Thus, substantial differences in phase compositions result in markedly distinct high-temperature oxidation resistances between pyrolyzed SiC and standard SiC.

Dynamic oxidation by an oxyacetylene flame involves significantly higher gas velocities than static oxidation does and combines high temperatures, high gas flow rates, and high oxygen contents. It is essentially a coupled process involving external flow fields, surface ablation, and internal heat transfer [57]. In this study, the oxyacetylene flame test was employed to evaluate the oxidation and ablation resistance of C/SiC composites. Figure 7(a) shows the surface temperature evolution curves for the MSI + PIP C/SiC composites under various oxyacetylene ablation conditions. The composite surface temperature sharply increased within the initial 10 s and stabilized at approximately 1600, 1800, and 2000 °C after 30 s (Fig. 7(b)). Upon removal of the oxyacetylene flame, the surface temperature rapidly decreases, indicating a significant temperature gradient, undoubtedly presenting a severe challenge to the thermal-shock resistance of the composites. The macroscopic surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the C/SiC composites after oxyacetylene ablation are presented in Fig. 7(c). At temperatures below 1800 °C, the structural integrity of the MSI + PIP C/SiC composites was maintained, and the materials survived without apparent damage. However, as the ablation temperature increased to 2000 °C, erosion pits gradually formed on the ablation surface. A comparison of the surface structure evolution of the two composites during thermal ablation (Fig. 7(d)) clearly revealed that the surface of MSI + PIP C/SiC remained consistent at 1800 °C for 300 s, demonstrating outstanding reliability. In contrast, the surface of the PIP-derived C/SiC composites showed pronounced pore enlargement, characterized by outward propagation from the central pore regions toward the periphery, accompanied by increased surface roughness. This microstructural degradation expanded the contact interface between the hot gas, the oxidative atmosphere, and the ablative surface, leading to localized heat buildup and intensified ablation damage. Representative microscopy images of both composites postablation are presented in Fig. 7(e). In the PIP C/SiC composites, the abundance of surface and subsurface pores resulted in localized erosion, where molten SiO2 failed to adequately infiltrate and seal the voids, thereby impeding the development of a continuous, protective oxide layer. As a result, the internal matrix regions and reinforcing fibers remained exposed and underwent sustained oxidative degradation. In contrast, the MSI + PIP C/SiC composites, with their inherently denser microstructure, retained structural integrity and showed no obvious signs of severe erosion, ablation, or delamination. This enhanced ablation resistance is attributed primarily to the formation of a compact SiO2 layer, which effectively shields the surface from flame and oxygen ingress.

On the basis of the experimental phenomena and microstructures of the composites, the oxidation and ablation mechanisms of the two C/SiC composites under 2000 °C oxyacetylene flame tests clearly differ. The microstructure of the matrix plays a critical role in determining the oxidation and ablation resistance of the composite. In the case of the MSI + PIP C/SiC composite, the matrix comprises an interpenetrating structure of “standard SiC and pyrolysis SiC phases”. Owing to the oxygen-rich and carbon-rich nature of pyrolyzed SiC, the content of amorphous SiCx*O*y* phases tends to increase. At approximately 1050 °C, the oxidation and decomposition of the SiC*x*O*y* amorphous phase can lead to degradation of the matrix structure [61]. Moreover, studies have shown that the oxidation activation energy of pyrolyzed SiC (~80 kJ/mol [62]) is significantly lower than that of highly crystalline standard SiC, which is approximately 300 kJ/mol in high-temperature air environments [63]. The highly ordered crystalline structure of standard SiC requires a greater energy barrier to initiate oxidation, making it a key factor contributing to its superior thermal stability and oxidation resistance. This result is consistent with our previous results (Fig. 6).

Hence, the oxidation process during the ablation of the MSI + PIP C/SiC composite at 2000 °C primarily involves the following steps:

(i) Oxidation of the surface matrix and fibers. At the initial stage of ablation, oxygen molecules/atoms are first adsorbed onto the surface of the SiC composite. These reactive species rapidly react with the exposed carbon fibers, forming ablation pits or holes (Eq. (7)). Owing to the poor oxidation resistance of the pyrolyzed SiC phase, the oxidation and decomposition reactions subsequently proceed rapidly (Eqs. (3)–(6)). Oxygen molecules and atoms rapidly diffuse through the pyrolyzed SiC phase. Concurrently, the oxidation products, primarily SiO2, interact with the low-reactivity standard SiC particles and form a coating. At the interface between the two phases, this mixed oxide layer impedes further diffusion of oxygen atoms into the standard SiC particles. As a result, a continuous, high-viscosity “solid–liquid” binary oxide layer forms on the composite surface. At elevated temperatures, this viscous layer not only slows the inward oxidation of the underlying matrix but also provides effective resistance against the mechanical shear stresses induced by high-temperature, high-velocity gas flows. Consequently, this structure significantly enhances the durability and ablation resistance of the C/SiC composite.

(ii) Subsequent inward diffusion of oxygen through the growing SiO2 oxide layer. After the initial oxidation, the oxygen molecules or atoms continue to diffuse inward through the formed SiO2 layer. A new oxidation front forms at the interface between the oxidized and unoxidized regions (SiC/SiO2 interface). This interface mainly comprises two regions: (1) the inner boundary of the surface “solid–liquid” composite oxide layer and (2) the boundary between the surface oxide layer and the underlying matrix. At this stage, the rate-controlling step is the diffusion of oxidants through the oxide layer. As the temperature increases (~2000 °C), under sufficiently high temperatures and low oxygen partial pressures, the oxidation mode at the SiC/SiO2 interface shifts from passive to active oxidation [64], resulting in the direct formation of volatile species such as SiO and CO (Eqs. (8)–(12)) [16,29,60,65,66]. These gaseous oxidation products escape through the oxide layer as bubbles, leading to the development of a porous surface structure. However, in the MSI + PIP C/SiC composites, the high-density SiC matrix facilitates the rapid replenishment of these pores with newly formed SiO2, enhancing the integrity of the protective layer. Notably, at the interface between the oxide layer and the matrix, the carbon-rich and oxygen-rich pyrolyzed SiC is prone to undergo active oxidation and decomposition under certain combinations of temperature and oxygen partial pressure [61,67], which is detrimental to the oxidation resistance of the composite. As a result, in this interfacial region, the pyrolyzed SiC binder phase is completely consumed, and residual standard SiC particles tend to accumulate. The presence of these solid-state particles not only serves as structural fillers and supports at the microscopic level but also enhances the mechanical erosion resistance of the surface oxide layer under high-temperature conditions.

(iii) Thermal decomposition of the surface SiO2 layer at high temperatures. In contrast, the PIP C/SiC composite has several shortcomings under flame ablation conditions. First, the high porosity of the matrix hinders the formation of a uniform and dense SiO2 oxide layer, thereby increasing the number of pathways through which oxidative species can penetrate into the interior of the composite. Moreover, gaseous oxidation products such as SiO and CO tend to escape through the oxide layer, leading to the formation of a porous and loosely structured SiO2 layer. The resulting voids cannot be effectively filled by molten silica, leaving the fibers and matrix exposed. These exposed regions act as new reactive sites for oxidation, which in turn accelerates the thermochemical ablation of the composite under high-temperature conditions. Second, the carbon-rich and oxygen-rich pyrolyzed SiC matrix is prone to undergo active oxidation under specific combinations of temperature and oxygen partial pressure [67], which negatively affects the oxidation resistance of the material. Third, when the surface temperature of the PIP C/SiC sample reaches approximately 2000 °C, the low-viscosity SiO2 layer can be partially removed by high-velocity gas flows. Once the protective SiO2 film is lost, both the carbon fibers and the matrix become directly exposed to the high-temperature, oxygen-rich environment, resulting in accelerated oxidation and ablation.

MSI + PIP C/SiC has high-density structures where pyrolyzed SiC and standard SiC are uniformly interlocked within the preforming pores of the fibers, forming a dual-continuous fiber and matrix structure. The dense SiC matrix promoted the rapid development of a continuous oxide layer at elevated temperatures, effectively suppressing the thermal flux and limiting oxygen penetration into the composite interior.

Compared with pyrolyzed SiC, standard SiC particles produced via the MSI technique have greater thermal stability and oxidation resistance owing to their lower carbon and oxygen contents and higher degree of crystallinity. The formation of an interlocking structure between standard SiC particles and the pyrolyzed SiC phase within the matrix contributes to sustained performance stability across a defined temperature range. During ablation, standard SiC integrated with the SiO2 layer formed through the oxidation of pyrolyzed SiC, producing a composite structure analogous to reinforced concrete. This configuration substantially reduces the fluidity of the SiO2 phase, thereby enhancing resistance to mechanical erosion caused by high-velocity gas flow.

## 4 Conclusions

This study presents a rapid-cycle fabrication strategy for high-performance C/SiC composites by integrating MSI with PIP. The findings confirm that the MSI method enables the uniform incorporation of a high volume fraction (34 vol%) of standard SiC ceramic powders into the fiber preform—approximately 50% more than that achieved via conventional slurry impregnation techniques. As a result, the mechanical performance of the resulting composites was significantly enhanced, reaching a flexural strength of 421±31 MPa after just seven PIP cycles. Additionally, the fracture toughness and work of fracture of the MSI + PIP C/SiC (PIP-7) composites were nearly double those of their PIP-only C/SiC (PIP-11) counterparts, with values of 16.33±1.70 MPa·m1/2 and 5931±560 J/m2, respectively. These improvements are attributed to the denser matrix achieved through MSI, which promotes more effective stress distribution and energy absorption during crack propagation. Owing to the superior thermal stability and oxidation resistance of the incorporated standard SiC ceramics, the MSI + PIP composites demonstrated excellent resistance to oxidation and ablation across a temperature range of 1600–2000 °C. Oxyacetylene ablation tests at 2000 °C further confirmed that the inclusion of standard SiC particles markedly improved the resistance of the molten oxide layer to thermal and mechanical erosion. Under such extreme conditions, the primary ablation mechanisms involved active oxidation and thermal degradation of the SiC matrix. The MSI + PIP C/SiC composites produced in this work—characterized by high mechanical strength and robust ablation resistance—exhibit strong potential for advanced aerospace applications. Furthermore, this hybrid processing approach offers a versatile platform that can be extended to the fabrication of other high-performance composites, including ultrahigh-temperature ceramic (UHTC) composites and SiC/SiC systems.
