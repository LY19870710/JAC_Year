## 1 Introduction

Solid oxide cells (SOCs) are widely recognized as one of the most promising energy storage and conversion technologies because of their fuel flexibility, high efficiency, and environmental significance [1−5]. SOCs encompass both solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) and solid oxide electrolysis cells (SOECs). Among them, SOFCs are power supply systems that directly convert hydrogen (H2) and hydrocarbon fuels into their oxidation products and thus deliver electricity. The elevated operating temperatures of these materials (600–850 °C) make further integration into hybrid systems that combine heat and power generation possible, resulting in enhanced overall efficiency [6,7]. In contrast, SOECs utilize electric energy to convert steam (H2O), carbon dioxide (CO2) or their mixture directly into H2, CO, or syngas (H2+CO), respectively [1,2]. SOEC technologies can be integrated with a series of downstream chemical synthesis methods, enabling the recycling of CO2 into green fuels if renewable electricity is used, which results in further efficiency improvements over low-temperature electrolysis technologies [1]. A high working temperature is necessary to guarantee sufficient ionic conductivity of the ceramic electrolyte for high current densities of the cell [8,9]. For high-temperature SOCs, yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) with a cubic fluorite structure is the state-of-the-art electrolyte material while rare earth-doped lanthanum cobaltite oxides with ABO3 perovskite structures, such as (La,Sr)(Co,Fe)O3−*δ* (LSCF) and (La,Sr)CoO3−*δ* (LSC), currently remain the predominant air electrode materials, especially in commercial applications, owing to their excellent mixed ionic conductivity and electronic conductivity [1,5,10,11]. The sintering temperature of the air electrode is between 1000 and 1200 °C, and at such elevated temperatures, LSCF or LSC strongly reacts with YSZ, generating insulating SrZrO3 (SZO) and La2Zr2O7 phases at the interface of the electrode and electrolyte [5,10−12] (Fig. 1(a)). Similar issues also exist for other air electrode materials, such as (La,Sr)MnO3−δ (LSM) [13] and (Ba,Sr)(Co,Fe)O3−*δ* (BSCF) [14]. To solve this problem, the employment of a barrier layer between the air electrode and electrolyte has become a common practice in this field [5,15].

Ceria-based oxides, particularly gadolinia-doped ceria (GDC) and samarium-doped ceria (SDC), are among the most suitable classes of barrier layer materials because of their good chemical compatibility with lanthanum cobaltite oxide electrodes, high ionic conductivity, and suitable thermal expansion coefficients (TECs) [2−5,10−12,15−17]. The efficiency of the barrier layer largely depends on its microstructure. The thickness of the barrier layer should be minimized so that the overall resistance of the cell remains low, as ceria-based materials have lower electronic conductivity than electrode materials. Moreover, a dense structure is essential for limiting the diffusion of Sr from the electrode and thus its reaction with the YSZ electrolyte during high-temperature sintering and operation. In addition, larger post-sintering grain sizes are normally beneficial for reducing elemental diffusion through grain boundaries [5,12,15].

Currently, the most common way of fabricating the ceria-based barrier layer combines screen printing and conventional sintering. This process typically requires temperatures of at least 1300 °C to achieve an adequate but far from full density, which is insufficient to fully prevent Sr diffusion and the formation of insulating phases such as SZO (Fig. 1) [18]. The other problem of sintering the ceria-based barrier layer at high temperatures is that the barrier layers would already interact with YSZ, forming low-conductivity (Zr,Ce)O2-based solid solution with Kirkendall voids promoting Sr dopant migration [5,16], which can significantly degrade the performance of SOCs [15,19]. Specifically, Zr begins to diffuse above 1000 °C while Ce begins to diffuse above 1200 °C, and the solid solution becomes much thicker at higher sintering temperatures and lower oxygen pressures [11,20]. It is therefore necessary to develop appropriate approaches to densify the barrier layer at lower temperatures so that the diffusion of Sr dopants and the formation of (Zr,Ce)O2-based solid solutions can be prevented or sufficiently reduced [12]. In addition, the developed method should not cause chemical or structural modification of the barrier layer, which would adversely affect its ionic conductivity.

Notably, physical vapor deposition (PVD) techniques have been utilized to produce thin and dense barrier layers with relatively low-temperature annealing processes [5,22]. This state-of-the-art technique includes magnetron sputtering (MS) [23], plasma-beam sputtering deposition [24], physical laser deposition (PLD) [22], and electron beam PVD (EB-PVD) [25]. Interested readers can look at several recent reviews on this topic for details [26,27]. Nevertheless, compared with PVD methods, which are generally more time-consuming and expensive because of the need for high-vacuum and complex equipment, the conventional approach of sintering green-body barrier layers obtained by powder processing remains attractive for its low cost and easy scalability.

This review therefore focuses on the sintering-based methods for fabricating barrier layers, with an emphasis on strategies to lower processing temperatures and densify barrier layers. To increase the driving force of sintering, the synthesis methods for nano-powders are first reviewed, followed by the different methods for depositing a green-body barrier layer on the electrolyte, which are summarized to discuss the importance of the packing status of starting particles. The third section discusses the sintering process, emphasizing the effect of sintering aids on the densification temperature. This review explores post-sintering processes that further increase the barrier layer density. Finally, future research directions are discussed, including the potential of novel techniques such as flash sintering (FS) and cold sintering. By addressing the key steps in the densification of ceramic layers, this review also offers insights into the lower-temperature fabrication of other ceramic-based and multilayer structured devices, such as oxygen membrane reactors and solid-state batteries.

## 2 Synthesis and optimization of ceria oxide-based nano-powders

The size and morphology of the original powder greatly influence the sintering temperature of ceria-based barrier layer. Generally, powders with smaller grain sizes, larger specific surface areas, less agglomeration or unique morphologies exhibit enhanced sintering activity, making them more effective than conventional powders. Section 2 summarizes previous research on the synthesis of ceria-based powders via various methods, aiming to identify optimal approaches for producing powders with high sintering activity and reduced temperature requirements through comparative analysis.

Ceria oxide-based powders can be synthesized by both solid-state reactions (SSRs) and wet-chemical methods [28]. With respect to the densification of bulk ceria-based oxides, conventional SSR powders need high sintering temperatures of 1350 °C to achieve a relative density (RD) of 95.5% [28,29], whereas powders produced by wet-chemical methods can be fully densified to RD 98% at a lower temperature of 900 °C as a result of the reduced particle sizes [30]. Severe powder agglomeration and undesired particle morphologies are the main limiting factors for further lowering the densification temperature of nano-sized powders [28,31,32]. In recent years, several works have demonstrated possible approaches to enhance the sintering ability of nano-powders by alleviating powder agglomeration or modifying particle morphologies [31,33,34].

### 2.1 SSRs

Classical SSRs are designed to fabricate desired products by reactions through solid-state atomic diffusion for extended periods at high temperatures due to their limited contact interfaces with solid reactants [35,36]. As a result, powders prepared by the SSR method usually have larger particle sizes than those synthesized by wet-chemical methods, even with the same heating processes [37].

In recent years, studies using the SSR method have often directly mixed oxides or salts of the desired elements, with alcohol commonly serving as the liquid medium for ball milling [29,38,39]. The product obtained by ball milling is dried and calcined to obtain the final powder needed. This method allows for the direct introduction of dopants or sintering aids by adding their oxides or corresponding salts to the initial ball milling mixture [38]. In this case, it is often adopted to synthesize doped ceria oxides. However, it should be noticed that the sintering activity of powders prepared by this SSR method is not as high as that of some wet-chemical methods. Garcia *et al*. [29] prepared Ca-doped cerium oxide through this method. They mixed calcium carbonate powder with CeO2 powder in a high-energy mill and calcined the dried powder at 900 °C to obtain the final Ce0.9Ca0.1O1.95 product. The RD of the pellet sintered at 1300 °C was 83.3%, which needed to be sintered at 1350 °C to obtain final densification, with a corresponding RD of 95.5%. In contrast, the powder prepared by sol–gel method can reach an RD of 94.2% at 1300 °C. In addition, the total conductivities of sol–gel pellets tested at 600 °C were approximately three times greater than those of SSR pellets, which might be due to the differences in RDs, post-sintering grain sizes or morphologies between different synthesis methods.

The specific mechanochemical (MC) process based on SSRs is another effective approach to produce ultrafine ceria particles. The MC method utilizes chemical reactions during the milling of raw materials (usually related metal salts and bases), followed by calcination or annealing process at elevated temperatures. This post-processing step is essential to complete the SSR reaction, enhance crystallization, and reduce the defect concentration in the as-milled powder samples [36,40,41]. Both the type of precursor and annealing temperature affect the final particle size and properties [42]. Tsuzuki *et al*. [43] synthesized ceria by mechanochemically milling CeCl3 and NaOH powders and then calcined them at different temperatures. They pointed out that when extra NaCl diluents were added to the starting powder mixture, the SSR became in a steady state rather than during the combustion event. Owing to the separate effects of NaCl diluents and milder reaction conditions, the synthesized powder exhibited weak agglomeration. The ceria powder calcined at 500 °C had a nanoparticle size of ~5–40 nm, and the particle size increased with increasing calcination temperature. Yu *et al*. [42] synthesized ultrafine CeO2 nanoparticles with an average size of ~3 nm by simply mixing and grounding reactants at room temperature. They considered that the use of NaNO3 as a byproduct coated at the surface of CeO2 particles could prevent not only the grain growth but also further agglomeration of CeO2 particles [42]. Lu *et al*. [41] used an organic base to synthesize nanoscale CeO2 with increased purity. When NaOH was substituted with (C4H9)4NOH·30H2O, the impurity content of Na+ decreased from approximately 100 ppm to less than 1 ppm in the as-synthesized CeO2. With increasing annealing temperature, the average size of particles increased as expected, whereas the distortion of the crystal lattice decreased to reduce the defect concentration and enhance the crystallite quality [41].

Many studies employing MC synthesis for ceria fabrication have explored how to reduce the agglomeration of nano-powders. Most of them present X-ray diffraction (XRD) or transmission electron microscopy (TEM) results of the powder’s grain sizes, showing less agglomeration to some extent. However, the test results of the Brunner–Emmett–Teller (BET) or particle size analyzer (PSA) methods show that severe hard agglomeration still occurs compared with that of powders using wet chemical methods. The specific differences between them are illustrated in Table 1. Although these studies do not include the sintering properties of MC-synthesized ceria powders, it can be reasonably inferred that even with the modified MC SSR method, the sintering activity of the fabricated ceria-based oxide powders is worse than that of the materials prepared by wet chemical synthesis, which is discussed in the following Section 2.2.

### 2.2 Wet-chemical synthesis

Compared with SSR, wet-chemical synthesis offers precise control over reaction conditions, enabling the production of nanoparticles with tailored surface structures, shapes, phases, and particle sizes [44]. The most commonly used wet-chemical methods, including sol–gel, co-precipitation and hydrothermal treatment methods, are discussed below.

#### 2.2.1 Sol–gel method

While the sol–gel method might be limited by the relatively long reaction time [45], it is popular in both the laboratory and industry, mainly because of its lower processing temperature and ability to produce powders with a homogeneous composition and high purity [46−48]. Figure 2 illustrates the typical sol–gel processes. For the synthesis of CeO2-based particles, suitable metal salts and complexing agents are added to solvents to form a colloidal sol, and the sol further reacts into a connected porous gel [49]. The formed gel is often supercritically dried into aerogel, thermally dried into xerogel or freeze-dried into cryogel, with further calcination to obtain final powder products [47,48].

The precursor content, reaction temperature, and reaction time strongly influence the composition, size, and morphology of the sol–gel powder. Shaw et al*. [50] used cerium acetylacetonate and gadolinium acetylacetonate as precursors to investigate how the synthesis parameters impacted the nature and morphology of sol–gel products. They noted that increasing the hydrolysis ratio and reaction temperature led to an increase in the condensation rate and crystallinity of final powders. A small amount of acetic acid in the precursors mainly worked as a catalyst to increase the hydrolysis rate while a large quantity of acetic acid led to the formation of other phases other than the fluorite structure [50]. Furthermore, elemental doping can be easily achieved through the sol–gel method, which can largely affect the sintering behavior. Rehman *et al*. [51] employed the sol–gel method to synthesize transition element-doped GDC by simply adding transition elements into the precursors. They found that doped GDC powders improved the sintering ability in the order of Fe > Co > Cu > Mn. In addition, with increasing dopant concentration, the crystallite sizes of the sol–gel powders gradually decreased [52].

In addition to conventional sol–gel processes, various modified sol–gel approaches have been explored and have demonstrated positive effects on the densification of powders. Prasad *et al*. [53] synthesized nano-GDC powders by sol–gel thermolysis process. In this method, urea acted as a combustion fuel and PVA worked as a dispersing agent; a secondary fuel was both added in precursors, followed by conventional drying and calcination processes. This method allowed powders with a reduced crystalline size of 10 nm to be obtained, which exhibited improved sintering ability and increased ionic conductivity after sintering [53]. The ability of sol‒gel combustion method to reduce the crystal size compared with that of the normal sol‒gel method was also confirmed in another work [52]. Moreover, the morphology of sol–gel powders was a series of plate-like monoliths covered with smaller polyhedral particles while the combustion powders consisted of fine and uniform particles with undefined shapes. In a similar approach, Mercadelli *et al*. [54] successfully coupled the conventional sol‒gel method with infrared heating to synthesize ultrafine powders under mild conditions. Although the powder was micrometric, agglomeration was avoided owing to the absence of further calcination steps. As a result, sintering might be improved, leading to a higher ionic conductivity of the sintered material than that produced by the conventional sol‒gel method.

#### 2.2.2 Co-precipitation method

The co-precipitation method fabricates metal-oxide nanoparticles by adding a precipitating agent, such as NaOH, urea, or aqueous ammonia, to alkaline metal precursors to form metal hydroxides, which subsequently precipitate into metal oxides [45]. Co-precipitation methods are well known for their high yield and monodispersed particle sizes [45].

In this method, the hydrolysis of metal ions in an alkaline environment can be easily controlled by the temperature and pH of the bath, and ions in higher oxidation states are more easily hydrolyzed [55]. The specific surface area and crystal size of prepared powders are strongly affected by the use and type of surfactant materials and digestion time [56]. In general, the introduction of surfactant materials increases the specific surface area of powders by reducing the crystal size, and the dosage of surfactants should be moderate. Extending the digestion time can also increase the specific surface area and pore volume. As Heidari *et al*. [56] reported, when poly ethylene glycol (PEG) was used as a surfactant, the specific surface area of CeO2 powder was higher than that of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP). When the digestion time was extended to 8 h, a similar promoting effect was observed.

The crystallinity of the as-synthesized products is largely influenced by the type of precipitating agent [57]. The most commonly used agents for ceria-based powders are NaOH, urea, ammonia, ammonium carbonate, ammonium benzoate, ammonium hydroxide, and tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH) [57−61]. Spiridigliozzi *et al*. [57] demonstrated that the as-synthesized products precipitated by urea crystallized well (oxycarbonate-based phase), while the products precipitated by ammonia and TMAH had poor crystallinity (fluorite phase), and the products produced by ammonium carbonate were amorphous. They noted that the crystallization capacity of different precipitating agents was in the order of urea > ammonia > TMAH > ammonium carbonate. In addition, the type of precipitating agent has a great influence on the powder morphology and sintering activity. The calcined powders precipitated by ammonia and TMAH were hardly agglomerated and had low green density of pellets. As a result, many residual pores were present in the sintered pellets. Among these four precipitants, the pellet prepared from powders synthesized with ammonium carbonate exhibited the best densification behavior. That is to say, in general, the precipitating agent that is easy to crystallize the fluorite phase tend to produce powders that hardly agglomerate during calcination. Consequently, the calcined particles become nonuniform, and the green density of the pellet decreases, affecting the final sintering behavior. Therefore, to fabricate ceria-based materials that can be densified at lower temperatures, precipitating agents with limited fluorite-phase crystallization capacity, such as ammonium carbonate, are more suitable.

Many studies have applied the co-precipitation method to synthesize ceria-based powders with high sintering ability [61,62]. Ghaemi *et al*. [58] used ammonium benzoate as the precipitant of the cerium and gadolinium nitrate solution to produce GDCs with particle sizes of 5–15 nm, which reached an RD of 98.43% when sintered at 950 °C and a total electrical conductivity as high as 5.90×10−2 S·cm−1 when tested at 750 °C without the addition of any sintering additive. Hari *et al*. [59] also employed ammonium hydroxide to precipitate cerium and gadolinium nitrate solutions. The obtained GDC powders with particle sizes of 8–12 nm reached RD of 97% when sintered at 950 °C and had an ionic conductivity of 1.64×10−2 S·cm−1 when tested at 600 °C. By combining the ammonium hydroxide co-precipitation method with the supercritical ethanol drying route, Liang *et al.* [30] further reduced the average particle size of GDC to ~5.6 nm with weak agglomeration, and these particles reached an RD of above 98% when sintered at 900 °C. Joh *et al*. [60] used ammonium carbonate as the precipitant to fabricate GDC particles with a particle size of ~100 nm and obtained a 96% RD when sintered at 1300 °C. Tok *et al*. [28] were able to prepare GDC nanoparticles of 30–50 nm using ammonium carbonate as the precipitant, and the particles achieved a > 99% RD when sintered at 1150 °C. They proposed that room temperature aging and air drying allowed the fabrication of weaker agglomerated particles [28]. Thus, the studies mentioned above verify that the use of ammonium carbonate as a precipitating agent can result in better sintered pellets.

The crystallinity of as-synthesized product is influenced not only by the type of precipitating agent but also by the chemical or physical synthesis conditions. Taking ammonium carbonate as an example [33], the mixing speed between the cation solution and precipitating agent, and the dosage of precipitating agent, could affect the crystallinity of as-synthesized powders. The fast mixing and high agent dosage tend to lead to coprecipitation with the cerium carbonate octahydrate phase, which would result in hard agglomerates during calcination. The other samples tend to be amorphous, and the calcined powders are well densified compared with the hard agglomerated sample.

It is also possible to achieve a novel morphology for ceria-based particles by the co-precipitation method. Moraes *et al*. [61] used NaOH as the precipitant and obtained two-dimensional GDC nanosheets without any surfactant or template. The preparation processes are shown in Fig. 3. Hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA), which functioned as a nucleating agent, was dropped into cerium and gadolinium nitrate solution and the formed solution was placed at 10 °C for 4 days. Ethylene glycol (EG) and NaOH were then mixed into the solution. Through a series of processes, they obtained GDC nano powders with a two-dimensional flake structure. The obtained powders were deposited on the YSZ electrolyte by spin coating. Compared with commercial GDC powders sintered at the same temperature, the nanosheets with a high specific surface area had higher sintering activity, and the maximum power density (*P*max) of the full cell increased from 45 to 116 mW·cm−2 when tested at 800 °C.

Torrens et al*. [63] compared the sintering ability of GDC powders synthesized by typical SSR and co-precipitation methods. The powders synthesized by SSR had a range of powder shapes and post-sintering grain sizes, whereas those synthesized by co-precipitation route had a predominantly flat plate-like shape and an even post-sintering grain size distribution. They also found that the sintered pellets of SSR powders showed inferior performance in terms of both their sintering ability and ionic conductivity even with similar initial particle sizes and the same sintering temperature [63]. Compared with that of the co-precipitated sample, the ionic conductivity of the SSR pellet was more than six times lower, which could be related to its much lower RD, as its larger post-sintering grain size should reduce the fraction of high-resistivity grain boundaries.

#### 2.2.3 Hydrothermal treatment

Hydrothermal treatment can be defined as heterogeneous reactions in aqueous solvents or mineralizers under high-pressure and high-temperature conditions [45,64,65]. Its advantages include simple operation, low cost, minimal material loss [45,66], low aggregation, and high crystallinity of the synthesized nano-powders [32,67]. It can be employed to fabricate all unary, binary, ternary, and quaternary metal oxide nano-powders [45], as well as the high-entropy oxides [68] that have received intensive attention recently.

Zhang *et al*. [69] used KOH or NaOH bases to synthesize ceria nanocrystals with a crystallite size of 2.6–6.9 nm by the alcohothermal synthesis method, as shown in Fig. 4(a). An attractive feature of hydrothermal treatment is the ability to fabricate powders with selective surface structures [34,44]. Dan *et al*. [34] once used hydrothermal method to prepare CeO2 nanocubes with predominantly (100) surfaces (Fig. 4(b)), while obtained crystallites by conventional citric acid–nitrate method with (111) surfaces. They dropped the ceria nitrate solution into the NaOH solution at a certain ratio and kept the solution at 180 °C for 24 h for hydrothermal reactions, followed by calcination at 400 °C for 2 h. The collected powders had a particle size of approximately 50 nm in a cube shape, whereas conventional powders were 30–40 nm in size with an irregular shape. The densification temperature of CeO2 nanotubes was reduced to 1200 °C from at least 1400 °C for conventional powders with similar particle sizes. Density functional theory (DFT) results revealed that the surface energy of the (100) crystal plane for CeO2 was 0.455 eV·A−2 while the energy of (111) plane was 0.084 eV·A−2. It is proposed that the higher surface energy promoted the rearrangement of CeO2 grains and increased their sintering activity.

*
Fig. 4TEM images of (a) ceria nanocrystals fabricated by alcohothermal synthesis method and (b)microstructure of CeO2 nanocubes. Reproduced with permission from Ref. [69] for (a), © American Chemical Society 2003; Ref. [34] for (b), © Elsevier Ltd. 2019.

Furthermore, hydrothermal treatment is beneficial for reducing particle agglomeration because of its ability to achieve high crystallinity at lower temperatures under pressure. When a subsequent hydrothermal treatment is introduced to the as-synthesized SDC coprecipitates, a high-temperature calcination step can be avoided, and well-crystallized nanoparticles with a size of 10–30 nm can be achieved [31]. In addition, this positive effect on size and sintering was minimally affected by the type of mineralizer used in the hydrothermal treatment, regardless of whether the environment was acidic, neutral or basic. However, the mineralizer type did affect the electrical properties, with samples synthesized in alkaline and neutral environments exhibiting higher electrical conductivity within a certain temperature range [32]. Furthermore, when the dopant concentration of Sm3+ increased, the particle sizes slightly increased, and the agglomeration phenomenon became much more apparent. When the doped concentration increased to 30 mol%, a few nanowires were triggered with a length of ~400 nm [31].

If we combine hydrothermal synthesis with microwave-assisted sintering, the reaction time can be reduced while increasing the crystallization driving force and maintaining a more uniform temperature field [70−72]. Compared with commercial powders, nano-powders prepared by this method have a uniform morphology, narrow particle size distribution, and higher sintering activity [71,72]. Carregosa et al*. [70] prepared CeO2 nanoparticles by this method and successfully reduced the sintering temperature to 1200 °C. In this case, the post-sintering grain size and RD of the pellets were comparable to those of commercial powders sintered at 1500 °C. Moreover, owing to the lower sintering temperature, the amount of Si impurities on the grain boundaries introduced during the high-temperature sintering process was reduced. The pellet prepared by this method had a high electrical conductivity, which was 1.98×10−5 S·cm−1 when tested at 500 °C, while that of commercial powder sintered at 1500 °C was only 3.3×10−6 S·cm−1.

#### 2.2.4 Spray drying method

The spray drying method is used to fabricate ceramic particles by spraying mostly water-based precursors into a concurrent flow of heated air [73,74]. The precursor atomizes and rapidly evaporates, forming ceramic particles that are typically followed by a calcination process to fully decompose nitrate precursors, remove residual moisture, and enhance particle crystallinity [74]. The spray drying method is versatile, inexpensive, and has high yields, and the fabricated powders have a homogenous composition and a high specific surface area [73,74].

For spray drying method, the particle morphology is related to precursors and their decomposition behavior [75]. During drying, the solute following the surface precipitation mechanism forms hollow and porous particles, while the solute following the volume precipitation mechanism forms solid spherical particles [75,76]. The ceria powders fabricated by spray drying are frequently hollow or porous due to their fast evaporation rate and poor solute diffusion [75,77,78]. To flexibly control particle morphology, several approaches, such as increasing evaporation time [79], decreasing solute diffusion distance (smaller droplets or particles) [80], and precursor modification (using metal-organic salts [81,82] or salts with high solubility [75]), have been reported. Shih *et al*. [75] compared three precursors with different solubilities in water in terms of the morphology of synthesized ceria particles. They pointed that the precursor with low solubility resulted in hollow concave particles, whereas a precursor with high solubility resulted in solid spherical particles and hollow spherical particles. The formation mechanism of different morphologies is shown in Fig. 5. Notably, the specific surface area of hollow concave particles (62.03 m2·g−1) was two times larger than that of hollow spherical particles (30.89 m2·g−1), even if the two kinds of particles were nearly in the same size.

Spray-drying products are agglomerative, which is a result of multiple nucleation sites in super-saturated droplets formed by water evaporation during the spray drying process [73,74]. Ideally, one nanoparticle agglomerate should be generated from one droplet of the atomized precursor, which indicates that the particle size depends on the concentration of starting solution and atomization degree (the size of droplets) and additional agglomeration between agglomerates might occur as electrostatic interactions arise from charged primary droplets. The agglomerate sizes and particle size distributions increase with increasing calcination temperature [73]. Blennow et al*. [73] used a commercial spray dryer to fabricate Ce0.9Gd0.1O1.95 powders with particle sizes between 0.5 and 3 μm. By introducing a custom-built and inexpensive spray dryer, Sharma *et al*. [74] were able to produce Ce1−*x*Pr*x*O2 (*x* = 0.03–0.20) nano-powders with particle sizes between 22 and 33 nm, which might be the result of improved atomization effectiveness.

Table 1 summarizes the particle sizes of powders synthesized by various methods and, where available, compares their sintering densification properties. In general, a lower calcination temperature results in a smaller particle size, which in principle is beneficial for improving densification at lower temperatures. A more accurate correlation between the particle size and sintering ability of ceria barrier layer materials is challenging because of two aspects. First, many studies do not investigate the sintering properties of the fabricated nano-powders. Second, the complexity arises from the various types of particle sizes reported in Refs. [41,43,73], as different characterization methods yield different sizes for the same powder owing to their distinct analytical principles. Strictly speaking, X-ray diffraction (XRD) provides information on the crystal or grain size, which is often not the particle size due to agglomeration. SEM/transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) can give similar results, as they are both capable of measuring the size of hard agglomerates, although it is not uncommon to find that the grain size measured from SEM/TEM images is reported as the particle size. Therefore, attention needs to be paid when the particle sizes analyzed from XRD and SEM/TEM are very similar unless the powder fabrication does not involve any process at elevated temperatures that could induce sintering of the particles. Conversely, PSA, which is based on light scattering, measures the size of soft agglomerates and uses the longest axis to determine the mean particle diameter, often reporting much larger apparent sizes. Considering these limitations, the data from Table 1 suggest that the sintering ability correlates better with the BET-derived particle size or, equivalently, BET values, which is reasonable since soft agglomerates retain exposed surface areas that are active for sintering. In addition, although very fine particles can be produced without the calcination step or can be calcined at lower temperatures, the higher-temperature calcination can completely remove the residual solvents at the powder surface [30] and improve crystallinity, thus usually benefiting the ionic conductivity. Therefore, a more ideal situation is to retain a smaller particle size after adequate heat treatment. In this regard, the modified co-precipitation method [30] appears to be most effective, which produces particles of hard agglomeration as small as their crystallites, achieving only 5 nm after calcination at 600 °C. As a result, densification to an RD of 98% was achieved at temperatures as low as 900 °C [30].

Finally, it is worth emphasizing that the mean particle size or BET is not the only important parameter for evaluating the sintering ability of nano-powders. The size distribution and morphology of the particle can also play crucial roles. The experimental data of Spiridigliozzi *et al*. [57] revealed that powders with higher BET values (smaller BET-derived mean particle sizes) but a broader size distribution and more irregular shapes led to a lower packing density of the green body and, ultimately, worse densification.

## 3 Deposition of the green body barrier layer

Despite the inherent low sintering ability of ceria-based materials [17,83−87], Table 1 shows that densification of ceria nano-powders to an RD of 98% can be achieved at 900 °C under free sintering conditions. However, green body layers deposited on electrolytes by conventional methods such as screen printing and dip coating typically exhibit lower packing density. In addition, sintering of the barrier layer on dense electrolytes has the additional issue of constrained sintering, where shrinkage of the barrier layer is restricted and induced stresses could lead to cracking of the layers. As a result, it is more difficult for the barrier layer to achieve high densities with sintering at lower temperatures than with free sintering of the bulk material. Modifications based on conventional methods and other methods, including electrophoretic deposition (EPD) and spray pyrolysis deposition, have been found to be useful for alleviating this problem, thus densifying the barrier layer at the lowest possible temperature.

Notably, the different deposition methods also affect the optimal sintering conditions for the green-body barrier layer. On one hand, sufficiently high temperature is needed to ensure an adequate density as a Sr diffusion barrier. On the other hand, ceria-based layers react with YSZ when the sintering temperature is higher than 1200 °C, forming (Zr,Ce)O2 solid solution with poor ionic conductivity [15,19]. However, which factor is more important appears to depend on the specific fabrication method. For example, Wilde *et al*. found that for screen-printed layers, the (Zr,Ce)O2-based solid solution formed a continuous ID layer at relatively high sintering temperatures, which significantly reduced Sr diffusion and overcompensated for the decreased conductivity of (Zr,Ce)O2 solid solution [88,89]. As a result, sintering at 1400 °C resulted in better performance than sintering at 1100 or 1250 °C. In contrast, for spin-coated barrier layers, Lyu *et al*. [89] reported that sintering at lower temperatures to ensure limited formation of the solid solution was more essential than to reduce the Sr migration and SZO formation. In their opinion, the best sintering temperature of barrier layer was 1200 °C compared to 1250 or 1300 °C. The above inconsistency is likely related to the difference in thickness, density, and morphology of the sintered layer even at similar temperatures for the two deposition methods, which largely depend on the nature of the ink/precursors used.

### 3.1 Conventional methods and their modifications

Conventional methods such as screen printing [21,88], spin coating [89−91], tape casting [92], and dip coating [93,94] are commonly used for the fabrication of barrier layers in their green body state.

Screen printing is a simple, flexible and cost-effective method for fabricating SOC components [95,96]. Screen-printed inks usually consist of powder, binder, solvent, and dispersant. The quality of screen-printed films is influenced by ink composition, ink rheology, and screen-printed parameters [95−97]. Achieving an optimal solid content in the ink is crucial for enhancing film densification. Otherwise, a low solid content can result in wet films with low particle packing density, which may cause delamination or cracking after drying [96]. The binder concentration should also be optimized to fabricate relatively dense and high-quality barrier layers. Too high binder concentration causes a relatively thick and porous film while too low concentration might induce film breaking [98]. As a result, the combination of higher solid content and lower binder content is usually used to improve the layer density [96,98]. In addition, the dispersant is also important to prevent particle agglomeration over time, thereby stabilizing the ink and fabricating defect-free films. The dosage of dispersant is commonly related to the particle surface area [96].

Spin coating is another widely used method for the deposition of green body barrier layer on electrolytes. In spin coating, the balance of the centrifugal force, viscous force, and surface tension promotes the retention of a thin residual film on the substrate [99−101]. The precursor viscosity and spin speed are two main parameters for film fabrication. For example, the concentration of the organic binder significantly impacts the GDC slurry: excessive binder increases the electrolyte impedance, while insufficient binder fails to form an effective barrier layer [102]. However, the rotational duration usually has little impact on the film thickness. Solvent evaporation is critical for achieving high-quality films, as a high evaporation rate can result in the formation of unreliable and fragile films [103,104]. Although the spin coating method wastes precursors at a proportion of more than 90% [103], one of its advantages is that it can fabricate thinner barrier layers than screen printing, decreasing the thickness from several microns to below 1 μm, which was found to reduce the cell ohmic resistance (*R*Ω) considerably for anode-supported cells [89].

Neither screen printing nor spin coating could obtain fully dense barrier layers at regular sintering temperatures (1100–1400 °C), as shown in Fig. 6. The film has a typical coral-like porous structure after sintering, while the coral branches become thicker and the particle sizes become larger with increasing sintering temperature.

Other conventional methods, such as tape casting and dip coating, have also been explored for fabricating barrier layers. In dip coating technology, film densification can be modified by the constitution of suspensions, which also include powders, solvents, binders, and dispersants [94]. The key parameters for layer fabrication include solid content, binder concentration, and final sintering temperature. The thickness of dip-coated layers can be adjusted by controlling the pulling rate. López-Robledo et al*. [94] used dip coating to fabricate barrier layers of micro-tubular cells. They employed Gd0.1Ce0.9O2−*x* as the initial powder, ethanol as the solvent, polyvinyl butyral as the binder, and phosphate ester as the dispersant to prepare dip coating precursors. The pulling rate of the precursor was set within 1.5–4.5 mm·s−1. The GDC layer sintered at 1300 °C had a porosity of 63% while 53% for the layer sintered at 1400 °C. Wang *et al*. [92] employed tape casting to fabricate assembled multilayers. Films of the GDC interlayer, electrolyte (scandia-stabilized zirconia (ScSZ)), anode functional layer, and anode substrate were cast onto a glass plate in turn by the “doctor blade” method. After drying for several minutes, the green sample was removed from the glass substrate and co-sintered at 1400 °C for 4 h. Due to the high sintering temperature, the GDC interlayer was firmly attached to the electrolyte and showed a decent RD. However, the formation of the Ce–Zr ID solid solution layer was significant, with a large thickness of approximately 3 μm, which may be one of the reasons for the high *R*Ω measured in this work.

Therefore, at sintering temperatures below 1200 °C, which is necessary to suppress the formation of solid solutions, conventionally deposited green body layers are difficult to be densified sufficiently. In this case, some approaches have been explored to improve the densification of the barrier layer at reduced temperatures based on above conventional methods.

The first approach involves modifying the material system to be deposited. Owing to the substrate constraint and the high-volume shrinkage of chemical particles derived from solution precursors in conventional spin coating, the barrier is likely to form anisotropic process flaws in the thickness direction and have a high residual porosity [91], which is a very common problem in constrained sintering. To address this, Oh *et al*. [91] reported that when glycerin was added as a drying control chemical additive (DCCA), it could effectively alleviate the drying stress of the solution and avoid the formation of dry cracks, even if there was physical inhomogeneity (such as surface roughness and thickness changes). They also added a small amount of GDC nano-powder to the precursor solution, which made the internal stress distribute relatively evenly, thus mitigating the constraint effect. In their experiment [91], a chemical solution containing 5 vol% GDC nanoparticles of ~10 nm was deposited on the top of YSZ electrolyte and then sintered at 1050 °C. The barrier layer could be sintered to a relatively high density with no significant process defects at this rather low sintering temperature, which also prevents significant ID between YSZ and GDC and reduces the interface resistance. The process was demonstrated with a relatively large cell of 10 cm × 10 cm. The microstructure is shown in Fig. 7. *P*max of this full cell reached 810 mW·cm−2 at 650 °C, indicating good electrochemical performance. However, it should be noted that when the grains of GDC, which were crystallized from the nitrate precursor, grew to sizes similar to those of the added GDC nano-powders, the effect of reducing the constraint sintering issue would disappear.

There was once a patent emphasizing another impact of initial powders with different particle sizes [105]. The larger powder controlled the overall sintering rate to avoid rapid shrinking of the green layer to reduce the number of sintering macropores. Moreover, fine particles can fill gaps between larger particles and promote densification. In the patent, the powders were blended at a specific ratio of 3 : 7 (nanoparticle : macroparticle) to form a single or bilayer structure. When coupled with a sintering aid, this approach can be used to fabricate a defect-free barrier layer and reduce the sintering temperature down below 1250 °C.

In another study, Hwang et al*. [90] reported that replacing the particle slurry with a gelatin hydrogel-based precursor during spin coating could prevent the formation of constrained pores for the fabrication of dense barrier layers. This hydrogel-assisted approach effectively mitigates issues associated with constrained sintering by increasing the density and uniformity of the GDC film at lower sintering temperatures. In their work, gelatin type A, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), cerium nitrate hexahydrate, and gadolinium nitrate hexahydrate were used in the preparation of ionic precursors. The functional side chains in gelatin molecules interacted with metal ions, making gelatin molecules a helpful dispersant as well as chelating agent to help suppress the random seed agglomeration. After optimization of the solution composition, the GDC film sintered at 1200 °C for 3 h exhibited no evident defects. The full cell with dense GDC layer had *P*max of 1.21 W·cm−2 when tested at 750 °C, which was 1.61-fold greater than that of the porous GDC. By lowering sintering temperatures and minimizing microstructural defects, this method has a high potential in large-scale applications because of its cost effectiveness and simple fabrication processes.

The second approach is related to the employment of external pressure as the additional driving force for sintering. An example was proposed by Kim *et al*. [93], who combined dip coating with cold isostatic pressing (CIP). They dip-coated YSZ and GDC bilayers onto the anode-supported substrate and then applied isostatic pressure to the coated samples, which were further co-sintered at 1250 °C. SEM images and EDXS profiles of the line element distributions along the GDC, YSZ, and anode functional layers at different isostatic pressures are shown in Figs. 8(a) and 8(b), respectively. With increasing isostatic pressure (up to 300 MPa), the density and adhesion properties of the bilayers were further enhanced. When the applied isostatic pressure was above 200 MPa, dense GDC layers effectively suppressed the formation of SZO at the interface. *P*max reached 1.251 W·cm−2 at 650 °C for samples pressed at 300 MPa [93]. The authors considered that this simple, time-saving modified method had a huge potential to be commercialized.

### 3.2 EPD method

In the EPD process, the material to be deposited consists of charged particles dispersed in a liquid medium, which are attracted and deposited onto a conductive substrate with an opposite charge under an electric field. Depending on the charge of particles, EPD can be classified into cathodic EPD and anodic EPD. EPD has been widely used in the processing of advanced ceramic materials and coatings because of its attractive advantages, including simple equipment, short reaction time, flexibility of matrix shapes, and containing little or no organics in green products and adjustable coating quality by simply changing the deposition time and applied potential. Compared with screen printing and spin coating methods, one limitation of EPD seems to be that water cannot be used as the liquid medium because of the electrolysis of water into hydrogen and oxygen, therefore reducing the coating quality [106]. As a result, the most common liquid medium used in EPD bath is ethanol [107,108]. The application of EPD to fabricate thin film layers in SOCs was reviewed recently by Kalinina *et al*. [109], and several more recent reports have focused on the deposition of barrier layers using EPD [107,108,110,111]. Before EPD, a conductive film such as polypyrrole (PPy) or platinum needs to be deposited on the sample surface. The cross-sectional images of symmetric cells with the spin-coated GDC layer and EPD layer are shown in Fig. 9. As the packing density in the green body state is not restricted by rheology, such as for the inks and solutions used in conventional methods, the barrier layers deposited by EPD could be densified at 1300 °C and have a relatively high density and good adhesion [107].

Direct current EPD has been more commonly used for interlayer deposition, but the bubble evolution caused by proton reduction could deteriorate the coating quality [108,112]. To overcome this problem, alternating current EPD is employed. Hu et al*. [108] used it to obtain a denser GDC layer after sintering at 1250 °C with the addition of 2 mol% FeO1.5 as the sintering aid. With such improvements, the sintered EPD films were denser than those obtained by conventional methods at similar temperatures.

### 3.3 Spray pyrolysis deposition method

Spray pyrolysis is a reliable synthesis method not only for fabricating ceria-based submicron-to-nano particles but also for their dense layers. Figure 10 illustrates the procedures of general spray pyrolysis deposition. The metal salts are dissolved in the liquid solvent to form a precursor solution. The solution is then fed into a nebulizer to be further atomized into microdroplets. These liquid-phase droplets can subsequently decompose into expected powders or thin layers deposited on a heatable substrate [113]. During spray pyrolysis, it typically produces tiny particles with large specific surface areas, which provide more reaction sites. Additionally, the precursor solution rapidly evaporates, and the precursors quickly react and crystallize during the sintering process. The increasing number of reaction sites and rapid reaction rate promote sintering between particles. They are able to lower the sintering temperature to approximately 900 °C [3,36,114−117]. It is also beneficial for highly shortening the reaction time, showing enormous potential for scaling-up from the laboratory to industrial applications [36,116,118].

*
Fig. 10Schematic for typical spray pyrolysis processes. Reproduced with permission from Ref. [113], © IEEE 2011.

The quality of spray-pyrolysis products depends on many processing variables, such as precursor composition or concentration, flow rate, distance between nozzle and substrate, substrate temperature, single deposition thickness, heat treatment temperature, and type of carrier gas [3,36,114,115]. In particular, for fabricating high-quality layers with fewer defects, the substrate temperature should not be too low to sufficiently evaporate precursor microdroplets or too high to trigger the Leidenfrost effect, resulting in the loss of partial droplets and the formation of heavily cracked layers [114]. When the substrate temperature was approximately 410 °C, the minimum cracks formed in the film was 36%, whereas at 350 °C, the discontinuous film was covered by 63% cracks, and at 450 °C, it was covered by 44% cracks. A solution flow rate higher than 4 mL·h−1 resulted in significant crack formation, but samples at 3 mL·h−1 had only 2% cracks. Similarly, continuous layers with cracks less than 2% were not achievable if the film thickness was greater than 400 nm. Thus, to obtain less-defect films, the substrate temperature needs moderate setting, and the solution flow rate or the single deposition thickness should not exceed a limiting value.

Szymczewska et al*. [114] employed multiple-step spray pyrolysis deposition to fabricate thin layers of GDC on YSZ electrolyte. Each deposition thickness was 400 nm, followed by annealing at only 900 °C to release accumulated stresses. They proposed that dense layers with a thickness of 400 nm were adequate to suppress the degradation of *R*Ω while 800 nm layers could achieve the most minimal total resistance based on electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data [114]. Another team [115] deposited GDC layers on the YSZ electrolyte with a thickness of 100 nm and annealed the sample at 900 °C after each deposition to fabricate a 200 nm thin film to release accumulated stresses. The substrate temperature, precursor flow rate, and spraying pressure were set at 400 °C, 1 cm3·h−1, and 2 bar, respectively. The symmetric cell with the barrier layers sintered at 900 °C had the lowest polarization resistance (*R*p) of 7 mΩ·cm2 while the cell without GDC barrier layers had *R*p of up to 477 mΩ·cm2. dos Santos-Gómez [3] deposited both a dense GDC barrier layer and a porous LSCF cathode sequentially on YSZ electrolyte via spray pyrolysis. During the deposition of GDC layers, the substrate temperature was set at 400 °C, the flow rate 20 mL·h−1, and the pressure 2 bar. The GDC barrier layers were annealed at 650 °C for 2 h to eliminate residual species, and then, the porous LSCF cathode was deposited on the GDC layers, followed by sintering at 800 °C. The SEM and EDXS cross-sectional images of the as-prepared cell and the cell after thermal annealing at 800 °C for 250 h to simulate the cell operation conditions are displayed in Fig. 11. Almost no SZO formed in the as-prepared samples, and less SZO formation was observed after annealing at 800 °C for 250 h compared to airbrushed samples, of which the barrier layers were sintered at 1100 °C. ### 4.1 General considerations and basic properties

The use of sintering aid is a common approach to enhance densification by forming a liquid phase at or before sintering temperatures to infiltrate faster grain-boundary diffusion. An effective sintering aid should have slightly lower solubility in CeO2 to facilitate preferential accumulation at grain boundaries without the formation of undesirable secondary phases. For most sintering aids, sublimation or evaporation should occur at temperatures below the final sintering temperature to prevent damage to cell performance [119], as many aids are relatively detrimental to the conductivity of ceramic materials [120,121]. However, it should be noted that there are also a few sintering aids, such as lithium oxide, that can promote ionic conductivity by changing the crystal structure [122]. Sintering aids that can promote conductivity may not necessarily need to be sublimated or evaporated if their existence does not have other negative effects.

Many compounds are used as sintering aids for densification of ceria-based materials, but most of them are fourth and fifth transition metals (TMs) or their compounds. Early research focused mainly on TM oxides such as copper or cobalt oxide. One of the reasons is that the size of TM cations is similar to that of Ce4+ [123], which is the predominant oxidation state of cerium in ceria-based materials. This ensures optimal ionic size compatibility with the TM sintering aids and facilitates effective densification without significant lattice distortion. In addition, the addition of these oxides probably results in higher electronic conductivity at the electrolyte surface, which promises a greater oxygen exchange rate [124]. The TM oxides include Bi, Co, Cu, Fe, Li, Mn, and Zn oxides [51,123,125−127]. Their specific influences on sintered ceria-based materials, such as porosity, grain size, and electrical conductivity, have been the focus of research.

Addition of suitable sintering aid can result in the densification of the barrier layer at a relatively low sintering temperature, which is normally associated with an increase in the grain size and an improvement in the adherence of the barrier layers and the air electrode with the electrolyte [12,120,122,123,126,128−131]. As a result, *R*Ω and *R*p of the cell can be reduced, and *P*max can be increased [123,125,132]. The most suitable concentration of sintering aids in specific situations is crucial and needs to be controlled carefully [133].

On the other hand, too high concentration of sintering aids can lead to the opposite effect, which decreases the conductivity [117,120,127,128,134,135]. The causes include forming unwanted impurity phases and reducing the ionic conductivity by hindering oxygen ion migration. It is also possible that sintering additives, such as high concentrations of Mn oxides, promote reactions between the barrier layer and YSZ electrolyte, resulting in the formation of detrimental phases at their interface [128,129].

An additional aspect that needs attention is the redox reactions that may be induced by sintering aids [121,136]. For example, the addition of Li ions as a sintering aid causes trivalent Ce ions to be oxidized to tetravalent ions [121]. Mn-based sintering aids are also reported to cause changes in the valence state of Ce ions [136,137]. Similarly, Fig. 12 shows that the doped CuO may react to form Cu2O, which produces trapped oxygen in the closed pores of this layer, thereby increasing the porosity [129].

#### 4.2.1 Cobalt-based sintering aids

Studies have found that a certain amount of cobalt oxide can effectively promote the densification of ceria-based materials [125,132,138]. Kleinlogel *et al*. [128] investigated the sintering and electrochemical performance of Ce0.8Gd0.2O2−*x* nano-powders doped with a small amount (3O4. The addition of 0.5 mol% cobalt oxide as the sintering aid was the most effective for final densification, which helped the sample reach > 98% RD at only 900 °C, approximately 300 °C lower than that of the sample without doping. However, excessive doping cannot result in significant improvement. In addition, the addition of Co3O4 allowed the preparation of flat and defect-free bilayers, although in a very narrow temperature range of approximately 1030 °C [139].

There are also reports focused on the effects of cobalt salts as sintering aids on the densification of barrier layers. Co(NO3)2 has proven to be a good sintering aid for obtaining almost dense GDC barrier layers in a study via *in situ* hydrothermal modification compared with a previous study that did not use sintering aids [125,140]. Compared with the addition of the pristine aid, the addition of the sintering aid helps increase the grain sizes of the particles on the GDC surface. This optimal hydrothermal modification with reaction temperature of 180 °C and time of 48 h performed well in the 1200 h long-term stability test [125]. The modified GDC barrier layer effectively reduces *R*Ω and *R*p, improving the electrical performance.

#### 4.2.2 Copper-based sintering aids

Several oxides of Cu are very effective as sintering aids over a wide range of sintering temperatures [12,128,129]. It induces the formation of high-temperature liquid phases to promote viscous flow sintering, which greatly reduces sintering temperatures of ceria-based materials [12,120,128,129]. A five-cell stack fabricated with a densified GDC barrier layer using CuO as the sintering aid also showed excellent stability when operated at 750 °C, with a degradation rate of only 0.004%/kh [120]. Zhang *et al*. [133] found that when the CuO content was below 2.14 mol%, the sintering shrinkage rate was largely accelerated, and the ductility and grain growth rate increased with increasing CuO content. The rate at which the samples shrunk increased significantly as the sintering temperature approached 1000–1050 °C. However, sintered balls with CuO concentrations greater than 0.75 mol% were defective, which were likely to hinder the movement of oxygen anions, reducing ionic conductivity [120]. On the other hand, another study reported that a relatively dense sample could be obtained by adding 3 at% Cu and sintering at 950 °C, although the defect formation was not discussed [129]. The study using impregnation and SSRs to fabricate a Cu-doped GDC barrier layer sintered at 1250 °C found that its porosity was less than 5%. In addition, the electrochemical performance of the multilayered system improved [130].

#### 4.2.3 Iron-based sintering aids

Studies have found that Fe2O3 or Fe-based salts as sintering aids can improve the densification of GDC barrier layers in planar ceramic fuel cells [12,123,131,136]. This leads to enhanced cell performance. Compared with those without the addition of Fe2O3, cells with Fe2O3 as the sintering aid presented a 24% increase in *P*max when tested at 800 °C. The cells also showed no significant degradation after stability and thermal cycling tests, highlighting the potential of GDC with Fe2O3 as a promising diffusion barrier layer [131].

Yu *et al*. [12] dissolved Fe(NO3)3·6H2O and Ce(NO3)3·6H2O to make Fe-doped GDC (GFDC). Compared with 1450 °C for pure GDC, the sintering temperature for achieving similar densities could be reduced by up to 350 °C for 1 mol% GFDC. Table 2 shows a comparison of the densification effects of Cu and Fe. Both additives were beneficial at the studied temperatures of 1100–1300 °C, while Cu oxide appeared to be more effective at the lowest temperature of 1100 °C. The greater effectiveness of the Cu additive than Fe, Co, and Zn additives was also reported in another study [51].

#### 4.2.4 Bismuth-based sintering aids

To achieve a dense interlayer (RD > 99.7%) and prevent possible decrease in ionic conductivity due to the addition of sintering additives, one approach is to use sintering aids that are fully sublimated during sintering [126,137,141,142]. It has been reported that bismuth oxide (Bi2O3) used as a sintering aid can melt at 817 °C and sublimate at approximately 1000 °C, suggesting Bi2O3 an excellent material for fabricating non-residual pure interlayers at lower temperatures [126,137,142].

Unlike conventional doping methods to introduce a sintering aid, Lee *et al*. [126] screen-printed a separate layer of Bi2O3 on top of the GDC barrier layer, which allowed Bi2O3 to penetrate GDC and act as a sintering aid during the co-sintering step. The detailed process is shown in Fig. 13. First, GDC was screen-printed on a sintered YSZ/NiO–YSZ substrate and dried at 100 °C for 1 h. Bi2O3 was then also screen-printed on the dried GDC layer and co-sintered at 1000 or 1200 °C for 5 h subsequently. Bi2O3 would firstly melt and seep into the underlying GDC layer at temperatures higher than 817 °C, promoting liquid phase sintering of GDC bulk. After sintering, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), XRD, and EDXS results demonstrated that Bi2O3 had sublimated, leaving a pure almost-dense GDC phase, which effectively prevented Sr diffusion. Compared with the cells without the sintering aid, those with the Bi2O3-sintered GDC layer presented an increase in the power density of 44%, reaching 426 mW·cm−2 when sintered at 1200 °C. The power density of the sample with Bi2O3 when sintered at 1000 °C reached 254 mW·cm−2, which was 6 times greater than that of the one without Bi2O3 when it was sintered at the same temperature.

#### 4.2.6 Manganese-based sintering aids

Owing to the limited existing research, Mn as a sintering aid is not as effective as Li and Cu in reducing the sintering temperature. Kleinlogel *et al*. [128] added MnO to nanosized Ce0.8Gd0.2O1.9 powders with the proportion ranging from 1 to 5 mol% and reported that MnO effectively densified the samples at above 1050 °C. The density of 2 mol% Mn–GDC sintered at 1100 °C was reported to be approximately 96.5%, but it decreased to 94.4% when Mn–GDC was sintered at 1400 °C unexpectedly [136]. Rehman *et al*. [51] found that RD of GDC barrier layer could reach 84.09% when Mn nitrates were added. However, the shrinkage data showed that Mn doping was at a disadvantage compared with Fe, Co, or Cu doping in enhancing sinterability.

#### 4.2.7 Lithium-based sintering aids

While many studies have investigated the use of lithium nitrate or oxide as a sintering aid and its effect on the densification of ceria-based materials, only one of them is related to the barrier layers [129]. As an alkali metal, Li ion is more active and has a certain degree of reducibility. Therefore, attention should be paid to the redox reactions that may occur. Research has suggested that LiNO3 as a sintering aid causes trivalent Ce ions to be oxidized to tetravalent ions [121]. The Li ions stay at the grain boundaries, increasing their mobility and thus sintering density. Doping Li ions into SDC is also believed to reduce the lattice binding and association energies, thus increasing the number of mobile oxygen vacancies. The separated lithium ions compensate for the positive charge in the grain boundary nucleus and enhance the grain boundary conduction. In addition, as shown in Table 3 below, Li ions as dopants in GDC may increase the grain size [127], which can affect the densification behavior. Compared with Zn, Li can significantly reduce the sintering temperature of GDC. At 1000 °C, the 3 mol% Li–GDC sample was almost completely dense (RD > 95%) [129]. At 1250 °C, the density of the 1 mol% Li–GDC sample was greater than 97% [127]. Zhu *et al*. [121] sintered 5 mol% Li2O–GDC at 900 °C, and the sample achieved 95% RD. The situation was similar for SDC, and 10% LiNO3 doped SDC sintered at 1100 °C had RD of 97%.

### 4.3 Sintering aid strategies for constrained sintering problems

In the sintering process of multilayer structured materials, localized constrained sintering frequently occurs. Constrained sintering refers to the restriction of material shrinkage due to external constraints, such as substrates or adjacent layers, leading to stress concentration and uneven shrinkage. This results in restricted shrinkage in the direction parallel to the substrate, causing biaxial tensile stress, increased internal stress within the material, and the potential for anisotropic microstructures [146]. The adverse effects of constrained sintering primarily include warping, crack formation, poor interlayer contact, and stress accumulation [147], all of which compromise the structural stability. Additionally, constrained sintering can lead to poor interlayer contact with defects such as microcracks or voids, which significantly hinder ion and electron transport, especially in multilayer structures such as SOFCs, ultimately impacting the electrochemical performance.

Current research has developed several methods to alleviate issues related to constrained sintering of SOCs with GDC barrier layers. Apart from addressing green body preparation [90,91] or introducing gradient layers to facilitate a smooth transition between materials and enhance interfacial adhesion [148], the content below emphasizes approaches involving sintering aids to mitigate constrained sintering problems [17,130,149]. By utilizing sintering aids, which promote densification at lower temperatures, the buildup of thermal stresses caused by shrinkage mismatch can be mitigated, thereby alleviating issues such as warping, crack formation, and interfacial defects. These low-temperature densification methods help maintain the integrity of the multilayer structure, improving the ion and electron transport efficiency essential for optimal electrochemical performance.

Ni *et al*. [130] investigated the use of SSR combined with sintering aids to address issues related to constrained sintering in GDC diffusion barrier layers within the multilayer structure of SOFCs. After pre-sintering the GDC barrier layer at *T*1 (1150–1250 °C), cobalt nitrate or copper nitrate sintering aids were introduced and uniformly distributed within the porous GDC layer through an impregnation method, as shown in Fig. 16. The sintering aids help fill pores and promote grain growth by initiating SSR at lower temperatures of *T*2 (1250–1275 °C). This approach reduces thermal stress accumulation during sintering, achieving high densification of GDC layer, significantly minimizing interfacial defects and stress-induced structural damage, and thereby enhancing the overall performance and reliability of SOFCs.

Lee et al*. [17] presented a technique to fabricate a double-layer diffusion barrier, as shown in Fig. 17. First, the prepared standard GDC paste was screen-printed onto a pre-sintered YSZ electrolyte substrate and sintered in air at 1200 °C. The same screen-printing method was subsequently applied to the Cu-doped GDC paste, followed by a second sintering step at 1100 °C. The top layer, containing 1 wt% CuO as a sintering aid, generates enough sintering driving force through liquid-phase sintering to achieve full densification. Moreover, the bottom layer without a sintering aid helps avoid undesirable chemical reactions and controls the overall sintering rate, thereby preventing the formation of macro-defects. Research shows that the double-layer approach enhances densification while greatly preventing defects and chemical reactions, ensuring a high-quality diffusion barrier compared with conventional single-layer barrier diffusion.

Although it was used as a bilayer electrolyte in the work of Kim et al*. [149], the approach of co-sintering YSZ and GDC with the help of sintering aids could offer a potential route to reduce the constrained sintering problem. They employed a naturally diffused Fe sintering aid to create a fully dense YSZ/GDC bilayer-electrolyte structure. Three different bilayer electrolyte structures were compared: the YSZ/GDC group without a sintering aid, the group with 2 mol% Fe added to both YSZ and GDC, and the group with 2 mol% Fe added only to YSZ. The YSZ/GDC sample without a sintering aid exhibited a thick ID layer (0.5–1.1 μm) due to sintering at high temperatures ranging from 1300–1400 °C, which significantly reduced its ionic conductivity. In the sample with Fe added to both YSZ and GDC, although good densification was achieved at a lower temperature (1250 °C), the presence of Fe in both layers led to the formation of a thick ID layer (0.5–2.0 μm) at the interface, which negatively impacted the conductivity. In contrast, the best-performing group was the one with 2 mol% Fe added only to the YSZ. The addition of Fe to YSZ improved its shrinkage to better match that of the GDC layer and, more importantly, naturally diffused into the GDC layer during sintering, forming a thin layer of Fe₂O₃, which reduced interparticle friction and promoted the particle rearrangement and densification of GDC through a “viscous flow” mechanism. This mechanism enabled the densification of the GDC layer at a lower temperature of 1250 °C while significantly suppressing the formation of the undesirable ID layer between YSZ and GDC (only approximately 0.3 μm), thus maintaining excellent ionic conductivity and structural stability.

## 5 Post-sintering impregnation for density improvement

Limited by the constrained sintering effect due to sintering of the barrier layer on the rigid electrolyte, it is usually difficult to densify the barrier layer to a high degree. This problem stands out particularly for the green body layer deposited by the common solution-based methods as reviewed in Section 3, and the degree of particle packing density is relatively low. To address this issue, an effective approach involves impregnation or infiltration of the barrier layer after an initial sintering or annealing step, which helps improve the density and performance of the layer [83,129,150]. The infiltration processes involve impregnating the pre-obtained layer with synthesis precursors and finally sintering. Nanoparticles growing in the porous matrix as crystal nuclei can further densify the barrier layer and thus enhance its ionic conductivity. Compared with non-infiltrated samples, dense impregnated layers can be fabricated at reduced sintering temperatures down to 1150–1200 °C [150], and the relative cell shows better electrochemical performance and long-term stability [4,62,89,125,150]. Powder synthesis methods, such as sol–gel, co-precipitation, and hydrothermal treatments mentioned in Section 2.2, are also common ways to impregnate a porous matrix, which will be highlighted below.

### 5.1 Sol–gel impregnation

For sol‒gel technology coupled with a screen-printed matrix, the annealing temperature [129], infiltration time [129,150], and precursor composition [129,150] can affect the density of final samples. In particular, the optimization of pre-sintering temperature is critical [129]. On one hand, a lower pre-sintering temperature of screen-printed layers results in a matrix with higher porosity, which facilitates the infiltration of the sol–gel precursor and is helpful in increasing the density after infiltration and final sintering under the same conditions. On the other hand, the porous matrix must maintain a certain mechanical strength to withstand infiltration, necessitating an optimized pre-sintering temperature to balance porosity and structural integrity.

Nicollet *et al*. [129] impregnated a screen-printed GDC matrix that was pre-sintered at 1150 °C with a nitrate solution of cerium and gadolinium and burned it at 450 °C in air. This procedure was repeated four times, and the samples were sintered at 1250 °C for final densification. The porosity of the GDC matrix after pre-sintering was about 50% while the porosity of the impregnated GDC matrix decreased to approximately 20% before final sintering and was more easily eliminated in the final sintering stage. *P*max of the infiltrated sample was 40%–50% higher than that using conventional GDC barrier layer. Choi *et al*. [150] proposed that when gadolinium(III) isopropoxide was used as the doping agent, the precursor sol was five to six times more crystalline than the one using the gadolinium(III) nitrate hydrate. They impregnated the porous GDC barrier layer with Ce isopropoxide and Gd isopropoxide precursors five times. As shown in Fig. 18, the density of the barrier layer increased with increasing infiltration time. Compared with those of the cells with porous layers, *P*max of the cells with densified barrier layers increased from 0.60 to 0.92 W·cm−2, and *R*Ω decreased from 0.315 to 0.217 Ω·cm2 at 750 °C. At 1.25 V, the degradation rate of the infiltrated cells remained below 1.72%/1000 h, while that of the cells with porous barrier layers reached 7.69%/1000 h. These findings demonstrated that the enhanced cell performance was due to the decrease in *R*Ω as a result of reduced SZO.

### 5.2 Co-precipitation impregnation

Co-precipitation could also be employed for impregnation after the initial deposition of the barrier layer. Park *et al*. [62] impregnated porous screen-printed and 1250 °C pre-annealed layers via co-precipitation method realized by the spin-coating route (Fig. 19). The coating precursor was a nitrate solution of cerium and gadolinium chelated by PVP, with glycerin added as DCCA. The impregnated precursors were applied to the porous GDC by spin coating, and the NaOH solution was then applied to precipitate the metal ion sol by spin coating. The procedure was repeated three times, and the sample was dried at 60 °C and then heat-treated at 340 °C for 10 min. The above spin coating, drying and heating processes were repeated five times. The sample was finally densified at 1200 °C for 5 h. Compared with the conventional one, the cell with infiltrated layers had 32%–48% better performance in fuel cell mode at 650–800 °C, presumably due to a higher density of barrier layers, and achieved a current density of ~1.2 A·cm−2 at 1.3 V when operated at 750 °C (0.8 A·cm−2 for the conventional cell). In addition, more than 100-h degradation tests confirmed the stability of these infiltrated cells.

### 5.3 Hydrothermal impregnation

Several previous studies have explored hydrothermal methods to impregnate screen-printed or spin-coated matrices for full densification of the barrier layer. As shown in Fig. 20(a), Lyu *et al*. [125] impregnated porous screen-printed GDCs pre-annealed at 1250 °C by hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C for 48 h, using a nitrate solution of cerium and gadolinium, with cobalt nitrate as the sintering aid. Without separate final sintering of the barrier layer, the cell fabrication was completed by sintering the air electrode at 1050 °C. Impregnation helped strengthen the interfacial connection, thus accelerating cation transport and improving the electrochemical performance as well as the durability and heat resistance of SOCs [125]. In another study, they also employed similar hydrothermal infiltration process on spin-coated and 1200 °C pre-annealed GDC layers [89]. At this temperature, no Ce–Zr solid solution formed. The thin and dense barrier layer can not only shorten the transport path of oxygen ions but also prevent the diffusion of Sr element, resulting in the corresponding cell with *P*max of 1.07 W·cm−2 when tested at 780 °C and good long-term stability. The same authors also immersed screen-printed GDC matrix pre-annealed at 1250 °C in cerium and gadolinium nitrate solutions directly at 150 °C for 24 h to conduct hydrothermal infiltration [4]. This approach not only led to the *in situ* growth of nanoparticles on the porous GDC barrier layer but also filled some electrolyte defects and therefore enhanced the open-circuit voltage (OCV) as well as the cell performance. Theoretically, the higher the thermal treatment temperature and the longer the thermal treatment time are, the denser the barrier layers are [4]. However, it should be noted that a small amount of GDC nanoparticles could invade the anode-supported layer during the impregnation process, as shown in Fig. 20(b). This phenomenon, on one hand, increases the gas transfer resistance. On the other hand, it promotes electrochemical conversion, especially for hydrocarbons, and improves the anti-coking ability of cells [4,151−153]. In this case, finding an appropriate temperature and impregnation time to maximize the advantages of this intrusion is worthy of further investigation [4].

## 6 Summary and perspective view

Producing a dense barrier layer at lower temperatures is highly beneficial for obtaining a well-structured air electrode/electrolyte interface, which is essential for fabricating high-performance SOCs with good long-term stability. Considering the processing steps of the barrier layer, improvements in the starting powder, deposition of green body layer and sintering/post-sintering are possible ways to achieve this goal. Figure 21 illustrates a summary of the progress made in these areas. To fabricate denser barrier layers at lower temperatures, appropriate methods should be chosen after their benefits and limitations are comprehensively considered.

For the synthesis of ceria-based powders, both the MC process and wet chemical synthesis methods can reduce particle sizes down to the nanoscale. The precursor composition and calcination procedure significantly influence the particle crystallinity, size, morphology, exposed surfaces, and agglomeration, all of which affect the powder’s sintering behavior. Compared with soft particle agglomeration, hard particle agglomeration is more crucial to affect the sintering ability. It increases significantly with the calcination temperature, which should be high enough to ensure adequate crystallinity for ionic conductivity. Some modified wet-chemistry methods, such as o-precipitation combined with supercritical fluid drying or hydrothermal treatment, have shown potential to alleviate this phenomenon. As a result, the densification of ceria-based powders to ~98% of RD can be achieved at 900 °C.

Unlike free sintering of powders, however, densification of the barrier layer involves sintering on a rigid substrate, which makes densification more difficult because of the constraint sintering effect arising from the different shrinkage behaviors of the barrier layer and the substrate. Consequently, the densification behavior depends largely on how the green-body layer is deposited on the substrate. While conventional film fabrication methods such as screen printing, spin coating, tape casting, and dip coating have difficulty in producing dense thin layers, improvements have been achieved by the modification of conventional methods and the employment of other techniques such as EPD and spray pyrolysis deposition. Among them, the first approach might be the easiest to employ, as it generally only requires modification of the material to be deposited but not on the instruments. EPD is a simple method. However, in most previous studies, this method can reduce the densification temperature only to 1250 °C, even with the assistance of a sintering aid [108]. Spray pyrolysis is very attractive because of its ability to achieve dense barrier layers with a diameter of approximately 13 mm at temperatures as low as 900 °C [114,154]. However, its effectiveness in depositing the barrier layer on large cells remains to be examined.

Moreover, the application of sintering aids is a classical and effective way to densify bulk ceramics, but its application to the barrier layer remains limited. Current studies have demonstrated that the best densification of RD approximately 96% can be achieved at a sintering temperature of 1000 °C with Fe- or Mn-based sintering aids [123]. The specific effects of the sintering aid type, concentration, addition method, sintering temperature, and heating rate on the densification of the barrier layer in its constrained sintering state need further investigation. This research is crucial not only for improving the density of the barrier layer but also for ensuring strong adhesion between the barrier layer and electrolyte layer. While many sintering aids significantly enhance densification, potential adverse effects on material conductivity and unwanted chemical reactions must also be thoroughly examined. Therefore, investigating effective sintering aids without degrading the required properties of barrier layer as a cell component remains an important area of research. Furthermore, the effects of sintering aids on the long-term stability of cells and stacks, especially at temperatures higher than 750 °C remain largely unknown and should be investigated carefully before the sintering aid approach can be commercialized.

Since the pre-sintered barrier layer is by nature difficult to densify due to the constrained sintering effect, post-sintering process such as infiltration would be an appropriate approach to further increase the density. Under optimized conditions, infiltration after pre-annealing or sintering can reduce the temperature for full densification to approximately 1100–1150 °C, and the resulting cell can have good electrochemical performance as well as long-term stability. However, the tedious and complicated fabrication process may limit its large-scale application.

Finally, it is worth noting that there are several new sintering techniques that may promote the densification of barrier layers at reduced temperatures [155,156]. Among them, cold sintering process (CSP) [157,158] and FS [159,160] have emerged as two interesting options. CSP is a technique that uses low temperatures but high pressure to densify ceramic powders with the aid of a transient liquid phase, offering advantages in energy efficiency and material compatibility. There has been extensive research on cold sintering of ceramic materials, and ceria as the bulk electrolyte can be sintered to approximately 95% of RD by combining CSP and a lower annealing temperature of 900 °C [161,162]. Research has shown that the sintering temperature decreases to 600 °C with the addition of Li [163]. Several studies have also carried out cold co-sintering of the barrier layer with an electrode and found that it can prevent delamination, cracking, and sample warpage while ensuring uniform, continuous, and sharp interfaces [164,165]. On the other hand, CSP of bilayer or multilayer materials faces certain technical challenges due to the necessity of applying pressure, which requires further studies.

FS is a technique that has gained significant popularity in research in recent years and ceramic materials are rapidly heated by applying an electric field, resulting in almost instantaneous densification through FS, which reduces energy consumption and processing time [156,166,167]. Studies focused on single ceria-based pellets using FS have achieved a high RD in a very short time [168]. In addition, attempts to fabricate ceria-based thin films or SOC multilayer structures have shown encouraging results. Some researchers found that FS enabled the rapid and efficient preparation of YSZ electrolytes, Ni–YSZ/YSZ multilayers, and LSCF/GDC bilayers, effectively addressing the issues of low density and cracking caused by constrained sintering in heterogeneous ceramics [156,166,169,170]. Notably, the striking feature of FS is the decrease in defects and delamination, which can occur in conventional sintering due to differences in the rates of sintering of the individual layers (Fig. 22) [156]. However, the common problem for FS lies in the non-uniformity of relatively large-sized sintered samples [166,171]. Research has shown that even with samples several millimeters in thickness, temperature heterogeneities exceeding several hundred degrees Celsius can occur [166]. However, it remains challenging at present, several new modifications have been proposed to solve the problem of non-uniformity, such as shape optimization of layers [172,173] and contactless FS [171].
