## 1 Introduction

Strain sensors are devices engineered to convert mechanical strain into proportional changes in electrical resistance. These sensors fall into three main categories based on their sensing mechanisms: piezoresistive, capacitive, and piezoelectric. Compared to capacitive and inductive sensors, the construction of piezoresistive sensors renders them relatively straightforward and cost-effective. Operating on the principle of measuring alterations in electrical resistance in response to mechanical strain, piezoresistive sensors exhibit heightened sensitivity, enabling the detection of minute deformations. This design simplicity is complemented by their typically expeditious response time. The economical production of piezoresistive sensors, which are often more affordable than their more intricate counterparts, makes them particularly suitable for applications requiring large-scale deployment. As an integral category within the realm of flexible strain sensors, flexible piezoresistive strain sensors possess distinct advantages over conventional rigid pressure sensors. These advantages include the capability to conform to and accommodate the shape of the object under scrutiny, coupled with a streamlined structure, enhanced sensitivity, an extensive operational range, rapid reaction capabilities, and robust stability under varying conditions. Therefore, flexible strain sensors have garnered significant interest due to their broad applicability in areas such as human activity and health monitoring, electronic skin (E-skin) [1−7], robotics, prosthetics [8−12], and human‒machine interfaces [13−18].

Despite their numerous advantages, flexible strain sensors encounter several challenges that impede their performance. These include a lack of sensitivity to subtle mechanical changes and a restricted detection range, rendering them unsuitable for many environments. Moreover, these sensors often exhibit limited linear response and inferior mechanical properties. The conundrum lies in simultaneously achieving cost-effectiveness, high performance, low energy consumption, and self-actuation. Simple approaches such as depositing conductive nanomaterials on an insulating elastic substrate or altering the composition of the substrate and filler have proven inadequate for enhancing the sensitivity and extending the sensing range. To overcome these limitations, researchers have employed innovative strategies, including the development of new sensing mechanisms, the integration of functionalized nanomaterials, and the adoption of cutting-edge fabrication techniques. Among these methods, the incorporation of microstructures has emerged as a highly effective method to boost sensor performance.

For instance, the creation of pyramidal [19,20], mountain [21], and dome [22−24] microstructures has been shown to enhance sensor sensitivity. Similarly, incorporating microsphere structures into sensing layers improves both the sensitivity and compressibility [25]. Porous structures in piezoresistive sensors have also been effective in boosting sensitivity and extending the dynamic range [26,27]. The presence of microstructures significantly increases the contact area between electrodes, causing deformation at the microstructural contact points and thus increasing the sensitivity [28]. Additionally, microstructured substrates deform more readily due to the spaces between microstructures, leading to higher dynamic performance and a broader range compared to unstructured sensors [29]. Furthermore, the use of microstructured substrates reduces hysteresis and accelerates response time by mitigating the rigid viscoelasticity inherent in composite elastomers [30]. Techniques such as molding, template removal, ultraviolet (UV)/ozone (O3) treatment, microsphere construction, lithography, three-dimensional (3D) printing, and laser-assisted microengineering have been employed to fabricate these structures and enhance the sensitivity of flexible strain sensors.

The development of flexible piezoresistive sensors featuring intricate microstructures has seen remarkable advancements due to the significant endeavors of researchers globally. These sensors exhibit superior characteristics, such as high sensitivity, robust stability, rapid response and recovery (Res/Rec) time, and a considerable degree of linearity in their output. The realization of various applications within the realm of flexible electronics has been made possible through innovations in material enhancement, fabrication techniques, sophisticated microstructural design, and exploitation of diverse operational principles (Fig. 1). This review delves into advancements in flexible piezoresistive strain sensors through the lens of microstructure design. It provides a comprehensive overview of the sensors’ operational principles and performance attributes across various epochs, which are presented in distinct sections for clarity. Section 2 elucidates the fundamental workings of flexible piezoresistive strain sensors, elucidating the enhancement mechanisms afforded by microstructures and the concomitant performance metrics. Section 3 encapsulates the diverse microstructures employed to enhance sensing capabilities. Section IV explores the unique traits of different microstructured flexible piezoresistive strain sensors and their synergy with real-world applications. The text introduces mathematical models to graphically represent the performance benefits conferred by diverse microstructured sensor types. Section 4 describes the methodologies for integrating microstructures into strain sensors. In conclusion, Section 5 discusses the future trajectories and challenges associated with refining flexible piezoresistive strain sensors via microstructure innovation.

### 2.1 Components of flexible piezoresistive strain sensors

Flexible piezoresistive strain sensors are composed of three main components: a flexible substrate, an electrode, and an active layer material. Flexible substrates, commonly made from polymers such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) [35−37], polyethylene terephthalate (PET) [38,39], polyimide (PI) [40], polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) [41], and polyurethane (PU) [42,43], are capable of withstanding a certain amount of tension or compression under mechanical stimulation. An electrode, which ideally maintains high conductivity and good stability under large loads, can be made from materials such as metals, carbon nanomaterials, ionic hydrogels, and conductive polymers [44,45]. The active layer material, the “core” of the sensor, must provide sufficient charge transport paths for current flow and good elasticity to resist deformation due to external loading [46]. Microstructures in the active layer material are crucial for the design of the sensor, as they allow for ease of deformation under small strains and increase the contact area under force, resulting in a large rate of change in resistance and improved sensitivity (Fig. 2).

### 2.2 Working principle of flexible piezoresistive strain sensors

A strain transducer is a device designed to quantify the extent of deformation or strain experienced by an object, whereas a pressure transducer serves as a precise instrument for measuring the force exerted per unit area on an object. Given that the strain experienced by an object often correlates directly with the pressure it endures, the discipline of microstructure design becomes integral to both the realms of strain measurement and pressure detection, informing the advancement of strain and pressure sensors. Flexible piezoresistive strain sensors leverage multiple microscopic mechanisms, such as the piezoresistive effect, electron tunneling effect, crack extension, and threshold effect, to achieve high sensitivity, reliability, and stability. These mechanisms enable sensors to precisely measure strain.

#### 2.2.1 Piezoresistive effect

A flexible piezoresistive strain sensor is designed to detect physical deformation, primarily utilizing the piezoresistive effect. This effect is characterized by a change in the internal resistance of a material in response to an applied force. Typically, these sensors employ thin-film piezoresistive materials as the sensing component. As an object experiences force, the thin-film material within the strain sensor deforms, altering its internal resistance and affecting the current flow. By measuring this current variation, the magnitude of the applied force can be determined. Flexible piezoresistive strain sensors have garnered significant research interest due to their simple construction and rapid response characteristics. The primary causes of resistance changes in these sensors are the interaction of conductive fillers and the design of microstructures. When subjected to external stimuli, the sensor’s strain results in an increased number of resistive contacts within the internal conductive network, thereby modifying its resistivity, as depicted in Fig. 3(a).

#### 2.2.2 Tunneling effect

The tunneling effect describes the phenomenon where electrons traverse barriers that are typically impervious to them in a composite conductor, leading to the creation of a tunnel current. This current converts applied pressure into an electrical signal and does not require direct contact between conductive particles for signal propagation. Yang *et al*. [48] employed a finite element method that integrates 3D modeling of the microstructure with electrothermal coupling analysis. They constructed a representative volume element (RVE) composed of a rubber matrix embedded with randomly dispersed graphene particles. This RVE was used to simulate the transport of electrical current within the composite under varying strains. In the scenario where the tunneling effect is disregarded, the current density within the rubber matrix of the composite remains minimal due to the insulating nature of the rubber. Consequently, the graphene particles do not form effective conductive networks, and the composite exhibits insulating characteristics. Conversely, when the tunneling effect is taken into account, electrons can traverse the energy barrier between graphene particles through quantum mechanical tunneling, significantly increasing the tunneling current density. This mechanism enables electrons to bypass the insulative rubber matrix, leading to a substantial enhancement in the composite’s overall electrical conductivity—a remarkable increase of a factor of approximately 106 compared to when the tunneling effect is overlooked. The inclusion of the tunneling effect facilitates the creation of efficient conductive pathways that interconnect the graphene particles, thereby significantly boosting the electrical conductivity of the composite. In Amjadi *et al*. [49], a composite sandwich structure was engineered based on the tunneling effect. It featured a thin film of silver nanowires embedded between two PDMS elastomer layers. When the separation distance (*d*) between adjacent nanowires exceeds the nanowire diameter but remains below a critical cutoff distance (*C*), electrons can quantum mechanically tunnel through the intervening polymer matrix. The total resistance in this scenario is the sum of the individual nanowire resistances (*R*1 and *R*2) and the tunneling resistance (*R*tunnel), as depicted in Fig. 3(b). External forces applied to a piezoresistive element can alter the microstructure’s geometric morphology due to the tunneling effect. Such morphological changes can impact the electron pathways within an element, leading to measurable resistance variations. These measurements provide insights into the magnitude of the external force. By carefully designing the microstructure, one can modulate the intensity of the electron tunneling effect, thereby enhancing the sensitivity and stability of the sensor.

#### 2.2.3 Crack expansion

Crack expansion in a piezoresistive element occurs when an external force induces the widening of existing cracks. This expansion leads to an increase in the circuit resistance, which can be measured to gauge the applied force. As cracks propagate, they interrupt conductive pathways and increase the overall resistance of the sensor. When the applied force is removed and the element returns to its original state, the cracks reconnect or “heal”, restoring conductivity and reducing overall resistance. The reversible nature of crack formation can be leveraged to enhance sensor sensitivity. Through strategic microstructural design, the rate at which cracks expand can be controlled, enhancing the bonding between the fibers and the matrix. This slowed expansion improves both the sensitivity and stability of the sensor, as it minimizes deformation and damage under stress. Park *et al*. [50] fabricated platinum (Pt) deposits with a thickness of 20 nm on polyurea-based (PUA) substrates, inducing predetermined crack densities through controlled bending. The depth of these cracks was then finely modulated by varying the extent of prestretching applied. By constructing an analytical model that characterized the crack geometry (Fig. 3(c)), it was determined that increasing the crack depth enhanced the sensor’s sensitivity, achieving a gauge factor (GF) of up to 16,000 over a strain range of 2% through alterations in the crack depth. Direct deposition of carbon nanotubes (CNTs) onto a thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) substrate promoted elongation and propagation of cracks [51]. The ratio of the cracked area to the total area increased progressively with increasing tensile strain. Notably, significant changes in resistance were maintained even under larger strain values. With a sensing capability extending up to 300% strain and a GF reaching as high as 83,982.8, this sensor effectively addresses the issue of low sensitivity commonly associated with high crack density sensors. The approach of employing microstructural design to regulate the performance of strain sensors through reversible crack expansion mechanisms is both intuitive and highly adaptable.

#### 2.2.4 Threshold effects

The threshold concentration is a critical point that governs the electrical conductivity of a sensor. Below this threshold, the sensor is nonconductive, whereas it becomes conductive when the concentration surpasses the threshold. An optimal sensing performance is achieved when the concentration of conductive filler is slightly above the threshold, enhancing the sensor’s response to external stimuli. Conversely, a concentration significantly above the threshold can diminish the sensing performance due to contact and tunneling effects within the conductive filler network. Wang *et al*. [52] attributed this behavior to the formation of pressure-induced percolation pathways, as illustrated in Fig. 3(d). By carefully controlling the volumetric content of the conductive filler, only a fraction of the conductive paths are established within the composite. The application of strain reduces the average distance between conductive particles, creating additional conductive paths and lowering the resistivity of the composite. Su *et al*. [53] engineered a flexible, switch-like piezoresistive strain sensor featuring an inverted microtaper (IMP) on the lower conductive layer, which adopts a bridge configuration. In the absence of applied pressure, the sensor’s upper and lower layers remain disconnected, akin to an open circuit, maintaining an off-state characterized by minimal substrate surface resistance. As the pressure surpasses a critical threshold, the upper and lower surfaces make contact, initiating the formation of an alternative conductive network—akin to flipping a switch. Further increments in pressure merely serve to enlarge the contact area, thereby further diminishing the overall sensor resistance. The microstructural design of the material can be fine-tuned to lower this threshold point, enabling even subtle pressure variations to elicit a sensor response, thus enhancing sensitivity. Conversely, raising the threshold can prevent false triggering and enhance stability. Therefore, by modulating the microstructural design, the threshold can be adjusted to effectively balance the sensitivity and stability of the sensor. An improper threshold can lead to inadequate sensitivity or excessive error, compromising the accuracy of the sensor. Moreover, incorrect threshold settings may cause the sensor to drift or fail over time, reducing its reliability and operational lifespan. This can affect performance by causing sluggish or unstable responses as well as increased power consumption or poor cost-effectiveness.

### 2.3 Microstructure-based interfacial resistivity change mechanism

Researchers have developed a design model predicated on the mechanism of interfacial contact resistance change. At the microscopic level, all solid surfaces exhibit roughness, and the interaction between the active and conductive layers primarily occurs at the mechanical contact points of surface asperities, with the actual contact area significantly smaller than the nominal contact area. Consequently, the quantity and dimensions of these contact points dictate the variation in the resistance of the active layer, which in turn impacts the sensor performance.

Flexible piezoresistive strain sensors, designed around the principle of interfacial contact resistance change, can be categorized into two types: (1) Sensors that utilize the contact interface between the active layer and electrode to create a resistance-changing interface, such as the flexible pressure sensor designed by Feng [54]. She employed CNTs as the active material and a single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT)/PDMS composite with a surface microstructure resembling that of ordinary sandpaper as the top electrode. The bottom electrode is a copper fork finger electrode, matching the irregular microconvex body structure on the sandpaper surface. When the PDMS film is subjected to mechanical pressure, the microconvex bodies deform, increasing the contact area between the copper electrode and the SWCNT/PDMS film, altering the current path and changing the resistance of the sensor (Fig. 3(e)). (2) These sensors form contact resistance through internal design within the active layer material, such as by using its conductive material or wrapping a layer of conductive material around the surface. Under pressure, this also results in different conductive pathways, modifying the resistance [55,56].

Therefore, by understanding the sensing mechanism of flexible piezoresistive strain sensors, we can overcome limitations and enhance sensor performance through active-layer microstructural design. By adjusting the size, number, and distribution of microconvex bodies, we aim to maximize the total contact area or ensure efficient contact under varying pressures. This study paves the way for innovative developments in flexible sensor technology.

Flexible piezoresistive strain sensors serve as a “medium” that translates applied loads into electrical signals, offering a broad spectrum of applications. To engineer these sensors with superior overall performance and practicality, several performance parameters must be employed to quantitatively assess the device’s efficacy. These crucial parameters are sensitivity, sensing range, response time, relaxation time, hysteresis, and stability [57]. The design and optimization of flexible piezoresistive strain sensors necessitate thorough consideration of the influence and impact of these performance metrics (Fig. 4).

### 3.1 Sensitivity

Piezoresistive sensors measure pressure or force by converting mechanical stress into a change in electrical resistance. The sensitivity of a piezoresistive sensor is its capacity to detect minimal changes in pressure or force, often reflected in the gauge factor (a dimensionless quantity that relates the fractional change in resistance to the fractional change in length). A higher gauge factor indicates a more sensitive piezoresistive sensor, which provides a greater electrical output for a given mechanical input.

Regarding flexible piezoresistive strain sensors, the sensitivity refers to how significantly the electrical resistance of the sensor changes in response to the applied strain, enabling the detection of even slight stretching or compression in the material. The design strategies discussed below, such as introducing single microconvex structures, composite microconvex structures, and 3D porous structures, enhance this sensitivity by increasing the surface area and creating intricate contact points between conductive elements within the sensor, leading to improved performance in detecting and measuring strain.

Sensitivity pertains to the responsiveness of the piezoresistive strain transducer in its normal, stable state. The strain (*ε*) applied to the device is defined as the ratio of the change in length (Δ*L*) to the original length (*L*0). The initial resistance is denoted as *R*0, where Δ*R* represents the resistance change due to strain at 0 kPa. The *G*F is a measure of the transducer’s sensitivity and is expressed as the slope of the curve that reflects the change in response to the applied mechanical pressure.

(1) GF=ΔR/R0ε

The *G*F quantifies the sensitivity of a strain sensor, which is essentially its ability to detect small changes in strain. This sensitivity is crucial for measurement accuracy. However, a higher *G*F can sometimes compromise the sensor’s detection range and stability. The performance of strain sensors made from different materials is often influenced by factors such as the layered structure of the material design and the configuration and dimensions of the conductive network [58].

### 3.2 Response time

For flexible piezoresistive strain sensors, the response time is defined as the duration needed for the output signal to change and achieve a steady state upon exposure to a specific pressure. Conversely, the recovery/response time denotes the interval required for the sensor to revert from the activated state back to its initial condition once the pressure has been removed. Both metrics are indicative of the transducer’s dynamic capabilities. The rate at which the strain transducer transitions from the activated state to the steady state is mirrored in the response and recovery time. Owing to the viscoelastic nature of flexible polymers, response and recovery delays often occur during testing. Therefore, when conceptualizing flexible piezoresistive strain sensors, it is imperative to take both response and recovery time into account to thoroughly assess their dynamic performance.

### 3.3 Effective linearity

Linearity is defined as the ratio of the maximum deviation (Δ*Y*max) between a sensor’s calibration curve and a fitted straight line to the full-scale output (*Y*) under specified conditions. It is also referred to as the “nonlinearity error”, with smaller values indicating better sensor linearity [59]. Flexible piezoresistive strain sensors often exhibit multiple linear regions due to the rearrangement of the conducting network and recombination with the substrate material during the stretching process. We typically select the first linear region as the effective linearity range of the sensor. This is because within this range, the internal structure of the sensor is most stable, almost undamaged, and offers the best stability. Therefore, in designing flexible piezoresistive strain sensors, the linearity parameter must be taken into account to fully assess the linear characteristics of the sensor.

### 3.4 Cyclic stability

Stability is a paramount parameter for strain sensors, encompassing the deterioration of the electrical function of the active material and the mechanical integrity of the flexible substrate. This denotes the ability of a strain sensor’s output signal to remain consistent over time, serving as a crucial benchmark for assessing long-term performance. The stability of flexible piezoresistive strain sensors is influenced by numerous factors, including zero drift, environmental effects, nonlinear behavior, and poor cyclic stability, all of which can alter the sensor’s output signal [60,61]. Primarily, the structure of the sensor and its operating environment are pivotal elements affecting its stability. As the unpredictability of the environment diminishes the reliance of the sensor on the environment, enhanced stability is achieved. In practice, selecting an appropriate strain sensor based on the application environment is essential for maximizing performance.

The interplay among the aforementioned four sensing mechanisms significantly impacts the sensor’s performance and functionality. Microstructures can modulate the characteristics of flexible piezoresistive sensors, such as strain sensitivity, linearity, and stability. In piezoresistive sensing, microstructures can increase conductive network contact points and manipulate electron tunneling effects through structural design to enhance sensor sensitivity and robustness. By tailoring the microstructure, one can adjust the threshold effect of the sensor and thus its sensitivity. Additionally, microstructures influence strain distribution, which in turn affects how strain alters resistance. For instance, improper microstructure design could lead to localized strain concentrations, resulting in uneven resistance changes and compromised sensor accuracy. Hence, a meticulous microstructure design is critical to the performance of flexible piezoresistive strain sensors.

Moreover, other considerations, such as material selection and fabrication processes, must be meticulously integrated into the sensor design to ensure superior performance and dependability.

### 4.1 Single microconvex body

The microstructure design of flexible piezoresistive strain sensors involves the incorporation of single microconvex bodies, which are small, raised structures that contribute to the sensor’s performance. These convex structures can have various shapes, such as pyramids, cones, or hemispheres, and they play a crucial role in enhancing the sensor’s sensitivity to applied strain.

When the sensor is subjected to mechanical deformation, these microconvex bodies experience changes in their contact area and contact resistance, leading to a piezoresistive effect. This effect results in a measurable change in electrical resistance, allowing the sensor to detect and measure strain with high sensitivity.

The design of a single microconvex body is critical for achieving optimal sensor performance. Factors such as the size, shape, and distribution of these microstructures must be carefully considered and tailored to the specific application requirements. Properly engineered microstructures can significantly improve the linearity, stability, and repeatability of the sensor’s response, making it more reliable for use in various strain monitoring applications.

#### 4.1.1 Pyramidal microstructure

Zhao [62] employed a single-screw mixing and extrusion method to prepare thermoplastic polyurethane/short-cut carbon fiber/carbon nanotube (TPU/SCF-CNT) conductive composites. By utilizing a molding technique, they created TPU/SCF-CNT composite replicas featuring inverted pyramid microstructure arrays on their surfaces. The sensor element, based on this architecture, offers an extensive conductive pathway with a sensitivity of 0.32 kPa−1 and rapid responsiveness (response time is less than 100 ms). Moreover, it maintains a relatively stable resistance response throughout a cyclic compression/release test lasting 500 s (approximately 1580 cycles) at a peak pressure of approximately 3 kPa. Choong *et al*. [63] engineered a stretchable pressure sensor using a micropyramid matrix. They achieved this by crafting a pyramidal microstructure on the PDMS surface and subsequently coating it with a poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene–poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) conductive layer (Fig. 6(a)). The application of pressure deforms the pyramid matrix structure, causing the pyramid tips to change from point contacts to rectangular contacts, which in turn increases the contact area between the active layer and the electrode (Fig. 6(b)). This design endows the sensor with a sensitivity of 10.3 kPa−1 and a response time of 0.2 s. The resistance alteration remained stable over 400 cycles (Fig. 6(c)). The benefit of the pyramid structure lies in its nonuniform stress distribution, which concentrates stress at the tips and results in greater localized stress concentration that leads to a more significant contact area change relative to an unstructured surface under a given applied pressure.

#### 4.1.2 Microtriangular cone structure

Hou *et al*. [64] engineered an innovative microtriangular cone structure and integrated it with an electrode layer (Fig. 6(d)). This configuration allows for a more focused application of force when pressure is applied, resulting in greater deformation of the composite material. This increased deformation yields a greater rate of change in electrical resistance, thereby enhancing the sensitivity of the sensor. The peak sensitivity of the sensor is recorded at −0.4426 N−1, enabling the detection of minute resistance changes even under slight pressure variations (Fig. 6(e)). The fabrication process for this sensor is straightforward, cost-effective, and readily achievable.

#### 4.1.3 Microcolumnar structures

Zhao *et al*. [65] developed a microcolumnar structure that resembles a microtriangular cone structure. They employed photolithography to create arrayed microstructures on a glass substrate (Fig. 6(f)). Subsequently, they transferred these metal microstructures onto a flexible PDMS substrate membrane using a template method and coated it with a metal film layer to yield a flexible thin-film metal strain sensor equipped with microstructures. This fabrication technique is economical because the created microstructure template can be reused multiple times. The design of this sensor incorporates square microstructures that effectively hinder crack propagation, extending the sensing range to a tensile strain limit of 25% while preserving high sensitivity with a strain coefficient of 154. Additionally, the sensor features a low hysteresis coefficient (approximately 8.8%) and a rapid response time (0.07 s) and demonstrates excellent repeatability and stability (Fig. 6(g)).

#### 4.1.4 Spherical microstructures

Hemispherical structures are commonly found in microstructural designs. For instance, Li [66] prepared conductive PDMS arrays featuring surface multilevel hemispherical structures. These were systematically aligned and packaged with fork-finger electrodes to create a flexible piezoresistive strain sensor. Notably, when the surface of an ABS plate is rough, laser etching can result in a smooth hemispherical microstructure, whereas a multilevel hemispherical microstructure can be produced on a smooth ABS plate surface. The sensor demonstrated high sensitivity (15.4 kPa−1) across an ultrawide linear range (0–200 kPa), a low detection limit (16 Pa), and a rapid response time (20 ms) and maintained high stability after 7500 pressure cycles.

The properties of individual microstructures can be enhanced when they are punctured. Park *et al*. [67] developed a flexible E-skin based on a composite elastomer film containing interlocking microsphere arrays that exhibited significant tunneling piezoresistive effects (Fig. 6(h)). This design significantly increases the contact area variation under load, leading to extreme resistive switching behavior. This results in high pressure sensitivity (Fig. 6(i)) with a sensitivity of −15.1 kPa−1 and a minimum detection limit of approximately 0.2 Pa, along with fast response/relaxation time of 0.04 s. These characteristics make it suitable for sensitive monitoring of human respiratory flow and voice vibrations, indicating its potential application in wearable health monitoring systems.

### 4.2 Composite microconvex

While individual microconvex structures can significantly enhance sensor sensitivity and provide a broad detection spectrum, with stability benefits, their fabrication into nonplanar ordered arrays on flexible substrates often demands high processing standards that may not be environmentally friendly and are not yet amenable to industrial-scale production. Consequently, research is increasingly gravitating toward more intricate designs such as folded and bionic structures. These complex microstructures surpass the aforementioned types in terms of performance, despite the added complexity.

The microstructure design of flexible piezoresistive strain sensors plays a crucial role in determining their sensitivity, stability, and overall performance. One approach that has been explored is the use of composite microconvex structures. These structures typically involve the creation of an array of small convex elements on the surface of the sensor, which can be made from various materials such as PDMS, elastomers, or other flexible polymers. The benefits of using composite microconvex structures include the following: (1) Enhanced sensitivity: Convex elements can concentrate applied pressure, leading to larger deformations and greater changes in electrical resistance. This increased sensitivity allows the sensor to detect even subtle strains. (2) Wide detection range: The design can accommodate a broad range of pressures or strains, making it suitable for a wide variety of applications. (3) Stability: A regular array of convex elements can help maintain the structural integrity of the sensor under repeated loading and unloading cycles, ensuring stable performance over time. (4) Fabrication challenges: While the fabrication of these nonplanar ordered array microstructures can be challenging and may require high processing standards, efforts are being made to develop more environmentally friendly and cost-effective production methods. (5) Industrialization potential: Despite current challenges, there is potential for these designs to be adapted for industrialized production, offering scalability and commercial viability. (6) Research direction: As the field evolves, researchers are exploring more specialized designs, such as folded and bionic structures, which offer even greater performance but also add complexity to the fabrication process.

In summary, the development of composite microconvex structures for flexible piezoresistive strain sensors represents a promising avenue for achieving high-performance sensing solutions with potential for future optimization and scale-up.

#### 4.2.1 Folded microstructure

Wei *et al*. [68] combined carbon nanofibers (CNFs), carbon nanotubes, and polyethylene to create conductive composite films. By utilizing CNFs as dispersants for CNTs, they added PVA to form a stable CNF–CNT–PVA solution. Conductive wrinkled microstructures were then developed on flexible PDMS substrates through prestretching. These flexible, conductive films with wrinkled microstructures were subsequently encapsulated into the sensors (Fig. 5(b)). The integration of PVA and CNFs not only enhances the mechanical properties and flexibility of conductive microstructures but also prevents cracking during both the fabrication process and sensor usage. This ensures that the sensors maintain good performance over multiple pressure application cycles at 10 kPa. Yan *et al*. [69] introduced a flexible wrinkled piezoresistive sensor based on an MXene composite, which was produced using a spray deposition method (Fig. 7(a)). A pleated structure was achieved by prestretching and then releasing a polyacrylate tape substrate. This fabrication technique is straightforward, efficient, and economical. The MXene composite sensors exhibit high sensitivity (148.26 kPa−1), a broad pressure detection range (up to 16 kPa), rapid response time (120 ms), and outstanding durability (over 13,000 cycles) (Fig. 7(b)).

#### 4.2.2 Bionic microstructure

Li *et al*. [70] replicated the surface microstructure of bionic Ginkgo biloba on a PDMS substrate, spin-coated graphene ink as a functional layer, and utilized the unique microstructure on the surface of Ginkgo biloba to improve the performance of flexible piezoresistive strain sensors. A negative PDMS mold was used to replicate the surface structure of the leaf surface; copper was sputtered onto the mold, and the mold was flipped to obtain a structured PDMS film, which was coated with a functional layer of graphene ink to fabricate the pressure sensor (Fig. 7(c)). When the sensor operates, the microstructures are in contact with each other, and a resistance can be generated, thus achieving a piezoresistive effect. The surface of Ginkgo biloba consists of parallel leaf veins and bumps of different shapes, which can concentrate the payload and thus increase the sensitivity and linear range of the sensor. The sensor has a high sensitivity of 1.56 kPa−1 and a wide linear range from 22.6 Pa to 20 kPa, with shortened response time of approximately 80 ms, and maintains stable and good performance after 10,000 cyclic pressure tests (Fig. 7(d)). This method is relatively easy for the fabrication of microstructured sensors of bionic Ginkgo biloba, which can be used to detect the movement of human joints, suggesting that it has great potential for application in health monitoring and electronic skin.

Ma [71] explored bionic microstructured flexible pressure sensors modeled on dragonfly wings. He used graphene and nanometal as conductive layer materials and found that adding polyurethane to graphene and hydrophilizing the PDMS surface improved the sensor stability. The sensitivity of the sensor increased and then decreased with increasing graphene concentration and PDMS thickness. The sensor has a sensitivity of 0.152 kPa−1, a linear response range of 0–4 kPa, fast response time of 83 ms, and reliable stability. It can detect pressure signals such as finger bends and pulse beats.

Tang *et al*. [72] selected four plant leaves as templates (Fig. 7(e)) to fabricate E-skin with different bionic microstructures. Real-time current signal monitoring and sensing performance evaluation revealed that the oleander template-based device exhibited outstanding performance. Its sensing range was 0–0.8 kPa, with a sensitivity of 8.5 kPa and a detection limit of 0.001 kPa (Fig. 7(f)). This device has fine radius/height values and a tendency toward high-density microstructures, resulting in high sensitivity and low detection limits with good durability.

Natural plant leaves offer a wealth of microstructures on their surfaces, which contrasts with the limited variety found in artificial microstructured silicon templates. This diversity opens numerous avenues to investigate the correlation between electrode surface microstructures and electronic device performance.

Nie *et al*. [73] replicated the layered microstructure of banana leaves to create PDMS substrates featuring aligned secondary and tertiary ridges at the micrometer scale. A thin silver layer was applied over these microstructures to serve as a piezoresistive sensor electrode for the E-skin. Pressure-induced microcracks within this layer, combined with the underlying microstructure, significantly increased the sensitivity of the E-skin to 10 kPa−1. Moreover, this novel E-skin boasts a detection limit of approximately 1 Pa, rapid response and relaxation time (36 and 30 ms, respectively), and exceptional durability, maintaining performance over 10,000 cycles.

Similarly, Cheng *et al*. [74] harnessed the unique structure and superior metallic conductivity of two-dimensional (2D) transition metal carbides and nitrides, known as MXenes, to develop an ultrasensitive piezoresistive sensor. The sensor mimics the random distribution of microstructured receptors found in human skin, adopting a bioinspired microspike microstructure crafted through a straightforward sandpaper stencil printing technique (Fig. 7(g)). The resulting device exhibits high sensitivity (151.4 kPa−1), fast response time of less than 130 ms, and an impressive pressure detection limit of 4.4 Pa. Stability is also notable, as it withstands over 10,000 loading cycles (Fig. 7(h)). The bionic microspike microstructure effectively elevates both the sensitivity of the sensor and its capacity for fine-pressure detection.

### 4.3 3D porous structure

Flexible piezoresistive strain sensors require a combination of good electrical conductivity, outstanding mechanical elasticity, and high compressibility to effectively serve diverse health monitoring applications. Conductive sponges or aerogels, characterized by their lightweight, porous hierarchical structures, and distinctive electrical properties, hold great promise for enhancing the performance of these flexible sensors due to their 3D porous architecture.

The dimensions and distribution pattern of the perforations significantly influence the performance characteristics of the sensor. Initially, the size of the pores is directly correlated with the relative density of the structure. The relative density is defined as the ratio of a material’s actual density to its theoretical density; in other words, it is the inverse of the porosity. Larger pores result in lower relative densities, whereas smaller pores yield higher relative densities. An increase in the relative density typically improves the mechanical properties of the sensor, such as increasing its modulus of elasticity and strength; conversely, it diminishes the sensitivity and flexibility of the sensor. Second, the radial distribution pattern of the perforations can also affect the sensor’s performance. A prevalent design strategy involves a structure exhibiting either an incremental or decremental radial distribution density [75]. In such a configuration, the distribution density of the pores varies progressively along the radial axis from the interior to the exterior. Density-increasing designs typically improve the sensor’s sensitivity since regions of higher density can detect strain variations more effectively. In contrast, a density-decreasing design may increase the sensor’s flexibility, as areas of lower density possess a greater capacity for deformation. In practical applications, the structural integrity of the pore architecture may be compromised or degraded due to environmental conditions, stress loading, and other extrinsic factors, potentially impacting the sensor’s performance. This can result in impaired sensitivity, flexibility, or stability of the sensor. Therefore, it is imperative to consider the stability and resilience of the pore structure during the design and fabrication process to ensure that the sensor maintains optimal performance under practical conditions.

Chen *et al*. [76] developed a continuous conductive layer on halloysite nanotubes (HNTs) by combining HNTs with pyrrolidone monomers in ultrapure water, followed by the addition of FeCl3·6H2O to induce the self-polymerization of pyrrolidone on HNTs. Silver nanoflowers (AgNFs) were uniformly distributed in deionized water by dissolving AgNFs and adding hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) powder at a specific mass fraction ratio. The fabrication of piezoresistive sensors involved dip-coating PU sponges with a composite material consisting of polypyrrole-coated HNTs (PPy/HNT) and AgNFs (Fig. 8(a)). This process was straightforward, scalable, and reproducible. The resulting sensor exhibited a broad sensing range (0–400 kPa), high sensitivity (3.97 kPa−1), and stable reproducibility over 12,000 s of cyclic testing (Fig. 8(b)). Luo *et al.* [77] created a graphene network on nickel foam using chemical vapor deposition under atmospheric pressure. They then infiltrated PDMS into graphene–nickel (GNi) foam by dipping it into a PDMS precursor mixture. Subsequently, the GNi foam was immersed in an HCl solution to remove the nickel, resulting in a graphene–PDMS hollow structure with PDMS serving as the elastomer template (Fig. 8(c)). The sensor achieved a high sensitivity of 15.9 kPa−1, rapid response time of 1.2 ms, and maintained stability within a linear range of up to 60 kPa (Fig. 8(d)).

Such sensors offer advantages such as a broad sensing range, heightened sensitivity, dependable repeatability, and swift response time, making them suitable for various health monitoring applications. Notably, conductive sponges or aerogels with 3D porous structures are particularly promising for further refining the performance of these flexible sensors due to their lightweight nature, intricate porous structure, and distinctive electrical properties.

The singular microconvex architecture ensures that it can be effectively deployed in structural health monitoring, biomedical applications, and other sectors. While this configuration enhances the sensor’s performance in specific facets, the limit of its improvement is finite. Conversely, an interlocking or array structure using a single microconvex body can achieve greater tunneling piezoresistive pressure and spatial resolution, indicating better application potential. Biomimetic microstructures replicate intricate natural designs within sensors. For instance, the detailed surface structure of ginkgo leaves effectively increases the sensor contact area, while the undulation of leaf veins enhances the sensitivity. The composite microconvex structure synergizes diverse structural advantages, resulting in superior sensor operation. This enhances its applicability within the realms of robotics and intelligent wearable technologies. For instance, it is utilized for tactile perception in robotic hands, enabling accurate grasping and manipulation of objects. The design approach for 3D porous structures offers numerous avenues for replicating microstructures onto substrates, effectively addressing fabrication challenges associated with microstructure and substrate production. It offers enhanced stability and efficiency in strain monitoring and data acquisition, thereby expanding its utility in industrial production and aerospace applications.

## 6 Advanced techniques for fabrication of microstructures in sensor design applications

The emergence of new requirements is propelling the advancement of flexible pressure sensors that can be mass-produced with feasibility and controllability. In the realms of health monitoring and human–computer interaction, tactile sensing holds significant importance. As a pivotal component of tactile sensing, flexible piezoresistive sensors have been the subject of extensive research. These sensors integrate conductive materials with a range of flexible elastomer substrates to form three essential parts: a flexible substrate, electrodes, and an active layer material. To enhance the sensitivity, stability, response/recovery time, and detection range of flexible substrates and active layer materials, researchers have devised numerous methods. Among these characteristics, sensitivity is the paramount performance parameter for piezoresistive sensors. The introduction of microstructures into a substrate has proven to be the most effective approach for improving sensitivity. Techniques commonly employed for fabricating microstructures include photolithography, natural epitope and commercial product molding, template removal, UV/ozone treatment, microsphere construction, 3D printing, and laser-assisted microengineering. This paper elucidates eight fabrication techniques for incorporating microstructures into piezoresistive sensors, each with substantial potential to enhance sensor sensitivity.

Utilizing molding techniques is a convenient approach for creating microstructures that can enhance pressure sensor performance, drawing inspiration from the rough or porous surfaces found in our daily surroundings. For instance, Ge *et al.* [83] employed a sponge as a template to construct piezoresistive strain sensors with porous microstructures (Fig. 10(a)). The molding process involves using the distinctive microstructures of natural biological epidermis, commercial products, or engineered molds as templates to produce elastomeric or composite materials with defined microstructures.

A prevalent technique for creating porous structures in flexible piezoresistive sensors is the template removal method. As depicted in Fig. 10(b), Park et al.* [84] produced piezoresistive strain sensors using solvent evaporation on resultant microdomes, a standard template removal approach to form porous architectures. In this process, sacrificial 3D templates—such as sugar, polystyrene beads, nickel foam, metal foils, or blends of aqueous and oil phases—are uniformly integrated into an elastomeric matrix. The porous structure is then realized by dissolving the sacrificial templates in specific solvents or by evaporating the solvents at elevated temperatures, either before or during the curing of the elastomeric matrix, without altering the substrate.

An alternative technique for creating microstructures involves UV/ozone treatment. Fu *et al.* [85] described a process in which crosslinked polysiloxanes on the surface of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) are transformed into rigid silicon oxide (SiO*x*) through the elimination of the organic components of PDMS via UV/O3 exposure. As shown in Fig. 10(c), Yu *et al.* [86] capitalized on the bending structure of a PDMS template formed by UV/O3 treatment to develop piezoresistive sensors with microstructured carbon nanotube/PDMS arrays. This approach significantly enhanced the sensitivity of the resultant sensors.

The incorporation of microspheres as embedded microstructures is another effective method. In contrast to porous designs, microsphere structures offer greater sensitivity enhancement, drawing the interest of researchers [87−89]. As illustrated in Fig. 10(d), Tian *et al. *[90] employed microspheres to create highly sensitive piezoresistive strain sensors. Upon application of force, the stress concentrates at the contact points between individual microspheres and between the flat electrodes and microspheres, causing localized deformation at these small contact areas. This concentrated deformation increases the contact area, thereby boosting the conductivity of the sensor.

Photolithography is a technique that transfers a pattern from a mask onto a substrate using photochemical reactions followed by chemical or physical etching to create pressure sensors with nano/micrometer-scale structures. As depicted in Fig. 10(e), Zhu *et al.* [91] utilized conventional photolithography on a silicon (Si) wafer featuring a concave pyramid structure and then developed a piezoresistive tactile sensor with the aid of reverse molding using PDMS.

3D printing technology enables the fabrication of highly intricate 3D objects for sensors, offering a rapid prototyping method that constructs 3D structures by sequentially depositing material layers based on computer-aided design (CAD) models. Several 3D printing techniques have been developed to date, including fused deposition modeling (FDM) [92], selective laser sintering (SLS) [93], stereolithography (SLA) [94], digital light processing (DLP) [95], and direct ink writing (DIW) [96]. As demonstrated in Fig. 10(f), Peng *et al. *[97] utilized multijet DIW 3D printing to replicate micropyramids and microhalf-cylinders as molds for creating piezoresistive sensors with defined microstructures.

Laser-assisted micromachining represents another approach for crafting microstructures. As depicted in Fig. 10(g), Dos Santos *et al.* [98] employed a laser-assisted microengineering technique to create microstructured piezoresistive strain sensors by fashioning microcones from an acrylic mold. Lasers, capable of emitting light through excited radiation, can generate extremely high localized temperatures, making them potent tools for the fabrication of flexible sensors. With controlled output energies, lasers can precisely cut substrates into bespoke shapes, engrave substrates to form microstructures, and even carbonize certain materials to construct sensing layers.

Freeze-drying, when applied to nanomaterials, has emerged as a highly effective method for creating 3D porous materials, a technique that has advanced in recent years. This process involves using a precursor—a mixture of liquid solvent and solute particles frozen solid within a mold—and then sublimating the solidified solvent crystals under reduced pressure and temperature conditions to convert the liquid‒gas interface into a solid‒gas interface. This transition eliminates the disparities between the gas and liquid phases, minimizing capillary forces, which ensures that the sample maintains its structural integrity and volume, resulting in a porous material [99,100]. As shown in Fig. 10(h), Ma *et al.* [101] employed the freeze-drying technique to create MXene/reduced graphene oxide (MX/rGO) aerogels with a 3D porous structure for use in the assembly and fabrication of flexible piezoresistive sensors.

Printing technologies represent a significant class of techniques for the preparation of microstructures, encompassing methods such as screen printing, inkjet printing, and spray coating. These techniques offer efficient and cost-effective means of fabricating microstructures that are amenable to a diverse range of substrate materials. In the production of flexible piezoresistive strain sensors, these methods facilitate simplistic and large-scale preparation at minimal expense. Wang *et al.* [102] introduced a methodology that employs screen-printing technology to create sensing films composed of multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MW-CNTs) embedded within a PDMS matrix, featuring periodically spaced microseams. The utility of screen printing lies in its ability to facilitate the precise insertion of right-rib features, which aids in the straightforward patterning and alignment of the sensing films. Despite their advantages, these printing methodologies are not without their challenges. These methods often suffer from limitations in terms of achievable resolution and the diversity of materials that can be employed. Consequently, the optimization of printing techniques to enhance performance metrics is a prominent focus of current research endeavors. Ongoing investigations seek to surmount these constraints and expand the capabilities of these printing methods for the fabrication of high-performance microstructures.

### 7.1 Opportunities

Flexible piezoresistive strain sensors, enabled by innovative microstructure designs, offer a broad spectrum of applications. These sensors boast high precision, ease of use, and low power requirements, all at an affordable cost. Their exceptional flexibility allows them to conform to the shape of deforming objects, making them well suited for application in flexible and curved environments. The versatility of these sensors has extended to various fields, including mechanical engineering, biomedicine, and smart textiles. Their high sensitivity enables the detection of minute strains, rendering them invaluable for a wide range of monitoring tasks. The ease of operation and integration further enhances their applicability across diverse settings. Moreover, these sensors exhibit long operational life within wireless sensor networks, underscoring their low energy consumption. Coupled with the economical nature of their production, mass manufacturing can achieve substantial cost efficiencies. As advancements in microstructure design and fabrication technology continue, flexible piezoresistive strain sensors hold immense potential for future research and development endeavors.

### 7.2 Challenges

Despite the commendable performance of flexible strain sensors engineered through microstructure design, such as their high precision and broad applicability, current research predominantly concentrates on enhancing individual sensor attributes. The concurrent realization of a large strain range, heightened sensitivity, and robust stability in strain sensors, along with multifunctionality, remains a formidable challenge. For instance, discrepancies between the relative changes in strain and resistance over time during loading and unloading can result in imprecise deformation monitoring. Moreover, strain sensors are typically evaluated under controlled test conditions, often neglecting the impact of real-world environments on performance and user comfort. Due to their susceptibility to environmental variables such as temperature and humidity, flexible piezoresistive strain sensors exhibit limited stability and environmental resilience, necessitating specialized protection and upkeep that could compromise measurement accuracy. Additionally, these sensors possess limited durability, raising concerns about their longevity in extended use. The fabrication of flexible piezoresistive strain sensors based on microstructure design is intricate and challenging to scale up, necessitating advanced technical expertise.

## 8 Conclusions

This work provides an overview of the advancements in flexible piezoresistive strain sensors, with a focus on the impact of microstructure design on sensing performance. The objective is to elucidate the microsensing mechanisms of these sensors and to identify the principles and techniques that enhance their performance. This review concentrates on sensor sensing principles, key performance metrics, structural designs and fabrication processes, as well as the enhancement of performance and specific applications. Researchers have conducted extensive and fruitful investigations into the structural design of sensors, material selection, and performance optimization. By synthesizing the latest findings in this field, this paper offers a summary and perspective on the future of flexible piezoresistive strain sensors that leverage microstructure design (Fig. 11).

There is a need to investigate novel strain-sensitive materials, such as innovative polymeric materials and nanocomposites, with the aim of enhancing their performance to boost sensor sensitivity and stability. Future studies should further explore the mechanical properties of these sensors, including their resistance to wear, pressure, and bending, which can be tailored for diverse applications.
