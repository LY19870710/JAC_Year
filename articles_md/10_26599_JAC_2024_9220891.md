## 1 Introduction

Due to their extremely high Young’s modulus, high specific strength, and low density, carbon fibers (Cf) are widely applied in advanced fiber-reinforced composites [1]. To take full advantage and improve the mechanical properties of composites, extensive research has been devoted to the surface treatment of Cf, such as electrophoretic deposition [2], plasma treatment [3], chemical grafting [4], chemical vapor deposition (CVD) coating [5], and sol–gel coating [6]. Most of these research efforts have focused on interface modification by coating on carbon fibers while still facing the challenges of technique complexity and high cost. The new technique of nanosecond pulsed laser annealing [7] uses the thermal spike of high-energy, short-duration laser pulses to rapidly and locally heat the materials, acting as on-site quenching and annealing. Therefore, this approach has the potential to locally modify the surface structures of carbon fibers under ambient conditions, which is significant for the interface engineering of fiber-reinforced composites.

Recently, Narayan *et* *al*. [8–10] performed plenty of works on pulsed laser annealing of thin a-C films under ambient conditions. They reported the fabrication of a variety of carbon nanostructures and their heterostructures with atomically sharp interfaces, including Q-carbon (~82% sp3), diamond, α-carbon (~40% sp3), and graphene. Furthermore, they stated that the pulsed laser annealing technique can directly convert carbon fibers and nanotubes into diamond fibers at ambient temperature and pressure in air. The conversion involves melting in a superundercooled state using nanosecond laser pulses and quenching rapidly to convert into phase-pure diamond [11] . By combining *in* *situ* laser heating and high-pressure technologies, Zeng *et* *al.* [12] described the yielding of amorphous diamond with purely sp3-bonded carbon. This was achieved by laser heating of glassy carbon under a very high pressure of 49.6 GPa at 1527 °C.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the carbon structure can be tuned by effective manipulation of laser techniques, as a postprocessing method for microstructure modification. It is promising to customize the local microstructures of engineering components such as carbon fibers, although theoretical guidelines are undergoing sluggish progress. Previously, several studies [13−17] have been performed to explain how the sp3 fraction is affected by the substrate temperature, compressive stress, and cooling rate (*R*), which strongly impact the resultant microstructure and local structural rearrangement (on the picosecond timescale). In addition, hydrogen [18] and nitrogen [19] play important roles in promoting the polyamorphic transition of carbon. Besides, the evolution of amorphous carbon (a-C) across densities has been simulated by several groups [20−22]. It is believed that the cooling rate plays a significant role in the thickness and quality of graphene by influencing the structural transformation of the a-C matrix. However, the cooling rates in both experiments and theoretical melt-quenching simulations are more often than not randomly chosen, which makes their impact on the structural features of a-C materials puzzling. Therefore, no consistent conclusion has been reached.

The vitrification behavior and formation mechanism of a-C structures are significant for their preparation and applications but are still elusive. In this work, we examined the effect of cooling rate on the structural features of a-C. Our results demonstrate that the structural order of carbon structures decreases when vitrification is boosted by higher cooling rates. Additionally, a very high critical cooling rate (*R*c) of 1014 K/s or 100 K/ps is first determined from the evolution character of the coarse-grained collective variable by measuring the local atomic environment similarity, which provides a criterion for experimentally processing and theoretically simulating a-C structures. Furthermore, it is located right into the capability of heat spikes generated by laser techniques, which makes the post-processing of the microstructures of carbon materials feasible and promising by laser techniques.

## 2 Simulation methods

Here, the liquid-quench hing method is used to generate a variety of a-C structures, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This method is a surrogate for the experimental preparation of a-C materials with physical vapor deposition, ion irradiation, or pulsed laser quenching techniques, where a liquid-like region is created for a fraction of a picosecond [23]. For such a purpose, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are carried out here by the open source LAMMPS code [24], with the Gaussian approximation potential (GAP) recently developed by Rowe *et* *al.* [25]. This carbon potential provides a better description of the complex interactions among carbon atoms. It is feasible to study an array of carbon structures and phase transitions among carbon allotropes [26]. A timestep of 1 fs is applied to numerically integrate Newton’s  equation of motion. The isothermal–isobaric (NPT) ensemble is adopted to control pressures and temperatures, where the Nosé–Hoover thermostat [27] is applied to control the coupling of the system to the thermal bath.

*
Fig. 1Schematic illustration of molecular dynamics simulation protocol for rapid quenching of undercooled liquid carbon using a wide range of cooling rates (1013–1015 K/s) at either 5 or 70 GPa, followed by release of external pressure at room temperature (300 K).

The starting configurations are all the same as a simple cubic lattice with 3240 atoms. Then, they are heated in a two-step way to obtain the equilibrium liquid. First, they are heated and held at 8000 K for 10 ps to randomize all atomic positions and to equilibrate the molten state quickly and efficiently. Second, the liquid carbons are cooled and annealed at 6000 K for 20 ps, after which they are prepared for quenching. The melting point of carbon is slightly below 6000 K when pressures are lower than 100 GPa. The finite size test showed that cells with 3240 atoms are large enough to simulate the energies and structural features of a-C. Thereafter, the undercooled carbon liquids are separately quenched to 300 K under a batch of different cooling rates (9 different cooling rates from 1013 to 1015 K/s). Finally, we equilibrated the systems and released the pressure to reach ambient conditions. The data analysis is performed by sampling atomic trajectories after an equilibration run of more than 25,000 steps, which has been shown to be long enough due to the fast dynamics of carbon atoms with a low mass and a fast vibration frequency. Note that two sets of MD simulations are separately run at 5 and 70 GPa to compare the amorphization behavior at low and high pressures. This aims at simulating the different amorphous carbon morphologies observed after the laser annealing of the carbon films [8−10]. Since the stress states within carbon films are uncertain in experiments, more different pressure values are expected to provide a detailed understanding of the effect of pressure on the structures of a-C in the future. Here, we randomly applied pressures of 70 and 5 GPa to perform NPT ensemble simulations, while high-pressure experiments on carbon materials applied pressures ranging from 1 bar to 60.1 GPa. They can separately illustrate the effects of low and high pressures on carbon structures during laser quenching processing.

The structure analysis was performed using the PLUMED library [28] (version 2.6 [29]) and the I.S.A.A.C.S. program [30]. Moreover, the local and global Steinhardt order parameters [31], which are denoted as q*6 and *Q*6, respectively, are built using the sixth order of spherical harmonics (*Y*6*m*). Among them, *Q*6s are coarse grained over their nearest neighbors. Therefore, they measure the degree to which the first coordination shell around an atom is ordered. *q*6s grade the extent to which orientation of the atoms in the first coordination sphere of an atom matches the orientation of the central atom. For the atom *i*, the order parameters *q*6 and *Q*6 are separately computed by Eqs. (1) and (2), respectively:

(1) q6i=∑jNi∑m=−6m=6q6m∗(i)q6m(j)

(2) Q6i=(4π2l+1∑m=−66|1Ni+1(q6m(i)+∑jNiq6m(j))|2)1/2

where * Ni and  q6m(i) are the coordination number and the Steinhart vector of atom  i, respectively.  q6m(i) is calculated from Eq. (3):

(3) q6m(i)=1Ni∑jNiY6m(rij)

where j* loops over all atoms in the nearest neighbors of atom * i; rij* is the radius between atom *i* and *j*. Furthermore, the environmental similarity is calculated to measure how similar the environment around atoms (*χ*) is to that found in some reference crystal structures (*χ*0), such as fullerene (C60). It is introduced based on the local atomic density around an atom defined as Eq. (4) [32]:

(4) ρχ=∑i∈χexp(−|ri→−r→|22σ2)

where *i* runs over the neighbors in the environment *χ*; * σ is a broadening parameter;  r→ and  ri→ are the coordinates of the neighbors relative to the central atom. The environment similarity parameters of χ* and *χ*0 are compared using the kernel (Eq. (5)):

(5) kχ=∫dr→ρχ(r→)ρχ0(r→)

The kernel is finally normalized (Eq. (6)):

(6) k~χ=1n∑i∈χ∑j∈χ0exp(−|ri→−rj→0|24σ2)

such that  k~χ0(χ0)=1. This quantity is multicollinear since the above kernel can be computed for multiple atoms. Therefore, both the average value for the atoms in the system (denoted as “mean”) and the number of atoms that are more than some target values (marked as “morethan”) can be derived as well.

### 3.1 Glass feature of carbon processed by fast quenching and/or higher pressures

The vitrification of carbon is not easily characterized by  the variation of either density or volume (which are not sensitive)  with cooling rates. Here, we apply a two-step method to determine the critical cooling rate. First, a rough guess is given by the variation in the energies and/or the short-range structure parameters. Second, it is refined by further analysis of the medium-range structure parameters. In particular, the environmental similarity of amorphous carbon with a reference structure of fullerene is considered since it consists of a large carbon cluster with 60 atoms. It shows a better resolution to the change in medium-range ordering with cooling rate, as illustrated later.

First, the quenching process is monitored by the variation in potential energy with the temperature. The dependence of the potential energies with temperatures, i.e., the *E*p(*T*) curves, is plotted in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b) for various cooling rates, *R*s. A comparison of Figs. 2(a) and 2(b) reveals different glass transition behaviors at either lower (5 GPa) or higher (70 GPa) pressures. At the same time, the hybridization ratios quenched from different *R*s are analyzed in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b) to capture the evolution of bonding features.

At lower pressures, the graphitization of carbon is inevitable, especially at lower *R,* while its verification is feasible only at extremely high cooling rates. When *R* 13.5 K/s, an abrupt decrease in *E*p(*T*) at 5 GPa is observed when the temperature decreases from 4000 to 3500 K, as shown in Fig. 2(a). This sharp decrease in potential energy during solidification is caused by the trapping of carbon atoms in the deep wells of crystalline solids in potential energy landscapes (PELs). In fact, graphite with almost 100% sp2 hybridization states is detected after quenching at 5 GPa and *R* = 1013 K/s, as shown in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b). This promotes the high-quality synthesis of graphene by tailoring the cooling rate, which has been demonstrated by experimental and MD results [17,33]. Only if the quenching rate is fast enough (*R* > 1013.75 K/s)  are two regions of *E*p(*T*) curves with different slopes detected, which is a typical behavior of glass transition separating the liquid and the glass states. At the same time, more sp1 and sp3 hybridization states from liquid carbon can be frozen to room temperature. Hence, graphitization easily nucleates at low pressures due to the fast dynamics of structural relaxation for carbon atoms with light weights. Vitrification or glass transition is only promised at much higher cooling rates.

Interestingly, the verification of liquid carbon or the amorphization of carbon can be enhanced not only by fast quenching but also by high pressures. As illustrated in Fig. 2(b), the *E*p(*T*) curves can be readily divided by the steepness differences between the lower and higher temperature regions, which separates the amorphous solids with slower relaxations from the supercooled liquids with faster relaxations. The lack of a sharp boundary between the liquid and the amorphous states for all cooling rates suggests that it is difficult to define the melting point temperature between 4000 and 6000 K at 70 GPa. In addition, the glass transition behavior is generally expected. Moreover, the slopes of the *E*p(*T*) curves at the higher pressure of 70 GPa are much more moderate than those with crystallization traces at the lower pressure of 5 GPa. The decrease in *E*p(*T*) with *T* largely slows down at 70 GPa. These results suggest that the high pressure has great potential for tuning nucleation kinetics and/or structure relaxation dynamics during solidification processes, thereby promoting amorphization via rapid quenching. Note that the densities of the a-C structures quenched from 5 and 70 GPa are approximately 2.25 and 3.75 g/cm3, respectively, which differ greatly from each other. This agrees with the characterization results of hybridization states, which separately show sp2-rich and sp3-rich structures. Therefore, the a-C structures formed at the two pressures are completely different, which also explains the morphological diversity of the laser-annealed carbon films [8−10]. Finally, the hybridization states do not always vary with the cooling rate, as illustrated in Fig. 3(b). Hence, the change in the hybridization state alone cannot indicate whether crystallization occurred. It is not possible to identify the critical cooling rates at the moment.

### 3.2 Local atomic distribution characteristics after quenching at different cooling rates

The variation in the radial distribution function (RDF) with the cooling rate is plotted in Fig. 4, which provides the simplest measure of short-range order in quenched a-C structures. Figures 4(a) and 4(b) show the behavior expected for carbon, with a first-neighbor peak at approximately 1.42 (1.48) Å at 5 (70) GPa, a second-neighbor peak slightly below 2.5 Å, and a minimum approximately at 2 Å. Note that the rightward shift of the 1st peak from 5 to 70 GPa is caused by the large differences in the hybridization states of quenched a-C structures at those two pressures. At 5 GPa, a graphite-like carbon is quenched, which has a first-neighbor radius of approximately 1.42 Å, similar to the bond length of carbon in graphene or within the graphite plane. This is also consistent with its sp2-rich hybridization state, as shown in Fig. 3(a). In contrast, the a-C quenched at 70 GPa has a diamond-like structure, which contains sp3-rich carbon atoms, as illustrated in Fig. 3(b). Moreover, its first peak is at approximately 1.48 Å. This peak is much longer than that in either graphene or graphite but closer to the bond length of carbon atoms in diamond. Therefore, the longer first-nearest neighbor radius of 1.48 Å is directly related to the diamond-like local atomic environment. At the same time, it is slightly shorter than that in crystalline diamond due to the compression effects at 70 GPa. In contrast, the second RDF peak is influenced purely by the compression of atomic distance (*r*) from high pressures. It decreases with increasing density at higher pressures, as expected. Therefore, the shift of the second RDF peak from 5 to 70 GPa is easily understood without the interference of atomic motifs.

Moreover, there is a gap between the first and second peaks, and *g*(*r*) decreases to zero at the minimum, which is typical of amorphous carbon. Additionally, the visible asymmetry of the second peak slightly below 2.50 Å for all a-C structures indicates the presence of four-membered rings. In addition, the heights of the first and second peaks of the *g*(*r*) curves decrease and widen with increasing *R*s, as shown in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b) for a-C structures quenched from both 5 and 70 GPa. However, shallow dips of crystallization features are observed for a-C structures from *R* 13.75 K/s at 5 GPa. The *g*(*r*) curves of a-C quenched from any *R* at 70 GPa rapidly converge to approximately one with increasing atomic distance, *r*, which is another characteristic of amorphous solids. In addition, the first peaks at 70 GPa in Fig. 4(b) are wider than those at 5 GPa in Fig. 4(a). Therefore, the disorder character is more obvious for a-C structures quenched at higher pressures. This agrees with the glass transition behavior discussed in Section 3.1.

Furthermore, the results of the angular distribution function (ADF) at various cooling rates are shown in Fig. 5. Basically, the ADF peaks become broader and more diffuse with cooling rates increasing. The ADF maxima at low (high) pressures are centered at approximately 120° and 109°, mirroring the structural features of the crystalline allotropes from graphite honeycombs and diamond tetrahedrons, respectively. In Fig. 5(b), only one single peak at approximately *θ* = 109° is observed for a-C structures quenched at 70 GPa. In contrast, a main peak at 120° and a nearby hump at approximately 109.5° are observed for those from lower pressures. With increasing *R*s at 5 GPa, a significant decreasing of the peak height at *θ* = 120° and the rising of its hump at *θ* = 109.5° can be observed in Fig. 5(a). These results agree with the decreasing sp2 ratio and increasing sp3 ratio shown in Fig. 3(a).

In general, similar features of near-neighbor structures among quenched carbons from different cooling rates are detected by the radial distribution function and angular distribution function. The randomness of quenched carbon increases with increasing cooling rate. It is still challenging to quantify the degree of structural ordering and identify the critical cooling rate required to avoid crystallization. In what follows, coarse-grained collective variables, which can describe the medium-range ordering of a-C structures, are further analyzed to map the evolution behavior of structure order parameters with cooling rates and to refine the critical cooling rates.

### 3.3 Critical cooling rate extracted from evolution behavior of structure order parameters

Here, we start with the Steinhardt parameters, also called bond order parameters. They can be constructed as either the global *Q*6 or the local *q*6 by using the sixth order of spherical harmonics. *Q*6 measures the degree to which the first coordination shell around an atom is ordered. *q*6 grades the extent to which the orientation of the atoms in the first coordination sphere of an atom matches the orientation of the central atom. As shown in Fig. 6, the calculated *Q*6 and *q*6 all decrease with increasing *R*. This indicates that the local coordination shells become more disordered at larger *R*. Therefore, they provide a clear picture of the evolution of the local ordering degree with increasing cooling rate. In particular, the bond order parameter of *Q*6 shows an almost linear relationship with *R*, suggesting a high resolution to the degree of disorder. However, it is still not feasible to locate the boundary between the a-C structures with or without crystallization. The a-C structures with different local atomic motifs show quite different values of *Q*6 and *q*6. They scatter within a wide range, as illustrated in Fig. 6. This affects the analysis of the *R*-dependent behavior of those ordering parameters with the variation in cooling rate. It is not feasible to identify the critical cooling rate by either *Q*6 or *q*6 since no critical behavior can be observed when varying the cooling rate.

To solve this problem, we map the evolution behavior of the environment similarity parameter * k~C60 with respect to the cooling rate. It measures how similar the local atomic environment is to some well-defined carbon allotropes, such as C60, in this case. C60, with 60 atoms, is a large carbon cluster. The environmental similarity parameter with reference to the C60 cluster,  k~C60, can help in identifying the variation in medium-range ordering with cooling rate. We calculate both the “mean” and the “more than” (the number of atoms with a kernel larger than 0.5) values to obtain a better descriptor of the statistical distribution characteristics of the atomic structures. In Fig. 7(a), they both show a steep change when R* increases from 1013.75 to 1014 K/s. This suggests that a large local atomic structure adjustment occurs if *R*s > 1014 K/s or 100 K/ps due to the variation in the degree of vitrification or crystallization. As illustrated in Figs. 7(b) and 7(c), obvious layered structures with the majority of sp2 hybridization states appear for a-C structures quenched below 1013.50 K/s, which signifies graphitization at lower *R*s. The cooling rates adopted for the Ni-catalyzed transformation of a-C into graphene range from 0.15 to 3 K/ps [17], which is far below the bar of 100 K/ps. In contrast, more disordered and more sp3-hybridized states appear above 1013.75 K/s, as displayed in Figs. 7(d) and 7(e). Hence, the critical cooling rate of monoatomic carbon, *R*c, can be taken as 1014 K/s or 100 K/ps.

Note: Reference structure is taken as fullerene, C60, while atoms in insets of (b–e) are illustrated with hybridization states. Here, red, gray, and blue balls denote hybridization states of sp1, sp2, and sp3, respectively.

Moreover, *R*c of 1014 K/s or 100 K/ps suggests that the monoatomic carbon is a bad glass former, which is difficult to process experimentally using traditional techniques. However, it is quite suitable for molecular dynamics simulations, which can easily achieve cooling rates greater than 109 K/s. This system represents an ideal system for investigating the nature of glass formation and the complex structural characteristics of glass materials.

Finally, *R*c of 1014 K/s or 100 K/ps corresponds to a thermal spike lasting for approximately 33 ps. Since the thermal spike from pulse laser techniques can be maintained either more promptly or more sluggishly, the cooling rate, which affects the transformation of carbon structures, can be customized on purpose. Therefore, it is also feasible to tune the structure order extent of a-C structures by manipulating or optimizing the on-site heat treatment craft of laser techniques. Hence, the physics behind the fabrication of a variety of carbon nanostructures with different degrees of structural ordering by Narayan *et* *al*. [8–11], such as Q-carbon (~82% sp3) and nanodiamond, can be explained by the variation in cooling rates related to the thermal spikes from the pulsed laser annealing of thin a-C films.

## 4 Conclusions

A systematic study of quenching simulations is undertaken to understand how the structural features of a-C are affected by cooling rates related to thermal spikes during laser processing. We have demonstrated that monoatomic carbon is a bad glass former. Since its vitrification requires a critical cooling rate as high as 1014 K/s, which results in easy crystallization, it is an ideal system for investigating the nature of glass formation and its structure. Interestingly, we note that the verification of liquid carbon or the amorphization of carbon can be enhanced not only by fast quenching but also by high pressure. At lower pressures, the crystallization of liquid carbon is inevitable, especially at lower *R*, while its verification is feasible only at extremely high cooling rates. However, it is much easier for liquid carbon to turn into glass states at high pressures, and more detailed studies are needed in the future to further explore whether there are pressure-induced phenomena, such as polyamorphism of a-C and liquid‒liquid phase transitions.
