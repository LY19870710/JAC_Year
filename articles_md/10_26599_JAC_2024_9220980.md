## 1 Introduction

The transition metal diboride ceramics, such as hafnium diboride and zirconium diboride, exhibit excellent comprehensive properties, including high melting points, remarkable hardness, superior conductivity, corrosion resistance, wear resistance, and excellent thermal and chemical stability, indicating that they have significant applications in cutting tools and aerospace fields, such as nose caps and wing leading edge of hypersonic vehicles [1−5]. In recent years, the extension of material design concept of “high entropy” to ceramic materials has sparked significant research interest in high-entropy diboride (HEB) ceramics owing to their increased hardness and superior oxidation resistance. HEBs have become extremely promising candidates for high-temperature structural components and thermal protection systems [6−11].

However, owing to their strong covalent bonds and low diffusion coefficient, HEBs are difficult to densify, which significantly affects their mechanical properties and limits their application. For example, Yang *et al*. [12,13] utilized the spark plasma sintering (SPS) technique to fabricate the (HfZrTaVNb)B2 ceramics at 1800 °C, resulting in a relative density of only 89%–94%, which was limited to 94.2% even at an elevated sintering temperature of 1900 °C. Consequently, researchers have implemented strategies such as introducing sintering aids and optimizing the preparation process to improve the sintering performance of HEBs. For example, Huang *et al*. [14] introduced metal Ni as a sintering aid in (Hf0.2Mo0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)B2 ceramics, sintering the sample with pressure-less sintering at 1800 °C. The addition of Ni resulted in highly dense HEBs; however, it also led to an abnormal increase in grain size, consequently deteriorating the mechanical properties. Feng *et al*. [15] fabricated (Hf0.2Zr0.2Ti0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2)B2 ceramics via a two-step sintering process. Initially, the sample was maintained at 1650 °C for 5 min to eliminate residual oxides, followed by rapid heating to 2000–2200 °C for 10 min. Despite achieving a density of up to 99%, the grain size of the sample increased uncontrollably due to the second step of sintering at higher temperatures, thus decreasing the mechanical properties. Therefore, the prevailing challenge lies in the preparation of HEBs with highly dense and size-tunable grain structures.

It is well known that grain size plays an important role in regulating the mechanical properties and wear resistance of ceramics [16−18]. For example, Rezaie *et al*. [19] reported that the bending strength of ZrB2–SiC composite ceramics decreased with increasing sizes of ZrB2 grains and SiC grains. Moreover, Zhou *et al*. [20] investigated the impact of grain size on the mechanical and wear properties of B4C ceramics and revealed that a reduction in grain size can significantly enhance the hardness, fracture toughness, and wear resistance of B4C ceramics. In addition, studies on the wear resistance of dual-phase high-entropy boride–carbide ceramics have demonstrated their outstanding wear resistance [21−23]. However, few studies have explored the wear characteristics of pure high-entropy diboride ceramics, and the effect of grain size on the mechanical properties of high-entropy diboride ceramics remains unclear.

To address the above problems, in this work, a series of highly dense (HfZrTaVNb)B2 ceramics with adjustable grain sizes were synthesized by SPS technology combined with a facile two-step sintering strategy. The effects of grain size on the hardness, fracture toughness, and abrasive resistance of high-entropy (HfZrTaVNb)B2 ceramics were investigated, and the underlying wear mechanism was revealed.

## 2 Experimental

The raw materials used for preparation of (HfZrTaVNb)B2 ceramics include commercial powders of HfO2 (99.9%, Macklin, China), ZrO2 (99.0%, Macklin, China), Ta2O5 (99.5%, Macklin, China), V2O5 (99.99%, Macklin, China), Nb2O5 (99.9%, Macklin, China), B4C (99.9%, 2–3 µm, Aladdin, China), and graphite (99.95%, Macklin, China). The boro/carbothermal reduction (BCTR) method was adopted to synthesize the HEB powder at 1600 °C, according to Eq. (1), with more details described in our previous study [24]. Briefly, the oxides were proportionally blended with B4C and graphite powders manually in an agate mortar and then subjected to heating at 1600 °C for 2 h under an Ar flow in a tube furnace. To ensure the completion of the BCTR process and reduce the amount of residual oxides, excess 3 wt% B4C and graphite powders were added. The added graphite powders can react with the byproduct B2O3 from the BCTR process to generate B4C, facilitating the conversion of B2O3 to B4C and thereby enhancing the BCTR reaction. Afterwards, the as-received HEB powders were loaded into a graphite mold with an inner diameter of 20 mm, lined with a layer of graphite foils around the internal surfaces of the mold and the top and bottom surfaces of the plungers to prevent adhesion. Each sample was sintered with 15.5 g of powder. Two-step sintering was carried out with an SPS apparatus (SPS-332HF, Fuji, Japan) under a uniaxial pressure of 30 MPa with a sintering atmosphere of Ar. The temperature was initially increased to 1200 °C at a rate of 100 °C/min, followed by a rise to 1750 °C at 55 °C/min, and it was maintained at 1750 °C for 12 min. Subsequently, the temperature was elevated to 1900 °C at a rate of 100 °C/min and held for 2, 5, 10, and 13 min, respectively. The obtained samples were named HEB2, HEB5, HEB10, and HEB13, as shown in Table 1. A grinding machine (ACC52SA1, Okamoto, Japan) was used to remove the carburized layer on the surface of the sintered samples for subsequent characterization.

### 3.1 Microstructures of HEBs

Table 1 lists the relative densities of (HfZrTaVNb)B2 samples obtained by two-step sintering with different holding time, i.e., HEB2, HEB5, HEB10, and HEB13, with 97.6%, 97.5%, 97.0%, and 97.3%, respectively. This finding indicates that dense structures in the HEBs were achieved via the two-step sintering strategy. According to Ref. [13], significant intracrystalline pores exist in the SPS-ed (HfZrTaVNb)B2 ceramics. This occurred because the rapid heating rate during the SPS process led to a sudden rise in temperature associated with unexpected fast grain growth, rendering that the grain boundary migration rate exceeds that of pore migration [26,27]. Once intracrystalline pores are formed, they cannot be eliminated by increasing the sintering temperature, resulting in a decrease in the relative density of the sintered ceramics [28]. Therefore, the heating rate ranged from 1200 to 1750 °C, and the sample was held at a lower temperature of 1750 °C for a long time, as shown in Fig. 2(a), which was conducive to reducing the number of intracrystalline pores and thus increasing the relative density of high-entropy diboride ceramics. The selection of 1750 °C was based on our pre-experimental results, as shown in Fig. 3, which shows that the (HfZrTaVNb)B2 ceramic exhibits a continual densification process, while the grain size remains almost unchanged with the extension of holding time during sintering when the sintering temperature is in the range of 1700–1800 °C. As a comparison, without the first step of holding at 1750 °C and the slowing down of the heating rate, the relative density of the (HfZrTaVNb)B2 ceramics obtained by direct rapid heating to 1900 °C for 2 min is merely 93.8%, validating the conclusion. Figure 2(b) presents the XRD patterns for the as-synthesized HEBs, showing that all the samples possessed the typical hexagonal AlB2 structure without other detected impurities, confirming the successful fabrication of (HfZrTaVNb)B2 samples. In the case of sample HEB2, a faint peak appeared at the left side of the diffraction peak corresponding to the (101) plane, which was attributed to the presence of trace amounts of unsolved secondary boride phase. The weak peak gradually diminished with increasing holding time, suggesting the occurrence of diffusion and solid-solution processes at high temperatures. EDS mapping analysis of the HEB samples was performed, and the results are shown in Figs. 2(c)–2(f). The transition metal elements displayed a uniform distribution, confirming the formation of single-phase high-entropy ceramics. There was slight segregation of Nb and Ta in HEB2 and HEB5, which was attributed to the short holding time and slow diffusion kinetics, as reported in Refs. [5,29]. However, with increasing holding time, all the elements exhibited a more homogeneous distribution, which is in agreement with the XRD results. Moreover, there is little difference in the relative densities of HEB2, HEB5, HEB10, and HEB13 samples, revealing that the sintering time of the second step has almost no effect on the relative density of samples.

Figure 4 displays the inverse pole figure (IPF) diagram and grain size distribution of samples HEB2, HEB5, HEB10, and HEB13 via EBSD analysis. The grain size of the samples increased from 1.64 µm for HEB2 to 8.53 µm for HEB13 as the holding time of the second step gradually increased from 2 to 13 min. These findings suggest that the grain structure of the (HfZrTaVNb)B2 ceramics grew rapidly at 1900 °C. Figure 5 shows SEM images of the polished surfaces in backscattered electron (BSE) mode and the fracture surfaces of the as-synthesized samples, revealing that the grain sizes of HEB2, HEB5, HEB10, and HEB13 increase gradually, which is in agreement with Fig. 4. Notably, there is a small amount of black phase in addition to the HEB phase in Figs. 5(a)–5(d), which should be residual B4C [12]. The residual B4C grains also grew gradually with prolonging the second-step sintering time. In addition, some microcracking can be found on the polished surfaces of samples HEB5, HEB10, and HEB13 by close observation, which can be attributed to the mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients between B4C and the high-entropy diborides [30] and the anisotropic thermal expansion of high-entropy diboride ceramics [5]. The spark plasma sintering technique utilized in this study is known for its extremely rapid cooling speed after the sintering process. Consequently, the sample experiences stress during the quick cooling process due to the mismatch of thermal expansion coefficients, leading to the formation of microcracking. Notably, no obvious microcracking was observed in sample HEB2, which was likely attributed to its refined grain size. On one hand, the refinement of grains led to a significant increase in the strength of the sample through the refinement strengthening effect, which helps to resist stress and prevent microcracking. On the other hand, grain refinement means a higher grain boundary density, facilitating the dissipation of stress, further preventing the formation of microcracking. SEM images of the fracture surface for the samples in Figs. 5(e)–5(h) reveal that the fracture mode of (HfZrTaVNb)B2 ceramics features transgranular fracture, and there are only a few pores except for B4C, which proves the successful preparation of highly dense high-entropy diboride ceramics.

### 3.2 Mechanical properties of HEBs

Figure 6 depicts the relationship between the hardness and grain size of the samples. The hardness of the (HfZrTaVNb)B2 ceramics clearly increases with the decrease in grain size. Specifically, HEB2, with a minimum grain size of 1.64 µm, has the highest hardness, reaching 26.7 GPa. The Hall–Petch equation given in Eq. (4) expresses the relationship between the hardness and grain size of polycrystalline ceramic materials [31]:

*
Fig. 6(a) Hardness and grain size of HEB samples and (b) hardness of HEB samples as a function of inverse square root of grain size.

(4) H=H0+kd−1/2

where H* is the hardness of ceramics; * H0 represents the intrinsic hardness of samples; k* is the Hall‒Petch constant, representing the degree of grain refinement that contributes to the hardness, and *d* is the grain size of ceramics. The relationships between the hardness and the inverse square root of grain size of HEB2, HEB5, HEB10, and HEB13 samples were plotted according to Eq. (3) and depicted in Fig. 6(b), exhibiting excellent linear fitting and yielding a Hall–Petch coefficient (*k*) of 9.8 based on the fitting results. These results indicate that the as-synthesized HEBs in this study possess a sufficiently dense structure and that grain size refinement greatly contributes to increasing the hardness of HEBs [32].

Figure 7(a) displays the fracture toughness and grain size of the samples. Similarly, the fracture toughness of (HfZrTaVNb)B2 ceramics tends to increase as the grain size decreases. Specifically, with the smallest grain size of 1.64 µm, HEB2 had the highest fracture toughness of 4.6 MPa·m1/2. This finding indicates that the refinement of the microstructure contributes to the improvement of fracture toughness of HEBs. However, the differences in fracture toughness values among the samples were slight, possibly because the fracture toughness of samples depended not only on the grain size but also on other factors, such as microcracking. As mentioned above, microcracking was observed in samples HEB5, HEB10, and HEB13. It is well known that toughening by microcracks has been rigorously verified [33], and the effect of microcracking on the fracture toughness of HEBs needs to be investigated in future research. Figure 7(b) displays the SEM image of the indentation sites for the HEB2 sample tested at 5.0 kg for fracture toughness assessment, with the lengths of the cracks and indentations clearly labeled. The locally enlarged crack is shown in Fig. 7(c), suggesting crack deflection and branching, which contribute to the high fracture toughness.

### 3.3 Tribological properties of HEBs

To characterize the wear resistance of the as-synthesized HEBs, Fig. 8 depicts the coefficient values of friction and the calculated average specific wear rates of the investigated samples of HEB2, HEB5, HEB10 and HEB13 at 20 N load. Figure 8(a) shows that the coefficients of friction for all the samples increased sharply in the initial stage and fluctuated greatly, with a steady state subsequently attained; a similar phenomenon has been reported by Murthy *et al*. [34]. For all the samples, the coefficients of friction eventually stabilized at approximately 0.6, which aligns with the values reported for single diborides and dual-phase high-entropy ceramics in Refs. [23,34−36]. The small difference in coefficients of friction for all the samples demonstrates that the influence of the grain size on the coefficients of friction for the samples is negligible. Figure 8(b) shows the typical cross-sectional profiles extracted from the wear track of all the samples after sliding at 20 N load, exhibiting that the width of the wear tracks for HEB2 is the narrowest and that the depth of the wear tracks for HEB10 is the deepest. Figure 8(c) presents the average specific wear rates for all samples calculated from Fig. 8(b), with 2.53×10−6, 3.47×10−6, 1.10×10−5, and 4.61×10−6 mm3/(N·m) for HEB2, HEB5, HEB10, and HEB13, respectively, indicating the excellent wear resistance of HEBs. In general, the HEB samples with smaller grain sizes have a lower wear rate, i.e., better wear resistance, because the mechanical properties of the materials, especially hardness and fracture toughness, influence their wear behavior [37]. The effects of hardness and fracture toughness on the wear rate of materials can be described by Eq. (5) [20]:

where W* is the wear rate; *P* is the applied load; *K*IC is the fracture toughness; *H* is the hardness; and *E* represents the elastic modulus. According to Eq. (5), there is an inverse relationship of the hardness and fracture toughness of the material with its wear rate. From Figs. 6 and 7, we can conclude that an increase in the grain size of a sample leads to a decrease in its mechanical properties, consequently resulting in an elevated wear rate. However, HEB10 exhibits a higher wear rate than HEB13, possibly because the slight difference in grain size between HEB10 and HEB13 leads to little variation in their mechanical properties. Hence, the influence of the relative density on the wear rate is highlighted. The relative density of sample HEB10 is relatively low, accounting for its higher wear rate. The optical three-dimensional (3D) profile image of the wear tracks for HEB10 under a 20 N load in Fig. 8(d) shows that the center of the wear tracks is the deepest, indicating that the center has been worn the most.

To further investigate the effect of the applied load on the friction behavior of HEBs, the worn cross section and calculated average specific wear rates of HEB2 tested under different loads for 1 h are illustrated in Fig. 9. Figure 9 shows that the wear rates of HEB2 under 5, 10, and 20 N loads are 3.40×10−6, 4.11×10−6, and 2.53×10−6 mm3/(N·m), respectively. Recently, Rodríguez-Rojas *et al*. [38] prepared an ultrahard B4C–SiC–TiB2 composite, which exhibited a high hardness of 35 GPa and a fracture toughness of 4.1 MPa·m1/2. When a Si3N4 ball was used as the friction pair, the wear rate of the B4C–SiC–TiB2 composite under a 20 N load was reported to be 3.1×10−6 mm3/(N·m). This proves the superiority of high-entropy ceramics. Furthermore, the HEB samples in the present study also exhibited better wear resistance than some other single-metal diboride ceramics and their composites reported previously [39−41].

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, high-entropy diboride powders were synthesized by the boro/carbonothermal reduction method in this study, followed by two-step spark plasma sintering, resulting in highly dense (HfZrTaVNb)B2 high-entropy diboride ceramics with regulable grain sizes. Furthermore, the influences of grain size on the mechanical properties and friction and wear properties of high-entropy diboride ceramics were investigated, and the following conclusions were drawn:

1) The influence of the sintering time of the second step on the relative density of samples was negligible but the effect on the grain size was great. When the second-step holding time was 2, 5, 10, and 13 min, the relative densities of the samples were 97.6%, 97.5%, 97.0%, and 97.3%, and their grain sizes were 1.64, 5.21, 7.34, and 8.53 μm, respectively.

2) The hardness and fracture toughness of (HfZrTaVNb)B2 high-entropy diboride ceramics increased with decreasing grain size, and the hardness and grain size were in accordance with the Hall–Petch relationship. The highest values were achieved for the sample with an average grain size of 1.64 μm, 26.7 GPa, and 4.6 MPa·m1/2, respectively.

3) The grain refinement in the (HfZrTaVNb)B2 high-entropy diboride ceramics resulted in a notable improvement in wear resistance. Specifically, at a load of 20 N, the sample exhibited the lowest wear rate when the grain size was 1.64 μm, which was only 2.53×10−6 mm3/(N·m). Under varying applied loads, (HfZrTaVNb)B2 exhibited distinct wear behaviors: oxidation wear predominated at 5 N; a combination of oxidation wear and fracture wear was evident at 10 N; and oxidation wear, fracture wear, abrasive wear, and fatigue wear were all observed at 20 N.
