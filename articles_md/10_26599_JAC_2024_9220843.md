## 1 Introduction

C/C–SiC composites, as high-performance structural materials, are widely used in high-temperature and aerobic environments because of their low density, good mechanical, and oxidation-resistant properties [1,2]. Among the main components of C/C–SiC composites, carbon fiber as a reinforcement mainly plays a toughening role, PyC can reduce the thermal stress damage to carbon fiber and deflect the cracks propagated from SiC matrix to carbon fiber, and good mechanical and oxidation-resistant properties are supplied by the SiC matrix because SiC shows high Young’s modulus, hardness, and resistance to oxygen corrosion [3,4]. Hence, the controls of SiC matrix are significant for the C/C–SiC composites with high mechanical and oxidation-resistant properties. Chemical vapor infiltration (CVI), reactive melt infiltration (RMI), slurry infiltration (SI), and precursor infiltration and pyrolysis (PIP) are traditional ways to introduce SiC matrix into carbon fiber fabrics coated by PyC. However, the high cost and low homogeneity of CVI, the serious fiber corrosion and abundant silicon residue of RMI, and the poor adhesion between ceramic particles of SI, both of them prove that these methods are not suitable for fabricating high-performance C/C–SiC composites with controllable component and structure in SiC matrix [2,5−7]. PIP can offer a simple and effective way to control the component and structure of SiC matrix due to the wide variety of organosilicon precursors and the designability of precursors in terms of molecular structure [8].

The common SiC precursors for PIP are the organosilicon polymers with a main chain of Si–C bond, and the typical product is polycarbosilane (PCS). PCS is usually prepared by two-step reactions: (1) preparation of ((CH3)2Si)*n* by Wurtz dechlorination reaction from (CH3)2SiCl2; (2) Kumada rearrangement of ((CH3)2Si)*n* at high temperatures and pressure. The multiple steps and harsh synthesis conditions give a high cost to PCS [9]. The C/C–SiC composites fabricated by PCS usually show a C-rich SiC matrix, and high flexural strength is difficult to realize [10,11]. Meanwhile, Si-rich and near-stoichiometric pyrolytic SiC are difficult to obtain from PCS due to the organosilicon compounds, used as modified components, with excess silicon after pyrolysis are extremely rare. In other words, the Si/C ratio in SiC pyrolyzed from PCS is difficult to adjust over a wide range. Previous studies demonstrated that the shear strength of SiC along the (100) direction declined as the decrease of Si/C ratio based on the first principles [12]. The experimental results manifested that the cleanliness of SiC grain boundaries declined with the decreased Si/C ratio due to the precipitation of free carbon [13]. Furthermore, the flexural strength of C/C–SiC composites also showed an increasing and then decreasing trend with the decreased Si/C ratio in SiC matrix [14]. All of these studies reveal that the Si/C ratio exerts important effects on the structure and mechanical properties of SiC matrix and their C/C–SiC composites.

However, the controls of Si/C ratio in these studies are only based on the C-rich SiC matrix, and the results are still unknown when the Si/C ratio makes a leap from Si-rich to C-rich SiC matrix due to the absence of suitable precursors. Finding suitable SiC precursors with controllable Si/C ratio (from Si-rich to C-rich) in pyrolyzed SiC is essential to investigate the structural evolution and mechanical properties of C/C–SiC composites with apparently different Si/C ratio in SiC matrix, and also helps provide worthwhile guidance for preparing high-performance C/C–SiC composites. Recently, a novel SiC precursor, polymethysilane (PMS), has attracted intensive attention due to its low cost (one-step Wurtz dechlorination reaction from CH3SiHCl2), wealthy Si−H group, and stoichiometric Si/C ratio [15,16]. However, pyrolyzed SiC with stoichiometric Si/C ratio is difficult to obtain by the direct pyrolysis of PMS due to the volatilization of small molecules in ceramization which will cause serious Si residue [17]. Fortunately, the excess silicon in pyrolyzed PMS is easy to eliminate due to the abundant Si−H groups which can react with unsaturated carbon-based compounds by the hydrosilylation reaction, and the Si/C ratio in pyrolyzed PMS can be controlled by the decent modification [18,19].

In this study, low-cost PMS modified by a reflux treatment or additions of vinyl-benzoxazine (V-BOZ) with different contents were used as the initial SiC precursors. The structure and ceramization of SiC precursors and the properties of their pyrolyzed SiC ceramics were studied. Then, C/C–SiC composites with Si-rich, near-stoichiometric, and C-rich SiC matrix were prepared by the synthesized precursors through PIP at a ceramization temperature of 1200 ℃, and the effect of Si/C ratio in SiC matrix on the structure and mechanical properties of prepared C/C–SiC composites was studied. Finally, the change in structure and mechanical performance of prepared C/C–SiC composites was also investigated when heat-treated at 1600 ℃, which can offer an important reference for the service of the composites at higher temperatures.

### 2.1 Materials

PMS with an approximate ceramic yield of 30% at 1200 ℃ was purchased from Hunan Bowang Carbon Ceramics Co., Ltd., China. V-BOZ with a hydroxyl equivalent of 230 g·mol–1 was provided by Chengdu Keyi Polymer Technology Co., Ltd., China. Xylene (purity ≥ 99.0%) was purchased from Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd., China. 2.5D carbon fiber fabrics (0.45 g·cm–3) were obtained by stacking weftless layers (T300/12 k, Toray, Japan) and chopped carbon fiber webs with a needle-punching technique. The needling density was 25 needles·cm–2, and the needling depth was 14 mm. PyC was deposited on carbon fiber fabrics by using the thermal gradient CVI at 1050 ℃ with a pressure of 2 kPa, propane and nitrogen were used as carbon source and dilution gas. Finally, porous C/C preforms (1.1–1.2 g·cm–3) were obtained after a graphitization treatment at 2000 ℃ for 1 h. All raw materials were used without any other treatment.

### 2.2 Synthesis of SiC precursors

SiC precursor with excess silicon was synthesized by a reflux heat treatment of PMS at 150 ℃ for 4 h in an argon atmosphere due to that raw PMS showed a low ceramic yield and was not suitable as a qualified precursor for PIP, the reflux-treated PMS was named as R-PMS. SiC precursors with near-stoichiometric and C-rich pyrolyzed SiC were prepared by mixing PMS with V-BOZ in flowing argon. Typically, PMS and xylene were added to a three-neck flask with a solid content of 50 wt%. Then, the three-neck flask was placed in an oil bath at 105 ℃, and the V-BOZ dissolved in 50 wt% xylene was slowly dropped into the three-neck flask. The reaction was maintained for 2 h, and the light yellow viscous liquid was obtained when the solvent was evaporated by distillation at 100 ℃ under reduced pressure. In this experiment, the weight ratios of V-BOZ to PMS were maintained at 10% and 15%, which were designated as 10-BPMS and 15-BPMS, respectively, with the numeral representing the percentage of V-BOZ to PMS.

### 2.3 Preparation of C/C–SiC composites

The preparation of C/C–SiC composites was illustrated in Fig. 1. R-PMS, 10-BPMS, and 15-BPMS were dissolved in xylene solution with a concentration of 75 wt%. The C/C preforms (65 mm × 60 mm × 70 mm) were cut into three equal parts along the *X*−*Y* plane, and three preforms with a size of 65 mm × 60 mm × 20 mm were obtained after polishing with 80-mesh sandpaper. First, the cut and polished C/C preforms were infiltrated in the precursor solution under vacuum conditions for 60 min. Then, the impregnated preforms suffered a direct heat treatment in a tube furnace at 800 ℃ for 1 h (2 ℃·min–1), which could reduce the escape of precursor from the C/C preforms. These steps of infiltration and heat treatment were repeated 24 times, and then the uncrystallized preforms were heated to 1200 ℃ (5 ℃·min–1) in a graphitization furnace for 1 h. All heat treatment processes were conducted in an argon atmosphere (1 L·min–1). Eventually, dense C/C–SiC composites were obtained. The composites prepared from R-PMS, 10-BPMS, and 15-BPMS were called as CR, C10, and C15, respectively. Meanwhile, an additional heat treatment at 1600 ℃ for 1 h was conducted for CR, C10, and C15, which was used to investigate the service potential of fabricated C/C–SiC composites at higher temperatures, and the heat-treated CR, C10, and C15 were named as CR-16, C10-16, and C15-16, respectively.

### 2.4 Characterization

The transformation of functional groups was monitored using Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR, Thermo Scientific iN10) spectroscopy. 1H-NMR spectra was collected from a Bruker Avance 400 spectrometer with CDCl3 as solvent and tetramethylsilane as internal reference. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC, Agilent PL-GPC50) was used to evaluate the change in molecular weight caused by the modification. The thermal behavior was recorded using thermogravimetric analysis and differential thermal analysis (TG-DTA, Netzsch STA 449 F3), and the heating rate was 10 ℃·min–1. An X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku Miniflex 600) analyzer was employed to detect the phase change and calculate the grain size of SiC. The contents of oxygen, nitrogen, and carbon were analyzed using an oxygen/nitrogen analyzer (LECO ON836) and a carbon/sulfur analyzer (LECO CS844), respectively. The Si content was calculated through a differential method. The microscopic morphology and elemental distribution were obtained using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Quanta FEG 250) with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM, Titan G2 60-300).

### 2.5 Mechanical properties

The mechanical properties of pyrolyzed R-PMS, 10-BPMS, and 15-BPMS at 1200 and 1600 ℃ were tested by nanoindentation with a diamond Berkovich indenter (CSM-UNHT). The indenter was loaded with a loading rate of 0.5 mN·s–1 up to 10 mN with a dwell time of 10 s and unloaded with the same rate. The Young’s modulus (*E*) and hardness (*H*) were calculated from Eqs. (1)–(3) based on the model of Oliver and Pharr [20,21]. Five points were tested for every sample.

(1) H=PmaxAp

(2) 1−v2E=1Er−1−vi2Ei

(3) Er=π2S1β1Ap

where *P*max is the peak load, *A*p is the projected area, *E* is the elastic modulus of the specimen, and *E*i is the indenter elastic modulus. *v* and *v*i are the Poisson’s ratio of the β-SiC and the indenter, and the values are 0.17 and 0.07, respectively [22]. *β* is a constant equal to 1.034 for a Berkovich indenter and *S* is the contact stiffness.

The mechanical properties of C/C–SiC composites were tested using a servohydraulic machine (Instron-5969), and the test procedure was also shown in Fig. 1. The prepared composites were cut into long strips and blocks at the end of the 20th PIP cycle. The flexural strength and flexural modulus were evaluated through three-point bending with a span-thickness ratio of 10 and a load speed of 1 mm·min–1. The compression tests took the same load speed. The results of five specimens were taken as the mechanical properties of every composite. The flexural strength (* σb)and modulus ( Eb) were calculated by using Eqs. (4) and (5):

(4) σb=3FL2bh2

(5) Eb=L34bh3×ΔFΔf

where F* is the maximum damage load, *L* is the span, *b* is the width, *h* is the thickness, and  ΔF/Δf is the slope of the straight line segment on the force–displacement curves.

### 3.1 Structure and ceramization of synthesized SiC precursors

The stability of synthesized SiC precursors is important in PIP. Here, the stability of precursors is evaluated by monitoring the weight change in air at 50 ℃ because PMS is highly sensitive to water and oxygen. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the weight change curves over time for PMS, R-PMS, 10-BPMS, and 15-BPMS were examined. The weight of all samples increases with time, but the V-BOZ modified PMS displays a lower weight gain than PMS and R-PMS. The weight gain of R-PMS is 11.87%, which is slightly lower than that of untreated PMS (15.74%), proving that the reflux treatment demonstrates insufficient ability to improve the stability of PMS. 10-BPMS and 15-BPMS only show a weight gain of 4.43% and 2.04%, respectively, indicating that the V-BOZ can effectively improve the stability of PMS in air, which is in favor of the operation of PMS in PIP.

To investigate the molecular structure of all samples, FT-IR is used to detect the existence of functional groups, which are shown in Fig. 2(b). From the FT-IR spectra of V-BOZ, the signals at 3055 and 3016 cm‒1 are corresponded to the C–H in vinyl group; the signals at 2960, 2870, and 2850 cm‒1 are assigned to the C–H in CH3 and CH2 groups; the signals at 1600, 1500, 1450, and 1635 cm‒1 are attributed to the C=C in benzene ring and vinyl group, respectively; the signals at 1227 and 920 cm‒1 are ascribed to the benzoxazine ring; the results indicate that the presence of vinyl group in benzoxazine resin [23]. The signals at 872, 774, 686, and 1250 cm‒1 are attributed to the Si–C and Si–CH3 groups, and the signal at 2100 cm‒1 is assigned to the Si–H group, all of which are identical absorption peaks of PMS [24]. Meanwhile, the signal at 1100 cm‒1 is ascribed to the Si–O–Si, which is derived from the reaction between PMS and water or oxygen in air. The intensity of Si–O–Si decreases with the increased V-BOZ addition also proves that the V-BOZ can enhance the stability of PMS. The signals of vinyl group are also detected in 10-BPMS and 15-BPMS, and the signal of Si–CH2–CH2 at 1132 cm‒1 is inconspicuous, proving that the hydrosilylation reaction between PMS and V-BOZ is extremely weak [25].

The change of molecular structure is further detected by 1H-NMR spectrum, as shown in Figs. 2(c) and 2(d). The conspicuous peaks centered at 3.5–4.0 and 0–0.65 ppm are correspondingly assigned to Si–H and Si–CH3, which are the identical peaks of PMS [26]. The signals of vinyl group in V-BOZ can be observed at 4.65–4.85 ppm, and the benzoxazine ring is characterized by peaks at 5.45 ppm (O–CH2–N) and 4.66 ppm (Ar–CH2–N) [27]. The values of  ASi−H/ASi−CH3 for PMS, R-PMS, 10-BPMS, and 15-BPMS are 0.2070, 0.1961, 0.2033, and 0.2016, respectively, which can be used to evaluate the change of Si–H because the Si–CH3 is an inert group. R-PMS shows a lower value of  ASi−H/ASi−CH3 than V-BOZ modified PMS due to the high reaction activity between Si–H groups. The value of  ASi−H/ASi−CH3 for 15-BPMS is lower than that of 10-BPMS, indicating that the hydrosilylation reaction between PMS and V-BOZ can be promoted by enhancing the addition content of V-BOZ. The results of 1H-NMR spectrum further prove that the occurrence of dehydrogenation reaction and hydrosilylation reaction. Meanwhile, the peaks of vinyl group in V-BOZ modified PMS are still evident (Fig. 2(d)), which also proves that the hydrosilylation reaction is weak.

To further study the effect of reflux heat treatment and the addition of V-BOZ on the molecular weight of precursors, GPC is used to calculate the number-average molecular weight (M*n), weight-average molecular weight (*M*w), and polymer dispersity index (PDI, *M*w/*M*n), the results are shown in Table 1. The reflux heat treatment and the addition of V-BOZ can effectively enhance the *M*n and *M*w of PMS, while R-PMS exhibits a higher *M*n due to the high activity of Si–H bonds which prompts the occurrence of dehydrogenation reaction [17]. V-BOZ modified PMS is easy to generate some macromolecules and the PDI of V-BOZ modified PMS is greater than that of PMS and R-PMS. The significantly improved stability in air and the generation of macromolecular chains in V-BOZ modified PMS manifest the occurrence of hydrosilylation reaction, even if it is relatively weak.

The TG-DTA curves of R-PMS, 10-BPMS, and 15-BPMS are used to evaluate the cross-linking and ceramization of synthesized SiC precursors. Three stages are observed in the TG-DTA curves (Fig. 4(a)). In the first stage (30–800 ℃), the volatilization of low-molecular-weight PMS and the escape of decomposed small molecules ((CH3)3SiH, (CH3)2SiH2, (CH3)SiH3, CH4, and H2) are responsible for the apparent weight loss of three samples [16]. Meanwhile, apparent exothermic peaks are observed in the enlarged part of the TG-DTA curves (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) for all samples. The peaks at 200–260 ℃ can be attributed to the cross-linking of V-BOZ, which are only seen in 10-BPMS and 15-BPMS. The peaks at 300–375 ℃ are ascribed to the dehydrogenation reaction between Si−H. Notably, the temperature of dehydrogenation reaction in 10-BPMS and 15-BPMS is higher than that of R-PMS, which can be ascribed to the consumption of Si−H caused by the hydrosilylation reaction. In the second stage (800–1200 ℃), slight weight loss and apparent heat absorption can be observed for all samples, indicating that the finish of inorganic reaction and the occurrence of crystallization. In the third stage (1200−1600 ℃), R-PMS shows an apparent weight loss, which can be attributed to the existence of amorphous SiCx*O*y* caused by the absorption of oxygen in the air during the TG-DTA test. The phase separation and decomposition of amorphous SiC*x*O*y* begin at a temperature above 1300 ℃, and accompanied by a loss of mass (Eqs. (8)–(10))[28,29]. 10-BPMS and 15-BPMS show an ignorable weight loss may be ascribed to their high stability in air which gives a relatively low oxygen content to them. Based on the TG-DTA curves, the ceramic yield of R-PMS, 10-BPMS, and 15-BPMS at 1600 ℃ is 46.57%, 50.51%, and 49.94%, respectively. However, the TG-DTA curves are difficult to reflect the real ceramic yield due to that the precursor with extremely low quality is susceptible to the water and oxygen in the air, especially for R-PMS. Hence, the real ceramic yield of SiC precursors pyrolyzed at different temperatures is also given by a heat treatment at different temperatures for 1 h with a mass of 10 g (Fig. 4(b)). The bulk ceramic yield of R-PMS, 10-BPMS, and 15-BPMS at 1600 ℃ is 47.58%, 53.90%, and 51.84%, respectively. The results indicate that both the reflux heat treatment and the addition of V-BOZ can effectively improve the ceramic yield of PMS (≈ 30%). The increased *M*n and *M*w (Table 1) caused by the reactions of dehydrogenation (R-PMS) and hydrosilylation (10-BPMS, 15-BPMS) are responsible for the enhanced ceramic yield. Relative to the results collected from TG-DTA curves, R-PMS only shows a slight weight loss after heat-treating at 1600 ℃ for 1 h because of the low proportion of absorbed oxygen. 15-BPMS reveals a higher weight loss than 10-BPMS, which can be attributed to its higher V-BOZ additions. The existence of benzoxazine ring in V-BOZ may increase the content of oxygen in pyrolyzed products.

Based on the analysis above, the PIP technique is identified. To avoid severe thermal stress damage to the C/C–SiC composites during the PIP process, the inorganization and ceramization of infiltrated precursor are operated separately. The inorganization temperature is identified by the lowest temperature which ensures that most of weight loss has been completed. In this study, an inorganization temperature of 800 ℃ is chosen according to the TG-DTA curves and the bulk ceramic yield–temperature curves of R-PMS, 10-BPMS, and 15-BPMS. The ceramization temperature of the C/C–SiC composites is set as 1200 ℃ due to that the pyrolyzed PMS is beginning to crystallize (Fig. S2 in the ESM) and the phase separation and decomposition of amorphous SiCx*O*y* are difficult to occur at this temperature [28].

### 3.2 Structure and properties of pyrolyzed SiC precursors

The structure and properties of R-PMS, 10-BPMS, and 15-BPMS pyrolyzed at 1200 ℃ are first investigated, which helps understand the properties of the introduced SiC matrix in C/C–SiC composites. The XRD patterns of all pyrolyzed ceramics are shown in Fig. 5. The peaks at 2*θ* = 35.6°, 41.4°, 60.0°, and 71.8° match the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of SiC (JCPDS#29-1129), which can be observed in the XRD patterns of all samples. The (111), (220), and (311) planes of Si (JCPDS#27-1402) are found at 2*θ* = 28.4°, 47.3°, and 56.1°, which is only observed in the XRD pattern of pyrolyzed R-PMS, indicating that the pyrolyzed R-PMS shows excess Si. Meanwhile, an inconspicuous peak at 26.6° can be seen for all samples which confirms that the existence of a small amount of SiO2 (JCPDS#99-0088). The XRD patterns demonstrate that SiC is the dominant phase for pyrolyzed 10-BPMS and 15-BPMS, but pyrolyzed R-PMS mainly consists of SiC and Si phases. The average grain size of all samples is calculated by the Scherrer formula [30]. The average grain size of pyrolyzed R-PMS, 10-BPMS, and 15-BPMS are 6.4, 2.9, and 2.7 nm, the average grain size decreases gradually with the decrease of the Si/C ratio, indicating that the growth of SiC grains may be inhibited by the carbon pyrolyzed from V-BOZ.

The cross-sectional SEM images of C/C–SiC composites heat-treated at 1200 ℃ with only two PIP are used to help understand the structure evolution of SiC matrix with different Si/C ratio in repeated PIP, as shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. The cross-linked and pyrolyzed SiC precursors prefer to form some small blocks due to their good thermosetting ability derived from wealthy Si−H groups, and abundant holes between pyrolyzed SiC ceramics are generated. The dense SiC matrix is obtained by the repeated filling of the holes, and numerous ceramic interfaces emerge. The compactness of whole dense SiC matrix, as shown in Fig. 10, is determined by the properties of ceramic interface when the simple physical stacking is difficult to occur. Previous studies have indicated that the diffusion coefficient of C atom in SiC below 10–40 cm2·s–1 at 1500 ℃ and it decreases with the decreased temperature, and it even reduces to 10–60 cm2·s–1 for the diffusion of Si atom in SiC [34]. The diffusion of C or Si atom in SiC may be not responsible for the bonding of ceramic interface due to their low diffusion coefficient after crystalline SiC has emerged in the interface. Hence, the reaction of ceramic interface is mainly controlled by the diffusion reaction of C atom in Si based on the reaction mechanism between Si and C [35,36]. The diffusion coefficient of C atom in melting Si can be experimentally calculated from Eq. (11) [37]:

(11) Dc=0.755×10−3e−9.2/(RT)

where D*c is the diffusion coefficient, *R* is the gas constant, and *T* is the temperature.

The melting point of amorphous or nano-sized Si is usually below the theoretical melting point of crystalline silicon (1688 K). Previous studies showed that the melting point of amorphous Si was about 1170–1420 K [38,39], and the melting point of nano-sized Si was 1388–1488 K [40], both of them prove that the use of Eq. (11) is reasonable in the ceramization. The calculated *D*c of C atom in melting Si is 3.25×10–5 cm2·s–1 at 1200 ℃, which is higher than the diffusion coefficient of C or Si atom in SiC.

From the analysis above, the possible structure evolution of ceramic interface in the SiC matrix with different Si/C ratio is shown in Fig. 11. The wealthy Si (Fig. 6(c)) will accelerate the diffusion of C atom into adjacent SiC matrix and the reaction between Si and C also occurs at the same time, which helps obtain an almost integrated SiC matrix (Fig. 10(c)) after the ceramization. With the decrease of Si/C ratio, the diffusion of C atom from one matrix to another becomes more difficult due to the absence of Si medium, the good-bonded ceramic interface is difficult to obtain, which generates more distinctive wrinkles in the ceramic interface, as shown in Figs. 10(f) and 10(i).

To investigate the fracture behavior of prepared C/C–SiC composites with different Si/C ratios in SiC matrix, the microstructure of fracture surface for all composites are shown in Fig. 13. The optical and low-magnification fracture images are exhibited in Figs. 13(a), 13(d), and 13(g). CR shows a near-linear macro crack, indicating that the load is directly transferred from the SiC matrix to the PyC coated carbon fiber without apparent deflection. Meanwhile, fiber bundle pullout is observed due to the low fracture load, which is consistent with the flexural strength–strain curve of CR. Relative to CR, the cracks in C10 and C15 are deflected by the PyC coated carbon fiber, and curved macro cracks are formed. C10 and C15 show only limited fiber bundle pullout due to carbon fiber is incapable of bearing the high fracture load. Figures 13(b), 13(c), 13(e), 13(f), 13(h), and 13(i) show the SEM images of damaged SiC matrix. The PyC coated carbon fiber shows an insufficient capacity to deflect cracks in the SiC matrix of CR, which helps generate a flat fracture surface with a large area. The cracks change the propagation direction when encountering the PyC coated carbon fiber, and a stepped fracture surface is formed, which can be seen in C10 and C15. The deflection of crack in SiC matrix could effectively absorb fracture energy, and improve the load-bearing capacity of the SiC matrix [42], which may be the reasons why C10 and C15 show higher flexural strength than that of CR. Meanwhile, the PyC in CR is partially stripped off by the SiC matrix (Fig. 13(c)), which can be ascribed to the good bonding between SiC matrix and PyC coated carbon fiber. Based on the reaction mechanism of RMI [43], the reaction between wealthy Si and PyC is responsible for the enhanced interface bonding. The diffusion of Si atom from the interface to the center of carbon fiber is shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM, which is collected by the line scanning in EDS. The difference in the diffusion of Si atom is very small for all composites, which also proves that the Si/C ratio in SiC matrix exerts negligible influence on the interface atom diffusion when the ceramization temperature is only 1200 ℃, and the reaction between wealthy Si and PyC only slightly occurs at the interface. Hence, the poor ability of crack deflection in the SiC matrix of CR may be attributed to the damage of PyC coated carbon fiber by the thermal residual stress (σ*TRS) from SiC matrix. The *σ*TRS is generated by the mismatched elastic modulus and thermal expansion coefficient between SiC matrix and PyC coated carbon fiber, which can be calculated by Eq. (12) [44]:

*

(12) σTRS=EmλEfVfλEfVf+EmVm(αf−αm)(Te−Tp)

where E*f, *V*f, and *α*f are the elastic modulus, volume fraction, and thermal expansion coefficient of the PyC coated carbon fiber, respectively. *E*m, *V*m, and *α*m are the elastic modulus, volume fraction, and thermal expansion coefficient of the SiC matrix, respectively. *λ* is a constant relative to the weave method. *T*e and *T*p are the experimental and process temperatures, respectively.

The dependence of elastic modulus (*E*) and thermal expansion coefficient (*α*) on porosity (*P*) can be experimentally estimated by Eqs. (13) and (14) [45,46]:

(13) E=E0(1−1.9P+0.9P2)

(14) α=α0e−4.2P

where *E*0 is the elastic modulus of the skeleton, which can be considered as the Young’s modulus of the single pyrolyzed SiC; *α*0 is the thermal expansion coefficient of the skeleton, which is regarded as the theoretical value of SiC.

The *σ*TRS decreases with the declined interface bonding between SiC matrix and PyC coated carbon fiber and it also reduces with the increased defects and pores in SiC matrix [44]. The defects and pores in SiC matrix increase with the declined Si/C ratio (Fig. 10), indicating that the SiC matrix with a good-bonded ceramic interface (CR) shows lower porosity than those with defective ceramic interface (C10 and C15). Hence, both the elastic modulus and thermal expansion coefficient of SiC matrix decline with the increased defects and pores. Considering that the weave method of all C/C preforms is the same and the volume fractions of PyC coated carbon fiber and SiC matrix for all composites are also similar, it can be inferred that the *σ*TRS decreases with the declined Si/C ratio in SiC matrix (* CR(σTRS) >  C10(σTRS) >  C15(σTRS) based on Eqs. (12)–(14). In the fabrication of CR, the PyC coated fiber will suffer significant σ*TRS damage due to its good bonding with the SiC matrix which shows fewer defects and cracks, and some microcracks emerge on the PyC (Fig. 13(c)). The propagated cracks in SiC matrix are easy to cross the PyC via the microcracks, and the load is directly transferred to the carbon fiber which shows poor load-bearing capacity, giving low flexural properties to CR. Meanwhile, direct contact between SiC matrix and carbon fiber is also observed at the surface of prepared C/C–SiC composites (Fig. S5 in the ESM). The defects in the interface between carbon fiber and PyC will help the infiltration of precursor and the subsequent introduction of SiC matrix at the interface. The residual Si may directly react with the carbon fiber, which causes serious damage to the carbon fiber [47]. This is also a reason that why CR with Si-rich SiC matrix shows lower flexural properties than that of C10 and C15.

### 3.4 Change in structure and mechanical properties of prepared C/C–SiC composites when heat-treated at 1600 ℃

As we all known, the maximum long-term service temperature of C/C–SiC composites can reach 1500 ℃. The structural stability at service temperature is very important for the long-term service of C/C–SiC composites. In this work, the preparation temperature of C/C–SiC composites is only 1200 ℃, the high-temperature unstable components (amorphous SiC*x*O*y*) may be removed and the grain growth may occur when the composites service at higher temperatures (near 1500 ℃), which is unfavorable to the long-term service of composites. Heat treatment above the service temperature is considered as an effective way to obtain C/C–SiC composites with stable structure [4]. Hence, it is important to investigate the change in structure and mechanical properties of the composites after heat treatment above the service temperatures, which can offer an important reference for the service of the composites at higher temperature. Here, a heat treatment at 1600 ℃ for 1 h is conducted for CR, C10, and C15 based on the bulk ceramic yield–temperature curves (Fig. 4(b)).

The bulk density, open porosity, and volume fractions of carbon fiber, PyC, and SiC matrix for all heat-treated composites are shown in Table 4. All heat-treated composites show a slight decrease in bulk density and a significant increase in open porosity, which may be ascribed to the decomposition of amorphous SiC*x*O*y*. The volume fractions of carbon fiber, PyC, and SiC matrix change less due to the only slightly decreased bulk density.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, we developed a simple and low-cost way to fabricate the C/C–SiC composites with Si-rich, near-stoichiometric, and C-rich SiC matrix based on the modified PMS. The properties and ceramization of SiC precursors, and the structure and mechanical properties of fabricated C/C–SiC composites were systemically studied. The specific conclusions were as follows:

1) V-BOZ modified PMS showed higher stability and ceramic yield than refluxed PMS due to the occurrence of hydrosilylation reaction during the synthesis of precursor.

2) The Si/C ratio in Si-rich, near-stoichiometric, and C-rich SiC ceramics at 1200 ℃ was 1.23, 1.01, and 0.88, respectively. With the decrease of Si/C ratio, the Young’s modulus of pyrolyzed SiC ceramics slightly declined.

3) The C/C–SiC composites with similar Si/C ratios in SiC matrix were fabricated through PIP at 1200 ℃. The composites with lower Si/C ratio in SiC matrix showed more apparent crack deflection behavior due to the declined interface bonding and the alleviated thermal residual stress damage. The composites with near-stoichiometric SiC matrix showed better comprehensive mechanical properties due to the effective deflection of cracks in composites and the moderate Young’s modulus of SiC matrix. The flexural strength, flexural modulus, and compressive strength were 423±27 MPa, 33.41±4.52 GPa, and 393±8 MPa, respectively.

4) After heat-treating at 1600 ℃, the role of carbon fiber toughening was improved and accompanied by a decrease in mechanical properties for all composites. The reasons were attributed to the increased defects in SiC matrix and the damaged interfaces.

This work revealed that controlling the Si/C ratio in SiC matrix is very important to fabricate high-performance C/C–SiC composites, and the excellent mechanical properties could be realized by adjusting the Si/C ratio near 1 with the V-BOZ modified PMS.
