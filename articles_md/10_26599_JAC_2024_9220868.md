## 1 Introduction

Since the author’s report on highly efficient laser oscillation using Nd:YAG ceramics in 1995 [1], transparent ceramics have been developed worldwide [2]. In recent years, (Tb*x*Y1−*x*)2O3 (TYO) and Tb2O3 (TO) ceramics, which are difficult to synthesize by the melt solidification method, have been reported, and their magneto-optical performance has been confirmed to be far superior to that of single crystals synthesized by the conventional melt solidification method [3]. Rare earth oxide ceramics, as well as magneto-optical materials [11], are promising materials for excellent window and laser applications [4−10]. In recent years, the transparent ceramics of Sc2O3, Y2O3, and other rare earth oxides ranging in atomic number from 71 to 65 (Lu to Tb) have been demonstrated, but only a few reported cases have achieved optical grade quality. Tb2O3 with an atomic number of 65 must be synthesized at temperatures lower than 1500 ℃ for ceramics because the phase transition point occurs near 1500 ℃ due to the large ionic radius of the cation, Tb. Because of this difficulty, only one paper has reported a successful optical grade [11]. Since the ionic radius of Gd is even larger than that of Tb, the phase transition temperature of Gd oxide (Gd2O3) is approximately 1200 ℃. Therefore, it is more difficult to synthesize a dense sintered body of this material below 1200 ℃, and there are no reported cases of synthesis of transparent ceramics. If the low-temperature synthesis of Gd2O3 ceramics is possible, not only can transparent ceramics with a cubic crystal structure be synthesized but also new radiation detection methods using their specific gravity (radiation blocking ability), and new magneto-optical materials can be created by utilizing the characteristics of Gd ions having unpaired electrons.

In the past, high-temperature sintering was indispensable for obtaining dense and transparent lanthanide rare-earth oxide ceramics, and the phase transition temperature was too low; hence, there have been no attempts to synthesize transparent Gd2O3 ceramics. In this work, we succeeded in densifying and making the transparent Gd2O3 ceramics at an extremely low temperature of 1070 ℃ by a combination of special sintering aids and an optimized HIP treatment. This is the first successful synthesis of the transparent Gd2O3 ceramics with a cubic crystal structure, and we investigated their fundamental optical properties.

## 2 Experimental procedures

Gd2O3 powders (purity: 99.99%, primary particle size: 0.3 μm) were used as starting materials. As sintering aids, Ti tetra ethoxide (Ti(OC2H5)4), Zn acetylacetone (Zn(C5H7O2)2), and Ga tri-i-propoxide (GaO-i-C3H7)3) were added in a combined amount of 1.2 wt% (0.4 wt% each). In addition, 2 wt% acrylic organic binder was added, and the mixture was pot-milled for 20 h in ethanol using yttria-stabilized zirconia (YTZ) balls for grinding. The resulting slurry was evaporated on a hot stirrer to obtain a dry powder. The powders were sieved through a #150 mesh sieve, and then the powders were uniaxially molded at 10 MPa using a *ϕ*18 mm mold, followed by cold isostatic press (CIP) molding at a pressure of 98 MPa. The powder compacts were heated to 600 ℃ for 15 h to thermally decompose the organic components of the organic binder and sintering aids. The dewaxed compacts were sintered at 1050 ℃ for 5 h in an oxygen atmosphere to obtain a pre-sintered compact with a relative density of approximately 98%. The pre-sintered compacts were subjected to hot isostatic press (HIP) treatment at 1070 ℃ for 2 h under a pressure of 180 MPa (pressure medium: Ar gas).

The crystal structure of the obtained ceramic materials was identified by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; MiniFlex, Rigaku Co.). The sample surfaces were mirror polished and thermally etched at 950 ℃ for 10 h before microstructural observation by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; X’Pert PRO Alpha-1, PANalytical Co.). A transmission polarized light microscope (BX50, Olympus) was used to observe residual porosity, grain boundary phases, birefringence, etc., inside the materials via transmission images and transmission-polarized light observation. The theoretical transmission curve was obtained by measuring the refractive index of the materials using an ellipsometer (SE-2000, Nippon Cemilabo Co.) and calculating Fresnel loss on the surface from that value. The linear transmittance of the polished specimens was measured using a spectrophotometer (V-670, JASCO).

For the magneto-optical property measurements, we used He–Ne (633 nm) and semiconductor lasers (~1.06 μm) as light sources in a magnetic field of 1 T to obtain Verdet constant [3,11]. In the present study, we used a commercially available spectrometer (BH-501, NEO Arc Co.) that can continuously measure wavelengths from 600 to 2000 nm with a step width of 0.5 nm in a magnetic field of 1 T using a xenon lamp as the light source.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1 compares the sintering characteristics of Gd2O3 powder compacts without and with sintering aids. The sintering temperature was raised to the target temperature at 5 ℃/min, held at the specified temperature for 2 h, and then cooled at 10 ℃/min. The relative density on the vertical axis is relative to the theoretical density of 7.44 g/cm3 for cubic Gd2O3. The relative density after CIP molding is approximately 56% both with and without the addition of sintering aids. The densification behavior of Gd2O3 powder compacts without a sintering aid was insensitive to the sintering temperature, and the relative density was as low as 67%, even at the phase transition temperature (1200 ℃). When the materials were heated to 1300 ℃, several cracks appeared, and the materials entirely collapsed at 1400 ℃. In the case of the Gd2O3 powder compacts with a small amount of sintering aid (i.e., ultimately Ga2O3–ZnO–TiO2 ternary type), densification occurs rapidly at approximately 950 ℃ and reaches more than 99% of the theoretical density above 1100 ℃.

When using commercial raw materials, the densification temperature is extremely high above 1600 ℃ not only for Gd2O3 but also for other lanthanide rare earth (including Sc and Y) oxide ceramics. A similar sintering test was also conducted using commercial nanosized raw materials (ca. 40 nm) for the possibility of low-temperature synthesis caused by the particle size effect of raw powders. However, the relative density reached only 72% at a sintering temperature of 1200 ℃ without sintering aids, and high-density sintered bodies were not obtained. There have been no reports of densification to near-theoretical density by pressureless sintering at approximately 1100 ℃. This suggests that the most important factor in the sintering and densification of rare earth oxide ceramics is the appropriate choice of sintering aid, which is more effective than the particle size effect of the starting materials.

Figures 3(a) and 3(b) show the appearance of the HIP-treated sample (with the mirror polished on both sides) and the fluorescent light observed through the sample. The prepared sample appears to be cloudy, but as shown in Fig. 3(b), the fluorescent light and surrounding scenery can be clearly observed. This is because the absorption edge of the materials is approximately 640 nm, as shown in the transmission spectrum in Fig. 6(b), and most of the visible wavelengths (blue to red) are not transmitted. Figure 3(c) shows the sample and magnet (Nd–Fe–B), and Fig. 3(d) shows the attraction of the sample by the magnet (magnetic field). Although there are reports that Gd2O3 is diamagnetic [12], the obtained sample exhibits paramagnetic-like behavior. The strongest paramagnetism among lanthanide rare earth oxides is in the order of (stronger) Tb3+ → Dy3+ → Ho3+ ions (weaker) from the perspective of Pauli’s exclusion principle. Tb3+ has strong optical absorption above 500 and 1500 nm, and Dy and Ho also have many optical absorption bands, which limits their application in paramagnetic Faraday devices in the visible to near-infrared region. However, Gd3+ ions do not have an optical absorption band in the wavelength range above 640 nm; therefore, if the Faraday effect is confirmed, they are expected to be applied over a wide wavelength range.

Figure 6(a) shows the refractive index of a single-sided mirror-polished sample (t* = 1 mm where *t* refers to the thickness) measured by an ellipsometer at wavelengths ranging from 600 to 1600 nm. The theoretical transmission line shown in Fig. 6(b) was calculated by using Fresnel loss of the sample surface obtained from the measured refractive index. Since the refractive index of Gd2O3 is as high as 2.08 and Fresnel loss (reflection loss on one side) calculated from this value is 12.3%, the theoretical transmittance of this material can be calculated to be approximately 75%. Figure 6(b) shows the in-line transmittance at wavelengths from 300 to 1600 nm for a sample with a thickness of 2 mm and a mirror polished on both sides. The baseline transmittance of the materials above 700 nm is approximately 72%, and the absorption edge is approximately 640 nm, which gives an optical band gap of 2.1 eV. Since it was impossible to synthesize single crystals by the melt-growth method or polycrystalline ceramics by the sintering method, Kuznetsova *et al*. [12] synthesized Gd2O3 films by the vapor phase synthesis method at 200 ℃. The refractive indices of reported Gd2O3 films are approximately 2.16–2.18 at wavelengths of 802 to 665 nm. Although the film thickness is only a few hundred nanometers, the reported transmittance is only 65%–70%. The theoretical transmittance calculated from the refractive index of the Gd2O3 film is 72.8%, which is only approximately 0.8% lower in this study. The theoretical transmittance calculated from either the refractive index by ellipsometry or the refractive index obtained from the Gd2O3 film is not much different from the transmittance measured from cubic Gd2O3 ceramics (approximately 72%). Since the obtained in-line transmission curves show no wavelength dependence of the transmittance, there is no significant Rayleigh scattering originating from fine defect structures such as grain boundaries or grain boundary phases.

Figure 7 shows the internal microstructure of the sample observed with a transmission-polarized optical microscope (BX50, Olympus). Figure 7(a) shows a transmission microscope image. Residual pores are almost undetectable in the materials. Figure 7(b) shows an observation using a single polarizing plate, and there is no residual stress or grains with a crystal structure other than a cubic crystal inside the materials. Figure 7(c) shows an observation using two polarizing plates with crossed positions, and a dark field is confirmed. The produced material was found to have a cubic crystal structure by X-ray diffraction, as shown in Fig. 2, and it was also confirmed by the optical microscopy that it had a cubic crystal structure with no birefringence. In principle, the materials with a cubic crystal structure do not have birefringence, but the significant birefringence is observed in most single crystals with a cubic structure [13]. For example, it is also observed in single crystals used in lasers and Faraday isolators, which require the highest optical quality [14]. We have previously reported that birefringence cannot be detected in optical-grade ceramics with low optical loss by polarized light observation. The phase transition point (cubic * ⇔ monoclinic) of Gd2O3 synthesized in this study is as low as approximately 1200 ℃; hence, the synthesis temperature was below that phase transition point. As a result, highly transparent polycrystalline Gd2O3 ceramics were obtained, consisting of a cubic crystal structure with no birefringence. The added sintering aids showed no significant segregation, and even their presence could not be detected by SEM‒EDS (energy dispersive spectroscopy) analysis near the grain boundaries. Similarly, the transmission polarized light microscopy confirmed that there was no segregation of the sintering aids to the grain boundaries by optical inspection. It is not easy to make rare earth oxide raw materials dense and transparent. In atmospheric pressure sintering, sintering at approximately 2000 ℃ is essential [7], and in pressure sintering, such as HIP and HP, high-temperature treatment at 1500–1800 ℃ is essential [5−10,15−21]. In this study, transparent rare-earth oxide ceramics were synthesized by HIP processing at a very low temperature of 1070 ℃. This study opens a new research area for the synthesis of new materials and the elucidation of their physical properties. Gd2O3 has phase transition points near 2100 ℃ (hexagonal  ⇔ monoclinic) and near 1200 ℃ (monoclinic  ⇔ cubic), making it impossible to synthesize single crystals or even polycrystalline ceramics. In 2023, Pustovarov et al*. [22] succeeded in synthesizing a cubic Gd2O3 single crystal with a diameter of approximately 5 mm using Li2O–B2O3 flux. Although this method has the advantage of low-temperature synthesis, it has disadvantages in terms of purity, size, and quality of single crystals. The absorption edge of the obtained Gd2O3 single crystal is approximately 300 nm, which is different from the absorption edge (640 nm) of the Gd2O3 ceramics shown in Fig. 6(b). It is reasonable to assume that this is because the added sintering aid (Ga2O3–ZnO–TiO2) dissolved in Gd2O3 and formed point defects, shifting the absorption edge to longer wavelengths. Therefore, even if a small amount of transition metal ions with a smaller ionic radius than rare earths are added, no grain boundary phase is formed.

Figure 8 shows the wavelength dependence of Verdet constants of the Gd2O3 ceramics at wavelengths ranging from 600 to 1100 nm. Tb–Ho oxide ceramics (atomic numbers 65–67) are paramagnetic materials with a high Verdet constant, and the wavelength dependence of Verdet constant has been reported [11,21]. Since Gd, with an atomic number of 64, has 4 unpaired electrons when it becomes Gd2O3 (i.e., Gd3+ ions), it attaches to the magnet, as shown in Fig. 3. However, as shown in Fig. 8, there is almost no wavelength dependence of Verdet constant, and Verdet constants at wavelengths of 633 and 1064 nm are 4.94 and 7.27 rad∙T−1∙m−1, respectively, which are very close to each other, and the polarity is opposite to that of paramagnetic materials such as Tb2O3. Hence, Gd2O3 is not paramagnetic like the previously reported Tb–Ho oxides but has a diamagnetic feature similar to that reported by Kuznetsova et al*. [12] in their synthesis of Gd-containing oxide ceramics.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, for the first time, we not only succeeded in producing dense Gd2O3 ceramics by the sintering method, which was considered impossible due to the existence of a phase transition point at low temperatures but also in controlling the crystal structure to cubic crystals and making it transparent. The obtained results are summarized as follows.

i) The use of the ternary Ga2O3–ZnO–TiO2 sintering aid enabled the densification of Gd2O3 powder compacts at low temperatures.

ii) Densification was further promoted by the HIP treatment at 1070 ℃ to obtain transparent polycrystalline Gd2O3 ceramics.

iii) The transmittance of the obtained Gd2O3 ceramics (*t* = 2 mm) reached 72%, which is almost equal to the theoretical transmittance.

iv) No birefringence was detected in the Gd2O3 ceramics, and it can be assumed that the materials have a high extinction ratio. It was confirmed that this material is optically isotropic, which is characteristic of the cubic crystal structure.

v) Verdet constant of the Gd2O3 ceramics is 4.94–7.27 rad∙T−1∙m−1, and its wavelength dependence is not observed. The materials are presumed to be diamagnetic because they have the opposite polarity to that of common paramagnetic materials.
