## 1 Introduction

The development of miniaturized and integrated electronic devices increases the demands for dielectric materials with high relative permittivity, low dielectric loss, and high electrical breakdown strength. There are several ceramics with good dielectric properties, such as BaTiO3, PbZr1−*x*Ti*x*O3 (PZT), and (K0.5Na0.5)NbO3 (KNN). However, PZT is a lead-based material that is unfavorable for the environment. Among all lead-free dielectric ceramics, BaTiO3 is a fundamental dielectric material for capacitor applications because of its excellent performance, such as high relative permittivity and low dielectric loss. It is widely used in electronic industries and is named as the backbone of the electronic ceramic industry. However, BaTiO3 ceramics have a low electrical breakdown strength, and their dielectric constant decreases dramatically above the Curie temperature (*T*C), which makes it difficult to meet the application requirements [1−5]. Compared with ceramics, polymers have excellent electrical breakdown strength and are lightweight, flexible, and easy to process at low temperatures, but their low permittivities limit their applications. Ceramic‒polymer composites combine the advantages of high relative permittivity of ceramics and high electrical breakdown strength of polymers, which can improve the comprehensive performance of single components [2,6,7]. However, owing to the difference in sintering temperatures between ceramics and polymers, the majority of composite research has focused on polymer matrix materials. For example, poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF)-based composites with the integration of phosphonic acid-modified BaTiO3 nanoparticles exhibit high relative permittivity and good electrical breakdown strength [8,9]. The relative permittivity of polymer-based composites is typically lower than 100, even though the addition of BaTiO3 can significantly increase the relative permittivity. It is challenging to reduce the dielectric loss of composites to less than 0.05 while maintaining a relative permittivity above 100.

BaTiO3-based ceramics are usually sintered at high temperatures, with traditional solid-state sintering at 1300–1400 °C [10], microwave sintering at 1200–1400 °C [11], and spark plasma sintering at 900–1200 °C [12]. It is challenging to manipulate the grain growth and interfacial structures using high−temperature sintering, which also leads to high cost and energy consumption. Recently, the Randall group proposed a novel sintering technique named the cold sintering process (CSP) [13−16]. Water, weak acids, and weak bases are utilized as transient solvents to densify ceramics at low temperatures between room temperature and 300 °C through dissolution‒precipitation processes. The low-temperature sintering conditions enable the co-firing of ceramics and polymers, which opens up a new route for ceramic‒polymer composites and device integration schemes [17−19]. Several dielectric composites with ceramic matrixes have been developed via cold sintering processes, such as SiO2–polytetrafluoroethyllene (PTFE) [20], BaTiO3–p-phenylene oxide (PPO) [21], and BaTiO3–PTFE [22]. Since the solubility of BaTiO3 in water is very low, obtaining a suitable transient liquid phase is difficult. When Ba(OH)2/TiO2 suspensions are used to cold-sinter BaTiO3, there are glassy phases and BaCO3 impurities after the cold sintering process, and further heat treatment at 700–900 °C is needed [23]. The NaOH–KOH eutectic flux proposed by Tsuji *et al*. [24] can densify BaTiO3 via a one-step cold sintering process at 300 °C (one-step process in which no post-annealing at high temperatures is utilized). Ba(OH)2∙8H2O and H2TiO3 are also effective transient liquid phases to cold sinter BaTiO3 at 150–300 °C without further heat treatment [25]. The significant reduction in the sintering temperature of BaTiO3 makes it feasible to obtain fine grains and tailor interfacial structures, which is critical to the comprehensive electrical properties. The densification of BaTiO3-based composites via a one-step cold sintering process is promising.

Polyetherimide (PEI) is an important dielectric polymer with high electrical breakdown strength, high thermal stability, and low dielectric loss [26,27], making it promising for regulating the performance of BaTiO3. However, there is no report on the cold sintering process of BaTiO3–PEI composites. In this work, BaTiO3 is integrated with PEI by a one-step cold sintering process using Ba(OH)2∙8H2O and H2TiO3 as the transient liquid phase. The densities, structures, microstructures, temperature-dependent dielectric properties, impedances, and electrical breakdown strengths of cold sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites are discussed in detail. A finite element model (FEM) has been built to study the influencing factors of electrical breakdown strength. Dense (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites with limited grain growth, high relative permittivity, low dielectric loss, good temperature stability, and high electrical breakdown strength can be successfully prepared via a cold sintering process, demonstrating that CSP is promising for the development of high-performance dielectric nanocomposites.

### 2.1 Powder preparations

BaTiO3 (99.99%) with a particle size of 150 nm was purchased from Shandong Sinocera Functional Materials Co., Ltd., and PEI (ULTEM1000) with a glass transition temperature (*T*g) of 217 °C was obtained from Saber Basic Innovative Plastics. Barium hydroxide octahydrate (Ba(OH)2∙8H2O, > 98%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar Chemical Co., Ltd. H2TiO3 (98%) and 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP, 99%) were obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.

PEI powder was added to a NMP solution, which was stirred with a magnetic stirrer for 2 h at 60 °C and then for 12 h at room temperature to completely dissolve the PEI. Subsequently, BaTiO3 powder was added to the NMP solution with dissolved PEI according to the formula of (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI (*x* = 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 vol%). The mixture was then ball milled for 4 h at 400 r/min in a planetary ball milling tank, followed by drying at 100 °C for 12 h.

### 2.2 Cold sintering process of (1−<i>x</i>)BaTiO<sub>3</sub>–<i>x</i>PEI nanocomposites

As shown in Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), the 5 wt% transient liquid phase is not sufficient to densify BaTiO3–PEI nanocomposites with the cold sintering process, while the relative density is improved to > 90% with 10 wt% transient liquid phase. Therefore, 90 wt% (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*–PEI powder was mixed with a 10 wt% transient liquid phase (7.8 wt% Ba(OH)2∙8H2O and 2.2 wt% H2TiO3) using a mortar. The resulting powder was then put into a die with a diameter of 10 mm and compressed uniaxially under a pressure of 300 MPa. Since the dehydration temperature of Ba(OH)2∙8H2O is 78 °C, the die was first heated to 100 °C for 0.5 h to dehydrate Ba(OH)2∙8H2O. Both a heating jacket and a press equipped with hot plates (Fig. S1 in the ESM) were used to heat the die homogeneously, as shown in Fig. 1(a). Afterwards, the cold sintering process temperature was set to 250 °C and held at 300 MPa for 2 h. The pressure was released immediately after the holding was completed, and the samples were cooled to room temperature and then removed.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The densities of the cold sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI samples were determined via the Archimedes method with anhydrous ethanol as the medium. The crystal structures of the samples were determined via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 ADVANCE, Bruker) with a scanning range of 10°–70° in a step of 0.026°. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were collected using a Bruker VERTEX70 instrument in the range of 400–4000 cm−1. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU8230) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-ARM200F) were used to observe the microstructures of the cold sintered nanocomposites. Before TEM observations, the samples were well polished with a focused ion beam (FIB; Helios 5 UX, Thermo Scientific). The impedance was obtained via an electrochemical workstation (Zennium-Pro 43139, ZAHNER) in the frequency range from 10 mHz to 5 MHz. Dielectric properties were measured in the frequency range of 100 Hz–1 MHz (Keysight E4990A), and temperature-dependent dielectric properties were tested using a measurement system for electrical properties of functional materials (Huace650T, Beijing Hua Ce Inspection Instrument Co., Ltd.). Polarization–electric (*P*–*E*) field curves were obtained via the measurement system for electrical properties of functional materials at a frequency of 1 Hz.

## 3 Results and discussion

The schematic for the one-step cold sintering process of (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites is shown in Fig. 1(a). During the cold sintering process, both a heating jacket and a press equipped with hot plates were utilized to heat the die homogeneously. Since the dehydration temperature of Ba(OH)2∙8H2O is 78 °C, the die was first heated up to 100 °C for 0.5 h to dehydrate Ba(OH)2∙8H2O. The water produced from the dehydration process at 100 °C provides a liquid environment, which promotes particle rearrangement and triggers the dissolution‒rearrangement process. Afterwards, the temperature was set to 250 °C and held at 300 MPa for 2 h. The cold sintering process temperature of 250 °C was higher than the glass transition temperature (*T*g) of PEI. Therefore, PEI particles are in the viscous flow state during the cold sintering process and accelerate the mass transport process. As shown in Fig. 1(b), all the samples have relative densities higher than 89%, indicating that (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI (*x* = 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 vol%) nanocomposites can be densified via a cold sintering process at 250 °C. The relative densities of the nanocomposites tend to increase as the PEI content increases, revealing that PEI polymers assist in the densification process of the (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites. A maximum relative density of 96.55% can be obtained with *x* = 40%. When the amount of PEI is 50%, the relative density decreases to 94.28%, which may result from the agglomeration of polymers. Furthermore, an increase in the PEI content results in a decrease in the density of the (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites due to the low density of PEI.

To demonstrate the effect of the cold sintering process on the densification of (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites, a hot press with no transient liquid phase addition was employed. The relative densities of the cold-sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites are greater than those of hot-pressed samples, as shown in Table S2 in the ESM, indicating that the dissolution‒precipitation process triggered by the cold sintering process increases the density of the samples. More details about the sintering mechanism of dissolution‒precipitation can be found in the TEM analysis. With increasing amount of PEI, the densities of the samples prepared by hot pressing tend to increase, revealing that the viscous flow of PEI also facilitates the densification of the samples. Therefore, it is concluded that both the dissolution‒precipitation process and viscous flow of PEI contribute to the densification of (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites during the cold sintering process.

The XRD results of the cold sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites are depicted in Fig. 1(c). All the samples show XRD peaks of BaTiO3 with a tetragonal structure, and no impurities can be observed, suggesting that BaTiO3 and PEI are chemically compatible under cold sintering conditions. The transient liquid phase of Ba(OH)2∙8H2O and H2TiO3 can form into a pure BaTiO3 phase during the cold sintering process. The PEI polymer has broad and weak peaks, which are difficult to be detected using XRD. The PEI phase can be detected via FT-IR analysis. Figure 1(d), Fig. S2, and Table S3 in the ESM show the results of Rietveld refinements of the pure BaTiO3 and 80% BaTiO3–20% PEI samples. The lattice parameters of BaTiO3 in the (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites are similar to those of pure BaTiO3, indicating that there are no structural changes for BaTiO3 in the (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites after the cold sintering process.

Figure 1(e) presents the FT-IR spectra of the BaTiO3, PEI, and cold-sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites. Clear vibrational bands of PEI can be observed in the cold sintered nanocomposites. The aromatic ether C–O–C peak is located at 1234 cm−1. The C–N stretching vibration (imide band II) is located at 1353 cm−1. The symmetric and asymmetric C‒O stretching vibrations (imide band I) are located at 1716 and 1774 cm−1, respectively [26,28,29]. The FT-IR results demonstrate that PEI can survive under cold sintering conditions. Furthermore, as the PEI content increases, the intensity of the FT-IR peaks of PEI is gradually enhanced, which is in good agreement with the composition design.

Figures 2(a)–2(f) show SEM images of BaTiO3 raw powders and fractured surfaces of the cold sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites. At low PEI contents (10–20 vol%), a dense microstructure with polygonal grains can be clearly observed. With increasing PEI content, polymer segregation becomes apparent, and the grains tend to have a more rounded shape. The porosities of the composites tend to decrease with increasing *x*, which is consistent with the density data in Fig. 1. The grain size distribution shows that the average grain sizes of the cold sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites (165.8–181.8 nm) are similar to those of BaTiO3 raw powders (160.5 nm), indicating that the grain growth of BaTiO3 is limited during the cold sintering process. The low sintering temperature and PEI addition could prevent the BaTiO3 grains from growing during sintering. Therefore, BaTiO3 ceramic-based nanocomposites can be obtained via a cold sintering process.

A schematic illustration of the grain boundary structures, high-resolution TEM images, and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) map of (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites cold sintered at 250 °C are depicted in Figs. 3(a)–3(g). Figures 3(a) and 3(b) show the microstructural schematic illustration and overview of the cold-sintered 80% BaTiO3–20% PEI nanocomposite, respectively, with PEI homogeneously distributed around the BaTiO3 grains. The average grain size of BaTiO3 is 166.7 nm, which is consistent with the SEM results. To better understand the densification mechanism during the cold sintering process, we further studied the amorphous‒crystal interface of the cold-sintered 80% BaTiO3‒20% PEI nanocomposite. The amorphous-crystal interface is arranged in a terrace-ledge manner (Fig. 3(c)), which is consistent with the terrace–ledge–kink (TLK) model used to describe the surface of growing crystals. In the amorphous phase region, nanoprecipitates nucleate on the crystal surface, as shown in Fig. 3(d). The phenomena of terrace–ledge interfaces and nanoprecipitates reveal that the dissolution‒precipitation process is one of the critical mechanisms during the cold sintering process. Combined with the density analysis, the cold sintering mechanism of the (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites includes a dissolution‒precipitation process triggered by a transient liquid phase and viscous flow assisted by the PEI polymer.

The PEI region is present as an amorphous phase and can be observed in Fig. 3(e). The high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF STEM) image and EDS map (Figs. 3(f) and 3(g)) provide additional evidence for the distribution of PEI, where PEI thin layers with thicknesses less than 10 nm are located along BaTiO3 grain boundaries. On the one hand, NMP can dissolve the PEI particles so that the PEI particles are uniformly distributed around the BaTiO3 grains. On the other hand, in the cold sintering process, the temperature is above the glass transition temperature of PEI, which is favorable for the formation of a thin layer of PEI under pressure. The microstructural results indicate that there is no reaction between BaTiO3 and PEI under cold sintering conditions and some thin PEI layers are located at the BaTiO3 grain boundaries, which may play an essential role in the performance of the composites.

The relative permittivity and dielectric loss with frequency for the cold sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI samples at room temperature are given in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b). The relative permittivity decreases with increasing frequency from 1 kHz to 1 MHz. Owing to the low relative permittivity of PEI (*ε*r ≈ 3) [30], the relative permittivity of the (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites decreases with increasing PEI addition. The presence of PEI and nanocrystals precipitated during the cold sintering process has a dilution effect on the dielectric properties of BaTiO3 [8,31]. Similarly, as the PEI content increases, the dielectric loss of the (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites decreases, which is lower than that of pure BaTiO3 ceramics (Fig. S3 in the ESM). Weak dielectric relaxation is observed at 100 kHz, which may be attributed to the accumulation of charges at internal grain boundaries [20]. The 90% BaTiO3–10% PEI composite has the highest relative permittivity of 174.87 with a tan*δ* of 0.0166 at 1 kHz, while the 50% BaTiO3–50% PEI composite has the lowest tan*δ* of 0.0094 with a relative permittivity of 84.82 at 1 kHz. Figure 4(c) compares the dielectric properties of BaTiO3–polymer composites reported in Refs. [32−39] with those of 80% BaTiO3–20% PEI in this work. The 50% BaTiO3–50% homogeneous sol (HGS)/PVDF composite prepared by the cold sintering process shows a high relative permittivity of 110.87, while the dielectric loss is high (0.12) [34]. The 70% BaTiO3–30% PVDF/HFP composite has a low dielectric loss of 0.05, but the relative permittivity is low (35) [38]. Both a low dielectric loss of 0.067 and a high relative permittivity of 110 are reported for the 50% BaTiO3–50% SiO2/PVDF composite [35]. BaTiO3−polymer composites with a relative permittivity higher than 100 typically have a high dielectric loss of over 0.05. If the dielectric loss is less than 0.05, the relative permittivity is usually lower than 100. However, cold-sintered 80% BaTiO3–20% PEI nanocomposites retain a high relative permittivity of 163 with a low dielectric loss of 0.014, suggesting that (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites prepared by one-step cold sintering process have excellent dielectric properties.

The temperature-dependent dielectric properties of the cold-sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites are presented in Figs. 4(d) and 4(e). There is a sharp and distinct dielectric peak in the BaTiO3 ceramic at approximately 120 °C with respect to the Curie temperature (*T*C) due to the ferroelectric and paraelectric phase transition. Interestingly, the addition of polymers facilitates the broadening of the phase transition peaks, which is promising for high-temperature applications [21,22]. The (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites with a low amount of PEI have a weak bump at 120–150 °C, which could be attributed to the ferroelectric‒paraelectric phase transition of BaTiO3. However, the dielectric dispersion phenomenon is no longer obvious when the amount of PEI is high, particularly when *x* ≥ 30%, because PEI is a nonferroelectric material. The above TEM analysis reveals that the PEI polymer fills the BaTiO3 grain boundaries and nanocrystals are precipitated during the cold sintering process, creating unique grain boundary conditions. This unique structure may affect the temperature dependence of the dielectric properties through the ferroelectric size effect, leading to broadening of the phase transition peak [22,31]. The relative permittivity of the (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites is stable over a wide temperature range, and in the meantime the dielectric loss remains low, indicating that cold-sintered BaTiO3-based nanocomposites are promising candidates for applications in high-temperature environments in the range of 150–200 °C. Figure S4 in the ESM shows the polarization–electric field loops of the cold sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites with different applied electric fields. Since BaTiO3 is a ferroelectric material, the samples show relatively high remanent polarization. PEI is a linear dielectric material with a low permittivity, and thus the maximum polarization and remanent polarization decrease with increasing amount of PEI.

Figure 4(f) presents Cole‒Cole plots of the impedance of cold sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites at room temperature. The complex impedance (*Z*) can be fitted via the RC circuit model, where BaTiO3 grains and grain boundaries are equivalent to resistors (*R*) and parallel resistive‒capacitive (RC) elements, respectively [40,41]. The accuracy of the fitting was confirmed with a fitting error of less than 5%. As shown in Table S4 in the ESM, the grain boundary resistance tends to increase with increasing amounts of PEI. The nanocomposite with *x* = 40% has the largest grain boundary resistance, which is an order of magnitude greater than that of the pure BaTiO3 ceramic. For the composite with 50% PEI content, the grain boundary impedance decreases, which may result from polymer agglomeration. Furthermore, all the (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites have a similar grain resistance of approximately 20 Ω, which is consistent with the previous microscopic analysis. When the PEI content is relatively low, it is uniformly distributed at the grain boundaries, forming thin layers, which increases the resistance of the grain boundaries.

For dielectric breakdown, the Weibull distribution reflects the probability of dielectric failure under the action of an electric field. Typically, the two-parameter Weibull distribution (Eq. (1)) is used to describe the probability distribution function of the electrical breakdown strength [8]:

(1) P=1−exp⁡(−(E/E0)β)

where *P* is the cumulative probability of failure; *E* is the electrical breakdown strength of the measured sample; *E*0 is the Weibull electrical breakdown strength; and *β* is a shape parameter reflecting the scattering of the measured data [42,43]. The value of *P* is determined according to the IEEE 930-2004 standard, as shown in Eq. (2):

(2) P=(i−0.44)/(n+0.25)×100%

where *i* is the number of tests ordered from the largest to the smallest according to the electrical breakdown strength of the samples, and *n* is the total number of tests. *E*0 and *β* can be obtained by fitting the curve of ln(−ln(1 − *Pi*)) vs. ln(*Ei*). Figure 5(a) shows the Weibull plots of the dielectric breakdown at room temperature for cold-sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites. The 80% BaTiO3–20% PEI nanocomposite has the best linear relationship between ln(−ln(1 − *Pi*)) and ln(*Ei*), with a *β* value of 10.37. Cao *et al*. [44] prepared BaTiO3 by high-temperature sintering with an electrical breakdown strength of 180 kV/cm, and Sada *et al*. [21] prepared BaTiO3 by a cold sintering process with an electrical breakdown strength of 60 kV/cm. With the addition of the polymer, the cold-sintered BaTiO3–PPO composites exhibited an increased electrical breakdown strength of 180 kV/cm [22]. In this work, with the addition of PEI, the electrical breakdown strength is dramatically improved from 154.96 kV/cm (BaTiO3) to 280 kV/cm (80% BaTiO3–20% PEI), which is superior to the reported data, as shown in Fig. 5(b) and Fig. S5 in the ESM. Therefore, the integration of BaTiO3 with PEI via a cold sintering process is an effective strategy to improve the electrical breakdown strength. Notably, the electrical breakdown strength decreases with increasing PEI content, possibly because the agglomeration of PEI limits the uniformity of the electric field distribution and enhances the local electric field. The breakdown tends to occur along the weakest paths, and thus, with increasing agglomeration of PEI, breakdown is likely to occur at the agglomeration regions [21,45−47].

FEM model and simulation results of current density for (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites with (c) no PEI addition (*x* = 0), (d) nonhomogeneous PEI, and (e) homogeneous PEI distributions.

To gain additional insight into how the addition of PEI improves the electrical breakdown strength of BaTiO3 ceramics, finite element models were employed, including the pure BaTiO3 model, BaTiO3–PEI model with nonhomogeneous PEI distribution, and BaTiO3–PEI model with homogeneous PEI distribution, as shown in Figs. 5(c)–5(e). The models have been simplified by setting the voltage potential at the upper boundary to 1 V, grounding the lower boundary, and calculating the area as a 1 μm × 1 μm square. As shown in Figs. 5(c) and 5(d), the current density decreases dramatically with the addition of PEI, and thus the electrical breakdown strength of BaTiO3 can be improved using BaTiO3–PEI composites. Figures 5(d) and 5(e) show that better dispersion of PEI further decreases the current density, indicating that the PEI distribution plays a critical role in the electrical breakdown strength. The aggregation of BaTiO3–PEI nanocomposites with high amounts of PEI results in a decrease in the electrical breakdown strength.

## 4 Conclusions

In conclusion, dense (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites are obtained via a cold sintering process. 60% BaTiO3–40% PEI shows the maximum relative density of 96.55%. Clear PEI regions can be observed in the (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites, and there are some PEI thin layers smaller than 10 nm at the BaTiO3 grain boundaries. The amorphous–crystal interface is arranged in a terrace-ledge manner, and nanocrystals are precipitated on the crystal surface, indicating that the dissolution‒precipitation process is one of the dominant mechanisms during the cold sintering process. The density comparison between the hot pressing and cold sintering processes reveals that both the dissolution‒precipitation process and the viscous flow of PEI contribute to the densification of the (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites during the cold sintering process. The average grain sizes of the cold sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites (165.8–181.8 nm) are similar to those of BaTiO3 raw powders (160.5 nm), indicating limited grain growth of BaTiO3 during the cold sintering process. The addition of the polymer results in a reduction in the dielectric loss, which is 0.014 with 20% PEI. The temperature-dependent dielectric properties show a weak relaxation peak at 150 °C due to the structural change at the Curie temperature, and the (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposites demonstrate excellent thermal stability in the range of room temperature–200 °C. The electrical breakdown strength can be significantly improved with a maximum *E*b of 280 kV/cm (80% BaTiO3–20% PEI), which is 80.65% higher than that of pure BaTiO3. In addition, the nanocomposite shows excellent dielectric properties, with a high relative permittivity of 163 and a low dielectric loss of 0.014. Therefore, the cold-sintered (1−*x*)BaTiO3–*x*PEI nanocomposite shows excellent comprehensive performance and is promising for dielectric applications.
