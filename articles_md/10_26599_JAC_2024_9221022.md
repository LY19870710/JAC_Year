## 1 Introduction

Materials used for the thermal protection systems (TPSs) of space vehicles must endure extreme temperatures and intense mechanical vibrations during launch and re-entry into the Earth’s atmosphere [1]. The combination of exceedingly high temperatures, chemically aggressive environments, and rapid heating and cooling surpasses the capabilities of current materials [2]. Ultra-high-temperature ceramic matrix composites (UHTCMCs) represent an innovative class of materials that potentially combine the high oxidation resistance of ultra-high-temperature ceramics (UHTCs) [3−6] with the damage tolerance and lightweight properties of ceramic matrix composites (CMCs). These composites can be produced with various processes and compositions [7−9] and exhibit different properties but are generally designed for application at temperatures exceeding 2000 °C [10−14]. Sintered UHTCMCs based on ZrB2 have been recently developed and tested by some authors of the present work in various arc jet facilities, showing excellent oxidation and ablation resistance [15−18]. Arc-jet facilities are indeed widely used to simulate atmospheric re-entry conditions on the ground and to evaluate material behavior before use in the TPS of hypersonic aerospace vehicles [19−23]. Exposure to dissociated plasma air generates a layered oxide scale whose thickness and composition depend on the heat flux conditions and time [17]. As discussed in detail in Ref. [24], other facilities can also be used for a preliminary screening of high-temperature ablation resistance (high-power lasers [25,26] and plasma [21,27] or oxyacetylene torches [28−30]) because of their ability to easily produce relevant heat fluxes. However, supersonic arc-jet wind tunnels [31−35] are the only kind of experimental apparatus where a realistic combination of heat flux, gas composition, surface pressure, and flow velocity can be reproduced, which complies with the suggestion provided in Refs. [32,33,36] of recreating an environment representative of hypersonic flight conditions.

One missing characterization is the determination of mechanical properties after arc-jet exposure tests to assess the impact of oxidation damage [34,37−40]. One reason for this gap in knowledge is the difficulty of conducting arc-jet experiments with samples of suitable shapes, such as large rectangular bars, for post-testing in a strength testing machine [34,41]. Repeated attempts to machine rectangular bars from cylindrical pellets after arc jet treatment were only partially successful because of damage to the oxide scale induced by machining. A previous study reported the oxidation of rectangular bars at 2000 and 2200 °C in static air, followed by subsequent flexural strength measurements [42]. The main difference is that arc jets can provide dissociated air and high heat fluxes that are not reproducible in regular air furnaces.

In this work, our previous work on UHTCMCs was further extended. The main novelties are as follows:

1) Design and manufacture of a special UHTCMC sample holder to hold 60 mm × 10 mm × 4.5 mm bars to replace standard graphite holders. Each bar was individually exposed to an arc-jet facility for one or two cycles up to 2200 °C and subsequently tested via 3-point (pt) bending. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the most viable option for assessing the impact of oxidation damage on strength.

2) Extension of the volatility diagrams of UHTCMCs at 2200 °C to better comprehend the peculiar oxide layering observed after testing. Volatility diagrams are a means for understanding gas–solid interactions such as oxidation reactions because they plot the vapor pressure of the predominant gaseous species as a function of  pO2 at specific temperatures [43−45].

3) Development of a numerical model to correlate the experimental data with the elastic properties of the material.

4) Effect of reusability tests on the mechanical strength retention.

### 2.1 Materials

Commercial products were used for the production of UHTCMCs: ZrB2 (grade B, H.C. Starck, Germany, particle size range of 0.5–6 µm, impurities (wt%): 0.2 C, 1.3 O, 0.19 N, 0.1 Fe, and 1.4 Hf); SiC (grade UF-25, H.C. Starck, Germany, average particle size, *D*50 0.45 µm); unidirectional (UD) preforms of pitch-based carbon fibres (Cf, XN80-6K, Granoc, Japan), diameter 10 µm, uncoated.

The composites were fabricated by infiltrating unidirectional fabrics stacked in a 0°/0° or 0°/90° architecture with aqueous slurries consisting of a mixture of 90 vol% ZrB2 and 10 vol% SiC. The green assemblies were sintered by hot pressing (HP) at 2173 K and 40 MPa of linear applied pressure [16]. The bulk densities were determined from mass and geometric volumes. Cross sections were prepared by a semi-automatic polishing machine (Tegramin-25, Struers, Italy) and analyzed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Carl Zeiss Sigma NTS Gmbh Öberkochen, Germany). The fiber and matrix volumetric amounts were evaluated by image analysis with software Image-Pro Analyzer 7.0 (v.7, Media Cybernetics, USA) following the guidelines of standard ISO 13383-1, method A2. At least 5 images were used to analyze the microstructure of the starting material, in the center and at the edges, and the areas of the fibers or other particles were measured from the two-dimentional (2D) images of the polished cross sections. The theoretical densities were calculated using the rule of mixtures. The relative densities were calculated as the ratio of bulk density to theoretical density.

### 2.4 Mechanical testing

For the bars, a 0°/0° architecture was chosen to simplify the mechanical behavior modeling and to avoid effects associated to the potentially uneven distribution of layers in the 0° or 90° configuration, while a 0°/90° configuration was chosen for the sample holder to guarantee uniform resistance along all directions.

Three-point bending tests were performed on 60 mm × 10 mm × 4.5 mm bars (length by width by thickness, respectively) with a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min according to the EN 658–3:2002 standard at RT (*σ*) with a span of 50 mm using a universal testing machine (Z050, Zwick Roell, Germany). At least 4 bars were tested before arc-jet experiments, and a batch of six bars were then subjected to arc jet, as illustrated in Fig. 1, and subsequently tested in the same mechanical testing facility, with the exposed face of the sample placed on the tensile side of the test to assess the negative impact of the oxidation scale.

A numerical model was developed to estimate failure stresses and assess the influence of the oxidized layer on the samples’ mechanical properties, aiming to determine whether the effect of oxidation is comparable to a reduction in thickness, thus suggesting that the material beneath the oxide layer remains unaffected. This simplified model does not account for any chemical or physical changes within the material. It was created in the Simulia/Abaqus environment using the explicit solver to manage material non-linearities without convergence issues. The dimensions were length (*L*) 60 mm *×* width (*W*) 10 mm × thickness (*T*) 4.495 mm. C3D8R solid elements with average side dimensions of 0.8 mm were used. The supports of the testing machine were modeled as rigid analytical surfaces, and a penalty contact rule was defined to reproduce the interaction between the rigid surfaces and the sample. This rule is composed of infinitely rigid normal behavior and frictional tangential behavior with a friction coefficient equal to 0.4. Boundary conditions were applied by fully constraining all degrees of freedom (DOFs) of the two lower supports, while a vertical displacement was imposed on the upper cylinder. All the other remaining DOFs were fixed.

The material was modeled with an elastoplastic law, which superimposes an orthotropic elastic behavior and a von Mises plasticity. This model was derived from the characterization performed in Ref. [48], in which direction 1 corresponds to the reinforcement direction. Von Mises plasticity was assumed for the sake of simplicity by defining a tabular hardening law that was manually calibrated to reproduce the experimental behavior of the as-built sample. Finally, a ductile damage law was implemented to capture the failure of the samples. In this law, a scalar variable (*D*) is defined, so that the effective stiffness (*K*eff) of the material is * Keff=(1−D)⋅K. This variable is driven by the equivalent plastic displacement with a triangular law [49]. This means that once a certain threshold of equivalent plastic strain ( εplDi) is reached, D*, which is initially null, starts increasing linearly, reducing the stiffness. If a certain threshold of plastic displacement (* uplDf) is reached, D* becomes 1, and the material is considered failed. The material properties used are reported in Ref. [48] and in Table 3. The stable time increment remained at approximately 7.75×10−8 s, resulting in computational time of circa 2 min when four Intel® CoreTM i7-10700 2.90 GHz CPUs were used.

### 3.1 Microstructure and mechanical properties

The physical properties of the two types of composites (0°/0° and 0°/90°) are reported in Ref. [48]. The material with 0°/0° fiber orientation used for the bars (Figs. 2(a) and 2(b)) had a density of 3.8 g/cm3 and a fiber content of approximately 45 vol%. The fibers were homogeneously distributed in the matrix, as shown in the inset of Fig. 2(b). Additional features are described in Ref. [16]. The sample with UD fibers in a 0°/90° architecture, used for the sample holder (Figs. 2(c) and 2(d)), displayed a regular texture with a bulk density of 3.4 g/cm3, a fiber content of approximately 50 vol%, and a calculated porosity of 9 vol%, as estimated by image analysis.

#### 3.3.1 Oxidation phenomena

After arc jet and bending tests, the fractured sections were polished for SEM investigation. Moreover, the bars were longitudinally sectioned to analyze the fiber status in the oxidized layer. The maximum thickness of the oxide layers was ~150 μm in the central zone, which decreased slightly along the bar width (Fig. 5(a)). The longitudinal section is shown in Fig. 5(b). Going from left (close to the fracture) to right, the oxide scale thickness decreased progressively, as testified by the energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) maps, highlighting the oxygen penetration depththrough the bar length. Two millimeters from the fracture, the oxide scale decreased to 90 μm from approximately 150 µm at the fracture.

*
Fig. 5Transverse and longitudinal sections of bars after one run. Oxygen penetration decreases from 150 µm at the fracture side to 80 µm at approximately 3 mm from the fracture.

The as-polished surface displayed the typical layered structure previously observed during the oxidation testing of ZrB2/SiC-based UHTCMCs [42], which consists of

—an outer ZrO2/SiO2 layer, made of large ZrO2 grains with SiO2 mainly distributed between the grains and on the surface;

—an intermediate porous layer of smaller ZrO2 grains with holes left by the oxidation of the fibers, which were partially filled with SiO2.

A peculiar microstructural feature was observed in the longitudinal section, which is generally not analyzed in oxidation experiments (Fig. 6). At the interface between the intermediate porous ZrO2/SiO2 layer and the unaffected bulk, a transition layer was identified where the matrix was oxidized to ZrO2 but the fibers were unaffected (Figs. 6(a) and 6(b)). This feature is difficult to observe in the cross section of the fibers because voids can be generated by both burn-off and pull-out of the fiber. In the central region of the bar that is the most heavily oxidized, this interfacial layer had a thickness of approximately 40 μm.

In a previous paper on the phase diagram of SiO2, Si3N4, and SiC by Heuer et al*. [44], for a temperature of 1500 °C, the threshold partial pressure of CO (*p*CO), for which C was a stable phase, was found to be 104.6 Pa (0.4 bar). In the present study, the threshold *p*CO for the stability of C at 2200 °C was on the order of 200 bar; therefore, no such  pO2 window was found where C and ZrO2 could coexist because the oxidation of carbon to CO(g) is highly favorable at high temperatures.

The retention of carbon in this intermediate layer may be explained by kinetics rather than thermodynamics. Under stagnant air or low-flow conditions, carbon can develop a boundary layer that effectively protects it from oxidation despite the relatively high oxygen level on the surface. Moreover, considering the strong fiber/matrix interface and highly dense matrix, oxygen should first diffuse along the ZrB2 grains before the fibers are exposed. Another possible explanation is that the fiber is oxidized to some degree, but its oxidation is not as evident or aggravated in the local conditions within the matrix for the low exposure time of this test. In fact, the average fiber diameter is approximately 10 µm, while the grain size of ZrB2 after sintering is 2–3 µm and that of SiC is < 0.5 µm, so the exposed surface area is significantly lower for the fibers.

#### 3.3.2 Flexural strength and numerical modeling

The average load at failure was approximately 1200 N for the UD as-machined bars, with a calculated flexural strength of 500 MPa. The typical load displacement curves before and after the arc jet tests are shown in Fig. 3. Notably, the bars exhibited a three-stage deformation process at room temperature (RT). This 3-stage behavior is common for fiber-reinforced ceramic composites with a brittle matrix, but these UHTCMC samples exhibited an unusual stiffness recovery in the last stage, almost reaching the pristine value [48,50]. This behavior was attributed to residual thermal stresses due to the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) mismatch between the matrix and fibers. During sintering, the higher CTE of the matrix results in contraction of the sample along the fiber axis. The residual compression of the fibers can be released during tensile or bending tests, which results in a peculiar plateau followed by steep stiffness recovery before fracture [51].

After the arc-jet tests, visual inspection revealed that the oxide was thicker in the central area, whilst the bars nearly had their original grey colour towards the ends. The results of 3-pt bending tests are listed in Table 5 and plotted in Fig. 8. After one run, the flexural strength was still > 80% of the pristine value and decreased to ~75% after two runs. Calculations were carried out considering both the as-machined bar dimensions and post-tests bar dimensions (Fig. 8(b)). The increase in volume caused by oxidation led to lower strength values. Fracture occurred in the central, most heavily oxidized region. After two runs, the oxides tended to detach in the fracture area. The load‒displacement curves showed a tendency toward linearity after the arc‒jet tests (Fig. 8(a)).

First, the non-linear constitutive law was tuned to reproduce the behavior of the “as-sintered” samples by calibrating the hardening law and the plastic strain at failure [48]. At this point, as explained in Section 2.2, a study was made to evaluate whether the overall effect of oxidation on the strength was comparable to that of a simple reduction in thickness. This suggests that the material below the oxide layer remained unaffected. Lateral oxidation of the sample was neglected because the sample was protected by the holder (Fig. 3(b)); moreover, the lateral faces had a negligible influence on the flexural stiffness and thus on the strength. For these reasons, the oxidation effect was modeled as a reduction in thickness with a trapezoidal profile, as shown in Fig. 8(d). Two nominal oxide thicknesses were used in the model: 160 µm, corresponding to the average oxide thickness, and 224 µm, corresponding to the maximum thickness observed. Considering the brittleness of the material, the maximum thickness reduction could be the strength-limiting dimension rather than the average one. The correlation with the experimental results as shown in Fig. 8(e) shows that (i) the constitutive law used is able to reproduce with precision the behavior of the as-sintered samples, (ii) the thickness reduction due to oxidation is able to reduce the force at failure but not the displacement at failure, contrary to what was observed in the experimental curves, and (iii) the numerical reduction in force at failure due to thickness reduction is comparable to the experimental one. The error in the correlation of displacement at failure could be related to the elastoplastic law, which tends to distribute the stress in the plasticized region and avoid stress concentration. The quantitative results, in terms of the force at failure and maximum stress reached in the external skins of the samples at the moment of failure, are presented in Table 6. They show that an error of 9.26% in the failure force was obtained using a thickness reduction of 224 µm in the numerical model. This could suggest that most of the reduction in force at failure is related to the thickness reduction and that the properties of the material underneath the oxide layers are not affected by exposure to the plasma flux. As shown in Fig. 8(f), the surface of the materials is characterized by channels and grooves left by burnout of the outermost fibers. This phenomenon is also evident in the fracture surface of the sample, where the oxide scale on top is characterized by voids left by the fibers, while some fiber pullout is visible on the unoxidized material below (Fig. 8(g)).

## 4 Conclusions

The carbon fiber-reinforced ZrB2/SiC composites were mechanically characterized after arc-jet testing at temperatures greater than 2200 °C. All the samples experienced a volume increase and weight loss after testing, with an oxide scale of 150 µm thickness. The oxidation behavior was slightly different from what is typically observed in conventional torch tests or oxidations in static air and consisted of an outer ZrO2/SiO2 scale, an intermediate porous layer of ZrO2/SiO2, and a thin, inner layer of ZrO2 with unoxidized fibers. Volatility diagrams were calculated for ZrB2 and SiC at 2200 °C to explain the observed layering. Even though the thermodynamic prediction was in agreement with the oxide layering observed on the surface, the retention of carbon in the inner layer could only be explained by the slower oxygen diffusion to the fibers due to the protection provided by the dense matrix around the fiber and the lower surface area of the latter compared with the fine grain size of the ceramic particles in the matrix. Following arc-jet testing, over 78% of the strength was retained (> 400 MPa), and a second arc-jet test led to a further decrease in strength of only ~5%. A numerical model was developed and calibrated to estimate the stress at failure and the effect of the oxide layer on reducing the strength. The results suggest that the reduced strength could be attributed mostly to the decrease in the thickness of the composite, i.e., the volume under tensile stress, assuming that the oxide layer does not contribute to the overall strength of the composite and excluding potential damage due to the thermal shock. The sample holder machined out of the same material and used for testing survived over 5 cycles for over 10 min without any failure and with negligible erosion. From the results obtained in this work, UHTCMCs have proven to be promising candidates for the fabrication of reliable and reusable materials for hypersonic re-entry conditions, with minimal strength degradation and negligible erosion, therefore constituting a new milestone and advancement in materials for hypersonics.
