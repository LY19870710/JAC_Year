## 1 Introduction

Polymer-derived ceramics (PDCs) represent a highly promising class of advanced materials. Since the 1960s, organic silicon polymers have been thermally decomposed to directly synthesize silicon-based PDCs [1]. Subsequent extensive research has led to significant advancements in PDC science, particularly through the seminal work of Fritz *et al.* [2] and Yajima *et al.* [3,4], who reported the production of SiC ceramic materials via the thermal decomposition of polycarbosilanes. These polymer precursor systems facilitate the creation of ceramics featuring customized chemical compositions and precisely engineered nanostructures, achieved through appropriate heat treatment processes, including solidification and pyrolysis, conducted under controlled atmospheric conditions [5]. As additive-free ceramic materials, PDCs exhibit desirable attributes such as ease of molding, resistance to oxidation and creep, and excellent high-temperature semiconductor properties [5].

In recent years, ternary systems such as SiCN have garnered significant attention because of their excellent mechanical characteristics and resistance to oxidation [6,7]. Wang *et al*. [8] achieved the densification of SiCN amorphous ceramics via low-temperature (1200 °C) and high-pressure (5 GPa) sintering processes, resulting in excellent mechanical properties, with hardness and elastic modulus values of 26.04±1.29 and 223.8±6 GPa, respectively. One of the key characteristics of Si-based PDCs is their exceptional oxidation resistance at extremely high temperatures ranging from 1500 to 1600 °C. This resistance is attributed to the development of a dense, continuous oxide layer, which is primarily composed of SiO2. Consequently, SiCN ceramics exhibit parabolic oxidation behavior, also known as passive oxidation, with rate constants and activation energies that resemble those of pure SiC and Si3N4 ceramics [9]. However, the insufficient high-temperature ablation resistance (> 2200 °C) of SiCN ceramics limits their application in the field of thermal protection materials. In general, polymer-derived SiCN ceramics are chemically stable up to 1500 °C [10]. The nanostructure of SiCN ceramics is commonly described as an amorphous network consisting of SiC*x*N4−*x* (0  11,12]. When temperatures exceed 1500 °C, the carbothermal decomposition of amorphous silicon nitride nanodomains enhances the crystallization process of SiCN ceramics [10]. Therefore, the severe decomposition reaction caused by carbon thermal reduction makes it difficult to achieve densification of SiCN ceramics. In addition, the glassy SiO2 produced by high-temperature ablation is not sufficient to protect materials.

To address this limitation, researchers have explored multicomponent modification strategies. The incorporation of elements such as B, Al, Hf, Zr, or Ti into Si–C–N systems significantly enhances their thermal stability. These elements effectively suppress crystallization and phase segregation under both inert and oxidative conditions [13,14]. Yuan *et al.* [15] synthesized SiHfBCN via the single-source-precursor (SSP) method, which increased its crystallization temperature (> 1600 °C) and reduced its mass loss (16−18]. For example, the Zr0.8Ti0.2C0.74B0.26 multielement ceramics developed by Zeng *et al*. [19] have been experimentally proven to have excellent antiablation performance in the temperature range of 2000–3000 °C. Its anti-ablation performance is significantly improved compared with that of ZrB2, ZrC, and Zr0.8Ti0.2C [19]. Similarly, (Hf0.2Ta0.2Zr0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)C-10SiC multielement ceramics have shown outstanding plasma ablation resistance [20].

The SSP method is universally recognized as an effective approach for precisely controlling ceramic composition and phase configuration through atomic-level precursor design [21,22]. Building upon this methodology, our previous work successfully synthesized TaC/SiC, (Hf0.2Ta0.8)C/SiC, (Hf0.7Ta0.3)C/SiC, and HfC/SiC ceramic nanocomposites via the SSP route via allylhydridopolycarbosilane (AHPCS) in combination with metal complexes [23,24]. Despite significant progress in the field, reports on (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN/SiCN multicomponent ceramic nanocomposites, with their mechanical properties and ablation resistance yet to be documented, are lacking. To bridge this knowledge gap, our study focused on the synthesis of a diverse array of polysilazane (PSZ) precursors, incorporating multiple metal elements through chemical modification of polysilazane. By using advanced spark plasma sintering (SPS) technology, we achieved a breakthrough in the fabrication of dense (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN/SiCN multicomponent ceramic nanocomposite bulks, representing the first reported instance of such materials. We subsequently characterized the microstructural evolution of these innovative materials and conducted an exhaustive examination of the mechanical properties and ablation resistance exhibited by the (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN/SiCN multicomponent ceramic nanocomposites. Furthermore, we explored the intricate relationships among the material composition, microstructure, and overall properties.

### 2.1 Preparation of dense monolithic multicomponent ceramic nanocomposites

The dense monolithic (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN/SiCN ceramic nanocomposites were prepared via spark plasma sintering of Si(Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN ceramics obtained by pyrolysis of metal-containing SSPs, denoted as (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)-PSZ. The (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)-PSZ SSPs were synthesized via chemical modification of PSZ via four transition metal amides (i.e., tetrakis(dimethylamino)titanium(IV) (TDMAT), tetrakis(dimethylamino)zirconium(IV) (TDMAZ), tetrakis(dimethylamino)hafnium(IV) (TDMAH), and pentakis(dimethylamino)tantalum(V) (PDMAT)). The reactions were conducted at 80 °C with weight ratios of (TDEAT + TDMAZ + TDEAH + PDMAT)/PSZ = 70/30 and 83/17, respectively. The molar ratios of the four transition metal amides were set as TDMAT : TDMAZ : TDMAH : PDMAT = 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 and 1 : 3 : 5 : 1. In this work, three typical SSPs were synthesized and denoted as 70(Ti0.25Zr0.25Hf0.25Ta0.25)-30PSZ, 70(Ti0.1Zr0.3Hf0.5Ta0.1)-30PSZ, and 83(Ti0.1Zr0.3Hf0.5Ta0.1)-17PSZ. For simplicity, these three groups of samples were further abbreviated as 70T1Z1H1T1-30PSZ, 70T1Z3H5T1-30PSZ, and 83T1Z3H5T1-17PSZ.

After pyrolysis in a nitrogen atmosphere at 1100 °C for 2 h, 70T1Z1H1T1-30PSZ, 70T1Z3H5T1-30PSZ, and 83T1Z3H5T1-17PSZ were transformed into 70ST1Z1H1T1CN, 70ST1Z3H5T1CN, and 83ST1Z3H5T1CN ceramics, respectively. To investigate the thermal stability and structural evolution of the Si(Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN-based ceramics, the as-pyrolyzed ceramics were annealed at 1300, 1500, and 1600 °C. Moreover, the Si(Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN ceramics obtained at 1100 °C were densified via spark plasma sintering at 2200 °C (75 MPa, 10 min) in argon at a high heating rate (450 °C/min) and denoted as 70ST1Z1H1T1CN-SPS, 70ST1Z3H5T1CN-SPS, and 83ST1Z3H5T1CN-SPS, respectively. The density and open porosity of the monoliths were measured via the Archimedic method.

### 2.2 Material characterizations

The as-synthesized SSPs were analyzed via attenuated total reflection-Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. FTIR spectra were recorded on a spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) from 500 to 4000 cm−1 in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode. Thermogravimetry (TGA) coupled with gas chromatography‒mass spectra was obtained on a spectrometer (TGA/STA8000-FTIR-GCMS-ATD, PerkinElmer, Massachusetts, USA) in a helium atmosphere with a heating speed of 20 °C/min.

X-ray powder diffraction (XRD), elemental analysis, Raman spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were used to characterize the phase/chemical compositions and microstructural evolution of the ceramics. The XRD patterns of the ceramics were recorded via a powder diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) with a Cu Kα1 radiation source (*λ* = 1.541874 Å) at a scanning speed of 5 (°)/min from 5° to 90°. Ti, Zr, Hf, Ta, and Si contents were measured by an inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectrometer (ICP-AES; iCap 6500, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The carbon content was measured via a combustion analysis method on an analyzer (LECO CS600, LECO Co., Ltd., USA). The nitrogen and oxygen contents of the ceramics were measured via a hot gas extraction technique with an analyzer (LECO TCH600, LECO Co., Ltd., USA). Raman spectroscopy was conducted on a micro-Raman spectrometer (dxr3, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with an argon laser (wavelength of 532 nm) from 400 to 3500 cm−1. The surface morphologies and cross sections of the monoliths before and after ablation tests were investigated via an SEM (CLARA, TESCAN, the Czech Republic), and an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS; Xplore 30, Oxford Instruments, UK) was used to examine the element distributions. The TEM images were obtained from ground powder ceramics via a microscope (Talos F200X, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV equipped with an EDS detector.

### 2.3 Nanoindentation and flexural strength experiments

The dense monolithic (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN/SiCN nanocomposites were finely ground by diamond grinding discs and polished with 0.25 μm particle polishing solution. The nanoindentation tests were subsequently conducted on an ultra-nano-hardness-tester micro-creep-testing micro-scratch-tester (UNHT-MCT-MST; CSM, Switzerland) with a maximum loading force of 20 mN (loading rate = 40 mN/min, dwell time = 10 s). The nanoindentation and elastic modulus were calculated via the Oliver & Pharr (O & P) method. In addition, flexural strength tests were performed on rectangular bar samples (dimensions: ~2 mm × ~2.4 mm × 18 mm) via a three-point bending fixture (span = 12 mm) mounted on a universal mechanical testing machine (Instron 3369, Norwood, USA). The tests were carried out at a crosshead speed of 1.0 mm/min until fracture. The flexural strength (* σf) was calculated according to Eq. (1):

(1) σf=3FL2bh2

where F* is the fracture load, *L* is the span length, *b* is the specimen width, and *h* is the specimen height.

### 2.4 Ablation test

The ablation test was carried out on an air-plasma device for 60 s. During the test, the flow rates of argon and hydrogen gas were set as 2000 and 68 L/h, respectively. The working current and voltage were set as 500 A and 59 V, respectively. The temperature of the central ablation region was measured via an optical pyrometer (SA-R60200A2W3, Wuxi Shiao Technology Co., Ltd, China). The linear ablation rate (*R*l) and mass ablation rate (*R*m) are calculated via Eqs. (2) and (3), respectively:

The (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)-PSZ SSPs were produced via chemical reactions involving PSZ and metal amide complexes. Taking the synthesis of 70T1Z1H1T1-30PSZ as an example, during the synthesis process, FTIR spectroscopy was employed to characterize the bonding changes in PSZ before and after the reaction, as well as after crosslinking at 250 °C for 2 h (Fig. 1(a)). According to Refs. [24−26], the main absorption bands observed in the spectra can be assigned to N–H (3382 cm−1), C–H (2947 cm−1), NC–H (2767 cm−1), Si–H (2124 cm−1), C=C (1593 cm−1), Si–CH3 (1244 cm−1), Si–N–H (1160 cm−1), Si–N–Si (895 cm−1), and Si–C (752 cm−1).

According to previous work [15,21,24,27], PSZ-containing functional groups, namely, Si–N–H and Si–H, are highly prone to react with metal amide complexes through the formation of Si–N–Me (Me = Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta) linkages, leading to a reduction in the relative intensity of the Si–H and Si–N–H bands in 70T1Z1H1T1-30PSZ. By computing bond dissociation energies via density functional theory (DFT) [28], which indicates that Si–N bonds are more favorable than Si–Me bonds for the reaction between PSZ and metal-containing amide complexes (Si–N > Si–Me), it is evident that the formation of Si–N bonds is more likely. Figure 1(a) shows the FTIR spectra of 70T1Z1H1T1-30PSZ. Compared with those of PSZ, the intensities of the N–H and Si–H absorption bands in 70T1Z1H1T1-30PSZ are significantly lower but do not completely disappear. Specifically, based on FTIR analysis, the ratios of N–H/Si–CH3 and S–H/Si–CH3 in the PSZ are 0.15 and 0.34, respectively, whereas in 70T1Z1H1T1-30PSZ, these ratios decrease to 0.05 and 0.16, respectively. When Si–CH3 was used as an internal standard, the ratios of N–H/Si–CH3 and Si–H/Si–CH3 decreased by 64.6% and 52.4%, respectively. This result indicates that the reaction between PSZ and the metal amide complexes does not fully consume all the N–H and Si–H groups, and this incomplete reaction may be attributed to steric hindrance effects [15,29].

However, the FTIR spectra of 70T1Z1H1T1-30PSZ depicted in Fig. 1(a) reveal a likely reduction in intensity, albeit not complete disappearance, of the N–H and Si–H absorption bands. This suggests that the reaction between PSZ and metal amide complexes does not fully consume all the N–H/Si–H groups, potentially because of steric hindrance effects [15,29].

The absorption bands corresponding to the vinyl groups (C–H stretching at 3047 cm−1 and C=C stretching at 1593 cm−1) [30] also disappeared during the synthesis of the 70T1Z1H1T1-30PSZ SSPs. According to previous reports [21], this is caused by the conversion of vinyl groups via hydrosilylation during the reaction between PSZ and metal-containing amide complexes, which indicates that the metal-containing amide complexes act as catalysts for the hydrosilylation process [31,32].

Figure 1(b) shows the reaction pathways for synthesizing (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)-PSZ SSPs on the basis of the aforementioned analysis. In this process, the vinyl groups of PSZ undergo hydrosilylation, increasing the degree of crosslinking of the precursor. Furthermore, metal amide complexes interact with the Si–H and Si–N–H groups of PSZ, resulting in the formation of Si–N–Me bonds and the release of gaseous CH4 and C2H7N, respectively (Me = Ti, Zr, Hf, and Ta).

### 3.2 Polymer-to-ceramic transformation

The transformation of (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)-PSZ SSPs into amorphous SiTiZrHfTaCN ceramics was studied via *in situ* TGA combined with gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). As illustrated in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b), thermogravimetric analysis (TG) revealed that the mass loss of the metal-containing PSZ was merely 9.26 wt% after 250 °C curing, which is dramatically lower than the 52.04 wt% loss observed in the unmodified PSZ. The remarkable 82% reduction in mass loss directly demonstrates how metal incorporation enhances the degree of cross-linking, as the introduced transition metals (Ti/Zr/Hf/Ta) serve as multifunctional nodes that bridge multiple polymer chains through metal–N–Si coordination bonds. The ceramic yield of the metal-amido-complex-modified polysilazane (70T1Z1H1T1-30PSZ) was approximately 68.76 wt%, which was significantly greater than that of pure polysilazane (ca. 39.55 wt%) when no crosslinking agent (e.g., dicumyl peroxide) was used. This increase in ceramic yield is attributed to the substantial increase in the degree of crosslinking of the polymer upon modification with metal-amido complexes.

The derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) curve (Fig. 2(b)) indicates that the transformation from polymer to ceramic takes place in three distinct phases. From room temperature to approximately 250 °C, the initial weight reduction was 9.26 wt%, which was attributed primarily to the evaporation of oligomers. During this period, the primary crosslinking mechanism for 70T1Z1H1T1-30PSZ is hydrosilylation [27,33,34]. These reactions take place with minimal mass loss and substantially increase the ceramic yield of the SSP.

During the second decomposition period from 250 to 800 °C (Figs. 2(b) and 2(c)), a major mass loss amounting to ~21.83 wt% occurred, which corresponds to the decomposition processes described briefly below. The release of H2 seems to continue throughout the whole conversion process from 250 to 800 °C, which is due to dehydrogenative coupling reactions (such as ≡Si–H and =N–H) or the decomposition of hydrocarbon substituents. Furthermore, a strong release of CH4 (*m*/*z* = 12–16, where *m* represents the ion mass and *z* is the charge number, as detected by mass spectrometry (MS) in the coupled TGA-IR-MS system) was detected during this process, which relies on the reaction between N–CH3/Si–CH3 and Si–H/N–H. As shown in Fig. 2(d), the C–H absorption bands assigned to CH4 were detected at 350–700 °C. Moreover, the emission of ethane (*m*/*z* = 30, 31) and ethylene (*m*/*z* = 25–28) was also observed [26]. They are probably the byproducts of the reactions between ≡Si–CH=CH2/≡Si–CH2–CH3 and =N–H. From 150 to 400 °C, amine fragments (*m*/*z* = 42–44) are released during the transamination processes that take place between the ≡Si–N= and ≡Me–N= groups, which could be further supported by the absorption bands (NC–H, N–CH3, and N–H) detected below approximately 400 °C in the *in situ* FT-IR curves (Fig. 2(d)) from ambient temperature to 800 °C [35]. Additionally, the decomposition of the –NMe2 end groups may also contribute to the formation of amines [26,36]. At temperatures above approximately 800 °C, only a slight mass loss (ca. 0.15 wt%) is detected, indicating that the conversion of the polymer to ceramic is considered complete at this temperature. In Fig. 2(d), it is worth noting that C=O and O–H were caused by the carbon dioxide and water molecules in the testing environment [37].

#### 3.3.1 Chemical composition and thermal stability

**Elemental analysis**. Following the pyrolysis of the prepared SSPs at 1100 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere, black ceramic powders, specifically 70ST1Z1H1T1CN, 70ST1Z3H5T1CN, and 83ST1Z3H5T1CN, were obtained. The chemical compositions of typical samples (i.e., 70ST1Z1H1T1CN obtained at 1100 °C) are shown in Table 1. Interestingly, the molar ratios of the transition metal elements in 70ST1Z1H1T1CN cannot be controlled precisely if the transition metal amides (i.e., TDMAT, TDMAZ, TDMAH, and PDMAT) react simultaneously with PSZ, which suggests the presence of a competitive reaction mechanism among the reactants. However, this problem can be solved by optimizing the reaction sequence between the transition metal amides and PSZ. As shown in Table 1, if the amides reacted simultaneously with the PSZ, the molar ratio of Ti : Zr : Hf : Ta was 0.5 : 1 : 1 : 1. This means that approximately 50% of the TDMAT did not react with the PSZ, and it escaped along with the extraction of xylene during the solvent extraction process. If we change the reaction sequence, which means that the TDMAT reacts with the PSZ prior to the other 3 amides (i.e., TDMAZ, TDMAH, and PDMAT), the molar ratio of Ti : Zr : Hf : Ta within the nanocomposites is approximately 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. Furthermore, even when the ratio of transition metal amides to Ti : Zr : Hf : Ta = 1 : 3 : 5 : 1 and the total metal amide content was increased to 83%, the final atomic ratios in the ceramic products remained consistent with the initial metal amide feed ratios. Therefore, it is possible to control the transition metal elements within the (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN/SiCN ceramic nanocomposites via the molecular design of the precursors and controlling the reaction sequence.

**Thermal stability.** To investigate microstructural evolution in terms of phase separation, crystallization, and decomposition, the as-pyrolyzed ceramics were subsequently annealed in a nitrogen atmosphere at temperatures ranging from 1300–1600 °C for 5 h. As shown in Fig. 3, the samples remained stable after being annealed at 1300 °C. A mass loss was observed when the annealing temperature was increased to 1500 °C (Fig. 3). Specifically, the 70ST1Z3H5T1CN sample experienced a mass reduction of approximately 3.6 wt%, whereas the 83ST1Z3H5T1CN sample, which contains a higher metal‒carbon nitride content, exhibited a more significant mass loss of approximately 9.5 wt%. Furthermore, when the annealing temperature was increased to 1600 °C, the mass loss increased, reaching approximately 11.2 wt% for the 70ST1Z3H5T1CN sample and 15.4 wt% for the 83ST1Z3H5T1CN sample. For comparative analysis, metal-free SiCN ceramics were examined under identical conditions (Fig. 3), showing substantially greater mass loss (24.7 wt%) at 1600 °C, representing a 60.4% increase relative to that of their transition metal-containing counterparts. This marked contrast clearly demonstrates the stabilizing effect of transition metals in suppressing high-temperature decomposition of the SiCN network. During the annealing process, mass loss primarily consists of two aspects: first, the substitution of N atoms by C atoms in SiCN during the crystallization process, resulting in the generation and release of N2; second, the carbothermal reactions between the carbon- and oxygen-containing phases during annealing lead to the release of CO.

The evolution of SiCN during annealing can be elucidated through three distinct processes, as previously reported [15,38]. #### 3.3.2 Phase compositions and structural evolution

**XRD analysis.** To examine the crystallization characteristics of the derived ceramics, XRD patterns were acquired for the ceramics annealed at various temperatures, and the outcomes of these measurements are presented in Fig. 4. The XRD patterns of SiCN, 70ST1Z1H1T1CN, 70ST1Z3H5T1CN, and 83ST1Z3H5T1CN prepared at 1100 °C indicated that the ceramics were predominantly amorphous. Notably, SiCN tends to be converted to SiC after annealing at 1600 °C, as explained above.

**Raman spectroscopy.** Raman spectroscopy is a highly sensitive and invaluable nondestructive technique for the characterization of free carbon, particularly for elucidating its structural transformations when subjected to elevated temperatures [24,41−43]. As depicted in Figs. 5(a)–5(c), the first-order Raman spectra present three distinct peaks of free carbon: the G band (~1349 cm−1) and the D band (~1588 cm−1). The G′ band represents an overtone of the D band, whereas the D + G band is characteristic of disturbed graphitic structures.

The exceptional nanohardness demonstrated by the multicomponent (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN/SiCN ceramic nanocomposites can be attributed to the cumulative influence of several fundamental mechanisms [59,60]. Primarily, solid solution strengthening is of paramount importance, wherein cationic sites within the (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN fcc crystal lattice are occupied by metallic elements, namely, Ti, Zr, Hf, and Ta, whereas anionic sites are filled by C and N. This arrangement induces substantial lattice distortions, markedly increasing the material’s capacity to resist deformation and, consequently, increasing its hardness. These distortions generate supplementary stress fields that cooperate with applied forces, rendering the initiation of slip events more challenging [59]. Second, the material’s nanoscale architecture, distinguished by a high surface-to-volume ratio and heightened surface atom activity, significantly contributes to its hardness. Moreover, the interfaces between nanosized grains serve as effective obstacles to dislocation motion, resulting in an increase in hardness. Collectively, the interplay of these mechanisms provides a comprehensive explanation for the remarkable nanohardness observed in multicomponent carbonitride nanocomposite ceramics.

During the ablation process, owing to differences in the surface temperature and high-speed plasma airflow mechanical flushing, three different regions appeared on the sample surface, namely, the central region, transition region, and edge region (corresponding to Figs. 11(j), 11(k), and 11(l), respectively). In the ablation center region, only high melting point oxide (e.g., Hf(Zr)O2) were observed, as other low melting point oxides (e.g., SiO2, Ta2O5, and TiO2) gradually flowed toward the transition region under high-speed plasma flame flushing. Therefore, continuous oxide scales of molten silica and in situ*-generated Ta2O5 filled the Hf(Zr,Ti)O2 skeleton can be observed in the transition region, which can be confirmed by the element distribution in Fig. 11(n), where the white contrast phase is Hf(Zr,Ti)O2 and the gray phase is SiO2 with Ta2O5. Moreover, similar oxide layer structures can also be observed in the edge region (Fig. 11(l)). However, microscopic examination of this area revealed that the surface of the ablated sample featured numerous micron-sized pores resulting from the significant amount of gas (e.g., CO, CO2, or N2) [65] generated during the rapid oxidation of the (Ti0.1Zr0.3Hf0.5Ta0.1)CN and SiCN substrates at extremely high temperatures (ca. 2200 °C).

To further elucidate the microstructure evolution of the multicomponent ceramic nanocomposite during ablation, a detailed analysis of cross-sections of the oxide scales was conducted. The cross-sectional microstructures of 70ST1Z1H1T1CN-SPS, 70ST1Z3H5T1CN-SPS, and 83ST1Z3H5T1CN-SPS after ablation at 2200 °C for 60 s are presented in Fig. 12. For 70ST1Z1H1T1CN-SPS, the oxide scale is loose and thin, with a thickness of only 12 μm (Figs. 12(a) and 12(b)). During the ablation process of the sample, the oxide layer was unable to provide protection, resulting in continuous oxidation inside the materials. HfO2 and ZrO2 have melting points significantly higher than that of TiO2. Thus, by increasing the content of Hf and Zr, the oxide generated during ablation can remain solid and play an effective role in stabilizing glassy SiO2 and enhancing ablation resistance at ~2200 °C (Figs. 12(d) and 12(e)). The oxide scale is filled by some holes, and the oxide is approximately 117 μm and well bonded to the matrix.

#### 3.5.2 Ablation mechanisms

Generally, the surface temperature of samples exposed to an air‒plasma environment sharply increased to 2200 °C. At this temperature, the dense (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN/SiCN monoliths were rapidly ablated, accompanied by the oxidation of (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN and SiC with O2. Without considering the solid solution effect, the oxidation process can be described by Reactions (7)–(10):

(7) 2(TiZrHfTa)CxN1−x+(172+x)O2(g)=2TiO2+2ZrO2+2HfO2+Ta2O5+2xCO(g)+(1−x)N2(g)

(8) SiC+32O2(g)=SiO2+CO(g)

(9) SiC+O2(g)=SiO(g)+CO(g)

(10) C+12O2(g)=CO(g)

In addition, the relative thermodynamic oxidation reaction curves are plotted in Fig. 13, which shows that all the oxidation reactions can occur spontaneously during the ablation process. (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN generates complex oxides after ablation. Figure 11(f) shows that some Ti, Zr, and Hf atoms coexist in the white phase to form the multicomponent oxide Hf(Zr,Ti)O2, whereas others form binary oxides that are dispersed in the oxide layer. In addition, Ta atoms only exist as Ta2O5 after oxidation. The influence of oxide products on ablation performance is contingent upon the vapor pressure of those oxides. SiC has a relatively lower threshold for oxygen partial pressure, making it prone to oxidize first to produce SiO and CO. According to previous studies [64,66], Hf and Zr are preferentially oxidized over Ti and Ta after the oxidation of SiC.

According to the microstructure and phase analysis, the ablation mechanism of (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN/SiCN is described in Fig. 14. The synergistic interaction among multiple metallic elements (Ti/Zr/Hf/Ta) and Si contributes to the formation of a distinctive composite oxide layer characterized by a refractory Hf(Zr,Ti)O2 oxide skeleton infiltrated with SiO2/Ta2O5 liquid phases. During the high-speed and high-temperature plasma ablation process, the surface temperature of a sample rapidly increases and is accompanied by intense oxidation reactions. Thus, a unique oxide layer structure composed of aggregated high melting point Hf(Zr,Ti)O2, micro/nanoparticles (Ta2O5, TiO2, ZrO2, and HfO2), and glassy SiO2 formed on the surface of the sample. Among them, the Hf(Zr,Ti)O2 skeleton plays a pinning role in the oxide layer, providing mechanical strength for the oxide layer. During the ablation process, glassy SiO2 fills the liquid phase in the Hf(Zr,Ti)O2 skeleton, and pores are generated during the oxidation process. The micro/nanoparticle oxides suspended in glassy SiO2 play a role in increasing the viscosity of the silica, thereby increasing the stability of the oxide layer. For materials with high contents of Ti and Ta (70ST1Z1H1T1CN-SPS), the stability of the oxide layer under plasma flushing cannot be maintained because of the production of less Hf(Zr,Ti)O2 and more liquid phase, leading to its poor ablation performance. Therefore, by increasing the atomic ratio of Zr and Hf metals and the content of (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN through design, a continuous and stable oxide layer can be formed after ablation. This structure effectively protects the material from the invasion of oxygen atoms and withstands mechanical erosion, ultimately helping 83ST1Z3H5T1CN-SPS to have excellent ablation resistance.

## 4 Conclusions

In the present work, novel (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN/SiCN ceramic nanocomposites containing a SiCN matrix (composed of SiC and/or Si3N4) and approximately 60–80 wt% multicomponent ceramic (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)CN were successfully prepared via atom-designable SSPs containing certain metals (i.e., (Ti,Zr,Hf,Ta)-PSZ). The monolithic nanocomposites were densified via SPS technology at 2200 °C with a high heating rate (450 °C/min). The phase composition and microstructure of the nanocomposites were investigated. The results indicated that both the atomic ratio of the metal elements within (Ti0.1Zr0.3Hf0.5Ta0.1)CN and its content can be precisely controlled during the synthesis process. Furthermore, the metal-containing phase was confirmed to be composed of nanoparticles surrounded by a carbon shell. The as-prepared (Ti0.1Zr0.3Hf0.5Ta0.1)CN/SiCN nanocomposites exhibit exceptional mechanical properties, with a nanohardness of 37.36±0.51 GPa (40% higher than that of other reported polymer-derived ceramics) and a remarkable flexural strength of 603.41±11.67 MPa, surpassing that of most high-entropy carbide ceramics. Moreover, the linear ablation rate and mass ablation rate of the (Ti0.1Zr0.3Hf0.5Ta0.1)CN/SiCN nanocomposites with approximately 80 wt% (Ti0.1Zr0.3Hf0.5Ta0.1)CN at 2200 °C were 0.033 μm/s and 0.183 mg/s, respectively, which are 2 orders of magnitude lower than those of other multicomponent UHTCs under similar conditions. This work extends the state-of-the-art ceramics by preparing dense monolithic nitrogen-containing multicomponent ceramic nanocomposites. This demonstrates that the complex multicomponent design is a promising approach toward achieving superior mechanical properties and ablation resistance at ultrahigh temperatures, which provides a preliminary basis for the future development of multiphase multicomponent UHTCs.
