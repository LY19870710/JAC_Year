## 1 Introduction

There is an urgent demand for the development of new thermal protection materials for use in hypersonic aerospace vehicles in hostile environments (i.e., severe oxidation, strong airflow scour, and mechanical denudation) [1]. Ultrahigh-temperature ceramics (UHTCs) have attracted much attention because of their high hardness, high melting point, excellent high-temperature resistance, and corrosion resistance [2−4]. In previous reports [5−7], hafnium carbide (HfC) was regarded as an effective ablation-resistant material due to its high melting point (3890 °C) and excellent chemical and physical characteristics. Importantly, the melting point of HfO2 is approximately 2800 °C, which is good for the structural stability of the oxide layer when working in an ultrahigh-temperature aerodynamic environment (> 2500 °C). However, it cannot provide effective high-temperature protection over a wide temperature range. In addition, the high density and elevated cost of HfC pose challenges for practical implementations. To address these issues, the integration of additional elements to form a multicomponent carbide solid solution or the introduction of a second phase can significantly expand the operational temperature range, effectively reducing both the density and cost of HfC.

Recently, the formation of multicomponent UHTCs, including ternary carbide ceramics and high-entropy carbide ceramics, has become a hot research topic, and their improved oxidation and ablation properties have attracted much attention [3,8−10]. Lun *et* *al*. [11] studied the oxidation behaviors of (Zr,Hf,Ti)C*x* solid solutions. The results indicated that the multicomponent carbides show excellent phase stability at high temperatures compared to that of HfC. Wang *et* *al*. [12] compared the ablation behavior of (Hf–Ta–Zr–Nb)C with that of HfC and Ta0.8Hf0.2C carbides. The results showed that the oxide layer with complex compositions formed by (Hf–Ta–Zr–Nb)C was beneficial for acting as an ablation barrier. Zhou *et* *al*. [13] investigated the oxidation behavior of high-entropy (Ti,Zr,Hf,Nb,Ta)C and mono carbides (i.e., TiC, ZrC, HfC, NbC, and TaC). The former high-entropy (Ti,Zr,Hf,Nb,Ta)C exhibited better performance due to its excellent thermal stability.

HfC and ZrC are usually used in ultrahigh-temperature environments because of their high melting points (HfC: 3890 °C; ZrC: 3540 °C) as well as the high melting points and low vapor pressures of their oxide products (HfO2: 2810 °C; ZrO2: 2670 °C) [14,15]. TiC, as a member of UHTCs, also has a high melting point of 3140 °C. Although its oxidation product TiO2 has a relatively lower melting point (TiO2: 1840 °C) than ZrO2 and HfO2, it has a lower diffusion coefficient for oxygen than do ZrO2 and HfO2 (in TiO2: 1.12×10−13 m2/s at 1800 °C, in ZrO2: 1.16×10−12 m2/s at 1800 °C, and in HfO2: 3.0×10−6 m2/s at 2060 °C) [2,16,17]. Moreover, TiO2 is more stable than SiO2 because of its lower vapor pressure, decomposition pressure, and vaporization rate at high temperatures [18,19]. Importantly, the generated Ti-containing low-melting-point phases involving ZrTiO4 and HfTiO4 with high-melting-point phases such as HfO2 and ZrO2 can form dense and continuous oxide layers. As reported by Ye *et* *al*. [20], the metal components in multicomponent UHTCs can be thermodynamically divided into preferentially oxidized components and laggingly oxidized components. The preferentially oxidized elements (Zr, Hf, etc.) in the multicomponent UHTCs can form a stable oxide skeleton because of their high melting point, while the oxides produced by the laggingly oxidized component (Ti, Ta, Nb, etc.) exhibit a lower melting point and can fill in the oxide skeleton, forming a stable and dense protective oxide layer. The unique oxide layer structure acts as an effective oxygen diffusion barrier and is important for improving the high-temperature ablation resistance.

In addition to the formation of single-phase UHTC solid solutions, the incorporation of SiC in multicomponent UHTCs is also a good approach for improving oxidation resistance and reducing density and cost. Zhang *et* *al*. [2] modified a ZrC coating using SiC/TiC nanocomposite powders via the single-source precursor (SSP) method. The results indicated that the enhanced ablation resistance was ascribed to the formation of a Zr*x*Ti*y*C solid solution and amorphous SiC, after which a Zr–Ti–Si–O glassy phase with high viscosity and low evaporation pressure was acquired during the ablation process. Wang *et* *al*. [21] studied the ablation resistance of (Hf–Ta–Zr–Nb–Ti)C–*x*SiC composites. The results showed that (Hf–Ta–Zr–Nb–Ti)C–10 vol% SiC has a linear ablation rate of 0.3 μm/s, which is only one-tenth of that of (Hf–Ta–Zr–Nb–Ti)C. This significant improvement probably results from the addition of an oxide layer with molten SiO2 and Hf–Zr–Si–O oxides.

Moreover, it is well known that decreasing the grain size to the nanoscale is important for improving the mechanical properties of ceramics [22]. Currently, powder mixing is the main method used to produce ceramic nanocomposites. However, as nanoparticles tend to agglomerate, it is not easy to produce uniformly mixed nanocomposites. Furthermore, it is difficult to prepare dense monolithic ceramic nanocomposites because of severe grain growth during sintering. In recent years, the single-source-preliminary method has proven to be a promising route for preparing ceramic nanocomposites [23,24]. Therefore, in the present work, four (Ti,Zr,Hf)C/SiC ceramic nanocomposites with different metal/carbide ratios were prepared by the SSP method and spark plasma sintering (SPS). The synthesis of (Ti,Zr,Hf)-containing precursors, polymer-to-ceramic transformation, phase and microstructural characterization, as well as the air-plasma ablation behavior at approximately 2200 °C and associated ablation mechanisms, were intensively studied and discussed. This work is expected to provide a better understanding of achieving improved ablation resistance by tailoring the atomic ratios of elements within UHTCs through the molecular design of SSPs and to help in the design of thermal protection materials used in hypersonic vehicles.

### 2.1 Material preparation

Ti-, Zr-, and Hf-containing silicon-based single-source precursors (Ti–Zr–Hf–VHPCS SSPs) were synthesized upon reaction of a commercially available vinylhydridopolycarbosilane (VHPCS; Institute of Chemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China) with a mixture of three metal amides (tetrakis(dimethylamino)titanium(IV) (TDMAT), tetrakis(dimethylamino)zirconium(IV) (TDMAZ), and tetrakis(dimethylamino)hafnium(IV) (TDMAH)) (Nanjing Ai Mouyuan Scientific Equipment Co., Ltd., China). The weight ratio of (TDMAT+TDMAZ+TDMAH)/VHPCS was set to 70/30. The molar ratios of the metal amides (TDMAT : TDMAZ : TDMAH) used for the synthesis of the single-source precursors were 1 : 1 : 1, 2 : 3 : 5, 2 : 3 : 3, and 1 : 2 : 1, respectively, and the associated SSPs were denoted as T1Z1H1, T2Z3H5, T2Z3H3, and T1Z2H1, respectively. The detailed information of the SSPs is listed in Table 1. The three metal amides (7 g in total) were first mixed together in 10 mL of xylene to obtain a yellow transparent solution. Then, the solution was added to a solution of 3 g VHPC in 5 mL xylene. The silicon-based SSPs with solvent were prepared after stirring and heating at 80 °C for 3 h. After further evaporating the xylene under vacuum at 80 °C for 5 h, the black solid Ti–Zr–Hf–VHPCSs were obtained. The synthesis process was carried out using the Schlenk technique with an argon atmosphere. Then, the as-synthesized 4 SSPs were ground and pyrolyzed at 1100 °C in argon to prepare ceramic powders, which are denoted as T1Z1H1-1100, T2Z3H5-1100, T2Z3H3-1100, and T1Z2H1-1100. The high-temperature phase evolution of the TZH-1100 ceramics was studied by heating the as-pyrolyzed ceramics at 1300, 1400, 1500, and 1600 °C in argon for 5 h. The dense monoliths (Ti,Zr,Hf)C/SiC were prepared from the corresponding TZH-1100 powders using a spark plasma sintering furnace (FCT HP D25, FCT Systeme GmbH, Rauenstein, Germany) at 2200 °C for 20 min under a uniaxial pressure of 45 MPa and were named T1Z1H1-SPS, T2Z3H5-SPS, T2Z3H3-SPS, and T1Z2H1-SPS, respectively. All ceramic monoliths were finally polished with 1/4 μm polycrystalline diamonds on felt cloth for the air-plasma ablation tests. The skeleton density and open porosity of the monoliths were determined by Archimedes’ method using deionized water as the immersion medium.

### 2.2 Ablation test

The ablation behavior of the as-sintered dense monolithic (Ti,Zr,Hf)C/SiC ceramic nanocomposites was tested on air-plasma equipment. The distance between the gun tip and the samples was 60 mm. The samples (*φ*20 mm × 4 mm) were placed on graphite molds with an external diameter of 29 mm and an internal pit with a diameter of 20 mm, ensuring that they could be firmly fixed in the pit without dropping during ablation. Then, they were fixed on a holder connected to the cooling system and exposed to air-plasma for 60 s. The fluxes of argon and hydrogen were set as 2000 and 150 L/h, respectively. The working current and voltage were 610 A and 42 V, respectively. The surface temperature of the samples was recorded by an infrared thermometer. The variations in the weight and thickness of the samples after the ablation tests were recorded by an electronic balance (±0.0001 g) and a micrometer screw (±0.001 mm), respectively. The mass and linear ablation rates can be calculated by Eqs. (1) and (2), respectively:

(1) Rm=Δmt=m0−m1t

(2) Rl=Δlt=l0−l1t

where *R*m is the mass ablation rate (MAR) (mg/s); *m*0 and *m*1 are the masses of the samples before and after ablation (mg), respectively; *R*l is the linear ablation rate (LAR) (μm/s); *l*0 and *l*1 are the thicknesses of the samples before and after ablation (μm) , respectively; *t* is the ablation time (s).

### 2.3 Characterizations

The as-synthesized precursors were characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscope (FTIR; Nicolet iS50, Thermo Scientific, USA). To study the polymer-to-ceramic process of the precursors, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) coupled with a quadrupole mass spectrometer (MS; TGA/STA8000-GCMS-ATD, PerkinElmer, USA) was performed under a flowing helium atmosphere with a heating rate of 10 °C/min from room temperature to 900 °C. The as-prepared ceramics were investigated by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), elemental analysis, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Smartlab SE, Rigaku, Japan) was used in a transmission mode with Cu Kα1 radiation. For elemental analysis, the carbon contents of the sintered ceramics were measured via hot gas extraction through a LECO C-200 analyzer (LECO Corporation, USA). Ti, Zr, and Hf contents of the sintered samples were analyzed using inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectrometer (ICP‒AES; iCap 6500 device, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Si content was calculated from the difference. Raman spectra were recorded using a spectrometer (LabRAM Aramis, Horiba JobinYvon, France) with a laser wavelength of 532 nm. The microstructure of the ceramics was analyzed by TEM (Talos, F200X, Czech Republic) equipped with an energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) detector. The surface morphologies and cross sections of the monoliths before and after ablation tests were investigated by the scanning electron microscope (SEM; CLARA, TESCAN, Czech Republic), and the element distributions were examined via EDS. The thermal conductivities of the ceramic nanocomposites were measured by a laser-flash apparatus (LFA427, Netzsch, Germany) from room temperature to 1200 °C (25, 200, 400, 600, 800, 1000, and 1200 °C). The thermal conductivity sample was a 10 mm × 10 mm block.

### 3.1 Synthesis of the Ti–Zr–Hf–VHPCS SSPs

The FTIR spectra of pure VHPCS and the as-synthesized Ti–Zr–Hf–VHPCS precursors are shown in Fig. 1. The characteristic absorption bands of VHPCS are C–H (3075 cm−1), C=C–H (3052 cm−1), CH3 (2965 and 2872 cm−1), =CH2 (2920 cm−1), Si–H (2115 cm−1), C=C (1600 cm−1), Si–CH2–Si (1355 cm−1), and Si–C (in Si–CH3, 747 cm−1) [24−26]. According to our previous work [24,25], the newly formed absorption bands on the SSPs at 816 cm−1 (N–Si on Ti/Zr/Hf–N–Si units) [27], 2788 and 1450 cm−1 (NC–H on the NCH3 groups), and 1287 and 1178 cm−1 (C–N on the NCH3 groups) suggest that VHPCS can react with TDMAT, TDMAZ, and TDMAH to synthesize Ti–Zr–Hf–VHPCS SSPs, which agrees well with our previous results based on the reaction between SMP10 and Hf/Ta-amides [25]. In addition, the gradual decrease in the absorption band of Si–H and increase in the CH3 absorption band further proved that the reactions occurred. Furthermore, the disappearance of the absorption bands of the vinyl groups and the decrease in Si–H vibrations confirmed the occurrence of the hydrosilylation reaction [24,28,29]. ### 3.2 Polymer-to-ceramic

The polymer-to-ceramic transformation of the precursors was studied by thermogravimetric (TG) combined with quadrupole MS. The TG curves show that the ceramic yields of the T1Z1H1, T2Z3H5, T2Z3H3, and T1Z2H1 precursors are 72.1%, 76.7%, 75.8%, and 71.0%, respectively (Fig. 3). The addition of Ti, Zr, and Hf amides greatly improved the ceramic yield of the metal-containing precursors (the ceramic yield of the VHPCSs was 65.2%). This is because the incorporation of TDMAT, TDMAZ, and TDMAH further increases the crosslinking degree of the SSPs by forming a more complicated network. Similar behavior has also been found in Zr-modified Durazane1800 in Refs. [30,31]. For the Ti–Zr–Hf–VHPCS SSPs, the DTG curves indicated that the polymer-to-ceramic process includes four main steps. Combined with the MS curves (Fig. 4), the results can be analyzed as follows. The first step was from room temperature to 150 °C. In this stage, 2, NH3, CH4, C3H8, C4H10, and amines. H2 originates from the dehydrocoupling of the Si–H bonds of VHPCSs [24,32,33]. NH3 and amines are released from the transamination reaction [24,30]. The alkanes are produced through the reaction between the N–CH3 groups of metal amides and the Si–H of VHPCSs [24]. The second step is from 150 to 300 °C. A small mass loss of *ca.* 6 wt% was produced from the release of H2, NH3, alkanes, and amines. The three steps ranged from 300 to 800 °C. A large mass loss (*ca.* 17 wt%) was observed at approximately 450 °C. This contributed to the further reaction between the Si–H groups of VHPCS and the amide groups by replacing the CH3 groups, organic substituents, and transamination process [24,31]. The polymer-to-ceramic process was nearly complete at approximately 800 °C (i.e., beyond this temperature, no significant mass loss was found), leading to a ceramic yield of *ca.* 71–77 wt%.

#### 3.3.1 Phase composition and evolution

To investigate the phase composition and evolution of the SiTiZrHfC-based ceramics, the XRD patterns of the ceramics after pyrolysis and annealing are shown in Fig. 5. All the as-pyrolyzed samples (TZHC-1100) are X-ray amorphous. With increasing annealing temperature, both the (Ti,Zr,Hf)C and β-SiC phases start to crystallize. When annealing at 1400 °C, only broad (Ti,Zr,Hf)C peaks appear, whereas when annealing at 1500 °C, (Ti,Zr,Hf)C peaks are found with β-SiC peaks. Unlike VHPCS, which retains obvious peaks even after pyrolysis at 1100 °C, the β-SiC peaks become progressively sharper with increasing annealing temperature. Annealing at 1600 °C results in well-crystallized (Ti,Zr, Hf)C and β-SiC phases. These results are in agreement with those of previously reported SiC/Hf*y*Ta1−*y*C*x*N1−*x*/C [25] and SiC/Ti0.25Zr0.25Hf0.25Ta0.25C ceramic nanocomposites [34].

The XRD Rietveld refinement patterns of the TZH-1600 samples are shown in Fig. 6, and detailed information on the two phases is presented in Table 2. The phase compositions and lattice parameters of (Ti,Zr,Hf)C and β-SiC in TZH-1600 were obtained upon Rietveld refinement of the powder XRD patterns using Fullprof Suite software (version 8.00) [35]. The pseudo-Voigt function is selected as the peak shape model for XRD refinement [36]. The grain sizes were calculated by the Scherrer equation ( D=Kλ/(βsinθ), where  D is the mean grain size;  K is the shape factor;  λ is the X-ray wavelength;  β is the line broadening at half the maximum intensity (FWHM);  θ is the diffraction angle) [37]. The estimated mean grain sizes of both (Ti,Zr,Hf)C and β-SiC within TZH-1600 are less than 100 nm. The weight fractions of (Ti,Zr,Hf)C in the 1600 °C annealed samples are ca.* 60 wt%, indicating that the phase composition can be conveniently tuned by adjusting the amount of the transitional metal amido complexes in the feed for precursor synthesis.

#### 3.3.2 Microstructural evolution

Figure 7 shows the typical microstructures of the pyrolyzed TZH-1100 powders, which were characterized by TEM. The T1Z1H1-1100, T2Z3H5-1100, T2Z3H3-1100, and T1Z2H1-1100 samples are all amorphous powders, which is consistent with the XRD results for amorphous X-rays. The microstructures of all annealed TZH-1600 powder samples were also studied by TEM (Fig. 8). The micrographs show the homogeneously mixed β-SiC and (Ti,Zr,Hf)C grains. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) was used to characterize the two phases. In the TZH-1600 annealed samples, the (Ti,Zr,Hf)C grains wrapped by an amorphous disordered carbon layer formed a (Ti,Zr,Hf)C@C core–shell structure. The carbon layer acts as a diffusion barrier to prevent grain growth of the nanoparticles during the high-temperature process. Apart from (Ti,Zr,Hf)C and β-SiC, some free carbon ribbons can be found connected to (Ti,Zr,Hf)C@C particles in the TEM images (Fig. 8(a)), which is very common in the field of polymer-derived ceramics [38]. The statistics of the grain size of the (Ti,Zr,Hf)C nanoparticles confirm a Gaussian distribution. The average grain sizes of (Ti,Zr,Hf)C in T1Z1H1-1600, T2Z3H5-1600, T2Z3H3-1600, and T1Z2H1-1600 are 23.7, 24.1, 24.0, and 23.8 nm, respectively (Figs. 8(b), 8(d), 8(f), and 8(h)), which is consistent with the results calculated from the XRD data via the Scherrer equation. This proves that ceramic nanocomposites can be formed through the SSP method.

### 3.4 Ablation behavior

To investigate the ablation resistance and ablation mechanism of the (Ti,Zr,Hf)C/SiC ceramic nanocomposites, dense monoliths (i.e., T1Z1H1-SPS, T2Z3H5-SPS, T2Z3H3-SPS, and T1Z2H1-SPS) were tested on an air-plasma device, and the ablated samples were denoted as A-T1Z1H1, A-T2Z3H5, A-T2Z3H3, and A-T1Z2H1, respectively. Photographs of the ceramics before and after ablation tests are shown in Fig. 11(a). The color and surface morphology of the ablated samples obviously changed after the high-temperature ablation test. However, no ablation pits formed on the ablated samples, suggesting improved ablation resistance. In particular, the A-T2Z3H5 monolith clearly has a white and continuous oxide layer that is tightly bound to the substrate, indicating its remarkable resistance to plasma erosion. The ablation curves show that the maximum surface temperature of all the samples during ablation is approximately 2200 °C (Fig. 11(b)). The different ablation curves indicate different thermal responses to the same heat flux. Zhang *et* *al*. [43] reported that the thermal conductivity, volumetric heat capacity, catalytic efficiency, emissivity, and oxidation characteristics play important roles in the thermal response of the samples. The thermal conductivity and volumetric heat capacity are essential to the rate of increase in the surface temperature and temperature gradient during the primary stage. Taking T1Z1H1-SPS and T2Z3H5-SPS as examples, T2Z3H5-SPS exhibits higher thermal conductivity than that of the T1Z1H1–SPS (particularly at high temperatures, Fig. 12), leading to a high energy storage rate during the initial ablation process. Consequently, the ablation curve of T2Z3H5-SPS experiences a lower rate of temperature increase than that of T1Z1H1-SPS. Therefore, T2Z3H5-SPS requires more time to reach its final stable state. Furthermore, the high thermal conductivity of the T2Z3H5-SPS ceramic nanocomposites also means a smaller temperature gradient inside the sample. The stress difference caused by the temperature gradient will also be smaller. This helps to reduce the thermal stresses due to increasing and decreasing temperatures, which in turn helps to improve the thermal shock resistance.

T2Z3H3-SPS (SiTi0.050Zr0.076Hf0.072C1.261) and T1Z2H1-SPS (SiTi0.062Zr0.121Hf0.067C1.258) monolithic materials have the same Ti and Zr+Hf contents and show similar ablation properties. Moreover, different ablation regions can also be observed on the surfaces of the ablated samples.

Figure 14 shows SEM images of the ablation center, which experiences the strongest heat flux during the ablation process. XRD analysis (Fig. 11(d)) revealed light and solid (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 oxide particles and surrounding (Zr,Hf)TiO4 and glassy SiO2 on the center of the ablated surface of A-T1Z1H1 (Figs. 14(a)–14(c)) (the melting points of SiO2, ZrTiO4, and HfTiO4 are 1720 °C [51], 1760 °C [52], and 1980 °C [53], respectively). However, the bare substrates without oxides are still visible (white dotted boxes in Fig. 14(a)), which can be verified by the strong signals of the bulk materials (i.e., (Ti,Zr,Hf) C and β-SiC) on the surface of the A-T1Z1H1 sample. Moreover, the unfilled areas in Fig. 14(b) are quite clear. This might be due to the newly formed unstable oxide layer, which has been blown away by the high-speed airflow or evaporated at ultrahigh temperatures, resulting in reduced ablation performance.

The center region of A-T2Z3H5 is smooth and consists of molten phases and uniformly dispersed white (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 particles. These mixed oxides possess a suitable viscosity that can completely fill the ablation center area, leaving only the holes left by the escape of gaseous products. Low-magnification SEM images of the ablation center areas of the four samples show that the (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 particles in A-T2Z3H5 are smaller in size and there is almost no aggregation (Figs. 14(a), 14(d), 14(g), and 14(j)). It is well known that the presence of high-melting-point (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 oxides in molten phases increases the viscosity of the oxide layer and hinders the diffusion of oxygen atoms (the so-called pinning effect) [15,54]. The uniform distribution of (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 particles in molten phases is attributed to the high content of (Zr+Hf) and the low content of Ti, preventing the agglomeration of (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 due to the low diffusion coefficient of Zr and Hf [55].

The ablation centers of A-T2Z3H3 and A-T1Z2H1 are relatively smooth and dominated by molten phases and white oxides but show more (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 agglomeration (red dotted circles in Figs. 14(h) and 14(k)) than those of A-T2Z3H5, especially in A-T1Z2H1 (Figs. 14(j)–14(l)). Additionally, there are bare areas resulting from incomplete filling with glassy phases (yellow dotted circles in Figs. 14(g) and 14(j)). Holes are also generated due to the escape of gases. These two samples have higher Ti contents and lower (Zr+Hf) contents, while the diffusion coefficient of Ti is relatively higher. As a result, at this extremely high temperature, a large amount of (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 aggregation occurs, accelerating the volatilization of SiO2 and reducing the ablation properties.

Interestingly, white spherical oxides can be observed in the high-magnification SEM images of the 4 ablated samples (Figs. 14(c), 14(f), 14(i), and 14(l)). The size of the spherical oxides indicates that the diameter of the spheres on the A-T2Z3H5 surface is smaller, which further illustrates the effect of the elemental diffusion coefficients on the oxides. Furthermore, the EDS results show that the molar ratios of the transitional metal elements (Ti : Zr : Hf) in the four white oxides are nearly equal to 1 : 1 : 1, 2 : 3 : 5, 2 : 3 : 3, and 1 : 2 : 1. This indicates that the composition of the initial carbide ceramics largely affects the composition of the generated oxides. During the cooling process, a significant number of spherical oxides precipitate from the melt as the solubility of the glassy melt decreases. A similar phenomenon was also observed during the ablation of Cf/(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C–SiC ceramic matrix composites, in which spherical high-entropy oxides formed during high-temperature oxidation [56,57]. Additionally, spherical oxides with varying sizes can be observed on the ablation surfaces. The small oxide particles gradually aggregate into large oxide particles with similar compositions. The driving force for small oxide growth can be analyzed from two perspectives: (1) Thermodynamics: The system tends to reach a stable state with the lowest energy, so small particles tend to diffuse toward larger particles to reduce the overall surface energy [58]. (2) Diffusion: Due to the presence of liquid phase concentration gradients around small and large particles, small particle oxides diffuse into larger particles [56].

As the temperature in the ablation transition region is still high, it retains characteristics similar to those of the ablation center (Fig. 15). There are still many unfilled oxide areas on the surface of the A-T1Z1H1 transition region because of the instability or poor adhesion of the oxides. However, the transition region of A-T2Z3H5 is composed of a continuous, well-filled glassy phase and uniformly distributed small oxide particles despite some holes remaining after gas escape. The stable oxide layer of A-T2Z3H5 is beneficial for resisting mechanical erosion and hindering the internal diffusion of oxygen atoms. The morphologies of the transition regions of ablated A-T2Z3H3 and A-T1Z2H1 are similar. The oxide layer is composed of a glassy phase and aggregated oxides, which have poor fluidity, leading to the remaining incompletely filled areas. Therefore, weaker oxide layers cannot provide effective protection for bulk materials.

The pinning effect of the uniformly distributed (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 nano/microparticles within the glassy phases significantly enhances the viscosity and stability of the oxide layer. More importantly, the microstructure of the (Ti,Zr,Hf)C/SiC ceramic nanocomposites consists of (Ti,Zr,Hf)C nanoparticles distributed within the β-SiC matrix. According to percolation threshold theory [60], when the particle size of the dispersive phase (i.e., (Ti,Zr,Hf)C) is on the nanoscale (particularly less than 50 nm), the percolation threshold of the (Ti,Zr,Hf)C nanoparticles within the SiC matrix is significantly reduced. Under these conditions, a small amount of the (Ti,Zr,Hf)C phase will be sufficient to form a continuous UHTC network within the matrix, which will facilitate the formation of a dense oxide layer with continuous (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 on the surface of the ablated samples. Considering ablation kinetics, although the oxide particles will aggregate over time, the homogeneously distributed (Ti,Zr,Hf)C nanoparticles can slow this process during ablation. In addition, the nanoscale uniformity of the (Ti,Zr,Hf)C phase within the SiC matrix can reduce the effects of volume expansion caused by oxidation of the (Ti,Zr,Hf)C phase (i.e., the formation of cracks), which is good for improving the oxidation/ablation resistance and thermal shock resistance of the composites. Therefore, the ablation resistance of A-T2Z3H5 is greatly improved by the use of uniformly distributed nano/micro(Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 particles within the molten phases. These results clearly demonstrate that the proportion of transition metal elements within the UHTC phase can effectively affect the ablation performance of ultrahigh-temperature ceramic nanocomposites by influencing the composition and structure of the oxide layer.

### 3.5 Ablation mechanism

Based on the characterization and analysis, the ablation mechanisms of the (Ti,Zr,Hf)C/SiC composite are summarized in Fig. 19. Under the combined conditions of heat flux, severe oxidation, and mechanical scouring, a dense oxide layer forms on the surface of the (Ti,Zr,Hf)C/SiC ceramic nanocomposites. The synergistic effect of various metal elements and Si contributed to the unique oxide layer composed of a high-melting-point (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 oxide skeleton and filling liquid phases, including (Zr,Hf)TiO4 and glassy SiO2. The varying proportions of metal atoms in the initial carbides gave rise to the formation of (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 oxides with distinct metal atom concentrations. The A-T2Z3H5 sample with a higher content of (Zr+Hf) and lower content of Ti exhibited a different oxide layer structure in the ablation center. This structure arises from the presence of a reduced amount of Ti with a higher diffusion coefficient and an increased proportion of (Zr+Hf) with a lower diffusion coefficient. Consequently, the (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 nano/microparticles are uniformly dispersed throughout the molten phase, preventing any significant agglomeration of oxide particles. As shown in Fig. 19, the oxide layer structure of A-T2Z3H5 differs significantly from that of the other three samples (i.e., A-T1Z1H1, A-T2Z3H3, and A-T1Z2H1), where oxides appear highly agglomerated, leading to a nonuniform distribution of (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 oxides within the glassy phases. Notably, the (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 nano/microparticles can also adjust the viscosity of the glassy phase through a pinning effect. The optimization of the oxide composition, along with the formation of oxides exhibiting varying melting points, has resulted in a unique and highly beneficial oxide layer structure. This structure effectively shields the material from the invasion of oxygen atoms and enhances mechanical scouring, ultimately contributing to the excellent ablation resistance of A-T2Z3H5.

## 4 Conclusions

Dense monolithic (Ti,Zr,Hf)C/SiC ceramic nanocomposites were successfully synthesized through the pyrolysis of novel Ti-, Zr-, and Hf-containing silicon-based precursors, followed by SPS. The addition of Ti-, Zr-, and Hf-amides greatly improved the ceramic yield (at approximately 71%–75%) of the SSPs by increasing their crosslinking degree. The (Ti,Zr,Hf)C/SiC ceramic nanocomposites were characterized by a unique microstructure with *in* *situ*-generated (Ti,Zr,Hf)@C core–shell nanoparticles homogeneously mixed with β-SiC.

The ablation tests revealed that the atomic ratio of transition metal elements within the (Ti,Zr,Hf)C/SiC nanocomposites plays a crucial role in determining their ablation resistance. Notably, the (Ti0.2Zr0.3Hf0.5)C/SiC (T2Z3H5-SPS) nanocomposite exhibited the lowest linear and mass ablation rates, reaching −0.58 μm/s and −0.13 mg/s, respectively. These exceptional results suggest nonablative characteristics, making it a standout thermal protection material. The superior ablation performance is primarily attributed to the formation of a protective oxide layer, which comprises glassy SiO2 and uniformly dispersed (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 oxides. The nano/microparticles of (Ti,Zr,Hf)O2 exhibit a pinning effect that significantly adjusts the viscosity and stability of the oxide layers. This, in turn, hinders the diffusion of oxygen atoms, further bolstering the ablation resistance of the nanocomposites. In conclusion, it is feasible to tailor the atomic ratio of transition metal elements within the (Ti,Zr,Hf)C phase of (Ti,Zr,Hf)C/SiC ceramic nanocomposites and, therefore, optimize their ablation resistance through the molecular design of single-source precursors, which offers a promising approach for preparing ultrahigh-temperature ceramic nanocomposites with excellent ablation resistance.
