## 1 Introduction

Silicon carbide (SiC) ceramics are excellent structural and functional integrated ceramic materials with superior properties such as high-temperature resistance, wear resistance, chemical corrosion resistance, high thermal conductivity, and good thermal stability [1−5]. It is the preferred choice for high thrust-to-weight ratio aviation engine hot-end structural components [6]. The synthesis of SiC ceramics requires a high temperature of 500–2100 ℃ [7,8]. Due to the low thermal diffusion coefficient of SiC and the presence of free silicon, it is difficult for SiC to be sintered and densified in an ultra-high temperature environment without applying any axial pressure and sintering additives [9,10]. Traditional sintering methods typically require prolonged timeframes and substantial energy consumption for ceramic preparation which hampers rapid fabrication of SiC [11−13]. Therefore, SiC must be prepared in a short cycle and low-cost manner.

The sintering process of ceramics involves two fundamental processes, namely densification and grain growth. These processes share the same thermodynamic driving force and compete with each other, but can be distinguished by adjusting sintering conditions and exploiting their distinct kinetics [14]. Therefore, achieving densification and inhibiting grain growth are essential for the sintering of ceramics. The control over ceramic grain sizes can be attained through strategies such as reducing sintering temperature, incorporating additives, enhancing green bodies’ density, and employing unique sintering processes [15]. With the deepening understanding of the sintering mechanism and the continuous development of sintering equipment, various unique and new sintering methods are constantly emerging, and some sintering systems have shown sound inhibitory effects on grain growth. Zawrah and Shaw [16] selected sintering additives composed of Al2O3, Y2O3, and CaO, and SiC ceramics with density of over 95% obtained by hot pressing (HP) at 1800 ℃. Based on HP, silicon nitride (Si3N4) ceramics with interlocking structures were prepared by Zhou *et al*. [17] through spark plasma sintering (SPS). The local spark plasma between ceramic particles is generated through high energy and low voltage pulse currents, and high-density ceramics are obtained under an uniaxial pressure [18,19]. However, HP and SPS are both external pressure assistive technology and external pressure easily damages materials that cannot be applied with pressure.

Flash sintering (FS) involves rapidly heating a ceramic green body while applying an electric current [20−23]. The longer densification cycle of ceramics at high temperatures can be significantly shortened through FS, and the furnace temperature required for FS is also much lower than the traditional sintering temperature of ceramic samples [24]. Due to the ultra-fast heating rate and extremely short sintering time (usually less than 1 min) of FS, the grain growth is effectively suppressed during the sintering process. The FS process does not need to use pressure, unique atmosphere, or mould and has been applied to many oxide ceramic composites with ionic or electronic conductivity, such as YSZ, ZnO, and SrTiO3 [25−27]. Most oxide ceramics exhibit negative temperature coefficient (NTC) characteristics, which gradually decrease their resistance value with increasing preset temperatures. During FS, the furnace body can provide a preset temperature atmosphere, and the sample resistance is reduced through the preset atmosphere, allowing for the flow of current, thereby producing a “flash phenomenon” [28]. SiC is a semiconductor with a large band gap and is affected by the high Schottky barrier at the ceramic grain boundary [29,30]. The electrical properties of SiC as a semiconductor or even an insulator do not meet the requirements of FS [31–34]. Al2O3 and Y2O3 used as FS aids for SiC can locally approach the theoretical density under the assistance of an electric field of 100 V/cm [28]. SiC powders containing B and C as sintering aids achieved flash sintering densification within 16 min in a Al2O3 tube furnace at 1500 ℃ [35]. Therefore, improving the conductivity of the SiC green body, reducing Joule heat loss caused by excessive local contact resistance during the FS process, and further exploring the conductivity mechanism during the FS process have important guiding significance for expanding its application in FS.

In this paper, the green powders were prepared by mechanical mixing and chemical coating modification methods. SiC green bodies with different electrical resistivity were successfully designed through molding and pre-pyrolysis. The pyrolysis carbon (PyC) “bridges” were constructed by carbonizing phenolic resin (PR), forming a uniform conductive pathway between SiC particles. Under an electric field intensity of 80 V/mm, FS of SiC was achieved within 60 s. The physical and mechanical properties of the SiC green bodies with different resistivity after FS were studied. The relationship between the temperature and the resistivity of SiC during flash sintering was discussed in detail, based on the phase evolution at instantaneous high temperatures. FS of the SiC green body with the specific conductivity is expected to be applied to regenerate and repair damaged substrates in SiC ceramic matrix composites.

### 2.1 Materials

Commercially available submicron β-SiC (*d*50 ≈ 5 μm, ≥ 99% pure, Guangzhou Metal Metallurgy Co., Ltd., China), and PR (Shanghai Tiao Chemical Co., Ltd., China) powders were used as raw materials; 1 wt% boron carbides (BC4; ~1 μm, ≥ 98% pure, Guangzhou Metal Metallurgy Co., Ltd., China) were used as sintering aids; 1.5 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA; Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.) were used as the binder.

### 2.2 Preparation of green body

The mixed powders were made from premix, consisting of β-SiC, PR (0, 6.25, 12.5, or 25 wt%), sintering additives (1 wt% BC4), and binder (1 wt% PVA). The mixed powders were firstly ball-milled using ethanol as a solvent and SiC as a medium in a polypropylene jar for 24 h. Subsequently, the slurry was dried for 12 h at 80 ℃, then ground, and sieved in a mortar (60 mesh). In a dry environment, the sieved composite powders were pressed into a cylindrical green body (height: ~10–15 mm, diameter: ~0.5±0.03 mm) under 0.8 MPa for 180 s. Green bodies were pretreated for 1 h under the protection of 800 ℃ N2 atmosphere to ensure complete pyrolysis of PR into pyrolytic carbon (PyC). The temperature schedule was shown as the following: firstly heated to 400 ℃ at 5 ℃/min, then increased to 800 ℃ at 2 ℃/min, and kept for 1 h. The PyC content was calculated based on TG shown in Fig. 2(g). The samples treated with preheating were labelled as PyC*x*–SiC (*x* = 0, 2.5, 5.0, 10.0).

### 2.3 Flash sintering apparatus and process

The device shown in Fig. 1 was used to achieve FS. The sample was provided with preheating and inert gas protection environment through an improved quartz tube furnace (TL 1200, Nanjing Boyuntong Instrument, China) with upper and lower window ends. Firstly, the green body was placed on a conductive platinum sheet. A 0.5 mm thick graphite paper was placed between the green body and the conductive platinum sheet to ensure sufficient contact between the sample and the electrode. Then, the other end of the conductive platinum sheet was fixed above the sample. The electrode plate and the sample were ensured to have sufficient electrical contact through the self-gravity of the upper electrode plate and the pressure exerted by the hexagonal bolt. The applied pressure was about 0.1 MPa, much smaller than auxiliary pressure sintering methods such as hot pressing sintering (> 10 MPa). The two ends of the sample were coated with conductive silver paste (01L-2211D, Guangzhou Metal Metallurgy Co., Ltd., China) to reduce the contact resistance between the green body and the electrode. The electrical connection between the electrode and the DC power supply was made using a molybdenum rod. The final power supply, molybdenum rod, and platinum electrode formed a conductive closed circuit.

The preheating rate of a preheating flash furnace (FSL 1200, Nanjing Boyuntong Instrument, China) used in all experiments was 5 ℃/min. To carbonise PR into PyC “bridges”, the sample was first isothermal maintained at 800 ℃ in flowing N2 (100 mL/min) for 2 h. To ensure an inert sintering atmosphere, the gas supply was switched from N2 to He for 30 min before the electric field was applied across the sample (the same flow rate). He suppressed the plasma formation upon applications of the electric field, which occurred in some conditions when N2 or Ar was used [35]. After injecting He for 30 min, the sample was subjected to a constant electric field of ~30 V/mm, and the maximum current was set to 2 A. When the current quickly reached 2 A after the “flash point”, the power supplied automatically switched from the constant voltage to the constant current mode and was disconnected at 60 s. The sample after FS was named FS/PyC–SiC.

### 2.4 Characterizations

A focused ion beam scanning electron double-beam electron microscope (FIB-SEM; LYRA3 GMU, TESCAN, Czech Republic) was employed to observe the composite powders’ morphology and sample cross-sections after FS. The TG test of PR was measured by a differential scanning calorimeter laser (STA 449F3, NETZSCH, Germany) under an N2 atmosphere with a heating rate of 20 ℃/min. The samples’ bulk density and apparent porosity were tested by Archimedes drainage method. A multifunctional powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Cu Kα radiation, Empyrean, the Netherlands) and a laser Raman spectrometer (Raman 532 nm, LabRAM HR Evolution, France) completed the phase composition analysis. Three equally spaced Vickers hardness indentations were made near the centre of the samples using a load of 5 kgf. The samples were reground sufficiently to remove the first row of indents and polished, and indents were made a second time for a total of six indents per specimen. The DC power supply (DSP1000-15A, Yangzhou Dinghua Electronics Co., Ltd., China) was used as the power supply for preheating FS equipment. The voltage was adjustable at 0–1000 V, accurately displayed to 1 V, and the current was adjustable at 0–15 A, accurately displayed to 0.1 A. A resistivity tester (ST2253y, Suzhou Lattice Electronics Co., Ltd., China) was used to test the conductivity of the sample, using a DC high resistance meter to measure the resistance (*R*) of the sample at a DC voltage of 10 V.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figures 2(a)–2(d) show SEM images of green bodies with different mass fractions of PR mixed with SiC powders after the carbonisation. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the bonding between SiC particles was relatively loose, with numerous and unevenly distributed pores, and there was a local particle aggregation phenomenon when no PR was added to the blank. The number of pores between SiC particles was generated by the force exerted by gas escape during the high-temperature decomposition of the PVA binder on the ceramic particles, and the pore size was relatively large. SiC powders in PyC0–SiC exhibited irregular shapes, such as diamond and flake, resulting in anisotropic contact between particles. There were various contact situations between SiC particles, such as point-to-point, point-to-point, edge-to-point, and face-to-face [36]. When the additional amount of PyC was 2.5 wt%, the number of pores in PyC2.5–SiC decreased, and their distribution became uniform after pyrolysis (as shown in Fig. 2(b)). During the carbonisation process, the chemical bonds of PR were interrupted and reorganized to generate PyC. The carbon residue rate of PR was about 40 wt%, as shown in the TG curve of PR under the N2 atmosphere in Fig. 2(g). The SiC particles were connected through the PyC “bridges”, and the surface PyC layer was rough and wavy (as shown in Fig. 2(b1)). As shown in Fig. 2(c), when the PR content increases to 5 wt%, excessive PyC overflows between the pores and the surface of SiC particles. In Fig. 2(d), the SiC particles covered by PyC exhibit an agglomeration phenomenon, which may be due to the high PR content during the carbonisation process, resulting in the formation of excessive PyC. Figure 2(e) shows the XRD pattern of PyC–SiC obtained by carbonising green bodies with the PR content of 0–10 wt% at 800 ℃. After the carbonisation, all green bodies added with PR were all at 2*θ* = 23.3° broad diffraction peak, indicating that the generated PyC structure was disordered. The diffraction peak at 2*θ* = 23.3° was similar to graphite’s C (002) peak. As the PyC content increases, the intensity of the carbon peak gradually increased, and the degree of graphitisation increased. Five peaks in the XRD pattern around 2*θ* = 36.1°, 41.5°, 60.5°, 72.1°, and 75.5° represented the diffraction of (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) crystal planes of β-SiC, respectively. The bulk density of PyC2.5–SiC decreased from 1.5 to 1.42 g/cm3 (PyC10.0–SiC), and the open porosity increased from 46.34% (PyC2.5–SiC) to 54.33% (PyC10.0–SiC). After coating SiC particles with PyC, the gaps between them were filled through carhonized PR, and highly conductive interface “bridges” composed of PyC were constructed. As shown in Fig. 2(f), the density of PyC–SiC reached its maximum value of 1.54 g/cm3 at *x* = 5, which was similar to that at *x* = 2.5. The porosity of PyC–SiC showed a trend of first decreasing and then increasing with the increase of the PyC content. During the pyrolysis process of PR, small molecule products such as H2O, CH4, H2, CO, and CO2 were released. More pores and cracks were generated during the excessive PR pyrolysis process, as shown in Fig. S2 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

The conductivity of pure PyC powders, pure SiC powders, and PyC–SiC powders under different pressures is shown in Fig. 3(a). The unique double Schottky barrier structure at the grain boundaries of SiC resulted in lower conductivity [37]. The conductivity of pure SiC powders under 28 MPa compression was 5.53×10−5 S/cm, and pure PyC was 5.66 S/cm, 100 thousand times higher than that of pure SiC. The conductivity of PyC0–SiC was 1.05×10−3 S/cm, which the residual carbon may have caused after the pyrolysis of PVA adhesives were added during the green body preparation. After introducing 2.5 wt% PR, the conductivity of PyC2.5–SiC after the carbonisation was 0.1026 S/cm, which was 97 times higher that of PyC0–SiC. According to the composite law of composite materials, an increase in the content of low-conductive components leads to a decrease in the conductivity of the composite materials [38]. On the other hand, as the carbon content increased, the density of PyC–SiC was reduced, the pores were increased, and the number of interfaces between SiC and PyC was increased. The collision between the pores and the phase interfaces in the materials, as well as the free electrons in SiC, results in an increase in the electron motion resistance, an increase in the material resistance, and a decrease in the PyC–SiC conductivity. The initial electric field of 80 V/mm was applied to the PyC–SiC sample at 600 ℃, and the changes in current density, electric field strength, and power density overtime during the FS process are shown in Figs. 3(b), 3(c), and 3(d), respectively. When the conductivity of PyC–SiC changed from 0.1026 to 0.0619 S/cm, the incubation time before FS increased from 10 to 60 s. The current density and power density increased dramatically to ~90 mA/mm2 and ~2950 mW/mm3, respectively. At the same time, the electric field strength rapidly drops to ~25 V/mm. Under the initial electric field, FS entered the incubation stage (light blue area), and Joule heat gradually occurred between the particles in the green body. When the “flash point” appeared (light purple region), a large amount of Joule heat was generated, promoting the formation and rapid growth of the neck between SiC particles in the green body. The green body transitioned from being a semiconductor to a conductor, resulting in a gradual decrease in the overall resistivity of the sample. As shown in Figs. 3(b) and 3(c), the electric field strength slowly rebounded, and the current density grew as the current approached the upper limit value. A large amount of current passed through the pyrolysis carbon “bridges” between SiC particles, which destroyed the carbon structure and slowed the increase in the sample electrical conductivity.

Figure 4 shows micrographs of the fracture surface in different regions of the samples sintered by FS in the direct current (DC) mode at an electric field of 25 V/mm and maximum current density of 95 mA/mm². As shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM, the central microstructure of FS/PyC−SiC was markedly lower in porosity compared to the edges, and the particle size was relatively large (~13.8 μm verse 8.50 μm as shown in Table 1). The heat loss on the surface of the sample was caused by the temperature difference between the sample and the environment, while the temperature difference between the central and the edge areas of the sample results in varying particle sizes. As shown in Fig. 4(a), FS/PyC0−SiC without conductive PyC “bridges” exhibited high contact resistance due to direct contact between SiC particles, resulting in the formation of numerous “hot spots” among the particles. Additionally, the instantaneous sintering temperature inside the sample was higher than outside, resulting in differences. The central area of the PyC0−SiC cross section showed that the shape and size of SiC particles gradually became uniform, and a clear sintered neck connection structure between ceramic particles was observed. Figure 4(a1) shows that the surface had yet to be sintered, which is the same as Fig. 2(a). In Figs. 4(b)−4(d), the presence of PyC prevented the phenomenon of local current concentration and breakdown of the sample, resulting in cracks during FS due to differences in conductivity. In FS/PyC2.5–SiC, phenomena such as smaller particle size, uneven cross section, and traces of particle tearing were observed in Figs. 4(b) and 4(b1). The fracture mode of FS/PyC2.5–SiC was mainly transgranular fracture accompanied by the intergranular fracture, and SiC ceramics had the highest densification (with open porosity of 14.79%). With the increase of the PyC content, particles in FS/PyC5.0–SiC and FS/PyC10.0–SiC exhibited aggregation phenomenon. Excessive residual amorphous carbon resulted in the formation of pores and cracks (Figs. 4(c) and 4(d)). The low contact resistance between particles was not conducive to the instantaneous densification and sintering of the materials.

Figure 5(a) shows XRD spectra of FS/PyC2.5–SiC under different FS time. The samples after FS had all formed complete crystal forms, but the degree of crystallisation varies. After 10 s of FS, the characteristic peak of 6H–SiC was found at 2θ* = 34.3°, 35.8°, 38.3°, 41.5°, 60.1°, 73.5°, and 73.7°, which may be due to the transformation of the SiC crystal structure under high temperatures. At ~2100 ℃, β-SiC began to move towards the α-SiC transformation [42]. The characteristic peak of α-SiC gradually strengthens as the FS time progresses to 60 s. At 0 s, the intensity of the diffraction peak intensity of FS/PyC2.5–SiC at 2*θ* = 23° and 45°, and both of them were wide. As the electrification time increased, the two broad peaks became more prominent. They tended to be narrow, indicating the ordering of amorphous PyC and an increase in graphitisation degree. The surface temperature predicted by the blackbody radiation model had been validated through the graphitisation process of amorphous carbon. The surface temperature of the sample was equally verified in the instantaneous ultra-high temperature environment β-SiC gradually transformed into α-SiC. The strongest peak of FS/PyC2.5–SiC after FS for 60 s belonged to α-SiC (4H and 6H). The preheated SiC particles were connected through PyC “bridges” to form the optimal current path (as shown in Fig. 6). The unique double Schottky barrier structure at the SiC grain boundary resulted in a high barrier height and a high band gap width of SiC, further hindering the electron conduction, thereby leading to uneven hot spots between particles. The conductive network between SiC particles was formed by introducing PyC “bridges”, and the current flow path was provided. Joule heat was preferentially generated between PyC through a large amount of current, and the thermal runaway occurred between SiC particles. The interface temperature increased due to Joule heating, and as the charging time increased, the neck formation and rapid growth between SiC particles were promoted. As the power on time increases, the temperature between interfaces is increased through Joule heating. SiO2 on the surface of SiC particles undergoes a carbothermal reduction reaction with free carbon, and some of the free carbon is consumed. At the same time, the free carbon generated by the pyrolysis of PR gradually became ordered after experiencing transient high temperatures, and the conductivity between particles was enhanced, leading to an increase in the densification rate. The SiC particles were filled with excessive PyC and fractured due to the introduction of excessive PyC, interrupting the current pathway.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, the SiC powders were prepared by mechanical mixing and chemical coating modification methods. SiC green bodies with varying electrical resistivity were successfully designed through molding and pre-pyrolysis. The SiC green body with conductivity of 0.1026 S/cm first reached the flash point of FS and completed sintering after being energized for 60 s under electric field strength of 80 V/mm. After switching from constant voltage control to continuous current control, the instantaneous maximum temperature on the surface of SiC samples exceeded 2450 ℃. In the green body, β-SiC was transformed into α-SiC in high-temperature environments. Many current paths are provided for the evolution of amorphous PyC from turbostratic to oriented graphite carbon. The sintering rate of SiC ceramics was increased by generating Joule heat through a large amount of current. The porosity of the SiC sample after FS was reduced to 14.79%, the Vickers hardness was 19.62 GPa, and the compressive strength reached 51.7 MPa. Flash sintering of conductive SiC was expected to be applied in the instantaneous local densification of damaged SiC ceramic matrix composites in the future, laying the foundation for applying ceramic matrix composites in aerospace.
