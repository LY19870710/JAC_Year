## 1 Introduction

SiC-based ceramic matrix composites (CMCs) are promising candidates for hot-section components in next-generation gas turbines, and higher fuel efficiency and greater thrust-to-weight ratios are desirable, necessitating elevated turbine inlet temperatures [1−3]. However, SiC undergoes rapid degradation in combustion gas environments containing water vapor, making environmental barrier coatings (EBCs) essential for protecting the SiC material from such harsh operating conditions [4−6].

A typical EBC system consists of a Si bond coat and a rare-earth (RE) silicate top coat, such as Yb2Si2O7 [7,8]. When oxidants (O2, H2O) diffuse through the top coat and reach the Si bond coat, Si acts as a sacrificial layer, undergoing oxidation. Simultaneously, a thermally grown oxide (TGO) scale, primarily composed of SiO2 [9], forms at the interface between the bond coat and the top coat, slowing down the further diffusion of oxidants towards the substrate. However, when the operating temperature exceeds 1200 °C, SiO2-TGO crystallizes into β-cristobalite. Upon cooling to approximately 220 °C, β-cristobalite transforms into α-cristobalite, leading to a volumetric decrease of ~4.5% [10−12]. Additionally, the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of α-cristobalite (10.3×10−6 °C−1 [13] or 3.0×10−5 °C−1 in Refs. [11,14]) is significantly greater than that of SiC (3.5–5.5×10−6 °C−1 [15,16]), introducing additional thermal stress during cooling. This reversible phase transformation and thermal stress can induce channel (mud) cracks within the TGO scale during thermal cycling, creating additional rapid transmission pathways for oxidants and thereby accelerating TGO growth [17]. Once the TGO scale reaches a critical thickness, typically 4–6 μm [8,13], the coatings may delaminate [10,18]. The thickening and phase transformation of the TGO scale are recognized as the primary failure mechanism of EBCs [19]. Consequently, suppressing the growth, crystallization, and phase transformation of SiO2 within the TGO scale under high-temperature service conditions has become a critical research focus.

To achieve this goal, researchers have investigated the effects of Al doping in the TGO on phase stability and crack propagation behavior. The primary approaches include adding Al2O3 or Al2O3-containing compounds to the top coat (Yb2Si2O7) [8,19], incorporating Al2O3 into Si to form a composite bond coat [20,21], or depositing a thin layer of Al2O3 or mullite on top of the Si layer to create a duplex bond coat [18]. Based on experimental results, all three methods successfully introduced Al elements into the SiO2-based TGO and effectively inhibited the formation of channel cracks at 1316 or 1350 °C, thereby extending the lifetime of the EBCs. The reduction in channel cracks is attributed primarily to the suppressed crystallization of amorphous SiO2 into cristobalite [8,19] and the suppressed β → α cristobalite phase transformation [18]. During thermal cyclic oxidation, Al doping significantly slows down TGO growth [8,19−21], while during isothermal oxidation, it accelerates TGO growth because of the introduction of additional oxygen vacancies compared with the undoped counterpart [18]. Since Al3+ enters the crystalline SiO2 lattice, replacing part of Si4+, additional oxygen vacancies are formed, which enhances oxygen diffusion and accelerates TGO growth [18]. However, since these approaches rely heavily on Si bond coats, whose operating temperature is constrained by the melting point of Si (~1414 °C), no studies have been conducted at temperatures exceeding 1350 °C. Moreover, this limitation significantly restricts the upper-temperature capability of EBCs.

In another approach, researchers have explored incorporating HfO2 into Si to form a composite bond coat [13,22−24] or depositing a thin layer of HfO2 on top of the Si layer to form a duplex bond coat [12], in which SiO2-TGO is dynamically consumed by HfO2, forming HfSiO4 with improved phase stability and better CTE matching with the SiC substrate. As a result, the thickening process of SiO2-TGO is effectively suppressed. However, owing to the low melting point of Si (~1414 °C), these approaches still fail to meet the requirements of next-generation EBCs, which demand higher temperature capabilities of up to 1480 °C [19,25].

To overcome this limitation, high-melting-point metal silicides [26−31] and oxides [25,32−40] have been proposed to replace Si as bond coat materials. Among the high-melting-point silicides, MoSi2 has excellent oxidation resistance and a relatively low CTE among transition metal disilicides [41], making it potentially suitable for use as an EBC bonding material at high temperatures. However, TGO-induced coating failure remains a persistent challenge and has yet to be fully resolved. Therefore, further research on TGOs in steam environments at elevated temperatures (> 1450 °C) is necessary. The development of novel TGOs is expected to inhibit SiO2 crystallization at high temperatures and prevent the β-cristobalite phase from undergoing phase transitions during cooling. In this case, the risk of coating cracking or delamination due to the thickening of the TGOs and phase transformation could be fundamentally mitigated.

In this work, a tri-layered TGO structure consisting of cristobalite, Hf-doped SiO2 glass, and particle-reinforced Hf–Si–O glass is formed *in situ* within a MoSi2/HfO2 duplex EBC system at 1500 °C in an environment containing 90 vol% H2O and 10 vol% O2. After 200 h of steam corrosion at 1500 °C, the average thickness of the tri-layered TGOs increased to 24.5 μm, maintaining considerable adhesion to both the MoSi2 and HfO2 layers. Additionally, this TGO structure effectively inhibits the propagation of channel cracks, providing effective protection for the underlying SiC substrate without corrosion. Moreover, owing to the high melting point of MoSi2 (~2030 °C) and its comparable oxidation resistance to that of the Si layer, the operation temperature of the bond layer is significantly improved, and thus, the entire EBC system is improved. This work provides a novel strategy to simultaneously increase the stability of TGOs and increase the operation temperature of EBCs, which contributes to the development of next-generation EBC systems with extended service lifetimes and improved thermal capabilities.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

The MoSi2/HfO2 duplex EBCs were deposited onto SiC substrates using the atmospheric plasma spraying (APS) process. The MoSi2 and HfO2 powders used for coating deposition were commercially available from Hunan Huawei Jingcheng Material Co., Ltd. and Shanghai Linghao Metal Material Co., Ltd., respectively. Both powders had irregular particle shapes with a size distribution of 40–90 μm. The coatings were applied to *ϕ*25 mm × 3 mm SiC substrates using an APS system (model 3710, Praxair Surface Technologies, USA). The specific deposition parameters are displayed in Table 1. Prior to coating, the surfaces of the SiC substrates were roughened via grit blasting using 60-mesh synthetic diamond particles. The coatings were applied to only one side of each SiC substrate.

### 2.2 Corrosion tests

Steam corrosion tests were conducted in a horizontal high-temperature tube furnace (GSL-1700×, Hefei Kejing Materials Technology Co., Ltd., China). Prior to steam corrosion testing, all the samples were placed inside a top-open Al2O3 crucible and annealed in static air at 1450 °C for 3 h. Following this pre-treatment, both ends of the flange of the tube furnace were sealed, and the furnace temperature was further increased to 1500 °C at a rate of 3 °C·min−1. Once the target temperature was reached, steam was introduced into the tube, creating an atmosphere composed of ~90 vol% H2O(g) + ~10 vol% O2(g), which flowed at a rate of 0.45 L·min−1. The samples were exposed to steam corrosion for 20, 50, 100, and 200 h. After each corrosion time, one end of the furnace flange was opened to slowly remove one sample from the isothermal zone for subsequent characterization of its corresponding corrosion condition.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The as-sprayed and corroded samples were cut into small pieces using a high-speed cutter equipped with a diamond external cutting blade. After ultrasonic cleaning with anhydrous ethanol, the samples were used for surface and cross-sectional morphology observation and phase analysis. For cross-sectional analysis, the samples were embedded in resin, ground, polished, and cleaned, enabling the cross-sectional flatness and smoothness.

The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the coatings were examined in backscattered electron (BSE) mode using a desktop scanning electron microscope (SEM; Phenom Pro, Phenom Scientific, China). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of both the coating surface and micro-regions of the cross-section were obtained using an X-ray diffractometer (Smartlab 9 KW, Rigaku, Japan; Cu Kα), with scanning speeds of 15 and 5 (°) min−1, respectively, within a 2*θ* range of 10°–70°. The micro-XRD analysis utilized an X-ray beam spot with a diameter of ~50 μm. Phase identification was performed using the PDF2 database. The weight percentages of the HfSiO4 phases were calculated using the internal standard method, based on the integrated intensity of the strongest peak in both phases from the XRD data and reference intensity ratio (RIR) values. Micro-region elemental analysis of the coating cross-section was conducted using an electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA; JXA-8530F Plus, JEOL, Japan) equipped with five wavelength dispersive X-ray spectrometers (WDSs) and an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS). Raman spectra were acquired using an InVia Raman microscope (InVia-Reflex, Renishaw, Inc., UK) with a 532 nm laser, and the spot diameter during measurement ranged from 1 to 2 μm. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) foil was extracted for observation from the polished cross-sections of the EBC samples exposed to steam corrosion at 1500 °C for 100 h using a microscopy, spectrum, and focused ion beam (FIB) system (Helios G4 UC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM F200 CF-UHR, JEOL, Japan) equipped with an EDS was used for detailed microstructural and phase analysis, with scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM), bright-field imaging, selected area electron diffraction (SAED), and high-resolution TEM modes.

The thicknesses of both the coating layers and the TGOs exposed to different durations of corrosion were examined based on the BSE microscopy. For each image, three random regions were selected, and four measurements were taken for each region via ImageJ software. The average thickness was then calculated after excluding the maximum and minimum values to ensure accuracy. The initial coating porosities were measured on the cross sections using image analysis method (ImageJ software). The porosities were obtained by averaging the results from 8 different measurement regions.

The Gibbs free energies of β-cristobalite with and without Hf doping at various temperatures were investigated using first-principles calculations. Initially, geometry optimization of the β-cristobalite unit cell was performed using conjugate gradient minimization, ensuring that all forces acting on ions were less than 10−8 eV/Å per atom. The K-point mesh was set to 4 × 4 × 3. Then, a supercell (containing 144 atoms) with and without Hf atom doping was constructed using VESTA software. The calculations were performed in the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP). Electronic exchange-correlation interactions were handled with the projector augmented wave (PAW), generalized-gradient approximation (GGA), and Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) pseudopotential. The cutoff energy of the plane wave function was set to 500 eV. The free energies of different crystal structures over a temperature range of −273–1500 °C were calculated using Phonopy software via the quasi-harmonic approximation (QHA) method [42,43].

### 3.3 Microstructure evolution during steam corrosion

Compared with the as-sprayed state shown in Fig. 1(a), no significant changes other than grain growth were observed on the surface of the MoSi2/HfO2 duplex EBCs after exposure at 1500 °C for 20 and 50 h, with only a few microcracks appearing, as shown in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b). After 100 and 200 h of exposure, an additional phase in grey color appeared on the coating surface, as shown in Figs. 3(c) and 3(d), which should be HfSiO4 formed via Reactions (3) and (4):

(3) 5MoSi2(s)+7O2(g)=Mo5Si3(s)+7SiO2(s)

(4) HfO2(s)+SiO2(s)=HfSiO4(s)

Additionally, the number of microcracks was significantly lower than that of the sample after 50-h corrosion. In the local magnifications (Figs. 3(e) and 3(f)), the areas composed of grey grains exhibited a higher degree of densification than the areas composed of white HfO2 grains.

Monoclinic HfO2 exhibits high thermal expansion anisotropy [44], promoting crack initiation and propagation. Moreover, pores and cracks were inevitable in coatings fabricated via APS, and thermal mismatching between coatings may introduce additional types of cracks. In MoSi2/HfO2 duplex EBCs, under high-temperature conditions, oxidants penetrated defects within the HfO2 layer and reacted with MoSi2, forming SiO2 and Mo5Si3 through Reaction (3). The inner HfO2 then contacted and reacted with SiO2, forming HfSiO4 through Reaction (4). In this process, the inner MoSi2 and outer HfO2 served as the donor and acceptor of Si4+, respectively. Furthermore, a comparison of the number and width of cracks over corrosion durations revealed that the formation of the HfSiO4 phase filled some microcracks, leading to a “self-healing” phenomenon, which enhanced the oxygen resistance of the coatings, thereby improving their long-term stability [30].

The XRD patterns of the coating surface (measured at the central position of the samples) in its as-sprayed state and after steam corrosion at 1500 °C for different durations are shown in Fig. 4. No significant changes in the phase composition were observed on the coating surface after 20 h of exposure compared with the as-sprayed state. After 50 h of exposure, a small amount of HfSiO4 was detected. However, after 100 and 200 h of exposure, significant HfSiO4 diffraction peaks were observed, with their peak intensities remaining similar. According to the easy quantitative analysis based on the XRD data, the content of HfSiO4 on the coating surface increased from 3.6 wt% at 20 h exposure to 5.3 wt% at 100 h and 5.8 wt% at 200 h. This indicated that the amount of HfSiO4 phase increased with increasing corrosion time and the growth rate clearly decreased during the period from 100 to 200 h.

At the local magnification around the cavity in Fig. 5(c), the TGO closest to MoSi2, designated as layer I, exhibited a grain structure, indicating its crystalline nature. Additionally, this layer contained numerous channel cracks, which is consistent with the findings from previous studies on pure SiO2-TGO [8,18]. This layer was likely composed of cristobalite, which existed in the form of β-cristobalite phase at high temperatures and underwent a phase transformation into α-cristobalite upon cooling to ~220 °C, resulting in a ~4.5% volume shrinkage. This phase transformation may be responsible for the formation of channel cracks.

In the second layer of the tri-layered TGOs designated as layer II, no through-thickness cracks were observed, which suggested that when cracks due to the cristobalite layer extended into layer II, their propagation either terminated or changed the direction. The presence of cavities in this layer (Fig. 5(c)) indicated the possible presence of a vitreous phase, which flew at high temperatures.

The TGO layer adjacent to the HfO2 side, designated as layer III, was the thinnest and extended into the HfO2 layer, filling the pores between HfO2 grains. This behavior may be attributed to the flow of the vitreous phase. Noticeable white and light gray particles within this layer indicated the possible presence of amorphization of HfO2 phase and the formation of HfSiO4 particles, respectively.

As shown in Figs. 5(e) and 5(g), some channel cracks extended into the MoSi2 layer, leading to its oxidation. The SiO2 formed during this process enabled a self-healing effect on these cracks. However, with prolonged exposure, the oxidation of the MoSi2 layer intensified, resulting in the transformation of MoSi2 into Mo5Si3 and SiO2 phases through Reaction (3). After 200 h of exposure, most of the inner MoSi2 was transformed into Mo5Si3 and SiO2 phases (Figs. 5(g) and 5(h)), while the SiC substrate remained unaffected. This indicated that the MoSi2 layer acted as an effective “sacrificial” layer, absorbing most of the infiltrating oxidants, despite the porous MoSi2 and HfO2 layers.

Unlike other oxidized EBCs, the MoSi2/HfO2 duplex EBCs exhibit a tri-layered TGO structure after oxidation. Channel cracks cannot completely penetrate these three layers of TGOs, which might be the key factor in decelerating the oxidation rate. A two-layered TGO structure was observed by Potanin et al*. [45] in their study on the high-temperature oxidation of MoSi2–HfB2–MoB ceramics at 1200 °C for 30 h. An amorphous Hf-doped oxide layer and a crystalline cristobalite (α-SiO2) layer were identified.

The relationship between the TGO thickness and exposure time is illustrated in Fig. 6, in which parabolic kinetic growth of TGOs was observed. As the holding time increased, the thickening of TGOs was initially dominated by the solid-state reaction between SiO2 and HfO2, which was governed by the contact area between these particles and followed a linear trend. Once a continuous TGO layer was formed, the oxidants had to diffuse through the TGO layer before reaching the MoSi2 layer for further oxidation. The interaction between the solid-state reaction rate and the diffusion speed of oxidants resulted in a more complex growth pattern, ultimately leading to parabolic kinetics. This phenomenon has been widely reported in Refs. [6,10,12]. Deijkers and Wadley [10] investigated the reaction kinetics between β-cristobalite SiO2 and monoclinic HfO2 powders at 1250–1400 °C and observed similar trends. They found that the initial rapid increase in TGO thickness slowed down once a thin layer of HfSiO4 formed between the reactants. Beyond this point, the thickening of TGO was controlled by the diffusion rate of Si4+ and O2− through the reaction product, transitioning to a parabolic growth pattern.

Owing to the diffusion of Si and Hf ions from the MoSi2 and HfO2 layers, respectively, to the other layer, gradient Hf-doped SiO2 glass and Hf–Si–O glass were formed. Hf doping effectively constrained the crystallization of SiO2, allowing layer II to remain amorphous even after 200 h of exposure at 1500 °C. Similar experimental observations were reported by Wang et al*. [49−52] in their study on the oxidation resistance of HfB2–SiC series coatings for C/C composites, and Hf was introduced into SiO2 glass, resulting in enhanced thermal stability.

Near the HfO2 layer, HfSiO4 formed due to the solid reaction between HfO2 and SiO2. The flow behavior of the Hf–Si–O glass phase at high temperatures, combined with the formation of HfSiO4, filled the holes in the HfO2 layer, increasing its densification degree. Furthermore, the HfO2 and HfSiO4 particles dispersed within the Hf–Si–O glass served as reinforcements, effectively suppressed the crystallization of SiO2, and inhibited crack propagation in the oxide scale. The *in-situ* formed amorphous Hf-doped SiO2 glass in layer II and the HfO2/HfSiO4 particle-reinforced Hf–Si–O glass in layer III effectively mitigated the thermal mismatch and prevented phase transition-induced volume changes associated with crystalline SiO2. This significantly decreased the internal stress level within the coatings. Additionally, channel cracks were observed to terminate at the interface between layers I and II within the TGOs. This channel crack-blocking effect may be attributed to the differences in the thermal and physical properties between layers I and II. This further enhanced the protection effect of the TGOs layer, safeguarding the underlying materials. As a result of these novel gradient tri-layered TGOs, no detectable oxidation or corrosion was observed in SiC after 200 h of exposure at 1500 °C in 90 vol% H2O and 10 vol% O2 atmospheres, even with relatively porous MoSi2/HfO2 composite coatings. This performance offered a new strategy for designing TGO layers to fundamentally enhance both the durability and temperature resistance of EBCs of the next generation.

### 3.5 Anti-corrosion mechanism of MoSi2/HfO2 duplex EBCs

Figure 11(a) shows a schematic diagram for the cross-section of the as-sprayed MoSi2/HfO2 duplex EBCs. Owing to the presence of numerous pores and microcracks in the coatings, the oxidants in the steam rapidly penetrate through the HfO2 layer on the surface, reaching the inner MoSi2 layer. As the exposure time increases, as shown in Fig. 11(b), MoSi2 undergoes oxidation, forming Mo5Si3 and SiO2. The internal pores within the MoSi2 layer become filled with SiO2, while a dense SiO2 scale develops on its surface. Since the SiO2 scale is in direct contact with HfO2, high temperatures induce the diffusion of Hf and Si elements towards the MoSi2 and HfO2 sides, respectively. This results in the formation of the amorphous Hf-doped SiO2 glass and Hf–Si–O glass. Simultaneously, HfSiO4 particles precipitate from the glass phase, driven by the reaction between SiO2 and HfO2.

Owing to the fluidity of the amorphous glass phase at high temperatures and the porous nature of the HfO2 coating, the glass phase mitigates into and gradually fills the pores and cracks within the HfO2 layer, increasing its densification degree. As a result of glass phase depletion, cavities form within the tri-layered TGOs. The filling of pores and cracks in the HfO2 layer effectively seals the rapid pathway for oxidants’ penetration into the MoSi2 layer. Consequently, oxidants can only diffuse inward through the coatings in the form of O2− via grain boundaries or oxygen vacancies, significantly inhibiting the oxidation of MoSi2. As a result, the growth rate of the tri-layered TGO slows down considerably.

After the formation of the tri-layered TGOs, a large number of HfSiO4 particles precipitate at the interface between SiO2 and HfO2, thereby blocking the direct contact between SiO2 and HfO2 and significantly reducing the reaction rate. That is, the formation of layer III hinders the solid-phase reaction between SiO2 and HfO2. At the same time, layers I, II, and III together constitute a barrier for the diffusion of oxidants from the outside in and Si elements from the inside out; that is, the diffusion rates of O element inward and Si element outward are greatly reduced, thereby slowing down the consumption rate of the MoSi2 layer and enabling it to maintain a longer life as a sacrificial layer.

During cooling, cristobalite undergoes β → α transformation, leading to the formation of channel cracks in layer I of the tri-layered TGOs. However, since the matrixes of layers II and III remain amorphous, few phase transition-induced channel cracks were observed. The amorphization of some TGOs effectively prevents the formation of thickness-through channel cracks that could otherwise serve as rapid transport pathways for oxidants.

According to the published SiO2‒HfO2 phase diagram [53], Hf has almost no solubility in SiO2 at 1500 °C. However, as previous reports lacked direct experimental evidence, we propose that Hf may be incorporated into SiO2 molecules at high temperatures. However, owing to the significant differences in the atomic radii of Hf and Si atoms, Hf incorporation introduces stretching of the Si–O–Si bonds, leading to lattice distortion and an increase in the system’s free energy. Consequently, this effect enhances the resistance of SiO2 to crystallization or its phase transformation.

To validate this assumption, a β-cristobalite lattice model was constructed using VESTA software for first-principles calculations, in which one Si atom was substituted with an Hf atom, resulting in doped SiO2 with ~2 at% Hf, as illustrated in Fig. 12(a). The Gibbs free energy of the system before and after doping was then calculated, as shown in Fig. 12(b). These results indicated that Hf incorporation led to an increase in the system’s Gibbs free energy. This increase suggested that Hf promoted the transformation of SiO2 from β-cristobalite to an amorphous state or, in other words, hindered the crystallization of SiO2, making it more resistant to phase transitions.

## 4 Conclusions

MoSi2/HfO2 duplex EBCs were prepared on SiC substrates, which were demonstrated to provide effective protection against detectable corrosion in a 1500 °C steam environment for at least 200 h. The formation of tri-layered TGO structure between the MoSi2 and HfO2 layers was identified as the key protective mechanism. This TGO structure maintained strong adhesion with both the MoSi2 and HfO2 layers and exhibited no through-thickness cracks, even when its average thickness reached ~24.5 μm. The tri-layered TGOs were composed of α-cristobalite, Hf-doped SiO2 glass, and Hf–Si–O glass, embedded with HfSiO4 and HfO2 particles. The interdiffusion of Hf and Si between the HfO2 and SiO2 layers generated *in-situ* formed gradient glassy phases, which were found to offer a crack-blocking effect that prevented the propagation of channel cracks through the TGOs layer. Simultaneously, the flow behavior of the TGO glass at high temperatures facilitated the filling of pores within the HfO2 layer, promoting the densification of coatings and self-healing of cracks.

This study demonstrated that *in situ* gradient Hf doping can effectively constrain the crystallization of SiO2 in the EBC system at high temperatures. Additionally, the formation of TGO multilayer structure containing both crystalline and amorphous phases mitigated the channel cracks caused by the cristobalite phase transformation, thereby eliminating rapid oxidant transport pathways through the coating. This work provides a novel strategy to simultaneously increase the stability of TGOs and increase the operation temperature of EBCs, which contributes to the development of next-generation EBC systems with extended service lifetimes and improved thermal capabilities.
