## 1 Introduction

The ongoing environmental deterioration and global energy crisis have highlighted the urgent need for innovative and sustainable technologies to address these challenges. Catalytic technologies, especially photocatalysis, electrocatalysis, and thermocatalysis, have garnered significant attention for environmental remediation and energy conversion because of their capacity to facilitate chemical reactions under relatively mild conditions [1,2]. However, these conventional methods are fraught with limitations. For example, photocatalysis is inherently constrained by its dependence on light energy, limiting its efficiency under natural sunlight [3−5]. Electrocatalysis proves effective but requires an external power source, undermining its viability for off-grid applications [6,7]. Similarly, thermocatalysis necessitates elevated operating temperatures, leading to increased energy consumption [8,9]. Collectively, these limitations underscore the necessity for more versatile catalytic systems capable of operating effectively under a broader range of conditions and harnessing alternative energy inputs [10].

In this context, piezocatalysis has emerged as a promising alternative because of its unique characteristics of converting mechanical energy into electrochemical energy [11−13]. This innovative process facilitates environmental remediation and energy conversion through mechanical stimuli, such as ultrasonic waves, vibrations, or mechanical pressure, effectively circumventing the limitations associated with light, electricity, or heat [14,15]. To date, a diverse array of piezocatalysts have been developed, each exhibiting varying degrees of efficiency across numerous applications. For example, materials such as ZnO [16,17], SnSe [18], MoS2 [19−21], and Sr0.5Ba0.5Nb2O6 (SBN) [22] have demonstrated considerable potential in water splitting because of their robust piezoelectric properties and chemical stability. Additionally, materials such as K0.5Na0.5NbO3 (KNN) [23,24], Bi2MoO6 [25,26], and BaBi4Ti4O15 (BBT) [27,28] have been effectively employed for the degradation of organic pollutants, and BaTiO3 (BTO) [29,30], CsPbBr3 [31], and SnS [32] have shown promise in CO2 reduction. Despite these advancements, piezoelectric materials still face challenges related to efficiency and suitable band structures for specific catalytic reactions [33,34]. Among the various piezocatalysts, BiFeO3 (BFO) stands out as an extraordinarily promising piezocatalyst because of its distinctive combination of strong piezoelectric, multiferroic, and optical properties, coupled with an ideal bandgap that supports efficient electron transfer, showing remarkable potential in multifield coupling applications [35,36]. Moreover, the multifunctionality of BFO makes it particularly suitable for diverse piezocatalytic applications, ranging from environmental remediation to energy conversion [37−39]. Furthermore, the piezoelectric performance of BFO can be rationally optimized through material engineering, increasing piezoelectric properties or regulating charge transport, thereby enhancing piezocatalytic activity [40−42].

Given the recent attention to BFO and its remarkable performance across various piezocatalytic applications, a comprehensive review of BFO in piezocatalysis is essential to drive further advancements and inspire the development of highly efficient BFO-based piezocatalytic systems. In response, this review provides a systematic overview of recent progress in BFO-based piezocatalysis, highlighting the structural characteristics, synthesis methods, performance optimization strategies, and diverse applications, as presented in Fig. 1. First, the structural and functional properties of BFO are intuitively introduced, and diverse commonly used synthesis methods are thoroughly evaluated. Then, the three prevailing mechanisms of piezocatalysis (i.e., energy band theory, screening charge theory, and displacement current theory) are elaborated, elucidating the essence of mechanical stress-induced piezoelectric effects that initiate redox reactions. Furthermore, the strategies to boost the piezoelectric properties or charge separation efficiency are delineated, confirming that BFO is a versatile piezocatalyst applicable to organic pollutant degradation, water splitting, CO2 reduction, biomedical sterilization, etc. Importantly, we critically analyze and highlight the underestimated role of ferroelectric polarization in enhancing CO2 reduction. Ultimately, the critical challenges and future research directions for constructing efficient BFO-based piezocatalysis systems are outlined.

## 2 Historical development and principles of piezocatalysis

The piezoelectric effect was first proposed by the Curie brothers in 1880, which opened the door to converting mechanical energy into electric energy [43]. Since then, a great deal of interest has been focused on the discovery and applications of piezoelectric materials [12,44,45]. In recent years, the application of piezoelectric materials has expanded significantly across various fields because of their distinctive ability to convert mechanical energy into electric energy and vice versa [46−48]. These materials are increasingly used in piezoelectric devices such as sensors [49,50], actuators [51,52], and nanogenerators [53,54], enabling advancements in consumer electronics, medical equipment, and automotive technologies [55−57]. In contrast, the piezocatalysis technology was first proposed in 2010 by Hong and coworkers [58]. Piezocatalysis is capable of harnessing mechanically induced piezoelectric charges to catalyze chemical reactions and is promising as an ideal candidate for environmental remediation and energy conversion [59−61]. Moreover, piezocatalytic applications have been widely extended to environmental remediation [62−64], water splitting [65,66], CO2 reduction [67−69], biomedical sterilization [70,71], tumor therapy [72−74], uranium extraction [75,76], organic synthesis [77], teeth whitening [78,79], etc. Notably, Fig. 2 exhibits that BFO has garnered great attention as a star piezocatalyst in the field of piezocatalysis, showing promise in water remediation [80], water splitting [37], H2O2 generation [81], CO2 reduction [81], and sterilization [82].

Despite the rapid development of piezocatalysis technology, the underlying mechanism of piezocatalysis is still unclear because of the complicated reactive process combining the piezoelectric effect and chemical redox reactions [83,84]. To date, three types of piezocatalysis mechanisms, namely, energy band theory, screening charge effect, and displacement current theory, have been developed and well accepted, all of which are validated by their respective fundamental concepts and experimental results, differing from each other and holding potential in different scenarios. ### 2.1 Energy band theory

Energy band theory is instrumental in characterizing the electronic structures of materials, particularly semiconductors and insulators. Within this theoretical framework, electrons occupy the valence band (VB), and when provided with sufficient energy, they can move to the conduction band (CB), leaving behind holes in the VB [61,85]. The bandgap determines the ability to conduct electricity or support charge carrier movement [15,86]. This theory is inspired by the photocatalysis model of semiconductors, where light excites electrons from the VB to CB, enabling redox reactions [5]. In piezocatalysis, energy band theory provides insights into the generation of an internal electric field under mechanical stress, which facilitates the separation of electron‒hole pairs without light irradiation (Fig. 3(a)) [83,87]. A significant phenomenon within this framework is energy band bending, which allows for adjusting band positions under mechanical vibrations [88]. This adjustment can effectively overcome the thermodynamic constraints associated with chemical reactions [87,89]. Moreover, energy band theory is particularly suitable for explaining the behavior of piezoelectric semiconductors with moderate band gaps, such as BTO and ZnO, which can generate substantial charge separation when subjected to mechanical stress [90−93]. For example, Wu *et al*. [90] clarified the piezocatalytic H2 generation of BTO nanowires via energy band theory. However, it is less applicable to materials without appropriate energy band structures.

Notably, energy band theory has greatly contributed to understanding piezocatalysis by providing a band structural framework to explain how mechanical energy drives charge separation and redox reactions. It has become an essential tool for interpreting the catalytic behavior of piezoelectric semiconductors, advancing the development of novel piezocatalysts.

### 2.2 Screening charge effect

The screening charge effect occurs when nearby charges rearrange in response to the electric field [83,94]. As depicted in Fig. 3(b), this effect emphasizes the role of the piezopotential and surface accumulated screening charge generated under mechanical stress rather than the simple electron‒hole separation explained by energy band theory [87]. The piezopotential, which arises from polarization in piezoelectric materials, governs the ability of the material to drive redox reactions [95]. The magnitude of the piezopotential must align precisely with the redox potential required for a specific chemical reaction, similar to the way electrocatalysts operate in electrochemical reactions [94,96]. This alignment is crucial because it determines whether the piezocatalyst can successfully trigger oxidation or reduction reactions. Additionally, the surface screening behavior caused by polarization directly influences the reaction environment, controlling the availability of active sites and the distribution of charge carriers on the catalyst surface [97,98]. For example, Wang *et al.* [99] presented a fascinating piezocatalytic teeth-whitening process of BTO, illustrating the mechanism via screening charge effect theory. In addition to being suitable for piezocatalysts lacking an appropriate band structure, this theory is especially applicable to those piezoelectric materials with strong polarization capabilities, which can produce substantial piezopotentials under mechanical stress [13,15,100]. However, it may not apply effectively to materials with weak or no polarization, where the piezopotential is insufficient to drive meaningful redox reactions. Non-polar materials or those with low piezoelectric coefficients would not benefit from this mechanism, as the lack of substantial piezopotential would result in poor catalytic performance [101].

Notably, screening charge theory has significantly advanced the understanding of piezocatalysis by highlighting the crucial roles of piezopotential and surface charge behavior in facilitating catalytic reactions. This framework elucidates why certain piezoelectric materials outperform in specific catalytic processes and provides insight into the conditions necessary for effective piezocatalysis.

### 2.3 Displacement current theory

Importantly, the above two theories do not fully capture the complexities of the piezocatalytic process. Although the piezoelectric effect generates charge carriers in response to mechanical stress, these electrons do not spontaneously engage in catalytic reactions [60,61,86,102]. Moreover, not all piezoelectric materials have a suitable energy band structure that facilitates redox reactions [103]. This underscores the necessity for a more comprehensive framework to deepen our understanding of piezocatalysis. In this context, Ren and his coworkers [84] have proposed a novel displacement current theory to help further comprehend and refine the piezocatalytic mechanism (Fig. 3(c)). According to this theory, upon the application of ultrasonic waves to a piezoelectric catalyst, a time-varying mechanical force induces a displacement current within the material, resulting in the formation of a dynamic electrostatic potential at its surface. This process promotes the accumulation of charge carriers, which, in tandem with a favorable energy band structure and active surface sites, drives the piezocatalytic CO2 reduction reaction [84]. Importantly, this mechanism supports the notion that the piezoelectric effect can directly produce electrons, extending beyond the electron generation typically associated with semiconductor catalysts [84,104].

Generally, a clear change in the electronic structure or bandgap would point to energy band theory, while localized surface charge accumulation or reaction rate shifts linked to surface properties would suggest screening charge effects, and dynamic current fluctuations during mechanical deformation would suggest displacement currents. Collectively, from the perspective of energy band theory, the piezopotential alters the band potential, thereby indirectly influencing the redox capacity of internal charges. In terms of the screening charge effect, the piezopotential directly affects the redox power of external charges, rendering the overall catalytic activity. Moreover, the displacement current theory emphasizes that, under ultrasonic vibration, the electrons produced by the piezoelectric effect are driven by the time-varying electrostatic potential established by the displacement current. Overall, these mechanisms elucidate the complex interplay that governs the efficiency of piezocatalytic reactions. Energy band theory is more frequently used to interpret piezocatalytic behavior, as most highly effective piezocatalysts are semiconducting.

### 2.4 Related physical mechanisms

In addition to the abovementioned three prevailing mechanisms, the physical mechanisms underlying piezocatalysis, especially for some multiferroic materials with narrow bandgaps, such as BFO materials, still remain an area of active research. When piezoelectric materials are subjected to mechanical stimuli, other related mechanical–electric conversion-induced catalytic phenomena may occur simultaneously [105]. These include mechano-friction-induced catalysis [106,107], contact-electro-catalysis [108], and flexo-catalysis [109], which have recently garnered attention as potential contributors to piezocatalytic activity. Furthermore, the typical pyroelectric effect, often observed in certain ferro-/piezoelectric materials, becomes significant under increasing temperature gradients [110,111]. The combined effects of mechano-friction-induced catalysis, contact-electro-catalysis, flexo-catalysis, and pyroelectric effects can influence the piezocatalytic efficiency of materials such as BFO by modifying their electrical properties and improving charge carrier generation [112,113]. These mechanisms may act synergistically, with mechano-friction and flexoelectric effects generating additional electric fields that lower activation energies, while the pyroelectric effect contributes under dynamic thermal and mechanical conditions [114,115]. Hence, understanding their interplay is crucial for optimizing catalytic performance and guiding the design of more efficient catalysts for applications such as water splitting, CO2 reduction, and pollutant degradation.

### 3.1 Structural characteristics

BFO is a prominent multiferroic material exhibiting a rhombohedral-distorted perovskite structure (*R*3*c* space group) with spontaneous polarization resulting from the displacement of Bi3+ and Fe3+ ions along the [111] axis, as presented in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b) [36]. The large polarization with a value of ~100 µC·cm−2 plays a crucial role in the catalytic properties of BFO by generating internal electric fields that promote charge carrier separation [116]. Moreover, the coexistence of ferroelectricity and antiferromagnetism in BFO enables magneto-electric coupling, allowing for the modulation of charge transfer processes and offering a pathway for enhancing catalytic efficiency in various redox reactions [117,118]. These characteristics position BFO as a promising material for multifunctional energy devices and multifield coupling catalysis, including ferro-piezocatalysis and magneto-piezocatalysis.

The integration of piezoelectric, multiferroic, and optical properties within BFO not only improves its multi-field coupling capability but also positions it as a versatile material for piezocatalysis and other energy conversion applications [35,36,124,125].

#### 3.2.1 Solid-state synthesis

Solid-state sintering is a process where powdered materials are heated below their melting point, causing particles to bond and densify through atomic diffusion. It is a conventional method for preparing piezoelectric materials (especially piezoceramics), which is advantageous for its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and scalability, making it suitable for large-scale production. Additionally, when conducted under controlled conditions, it can yield high phase purity, which is crucial for optimizing piezoelectric properties [35]. However, this process typically requires high temperatures and extended heating time, which can lead to the formation of unwanted secondary phases. Furthermore, solid-state synthesis often results in larger and irregular particle sizes, which may reduce the specific surface area (SSA) and subsequently diminish the piezoelectric performance of the resulting material. BFO was first synthesized by Royen and Swars in 1957 using solid-state synthesis [127]. However, achieving single-phase BFO is often challenging. The volatility of Bi3+ ions and the aggregation of oxygen vacancies during the sintering process can lead to the formation of secondary phases, i.e., Bi2O3, Fe2O3, Bi2Fe4O9, and Bi25FeO40 [125,128]. Hence, several strategies have been proposed to obtain pure BFO, such as adding excessive 5%–10% bismuth salts in advance [129], dissolving bismuth salts with nitric acid [130], and washing products with water, nitric acid, ethanol, or other appropriate solutions [131,132]. Notably, the solid-state synthesis method can only achieve BFO particles that are tens of micrometers in size, which makes it difficult to trigger a highly effective catalytic reaction.

#### 3.2.2 Hydrothermal/solvothermal method

Hydrothermal/solvothermal synthesis involves the reaction of precursors in a sealed vessel at elevated temperatures and pressures in an aqueous solution. This method is capable of synthesizing nanomaterials with controlled size and morphology, which are pivotal for optimizing the piezoelectric and catalytic properties [133−135]. Significantly, hydrothermal/solvothermal synthesis can produce various nanostructured BFOs (e.g., nanosheets, nanowires, and nanodisks), leading to modified piezoelectric performance [80,122,136]. For example, in 2018, Arazas *et al*. [135] reported the fabrication of vertically oriented BFO nanorods on indium tin oxide/glass substrates through hydrothermal synthesis, and Mushtaq *et al*. [80] hydrothermally fabricated single-crystalline BFO nanosheets and nanowires that could successfully harness visible light and mechanical vibrations for degrading organic pollutants. In 2019, You *et al*. [37] hydrothermally synthesized BFO square nanosheets, realizing a hydrogen yield rate of 124.1 μmol·g−1 under 100 W vibration excitation at the resonant frequency for 1 h. In 2024, Zeng *et al*. [137] prepared BFO nanosheets via a simple hydrothermal method for H2O2 production.

Additionally, this method typically results in high phase purity due to the controlled environment, minimizing the risk of contamination from secondary phases [38,138]. Nonetheless, the process often requires long reaction time to achieve the desired crystallinity and morphology, which accordingly hinders scalability for industrial applications. Moreover, the hydrothermal/solvothermal method demonstrates constrained reproducibility, primarily owing to its heightened sensitivity to key reactive parameters, including temperature, pressure, and pH. This sensitivity poses significant challenges in achieving a stable and consistent process.

#### 3.2.3 Sol–gel method

The sol–gel method for synthesizing BFO involves the transition of a solution containing metal precursors into a gel phase, followed by drying and heat treatment to obtain the final product [139]. This process allows for precise control over the stoichiometry and homogeneity of the resulting material, facilitating the formation of high-purity BFO. One of the prominent advantages of the sol–gel method is its ability to produce BFO with uniform particle sizes and shapes, which can enhance the piezoelectric properties [140]. Additionally, the lower synthesis temperatures associated with the sol–gel process minimize the risk of forming unwanted secondary phases, thereby improving phase purity [141]. For example, Wu *et al*. [142] synthesized high-purity BFO nanoparticles in the range of 30–200 nm via a sol–gel process in 2018. Remarkably, the method is also relatively versatile, allowing for the incorporation of various dopants and modifications to tailor the properties. For example, Wang *et al.* [143] reported the sol–gel synthesis of Nd-doped BFO nanoparticles. Muduli *et al.* [144] synthesized 0.67BiFeO3–0.33BaTiO3 (BF33BT) solid solution via the sol–gel route. Nevertheless, the sol–gel process can be time-consuming because it involves multiple steps, including gel formation, drying, and heat treatment. Furthermore, scaling up the sol–gel process for industrial applications will pose challenges because of these complexities.

#### 3.2.4 Electrospinning

Electrospinning is an advanced technique exploited to synthesize BFO nanofibers or nanotubes by applying a high-voltage electric field to a polymer solution containing BFO precursors [145]. As the electric field draws the solution into fine fibers, they are collected on a substrate, followed by heat treatment to remove the polymer and form the desired BFO structure. Electrospinning can produce highly uniform and flexible nanofibers/tubes with large SSAs, which can significantly enhance the piezoelectric properties of BFO. In addition, the nanofiber/tube morphology can facilitate optimal dispersion in composite materials, improving the overall performance [146,147]. For example, Queraltó *et al.* [145] reported the synthesis of piezoelectric BFO nanofibers using an electrospinning approach on SnO2:F/glass substrates. Long *et al*. [146] fabricated nanofiber-like BFO@CdS heterojunctions via a combination of electrospinning, annealing, and hydrothermal treatment for piezocatalytic degradation of bisphenol A (BPA) in water. Additionally, the process can easily incorporate various additives or dopants, enabling tailored material properties. Specifically, Yao *et al.* [147] produced piezoelectric BFO nanotubes doped with different contents of Gd and La (Bi0.9(Gd*x*La1−*x*)0.1FeO3) by electrospinning for efficient degradation of methylene blue. Nevertheless, electrospinning is sensitive to various parameters, such as solution concentration, flow rate, and voltage, making it challenging to achieve reproducible results. Moreover, the electrospinning technique typically requires specialized equipment and careful optimization of processing conditions, rendering it hard to scale up.

### 4.1 Modulating piezoelectric properties

In the theory of piezoelectric effect, when an asymmetrical structural piezoelectric material is subjected to external forces, piezoelectric polarization can rapidly occur, and positive and negative charges will accordingly migrate to two opposing surfaces and form a piezoelectric potential, thereby rendering a built-in electric field [61,86]. This internal field facilitates the efficient generation and separation of free carriers (electrons and holes), eventually yielding ROS [58,59]. Therefore, the piezoelectric

potential acts as the driving force to trigger the piezocatalytic reaction [60,61]. For a perfectly insulating piezoelectric material, the total energy shift of piezoelectric potential across the materials induced by the piezoelectric effect can be expressed as * VPmax (Eq. (1)):

(1) VPmax=WxTkdxkϵ0ϵr,x

where T**k* is an exerted stress in the *k* dimension; *d**xk* represents the piezoelectric modulus, *ɛ*0 represents the electrical permittivity of free space; *ɛ*r,*x* represents the relative permittivity in the *x* dimension; *W**x* represents the width of the piezoelectric material in the *x* dimension [61]. Certainly, the subscripts can be dropped in a one-dimensional case where *x* and *k* are equal. Thus, modulating the desirable piezoelectric potential is a feasible and effective way to mediate the energy band structure and drive the migration of highly energetic carriers. On this basis, several proposed strategies for improving piezoelectric properties to increase the piezocatalytic effect are summarized.

#### 4.1.1 Morphology modulation

Morphology modulation significantly influences the piezocatalytic performance of BFO. By optimizing the morphology, the crystal orientation and polarization direction can be effectively adjusted, enhancing the alignment with the applied mechanical stress [123]. This improved alignment subsequently amplifies the piezoelectric effect, contributing to superior piezocatalytic activity. Moreover, variations in morphology can alter the distribution and transmission of internal stress within a material. The corresponding synthesis methods for modulating these morphologies are detailed in Table 1. Nanostructures exhibit a distinct ability to effectively transmit and distribute applied stress, thereby improving piezocatalytic reaction activity [151]. Previous studies have demonstrated that low-dimensional materials such as nanosheets, nanowires, and nanofibers outperform nanoparticles and nanospheres in terms of piezocatalytic performance [37,123].

#### 4.1.2 Crystal facet engineering

Crystal facet engineering represents a powerful strategy for enhancing the piezocatalytic performance of BFO by precisely modulating atomic configurations, surface energies, and electronic properties across various crystal planes [123]. Distinct facets exhibit diverse charge separation and transport behaviors, with those characterized by significant electronegativity differences demonstrating improved polarization under mechanical stress [154]. This enhancement facilitates efficient electron‒hole separation, minimizes recombination, and promotes directional charge transport. Furthermore, facets with elevated surface energies typically possess a greater density of active sites, which enhances the adsorption and activation of reactants. The interactions between specific facets and reactants can preferentially stabilize certain reaction pathways, thereby influencing both kinetics and thermodynamics [155,156]. By selectively exposing facets with favorable electronic structures, crystal facet engineering optimizes charge carrier migration to active sites, further reducing recombination and enhancing carrier mobility [156]. The application of mechanical stress selectively polarizes facets, strengthening the piezoelectric effect and improving electron transport and surface reactivity. Moreover, targeting specific crystal facets can lower activation energies, thereby accelerating reaction rates and enhancing catalytic efficiency [157,158]. The modulation of BFO through crystal facet engineering is illustrated in Figs. 7(a)–7(e) [123]. As shown in Fig. 7(a), BFO nanomaterials were synthesized via modified hydrothermal methods. Notably, BiFeO3-NS, with highly exposed (012) facets, exhibits optimal piezoelectric properties. This methodology offers critical insights for advancing the application of BFO in piezocatalytic systems.

#### 4.1.3 Solid solution construction

The construction of solid solutions in BFO can significantly influence its piezocatalytic performance by altering both its structural and electronic properties [159]. In solid solutions, the incorporation of secondary elements into the BFO lattice modifies its crystal structure, potentially inducing lattice distortion and enhancing polarization under mechanical stress [160,161]. This adjustment can improve the piezoelectric response, which is crucial for efficient charge generation and separation in piezocatalysis [162]. Furthermore, solid solution formation often leads to improved charge carrier mobility and reduced electron–hole recombination, both of which are vital for enhancing catalytic efficiency [144]. The introduction of new phases or the stabilization of metastable phases through solid solution construction can also optimize the distribution of active sites and enhance the material’s surface reactivity, further promoting catalytic reactions [144,159]. Thus, by carefully selecting the composition of the solid solution, the piezocatalytic properties of BFO can be tuned to achieve higher performance in energy conversion and environmental applications. To date, numerous BFO-based solid solutions have been reported, such as BiFeO3–BaTiO3 (BFO–BTO) [144,162−164], SrTiO3–BiFeO3 (STO–BFO) [165,166], and Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3–BiFeO3 (BNT–BFO) [159,167]. Figures 7(f)–7(k) show a typical example of BNT–BFO solid solution [159]. As shown in Fig. 7(f), the modulated BNT–BFO solid solution displayed a tunable band gap, with values intermediate between those of pristine BFO and BNT and adjustable based on the BNT-to-BFO ratio. In addition, the EIS results of 0.5BNT–0.5BFO are also in the middle of those of pristine BFO and BNT, revealing an apparent modulation effect (Fig. 7(g)). However, the piezo-current density of the 0.5BNT–0.5BFO solid solution is greater than that of the pristine BFO and BNT solutions (Fig. 7(h)). In particular, the well-modulated 0.5BNT–0.5BFO sample demonstrates optimal piezocatalytic performance among the (1−*x*)BNT–*x*BFO catalysts (0.0 ≤ *x* ≤ 1.0) and exhibits excellent cyclic stability, as shown in Figs. 7(i)–7(k). This strategy offers an effective approach for further optimizing the piezocatalytic performance of BFO and holds broad application potential.

#### 4.1.4 Defect engineering

Defect engineering, including intrinsic and extrinsic defects, plays a critical role in enhancing the piezocatalytic performance of BFO. This approach tailors the electronic structure, improves the charge carrier dynamics, and increases the density of active sites, thereby optimizing the catalytic efficiency [34,168,169].

Intrinsic defects: Oxygen vacancy engineering is widely recognized as a typical intrinsic defect strategy for boosting the piezocatalytic performance of BFO [119]. Introducing oxygen vacancies facilitates the generation of additional charge carriers and intermediate energy states, thereby altering the electronic properties and effectively reducing the bandgap. This adjustment facilitates more efficient electron‒hole separation under mechanical stress, minimizing recombination and improving charge carrier mobility [170]. Additionally, oxygen vacancies significantly increase the number of active sites on the BFO surface, thereby improving reactant adsorption and activation, which is essential for accelerating piezocatalytic reactions [171]. Furthermore, these vacancies modulate the interaction between the catalyst and reactants, refining reaction pathways and improving overall kinetics [137]. Figures 8(a)–8(d) illustrate the impact of oxygen vacancy engineering on BFO [171]. As shown in Fig. 8(a), pristine BFO was synthesized via a hydrothermal method, and oxygen vacancies were introduced by annealing a mixture of BFO and NaBH4 under an argon atmosphere. Figures 8(b) and 8(c) demonstrate the enhanced current density and reduced EIS in oxygen-deficient BiFeO3−*x*, indicating stronger piezoelectric properties and higher charge carrier mobility. In addition, the correlation between these electrical changes and the proposed piezocatalytic mechanism (Fig. 8(d)) underscores the critical role of oxygen vacancies in regulating the piezocatalytic performance of BFO [108]. Therefore, strategically introducing oxygen vacancies into BFO can effectively enhance its piezocatalytic properties, offering valuable insights for its development in catalytic applications.

Extrinsic defects: Elemental doping serves as a significant extrinsic defect strategy to enhance the piezocatalytic performance of BFO [172]. Similar to intrinsic defects such as oxygen vacancies, doping modifies the structural and electronic properties, thereby affecting the overall piezocatalytic activity. Specifically, the introduction of rare earth or transition metal dopants can alter the crystal structure, leading to increased spontaneous polarization and a strengthened piezoelectric effect [42,173]. Furthermore, doping introduces intermediate energy states into the band structure, which reduces the band gap and enhances the charge carrier mobility. This adjustment facilitates efficient electron‒hole separation, minimizes recombination, and ultimately increases catalytic activity [39]. Additionally, doping influences surface chemistry by increasing the density of active sites and altering the binding energy of reactants, thereby improving reactant adsorption and activation [147]. Moreover, it can induce intrinsic defects, such as oxygen vacancies, which further enhance the piezoelectric performance by improving the charge mobility and modifying the surface reactivity [42]. A range of element-doped BFOs have been studied, such as La-doped BFO [42,172], Ca-doped BFO [174], Co-substituted BFO [175], and Gd-doped BFO [39,147]. In summary, elemental doping can tune the piezoelectric, electronic, and surface properties of BFO, leading to significantly enhanced piezocatalytic performance.

### 4.2 Regulating charge transport

The influence of electronic transport paths on catalytic performance is a critical factor in determining the efficiency of catalysts, especially in systems such as piezocatalysis. Efficient electronic transport ensures the rapid migration of charge carriers (electrons and holes) to reaction sites, minimizing recombination and enhancing reaction rates [176]. In catalytic systems, particularly those involving semiconductor materials, the electronic transport path can directly impact the separation and mobility of charge carriers. Materials with well-designed transport paths facilitate faster electron movement, reducing the chance of electron–hole recombination [177]. This efficient charge transfer is crucial for reduction reactions (e.g., CO2 to fuel or N2 to ammonia) [178,179] or oxidation reactions (e.g., water splitting) [180]. Optimizing the electronic transport path often involves engineering strategies such as creating heterojunctions [181], surface functionalization [182], or designing a multifield catalytic system [183,184]. These approaches increase the conductivity and improve the ability of a material to engage with reactants, increasing the overall catalytic efficiency.

#### 4.2.1 Heterojunction construction

Heterojunctions significantly enhance piezocatalytic performance by optimizing electronic transport paths, ensuring efficient charge separation, and reducing recombination. The built-in electric field at the interface between two materials with different band structures facilitates directional electron and hole migration, improving the availability of charge carriers for catalytic reactions [183,185]. Specifically, different types of heterojunctions include type I (straddling gap), type II (staggered gap), and type III (broken gap) structures. Type I heterojunctions confine both electrons and holes within one material, limiting the charge separation efficiency. In contrast, type II heterojunctions have staggered band alignments, where electrons and holes migrate to different materials, enhancing charge separation and transport. Type III heterojunctions have a more complex band structure but can also contribute to efficient charge transfer in certain cases [186].

In piezocatalytic systems involving BFO combined with materials such as ZnO, AgCl, TaPa-1-COF, and In2O3, type II heterojunctions are particularly effective because of their staggered band alignment, which facilitates efficient charge separation and transport [149,187−189]. Figure 9(a) shows the BFO/ZnO heterojunction. The wide bandgap (~3.37 eV) of ZnO aligns favorably with BFO, enabling electrons from BFO to transfer to ZnO, while holes migrate in the opposite direction, improving the separation and migration of piezo-induced charges [189]. This efficient charge transfer under mechanical stress increases the catalytic activity, enhancing redox reactions at the interface. Similarly, in the BFO/AgCl system shown in Fig. 9(b), the staggered band alignment between AgCl and BFO directs electrons to AgCl and holes to BFO [149]. AgCl can serve as an electron acceptor, enhancing electron mobility and suppressing recombination, leading to increased catalytic performance under ultrasonic-induced mechanical pressure. In more complex systems such as BFO/TaPa-1-COF (covalent organic frameworks), the extended π-conjugated structure of TaPa-1-COF facilitates electron transport, while BFO enables hole migration (Fig. 9(c)) [187]. The heterojunction forms an efficient electronic transport network, improving piezoelectric charge migration and contributing to increased catalytic efficiency. As depicted in Figs. 9(d)–9(f), the wide bandgap (~2.7 eV) of In2O3 enables effective charge separation, with electrons transferred to In2O3 and holes to BFO within the BFO/In2O3 heterojunction [177]. This system also benefits from reduced electron‒hole recombination, ensuring efficient charge migration and increased piezocatalytic activity under mechanical stress. The above examples underscore the enhancement of electronic transport pathways attained through integrating BFO with diverse wide bandgap materials, fostering efficient charge separation, and markedly elevating piezocatalytic performance.

#### 4.2.2 Combining BFO with polymer scaffolds

Combining BFO with polymer scaffolds enhances its piezocatalytic performance through improved mechanical flexibility, charge separation, and surface area. Polymer scaffolds facilitate effective mechanical stress transfer to BFO, ensuring uniform stress distribution and more consistent piezoelectric charge generation, which is critical for piezocatalysis [190]. Additionally, polymer matrices can reduce charge recombination by physically separating piezo-generated electrons and holes, with conductive or semiconducting polymers further promoting charge transport [191]. Moreover, the porous structure of scaffolds increases the effective surface area of BFO, providing more active sites for catalytic reactions and enhancing reactant diffusion [192]. These factors collectively improve the piezocatalytic efficiency of BFO under mechanical stimulation.

In the case of BFO/chitosan (BCH) films shown in Fig. 9(g), the biocompatibility and flexibility of chitosan enable efficient stress transfer to BFO during mechanical deformation [193]. The natural polymer structure of chitosan allows for a uniform stress distribution, resulting in more consistent piezoelectric charge generation. Additionally, chitosan has inherent antibacterial properties and can act as a reactive matrix for certain catalytic reactions, further broadening the range of applications for BCH composites in piezocatalysis. In addition, the BFO–PVDF–HFP membrane was fabricated [192], as shown in Fig. 9(h). The strong piezoelectric nature of BFO–polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF)–hexafluoropropylene (HFP) amplifies the overall piezoelectric response of the composite, significantly increasing the mechanical-to-electrical energy conversion. Its porous structure also increases the catalytic surface area, providing more active sites for catalytic reactions and enhancing reactant interactions. Furthermore, the BFO–PVDF–HFP membrane enhances the flexibility and durability of the composite, enabling efficient long-term application of mechanical stress and charge generation, which underscores its potential for purifying industrial wastewater, as presented in Fig. 9(i) [192]. The combination of BFO with polymer scaffolds enhances its piezocatalytic performance by optimizing mechanical stress transfer, improving charge separation, and increasing surface area, all of which contribute to more efficient catalytic reactions under mechanical stimulation.

#### 4.2.3 Surface modification engineering

Surface modification engineering significantly impacts piezocatalytic performance by optimizing surface properties to enhance charge separation, reaction kinetics, and overall catalytic efficiency.

Cocatalyst engineering: Cocatalyst engineering enhances piezocatalytic performance by improving charge separation, lowering the activation energy, and accelerating surface reaction kinetics [179]. Cocatalysts act as electron or hole acceptors, reducing charge recombination and facilitating redox reactions. They also provide additional active sites, increasing reaction rates while lowering activation barriers for specific processes such as water splitting or CO₂ reduction [194]. In some cases, cocatalysts with piezoelectric properties create a synergistic effect, further increasing charge generation [195]. Additionally, cocatalysts enhance selectivity in redox reactions and improve the overall stability and durability of the piezocatalytic system [158]. Figures 10(a)–10(d) illustrate the impact of incorporating Pd as a cocatalyst in BFO to enhance the piezocatalytic performance [196]. BFO/Pd nanocomposites were synthesized via hydrothermal methods (Fig. 10(a)). Pd efficiently accepts electrons, extending charge carrier lifetimes and improving redox reaction efficiency, particularly for hydrogen evolution. Modulating the Pd loading influences H2 production, with the optimal performance achieved at a 4.8% Pd ratio (Fig. 10(b)). Figures 10(c) and 10(d) exhibit that the excellent electrical conductivity of Pd aids in faster electron transfer, promoting more efficient redox reactions. The synergistic effect between the piezoelectric properties of BFO and the catalytic capabilities of Pd increases the overall catalytic performance, especially under mechanical stress, making the BFO/Pd system highly effective for water splitting.

#### 4.2.4 Piezo-photocatalysis coupling

Piezo-photocatalysis enhances piezocatalytic performance through the synergistic effects of mechanical and light energy, resulting in enhanced charge separation and transfer [200,201]. Furthermore, Han *et al*. [202,203] noted that the combined activation from mechanical and light stimuli increased the number of reactive sites, thereby enhancing surface reactivity. The integration of ultrasonic vibrations with light irradiation further boosts the catalytic efficiency by improving the catalyst–reactant contact [34].

As illustrated in Fig. 11(a), mechanical stress induces a piezoelectric effect that generates an internal electric field, promoting the separation of photoexcited electron–hole pairs and reducing recombination [147]. This synergy optimizes charge transport and accelerates redox reactions, resulting in superior catalytic performance compared with that of individual piezocatalytic or photocatalytic processes (Fig. 11(b)) [120]. A favorable polarization direction enhances surface potential gradients, activating additional reactive sites and thus maximizing the synergy between piezoelectricity and photocatalysis (Figs. 11(c) and 11(d)) [187]. Hence, this multi-field coupling process of light irradiation and mechanical stimuli significantly elevates piezocatalytic activity, particularly in complex reactions, highlighting its potential for practical applications in environmental remediation and energy conversion.

## 5 Applications of BFO in piezocatalysis

Since the introduction of piezocatalysis technology in 2010, numerous applications for environmental remediation and energy conversion have emerged [58]. Notably, BFO has emerged as a standout material in the field of piezocatalysis. This section highlights several representative applications of BFO in piezocatalytic processes.

### 5.1 Piezocatalytic organic pollutant degradation

Water pollution represents a significant environmental challenge, primarily driven by various organic pollutants, including dyes and antibiotic residues. Dyes, which are prevalent in textile waste, are often resistant to conventional treatment methods, while the presence of antibiotics poses a risk of developing resistant microorganisms. Piezocatalysis has emerged as a promising solution for the treatment of these pollutants [44,204]. Under mechanical stimuli, piezoelectric materials generate electric charges that promote the formation of reactive species, such as hydroxyl radicals and superoxide radicals [205]. These radicals are essential for the oxidation and degradation of complex organic molecules, effectively addressing both dye and antibiotic contamination [64,89]. This process not only reduces the ambient concentration of organic pollutants but also diminishes the potential for antibiotic resistance development in microbial populations.

BFO has garnered significant attention in the field of piezocatalysis because of its effectiveness in degrading organic pollutants [40]. In the context of dye degradation shown in Fig. 12(a), BFO has demonstrated remarkable performance in breaking down various dye molecules, such as methylene blue (MB), rhodamine B (RhB), and methyl orange (MO) [80]. The generated radicals interact with dye molecules, facilitating their oxidative degradation into less harmful byproducts such as CO2 and H2O. In particular, Fig. 12(b) presents an experimental setup for degrading RhB with fixed BiFeO3–BaTiO3 (BF−BT) nanoparticles on nickel foam, showing the effective utilization of low-frequency mechanical power for piezocatalysis [162]. Furthermore, as depicted in Figs. 12(c) and 12(d), BFO has also proven effective in the degradation of antibiotic residues, such as carbamazepine (CBZ), tetracycline (TC), ciprofloxacin (CIP), and dexamethasone (DXM) [172,206]. Notably, as shown in Fig. 12(c), the residual BFO-based catalysts could be magnetically separated and recovered from the reacted suspension [172]. Table 2 summarizes the piezocatalytic decomposition performance of various BFO-based piezocatalysts. The piezocatalytic performance of BFO, particularly in the degradation of persistent organic pollutants in wastewater, can be further optimized via morphology modulation, heterojunction construction, solid solution construction, elemental doping, etc.

### 5.2 Piezocatalytic H2 generation

The ongoing energy crisis, characterized by the depletion of fossil fuels and rising greenhouse gas emissions, has necessitated a global transition toward sustainable energy sources. H2 energy is viewed as a clean and renewable alternative, offering significant benefits, including zero carbon emissions during combustion and diverse applications in transportation, industry, and power generation [207]. In this context, piezocatalysis has surfaced as a promising method for H2 production, utilizing mechanical stress to increase the catalytic activity of piezoelectric materials [58,208]. When subjected to external forces, these materials generate electric charges that facilitate the formation of reactive species, promoting the efficient splitting of water molecules into H2 and O2 [65].

BFO has demonstrated potential for H2 production because of its favorable piezocatalytic properties, which enable the generation of high concentrations of reactive species under mechanical stress [37,136,187]. The first application of piezocatalytic H2 generation using BFO nanosheets was reported in 2019, yielding H2 at a rate of 124.1 µmol·g−1 for 1 h when Na2SO3 served as a scavenger, as shown in Fig. 13(a) [37]. In 2021, Sun *et al*. [162] demonstrated that a remarkable H2 production rate of 1.322 mmol g−1 was achieved in 1 h when lead-free 0.7BFO–0.3BTO (BF–BT) solid solution nanoparticles were used when methyl alcohol served as a scavenger, as illustrated in Fig. 13(b). Moreover, the underlying mechanism indicates that the favorable band structure along with the strong internal electric field generated by mechanical vibrations promotes the movement of electrons to the surface of the nanoparticles. This interaction enables the absorption of hydrogen ions from the solution, ultimately facilitating H2 generation. In 2024, Wang *et al*. [123] revealed that BFO-NS significantly outperformed BFO-NP in H2 generation, achieving a yield of 619.2 μmol·g−1·h−1 compared with 410.2 μmol·g−1·h−1 for BFO-NP (Fig. 13(c)), both measured at a frequency of 45 kHz without a scavenger. Additionally, experiments evaluating piezocatalytic H2 generation with various scavengers confirmed that the highest hydrogen yield of 2598.6 μmol·g−1·h−1 was achieved when triethanolamine was used as the scavenger, surpassing yields obtained with ethanol, methanol, and Na2SO3 [123]. The piezocatalytic H2 generation performance of various BFO-based piezocatalysts is listed in Table 3. Piezocatalytic optimization strategies, such as morphology modulation, elemental doping, solid solution construction, surface modification, and heterostructure construction, can play a vital role in increasing the piezocatalytic H2 generation performance of BFO-based piezocatalysts. In addition, the sacrificial agent is also a key factor for designing advanced BFO-based piezocatalytic H2 production systems. Taking the above H2 production rates into account, BFO represents a leading candidate for advancing piezocatalytic technologies in H2 energy production.

### 5.3 Piezocatalytic H2O2 generation

H2O2 is an indispensable chemical with a wide range of applications, functioning as a disinfectant, bleaching agent, and oxidizing agent in numerous industrial processes. Its importance is further amplified in environmental remediation, where it effectively degrades pollutants, and in the medical field for sterilization [209,210]. The increasing demand for sustainable and efficient H2O2 production methods has sparked considerable interest in piezocatalysis as an innovative synthesis approach [211]. This technique exploits piezoelectric materials that generate electric charges under mechanical stress, thereby enhancing catalytic activity. By promoting the reaction between oxygen and water, piezocatalysis substantially increases the efficiency of H2O2 production, paving the way for its broader application [212].

BFO has emerged as a standout candidate for piezocatalytic H2O2 production because of its exceptional piezoelectric characteristics [137]. The UV–Vis absorbance spectra shown in Fig. 14(a) demonstrate that BFO nanosheets can achieve impressive H2O2 yields, which improve with increasing ultrasonic vibration time, highlighting their potential for use in eco-friendly and efficient H2O2 synthesis [81]. Figures 14(b)–14(d) present the hydrothermal methods used to modulate BFO NSs for H2O2 generation [137]. The BFO-12h sample exhibited a superior H2O2 generation efficiency via piezocatalysis, reaching 110.07 mmol·g−1·h−1 in pure water without a sacrificial agent. Moreover, it maintained a high H2O2 yield of 107.12 mmol·g−1·h−1 after four recycling processes, indicating good durability. In particular, the mechanism illustrated in Fig. 14(e) reveals an innovative dual-channel pathway for H2O2 generation. This fascinating route comprises two synergistic channels: The first channel facilitates the electron reduction of O2, while the second enables the hole oxidation of water. This intricate dual-channel approach not only amplifies the H2O2 production capacity but also highlights the harmonious interplay of reactions that enhances the overall efficiency of synthesis. Furthermore, a comparative H2O2 generation rate under photocatalytic, piezocatalytic, and photo-piezocatalytic activities for BFO-based catalysts is shown in Fig. 14(f) [213]. The findings highlight that the peak H2O2 generation attained was 178.7 mmol·g−1·h−1 with BiOCl/BiVO4/BiFeO3 (BCVF) under photo-piezocatalysis. Remarkably, reusability tests revealed that BCVF sustained a robust production rate of 157.8 mmol·g−1·h−1 across five consecutive cycles, underscoring its exceptional efficiency and stability. Accordingly, the H2O2 generation durability of BFO-based piezocatalytic systems is well established, but their production can be further improved via photo-piezocatalysis coupling, morphology design, charge transport regulation, etc. Overall, BFO has served as a promising material in advancing piezocatalytic technologies for H2O2 production, paving the way for groundbreaking applications across diverse fields.

### 5.4 Piezocatalytic CO2 reduction

Piezocatalytic CO2 reduction is an emerging approach that utilizes the distinctive properties of piezoelectric materials to convert CO2 into valuable chemicals or fuels [67,68]. This process relies on the generation of electric charges under mechanical stress, which enhances catalytic activity and facilitates the activation of CO2 molecules [32]. When subjected to mechanical stress, piezoelectric materials create an internal electric field that lowers the energy barrier for chemical reactions, promoting the formation of reactive species crucial for CO2 reduction [69,214]. The products of CO2 reduction can vary widely and often yield CO, methanol, or hydrocarbons, which serve as energy carriers or chemical feedstocks [67,84,215]. Nevertheless, as depicted in Fig. 15(a), He *et al*. [81] emphasized that UV-activated wide bandgap materials, including LaTiO3, BaTiO3, and SrTiO3, possessed sufficiently negative conduction band minimum (CBM) positions, facilitating the conversion of CO2 into reduction products via a photo-driven mechanism [81]. Conversely, the CBM positions of commonly used narrow bandgap perovskite oxides, such as BFO, LaNiO3, and LaFeO3, lack the thermodynamic feasibility required for effective CO2 reduction under their pristine band structures. Hence, the mechanically induced piezoelectric polarization provided a feasible route for breaking the intrinsic energy barriers of BFO to catalyze CO2 reduction via energy band tilting (Fig. 15(b)). Figure 15(c) illustrates that the yield of products in CO2 reduction peaks at 1.0 g·L−1 BFO nanosheets.

The piezo-photocatalytic mechanism highlights that BFO creates a time-varying electrostatic potential that drives electron flow to the Co–N–C interface. This process enhances the generation of photogenerated carriers and promotes the separation of electron holes, directing more electrons to active sites and thereby facilitating CO2 reduction. Notably, the Co–N–C@BFO catalysts demonstrate a remarkable increase in performance compared to BFO and Co–N–C individually, with Co–N–C@BFO (1 : 7) emerging as the most effective for CO2 reduction (Fig. 15(f)). This optimal molar ratio of 1 : 7 between Co–N–C and BFO highlights its superior ability to drive piezo-photocatalytic CO2 reduction, demonstrating a significant improvement in catalytic efficiency.

Interestingly, He *et al*. [81] highlighted that the CBM positions of BFO, LaNiO3, and LaFeO3 lacked sufficient thermodynamic feasibility for CO2 reduction under light stimuli (Fig. 15(a)). In contrast, Xu *et al*. [171] reported notable photocatalytic CO2 reduction yields for both pristine BFO and oxygen-deficient BiFeO3−*x*, as shown in Fig. 15(g). Moreover, as shown in Figs. 15(h) and 15(i), higher yields are achieved through piezocatalysis, with optimal results obtained via piezo-photocatalysis, illustrating a clear synergistic effect that enhances the catalytic performance. Certainly, this piezocatalytic CO2 reduction is reasonable and thermodynamically favorable due to energy band tilting under mechanical stress. Next, the following question arises: how is this photocatalytic CO2 reduction realized using BFO? This is a very intriguing phenomenon that deserves deep consideration and research. Subsequently, a fascinating ferroelectric characteristic of BFO is that it has a large polarization of approximately 100 μC·cm−2 [116]. Additionally, Yu *et al*. [216] and Wu *et al*. [217] noted that oxygen vacancies could exert a pinning effect on ferroelectric domains that helped to maintain superb ferroelectric polarization to trigger catalytic reactions. These two factors may provide novel insights into tackling the above-mentioned key challenges in photocatalytic CO2 reduction with BFO, illustrating the underestimated effect of ferroelectric polarization in enhancing CO2 photoreduction performance.

Hence, the specific energy band tilting effect makes CO2 reduction thermodynamically favorable within BFO-based piezocatalytic systems. In addition, oxygen vacancies and ferroelectric polarization can have a synergistic effect on BFO-based piezocatalytic or photo-piezocatalytic systems.

### 5.5 Piezocatalytic sterilization

The increasing prevalence of antibiotic-resistant pathogens underscores the urgent need for innovative sterilization methods, particularly in water treatment and biomedical applications [218,219]. Piezocatalysis has emerged as a promising approach, utilizing piezoelectric materials to generate electric charges under mechanical stress, yielding ROS effectively for inactivating microorganisms [220]. In water treatment, this method is adopted at targeting pathogens such as *Escherichia coli* and *Staphylococcus aureus*, thereby significantly improving water safety and quality [44,221]. In the biomedical realm, piezocatalysis can be applied to sterilize infection sites or inflamed tissues within the body using ultrasound-assisted techniques, effectively combating bacteria such as *S. aureus* at the source of infection [70,222]. The ongoing development of piezocatalytic technologies holds significant potential for enhancing sterilization processes in water disinfection and biomedical applications.

Recently, considerable studies have substantiated the promising practical application prospects of BFO in piezocatalytic sterilization [82]. Figures 16(a)–16(c) show intuitive *in vitro* *S. aureus* bacterial eradication using BFO-based piezocatalysts [193]. The recorded images in Fig. 16(a) visualize the antibacterial process and show apparent *S. aureus* inactivation. The bacterial mortality data (Fig. 16(b)) and the fluorescence intensity of DCF (signifying ROS generation during the experiment, Fig. 16(c)) verified the optimal piezocatalytic sterilization activity achieved with BCH7.5 (7.5% BFO doped in biopolymeric chitosan). Moreover, *in vitro* *E. coli* bacteria eradication studies have also been reported, as shown in Figs. 16(d)–16(f). Roy *et al*. [39] ablated pathogenic *E. coli* bacteria by employing a gadolinium-doped BFO (GBF) piezocatalyst. The images in Fig. 16(d) visually illustrate the effective piezocatalytic *E. coli* ablation of GBFs under ultrasound (US) irradiation, which almost completely eliminates the whole bacteria in 30 min. Meanwhile, the time-dependent log degradation of CFU/mL *E. coli* (Fig. 16(e)) and fluorescence intensity (Fig. 16(f)) further confirmed this process statistically. Since piezocatalytic bacterial eradication *in vitro* has been confirmed, the antibacterial research on piezocatalysis *in vivo* has been further proposed. In 2023, Li *et al*. [223] reported a rapid and effective therapeutic strategy to treat osteomyelitis through interfacial engineering of ferro-piezoelectric polarization on BFO/MXene (Ti3C2) triggered by the US. The US-responsive BFO/Ti3C2 had an effective antibacterial effect of 99.87%±0.05% against *S. aureus* in 20 min for treating bacterium-infected osteomyelitis under US irradiation. Micron-scale computed tomography (micro-CT), as shown in Fig. 16(g), was used to examine bone growth and revealed the highest bone volume (14.71%±1.93%) in the BFO/Ti3C2-US group compared with the control (9.47%±1.90%) and BFO/Ti3C2 (8.91%±1.25%) groups, revealing the effectiveness of therapy for deep tissue infection. The successful application of *in vitro* and *in vivo* BFO-based piezocatalytic sterilization proved the significant promise of BFO in biomedical applications.

## 6 Summary and perspectives

Piezocatalysis has presented promise in various applications, as it shows highly efficient performance in the conversion of mechanical energy and electrochemical energy. The unique piezoelectric effect, induced by mechanical stimuli, generates an internal piezoelectric field, which promotes the separation of free carriers and triggers the generation of ROS to initiate redox reactions. This concept endows piezocatalysis technology a promising candidate for environmental remediation and energy conversion since mechanical energy is a type of endless power. Notably, BFO stands out as a star piezocatalyst for its integration of piezoelectric, multiferroic, and optical properties. This review summarizes the historical development of BFO in piezocatalysis. Significantly, the dominant piezocatalytic mechanisms, including energy band theory, screening charge effect, and displacement current theory, are critically discussed and analyzed, shedding light on the complicated interactions between internal charges, screening charges, and electrons generated by the piezoelectric effect that drives piezocatalytic reactions. In addition, a detailed overview of the structural characteristics and synthesis methods of BFO is provided, revealing the relationship between its structure and performance. Furthermore, optimization strategies to enhance piezocatalytic performance are exemplified by improving piezoelectric properties and modulating charge transport. In addition, this review highlights the superior piezocatalytic activity of BFO in diverse applications, such as organic pollutant degradation, H2 generation, H2O2 production, CO2 reduction, and sterilization, encapsulating its attractive piezoelectric characteristics and underscoring the underestimated ferroelectric polarization effect on CO2 reduction. Despite the promising catalytic performance of BFO-based piezocatalysts, several challenges remain to be addressed. Key issues include synthesis, catalytic efficiency, long-term stability, and the adaptability of these materials in practical applications. Future research should focus on the following aspects:

**Synthesis and scalability:** Current synthesis methods for BFO face challenges in controlling purity and phase stability, especially when scaling up for large-scale applications. Overcoming these bottlenecks, optimizing phase purity, or developing cost-effective scalable production methods will be a critical direction for future research.

**Adaptability in real-world applications:** While BFO-based catalysts perform well in laboratory settings, their integration into large-scale systems or real-world applications remains a significant challenge. Specifically, adapting BFO-based systems for energy harvesting and environmental remediation applications, where efficiency and scalability are critical, will require overcoming several technical barriers. Moreover, the multiple-field coupling of mechanics, optics, and magnetism should be further exploited and developed in response to the distinctive integration characteristics of BFO.

**Catalytic efficiency:** Piezocatalytic efficiency is generally lower than that of other catalytic technologies, such as photocatalysis and electrocatalysis. Thus, enhancing the piezoelectric response and improving electron transport within the system are critical to increasing the overall catalytic efficiency. Future studies could focus on enhancing BFO-based catalytic efficiency through hybridization with other materials (e.g., conductive polymers or semiconductors) or by designing nanostructures to increase surface activity.

**More application scenarios should be discovered:** BFO-based piezocatalysts can be incorporated into self-powered environmental monitoring systems to detect pollutants or toxins in water and air. These systems can operate in remote or resource-limited regions by utilizing ambient mechanical energy. In addition, BFO-based piezocatalysts could also be embedded into textiles or wearable devices, enabling them to perform dual functions, such as energy harvesting from movement and simultaneous environmental cleaning or pollutant degradation. Furthermore, the ability of piezocatalysts to harvest ultrasonic waves or mechanical stress makes them suitable for underwater pollutant degradation or oxygen production in deep-sea habitats where sunlight is scarce but mechanical energy is abundant.

Overall, this review addresses critical gaps in the understanding and practical implementation of BFO as a piezocatalyst, emphasizing the integration of advanced materials engineering approaches. By demonstrating BFO’s ability to harness mechanical energy efficiently, this study provides a foundation for future research and inspires innovations in the design of next-generation piezocatalysts.
