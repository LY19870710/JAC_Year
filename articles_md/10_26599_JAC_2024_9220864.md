## 1 Introduction

Thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) are widely used in gas turbine engines to obtain elevated working temperatures and improve engine efficiency [1−5]. TBCs consist of multilayered structures, including a top ceramic coat (TC), bond coat (BC), and superalloy substrate (SUB) [6,7]. The failure of TBCs is generally attributed to the following factors such as the rapid growth of thermally grown oxide (TGO) [8], phase transformation within ceramic layers [9−11], and thermal expansion misfit between multilayered structures [12,13], leading to the accumulation of the thermal stress (*σ*) in coatings, especially at the interface region. The phase transition of the ceramic layer is of significant importance as commonly used TBCs. Also, 6–8 wt% yttria partially stabilized zirconia (YSZ) will be transformed into a mixture of tetragonal phase (t) and cubic phase (c) at higher temperatures (> 1200 ℃). During cooling, the t phase experiences a tetragonal–monoclinic (t–m) phase transformation accompanied by a large volume difference, causing TBCs to fall off and fail [14−17]. This may be due to the generation of thermal stress resulting from the phase transformation in the ceramic layer. Therefore, it is necessary to quantitatively evaluate the magnitude and distribution of *σ* induced by the phase transition in the ceramic layer [18−20]. However, it is challenging to directly and quantitatively evaluate the magnitude and distribution of *σ* by experiments. Raman microscopy mapping [8,21] and photoluminescence spectroscopy combined with terahertz time-domain spectroscopy technologies [22,23] successfully captured the stress distribution caused by phase transformations in multilayer systems. However, as an *in situ* testing method, Raman spectroscopy has stringent requirements for experimental environments and conditions that limit its application scope [24]. Meanwhile, this method also lacks efficacy in capturing dynamic evolution of stress during the phase transition. The empirical model can provide a straightforward and efficient approach for determining thermal stress, mainly aimed at assessing the interface of heterogeneous structures [25] and two-phase structures [26,27]. This empirical model solely focuses on the thermal stress resulting from thermal expansion coefficients (TECs) misfit and fails to visualize the thermal stress distribution, neglecting the real structure and service environment of TBCs. The finite element model (FEM) is an effective tool for visualizing and quantitatively evaluating thermal stress. Lim *et al*. [28] constructed an FEM model to evaluate the thermal stress of TBC under operating conditions, leading to optimal design criteria for minimizing thermal stress across a wide range of TBC systems. FEM was also conducted to clarify the influence of the martensite transformation in a bond coat on the development of stress in a multilayered system during thermal cycling [29], and the results show that the top ceramic coat stress is greatly influenced by the presence of the martensite transformation. However, the thermophysical properties required for FEM, including thermal conductivity (*κ*), TECs, heat capacity (*c**p*), density (*ρ*), Poisson’s ratios (*ν*), and Young’s modulus (*E*), are derived from experimental measurements, which ignores effects of the phase transition and temperature.

Multiscale simulation methods have been widely utilized in material science to study fundamental properties and phenomena, providing fresh insight for material design [24,30,31]. Cross-scale simulations are established by coupling density functional theory (DFT), micromagnetism, and FEM to explore the coupling of atomic orbital and magnetic moment [30]. The combination of DFT and FEM has proven efficient for investigating the evolution of hydrogen-induced intergranular cracking, where the grain boundary cohesive energy calculated from DFT was used as inputs for the fracture energy in FEM [24]. Singh *et al*. [31] successfully designed a Pt-functionalized graphene-based field effect transistor by DFT-coupled FEM, in which electronic and surface properties calculated by DFT were used as the input for FEM to determine transport properties of the device. Unfortunately, the new concept of multiscale simulation has not yet played a role in the field of life prediction in TBCs.

Here, we develop a high-throughput multiscale evaluation method for *σ* in multilayered systems, which considers the phase transition of top ceramic materials by coupling the first-principles calculations with finite element simulations. This approach can quantitatively evaluate and visualize the thermal stress in TBCs based on real structures, considering the actual service environment subjected to thermal cycling. The thermophysical properties input in FEM are calculated by the first-principles calculations, in which the multiscale method can consider the influence of the phase transition and temperature and simultaneously reduce cost and time of obtaining thermophysical properties by experiments. Our previous studies found that rare earth tantalates (RETaO4) possessed outstanding toughness induced by ferroelastic characteristics, thermal insulation performance (*κ* = 1.5 W∙K−1∙m−1, 900 ℃) and matching TECs (9.73×10−6–10.6×10−6 K−1, 1200 ℃) [32−38]. Herein, RETaO4 has been widely considered as candidate TBC materials. In this work, taking RETaO4 (RE = Nd, Sm, Gd, Dy, Ho, Er) with the ferroelastic phase transition as an example, this multiscale evaluation method for *σ* is successfully implemented, and the distribution of *σ* considering phase transition during heating/cooling is obtained. The results provide an important theoretical basis and guidance for life prediction and reverse design of coating materials.

### 2.1 Details of first-principles calculations

The first-principles calculations are performed using the plane wave basis and projector augmented wave (PAW) method [39] as implemented in the Vienna *Ab initio* Simulation Package code (VASP) [40]. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation function is used [41]. The kinetic energy cutoff value of 520 eV is used for plane wave expansion in a reciprocal space [39], where the *k* mesh with separation of 0.03 Å−1 according to Monkhorst–Pack method is adopted in the first Brillouin zone [42,43]. Lattice constants and internal atomic coordinates are fully relaxed. The convergence criterion for electronic optimizations is 1×10−8 eV per atom for total energy, and the force per atom isreduced to 1×10−3 eV∙Å−1.

Lattice dynamic calculation is carried out using the Phonopy package [44,45]. The second-order interatomic force constant (IFC) matrix is calculated within the framework of density function perturbation theory (DFPT) [46]. The samplings of the *k* mesh are 3 × 3 × 3 for a supercell with 96 atoms of polyphase RETaO4. The *q* mesh of the phonon density of states (DOS) is 20 × 20 × 20.

(10):

(10) γ=12(1+B′)+x

Thermodynamic properties, e.g., volume, *c**p*, and TECs, are obtained by Debye model, and the temperature dependence elastic modulus is estimated based on the quasi-static approximation (QSA) [60]. First, the static elastic stiffness coefficients (*C**ij*) at 0 K as a function of the volume using a stress–strain approach, and then the equilibrium volume (*V*(*T*)) at the given *T* is calculated using Debye model. Finally, *C**ij* at the volume *V*(*T*) are predicted as those at the finite temperature.

(11) κ=AMθD3δγ2n2/3T(T⩾θD)

where *δ* (m3) is the average volume of one atom in the unit cell, *T* (K) is the absolute temperature, *n* is the number of atoms per primitive cell, and *A* (W∙mol∙kg−1∙m−2∙K−3) is a coefficient determined by *γ*. Details about these parameters are available in the previous work [62].

### 2.3 Details of finite element method

Theoretical model: In this work, the COMSOL Multiphysics software is used for simulating the temperature and stress fields under a cooling/heating process. The heat transfer equation is expressed as Eq. (12):

(12) ρcp∂T∂x=∂∂x(κ∂T∂x)+∂∂y(κ∂T∂y)+∂∂z(κ∂T∂z)+ρQ

where *ρ*, *c**p*, *Q*, and *κ* are the density, the heat capacity, the heat, and the thermal conductivities, respectively. The coordinates *x*, *y*, and *z* represent the relative coordinates of the subsystem. The relationship between the stress and the strain can be written as Eq. (13):

(13) ε=Eσ

where *ε* is the strain.

Geometry and meshed model: A two-dimensional FEM model shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) is established in COMSOL to visualize the thermal stress distribution of TBCs under the cooling/heating process. The TBCs model consists of four parts, namely TC, TGO, BC, and SUB layers. RETaO4, Al2O3, NiCoCrAlY, and GH4099 represent these four layers. Because of close to the actual microstructure and shape, the TBCs model depicted adopts a sinusoidal model for the TGO layer [63−65]. The free quadrilateral grid is used to divide the model. The grid should be especially refined for the thin BC layer and the TGO layer.

Boundary conditions: To assess the distribution of *σ* caused by the phase transitions, two parts are simulated: the cooling process (t→m phase transitions) and the heating process (m'→t phase transitions). In the cooling process, the temperature of the ceramic top (*T*hot) drops from 1973 to 1773 K after 30 s, while *T*hot rises from 1573 to 1773 K in the heating process. The final *T*hot is always set to 1773 K, which is the expected operating temperature of TBCs, and the temperature at the cooling end (*T*cool) is always 873 K. According to the literature on gas turbines [66], the surface convection heat transfer coefficient is set at 1900 W·m−2·K−1. To ensure that the model does not move and rotate during the calculation, the degree of freedom of the model is constrained, specifically, the lower left corner is completely fixed, and the lower right corner is partially fixed. The thermal stress in the initial state is set to 0.

Material properties: The corresponding input data of TGO, BC, and SUB can be obtained from Refs. [67,68]. Also, the properties of materials with temperature of the RETaO4 ceramic layer are obtained from the first-principles calculation in this work.

### 3.1 Crystal structure

RETaO4 possesses three crystalline structures (Fig. S2 in the ESM): a monoclinic phase (m-RETaO4, space Group *C*2/*c*), a monoclinic-prime phase (m'-RETaO4, space group *P*2/*a*), and a tetragonal phase (t-RETaO4, space group *I*41/*a*). The conventional unit cell of m-RETaO4 and t-RETaO4 consists of four formula units (24 atoms), while m'-RETaO4 accommodates only two formula units (12 atoms). Within the unit cell of these three structures, each RE atom forms a [REO8] polyhedron with eight oxygen atoms. In m-RETaO4, the arrangement of RE, RE, Ta, and Ta atoms occurs alternately in pairs along the [010] direction, whereas the order is Ta, RE, Ta, and RE atoms along the *b*-axis in the m'-RETaO4 system. The atomic arrangement in the t-RETaO4 system aligns with that of the m phase, but there is a displacement of 0.25 times than the lattice constant along the *b*-axis. According to symmetry theory based on space groups, the 4e and 8f Wyckoff positions are occupied by the cation Ta/Y and the anion O in m-RETaO4, and Ta atoms, Y atoms, and O atoms are located at the 2f, 2e, and 4g Wyckoff positions in m'-RETaO4, respectively, while Y, Ta, and O atoms in t-RETaO4 occupy the 4b, 4a, and 16f Wyckoff positions, respectively.

The calculated equilibrium lattice parameters (*a*, *b*, *c*), lattice dihedral angle (*β*), and equilibrium volume (*V*) summarized in Table 1 indicate that the calculated lattice parameters are slightly larger than the experimental values [33,38], which may be due to the overestimation of lattice parameters by the GGA exchange-correlation functional [69]. The results indicate that the lattice constant gradually decreases as the ionic radius of RE3+ decreases. Additionally, the volume of t-RETaO4 is slightly larger than that of m-RETaO4 and m'-RETaO4.

It is demonstrated that the RETaO4 ceramics undergo the phase transformation during the heating and cooling process. Figure 2(a) shows a schematic diagram illustrating the multiphase transition of RETaO4. Specifically, during heating, the m' phase transforms to the t phase when the phase transition temperature (T*t) is exceeded. This transformation is accompanied by a change from approximately 97° to 90° in the lattice dihedral angle (*β*) and a doubling of the lattice constant of the *c*-axis. During the cooling stage, the tetragonal–monoclinic (t–m) phase transformation will occur when the temperature falls below *T*t, resulting in a change in *β* from 90° to approximately 95°. Moreover, some studies [35,76] have focused on determining *T*t of RETaO4. According to Stubičan’s work [76], the formation temperatures of the tetragonal phase in RETaO4 (RE = Nd, Sm, Gd, and Ho) systems were 1600, 1660, 1677, and 1681 K. Moreover, Stubičan [76] observed that *T*t increases as the ionic radius of RE3+ (*r*) decreases until reaching a threshold, beyond which *T*t plateaus, specifically when *r* is smaller than that of Gd3+. The data and polynomial fitting curve between *T*t and RE3+ ionic radius is shown in Fig. 2(b). Based on the fitting curve, *T*t of DyTaO4 and ErTaO4 can be estimated at 1680 and 1681 K, respectively. Because *T*t of RETaO4 with a small RE3+ ion radius is relatively flat, the precision of the *T*t values of DyTaO4 and ErTaO4 estimated in this work is considered acceptable.

### 3.2 Thermophysical properties of polymorphs RETaO<sub>4</sub>

The thermophysical properties of polymorph YTaO4 obtained from the phonon model and Debye model, including TECs and *c**p*, are estimated to verify the accuracy of Debye method, as shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM. The calculated values are found to be consistent with the experimental values, so in this work, the thermophysical properties of polymorph RETaO4 are calculated by Debye model to save computing resources. It is significant to investigate the variation in the volume and TECs of multiphase RETaO4 before and after the phase transition considering the important influence of their fluctuation on the service life of TBCs [79]. First, Figs. 3(a)–3(f) depict the volume with the temperature of RETaO4. Notably, the volume decreases as the RE atomic number increases, which may be attributed to lanthanide contraction [71]. The m and m' phases exhibit comparable volume values, while the t phase has a slightly higher volume owing to its high-temperature phase.

As illustrated in Figs. 3(g)–3(l), the calculated TECs range for m-RETaO4, m'-RETaO4, and t-RETaO4 at 1600 K are 9.58×10−6–10.46×10−6 K−1, 8.66×10−6–9.09×10−6 K−1, and 9.72×10−6–10.96×10−6 K−1, respectively. Simultaneously, the calculated TECs values agree with the experimental measurements [33,36]. The t phase exhibits the largest TECs value, while the m' phase has the lowest TECs value. The similarity in TECs among polymorph RETaO4 at *T*t helps to prevent coating detachment caused by the TECs mismatch in alternating hot and cold environments. *c**p* is the significant thermodynamic property for accurately evaluating thermal properties. In Figs. 3(m)–3(r), the *c**p* values of the RETaO4 polymorphs rapidly approach a constant value (approximately 28 J∙K−1∙mol−1)). Additionally, the calculated *c**p* values of RETaO4 reproduce the value predicted by Neumann–Kopp rule [80], further validating the accuracy of the calculation method employed in this study.

Lower *E* can reduce the risk of delamination and cracking caused by the thermal stress mismatch, thereby enhancing damage tolerance and fatigue life of TBCs. Therefore, it is essential to examine the variation in *E* during the phase transition of RETaO4. Then, Fig. 4 explicitly depicts the variation in *E* with the temperature for the RETaO4 polymorph. The calculated values of *E* align with those obtained from Ref. [70], confirming the accuracy of the employed method. In the monoclinic phase, *E* gradually increases as the ionic radius of RE3+ decreases, primarily due to volume shrinkage. The tetragonal phase of RETaO4 possesses weaker *E* due to its larger volume and higher TECs compared to the monoclinic phase. This can also be analyzed from a microscopic perspective: the sudden modification in chemical bonds and structural characteristics resulting from the monoclinic to tetragonal phase transformation.

In Fig. 5, the bond length and force constant of polymorphs RETaO4 are presented, providing an atomic-level explanation for the changes in mechanical properties. The larger elastic modulus is influenced by both strong bond strength and high atomic electronegativity [81]. For m-RETaO4, the bond strength and atomic electronegativity gradually increase with an increasing atomic number of RE, which implies that the bond strength and atomic electronegativity jointly contribute to a larger elastic modulus in RETaO4 with a smaller RE3+ ionic radius. However, t-ErTaO4 exhibits the most robust bond strength yet the smallest modulus, suggesting that atomic electronegativity may be the decisive factor in determining the elastic modulus in t-RETaO4 systems [81]. The maximum force constant of Ta–O in monoclinic phase systems is more significant than that in the tetragonal phase system, aligning with the results obtained for elastic modulus.

As a crucial physical property, κ* directly influences the temperature drop gradient of TBCs. It can be obtained by Slack’s model shown in Eq. (8), where *κ* is determined by factors such as density and *ν*. In Fig. 6, the abrupt change in the density at *T*t is much smaller compared to the change in *ν*, thereby confirming the results of the volume and *E* mentioned in Figs. 3 and 4.

Then, as illustrated in Fig. 7, the dependence of *κ* on the temperature during cooling and heating is sufficiently described by Slack’s model. *κ* calculated from Slack’s model conforms well to the results obtained from ShengBTE (ShengBTE package is a solver of the Boltzmann transport equation for phonons to obtain the thermal conductivity) [82] and experimental values under high temperatures [33,38]. The slight deviation in the *κ* value at low temperatures possibly originated from the extra scattering of phonons from inherent defects present in the specimens, such as oxygen vacancy [83], dislocation [83], and domain boundary [84]. *κ* of the RETaO4 polymorph monotonically decreases with increasing the temperature, indicating that the RETaO4 ceramics are mainly transformed through phonons. *κ* basically decreases in the order of m'-RETaO4, m-RETaO4, and t-RETaO4, implying a significant change in *κ* at *T*t.

It is essential to examine physical characteristics of *κ*. In Table S2 in the ESM, some parameters associated with *κ* are presented [85], including the atomic number in the conventional cell (*n*), *ν*, *γ*, density (*ρ* (kg∙m−3)), sound velocity (*v*s, *v*l, and *v*m), and *θ*D. It is observed that the m and m' phases of RETaO4 exhibit similar values of *γ* and *θ*D but conspicuously diverge in terms of *κ*. Specifically, high *κ* in the m' phase may be linked to the inherent complex structure. Poisson’s ratio (*ν*) is a parameter used to classify compounds as brittle or tough. A compound is typically classified as brittle if its *ν* falls below the threshold value of 0.25 [51,86]. The relationship between *ν* and *κ*, as depicted in Fig. 8(a), indicates that both m and m' phases display toughness with *ν* values of 0.3 and 0.289–0.302, respectively. The t-RETaO4 phase demonstrates outstanding toughness with *ν* ranging from 0.36 to 0.38, which probably stems from the significant reduction of shear modulus in the t phase. Undoubtedly, t-RETaO4 with minimal *κ* can provide a significant temperature gradient for coatings, making it more suitable for coating applications. To investigate the underlying reasons for the lowest *κ* in the t phase, the responses of *γ* and *θ*D to *κ* are predicted in Figs. 8(b) and 8(c). t-RETaO4 exhibits the highest value of *γ*. A large *γ* usually indicates the strong inharmonicity of lattice vibrations and strong phonon–phonon scattering, leading to lower *κ*. Figure 8(c) demonstrates that t-RETaO4 has the lowest *θ*D, which may stem from the lowest elastic modulus in the t phase. According to Slack’s model, *κ* is proportional to * θD3, meaning that smaller θ*D leads to lower *κ*. Consequently, compared to the two monoclinic phases, the t phase exhibits higher *γ* and lower *θ*D, directly resulting in its minimum *κ* value.

*
Fig. 8Variation trends in thermal conductivity (κ*) at 1600 K with related parameters of polymorphs RETaO4: (a) Poisson’s ratios (*ν*), (b) Grüneisen parameter (*γ*), and (c) Debye temperature (*θ*D).

Additionally, Fig. 9 depicts the variations in the volume, TECs, *E*, and *κ* of the three phases of RETaO4 at *T*t. The volume shrinkage at *T*t ranges from 1.15% to 2.1% in the cooling process and is accompanied by 0.6%–3% volume expansion near *T*t in the heating process, which is less than the volume mutation (3%–5%) induced by the phase transition in YSZ ceramics, decreasing the risk of coating detachment during thermal cycling. At *T*t, there are some fluctuations in TECs, which increase by 3%–10% during cooling and decrease by 11%–20% during heating. However, there is a significant discontinuity in *E* and *κ* at *T*t, specifically as follows: *E* increases by 27%–78% in the cooling process and decreases by 33%–43% during the heating process. Similarly, *κ* increases by 53%–146% during cooling and decreases by 60%–76% during heating. The lower values of *E* in t-RETaO4 may be attributed to weak bonding and the lower values of *κ* may be due to higher inharmonicity and lower *θ*D. These findings provide valuable theoretical guidance for studying the phase transition of ceramic layers in TBCs.

### 3.3 Distribution of thermal stress mediated by phase transformation

The temperature field and stress field during cooling and heating are simulated and analyzed using FEM. In the COMSOL software, the corresponding material properties of TGO, BC, and SUB can be obtained from Refs. [67,68]. As the TC layer in this work, the thermophysical properties input into FEM of RETaO4 are derived from the first-principles calculation. It is notable that the materials used for the TC layer is different only in this work, and TBCs_RETaO4 simplifies a TBC system consisting of different ceramic layers, e.g., TBCs_NdTaO4 represents a thermal barrier coating system consisting of a NdTaO4 ceramic coating, Al2O3 thermally grown oxide layer, NiCoCrAlY bonding coating and GH4099 superalloy substrates. Initially, the simulated temperature field during cooling and heating is depicted in Figs. S10 and S11 in the ESM, wherein a temperature drop of approximately 200 K is observed across the ceramic top coating. The phase transition temperature from the t phase to the m phase in cooling falls within the range of the ceramic layer. Figure 10 shows the *σ* distribution in TBCs during cooling, and the ceramic layer primarily experiences compressive stress due to its relatively lower TECs compared to substrate materials. Furthermore, the ceramic layer exhibits significant fluctuation in *σ* near the location of *T*t, which may be attributed to the abrupt changes in *E* and *κ* at *T*t. The corresponding compressive stress reaches its maximum value at the peak regions of the TGO layer, increasing the potential risk of crack initiation and propagation. Similar stress discontinuities also occur within the ceramic layer during heating, as shown in Fig. 11, which are also caused by changes in thermophysical properties resulting from the m'–t phase transformation. The TGO layer mainly experiences compressive stress, and the maximum tensile stress is located at the peak region of TGO. Then, the amplitude of the TGO sinusoidal layer in TBCs_NdTaO4 is adjusted to half of the original, and the thermal stress distribution in this modified TBC system is shown in Fig. S12(b) in the ESM. The results show that the maximum tensile stress is reduced from 375 to 340 MPa, and the maximum compressive stress is reduced from 975 to 820 MPa. As shown in Fig. S12(c) in the ESM, the half amplitude of the TGO layer significantly reduces the maximum compressive stress at the TGO position, demonstrating that improving the flatness of the TGO layer is an effective way to reduce the thermal stress in the TBC system, which significantly improves the service life of the coating.

This significant temperature drop can be attributed to the extremely low *κ* of the ceramic layer, which significantly restricts the transfer of heat to the metal base. Notably, TBCs_NdTaO4 and TBCs_SmTaO4 possess the most significant temperature drop gradient due to the low *κ* of their m and m' phases. The result shows that the thermal stress in the ceramic layer changes continuously when the thermophysical properties of the m phase are continuous. Therefore, the abrupt change of the thermal stress in the ceramic layer is caused by the sudden change of phase transition thermophysical properties. Thus, the discontinuity of *σ* in ceramic layer may derive from the abrupt changes in *E* and *κ*. The changes in *σ* before and after the phase transition in the TBCs_NdTaO4 and TBCs_SmTaO4 systems are the most gradual, as indicated in Table S3 in the ESM. During the cooling process, the ceramic layer primarily experiences compressive stress, increasing sharply to tensile stress as it reaches its peak magnitude when passing through the TGO layer. In Fig. 12(d), the TBCs_SmTaO4 system exhibits the minimum *σ* value, while the TBCs_GdTaO4 system consists of the maximum *σ* value. In the heating process, the initial compressive stress in the ceramic layer undergoes a mild shift toward the tensile stress, and then it abruptly changes back to the compressive stress when passing through the TGO layer. In this part, as shown in Fig. 12(h), the TBCs_NdTaO4 system exhibits the minimum *σ* value, while the TBCs_ErTaO4 system consists of the maximum *σ* value. These analysis results align with the distribution map of *σ*, indicating that the TGO layer is the most vulnerable position in TBCs, and the cracks are prone to initiate and propagate here due to significant changes in the stress. Moreover, considering the large temperature drop gradient and low *σ* change caused by the phase transition in the ceramic layer, the TBCs_NdTaO4 and TBCs_SmTaO4 systems are excellent for extending the service lifetime of TBCs.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, this study presents a thermal stress evaluation system for TBCs considering the phase transformation of ceramic layers by integrating first-principles calculations and the finite element method. The key findings and results of the research are as follows:

i) The thermal stress distribution during the heating/cooling cycle is successfully obtained. The thermal stress rapidly increases during the cooling process before and after the phase transition, especially in the TBCs_GdTaO4 system, where the compressive stress increases from 224 to 435 MPa, nearly doubling in magnitude.

ii) The factors contributing to the thermal stress discontinuity are clarified. The volume and thermal expansion coefficient of the RETaO4 ceramics possess weak fluctuations before and after the phase transition, which has little effect on *σ* discontinuity in TBCs. However, in the cooling process, Young’s modulus and the thermal conductivity in *T*t increase by 27%–78% and 53%–146%, respectively, which are the main causes of the thermal stress discontinuity in the ceramic layer.

iii) This work provides valuable guidance for the optimization of coating structures. In TBCs, the maximum compressive stress and tensile stresses occur at the peak region of the thermally grown oxide layer, where cracks are prone to initiate and propagate. Controlling the flatness of the thermally grown oxides is an effective way to reduce the thermal stress while maintaining the original interfacial adhesion.

iv) This work guides the reverse design of low thermal stress coating materials. The TBCs_NdTaO4 and TBCs_SmTaO4 systems possess large temperature gradients and small changes in the thermal stress induced by the phased transition, which is beneficial for extending the service lifetime of TBCs.
