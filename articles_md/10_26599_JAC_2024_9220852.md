## 1 Introduction

Although currently restricted to a niche market, ferroelectric-based memories are expected to have more widespread use in storage-class memory applications, bridging the gap between dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) and storage memory in traditional von Neumann computing systems [1−6]. Furthermore, ferroelectric memories are among the most promising candidates for emerging technologies in computing systems for future paradigms, such as processing-in-memory or neuromorphic computing [4,6−10]. Various types of memory devices, including ferroelectric random-access memory, ferroelectric field-effect transistors (FeFETs), and ferroelectric tunnel junctions (FTJs) based on fluorite-structured ferroelectrics, have been thoroughly researched. The performance of ferroelectric-based memories, such as the conductance level in FTJs and memory window in FeFETs, is typically determined by ferroelectric properties, such as the remanent polarization (*P*r) and coercive voltage (*V*c), respectively. From the perspective of materials science, the ferroelectric properties are significantly affected by the distribution of phase, orientation, impurity, microstructure, and interface quality, all of which can be engineered during processing.

Among many ferroelectric materials, fluorite-structured oxides, such as HfO2, ZrO2, and (Hf,Zr)O2 are gaining significant attention in the ferroelectric-based memories market these days and extensive research has been conducted on ferroelectricity in fluorite-structured oxides [11−13]. Indeed, the HfO2 and ZrO2 dielectrics have already been widely used in commercial products as gate insulators in metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistors and cell capacitors in DRAM [14,15] owing to their advantageous properties, which include a large band gap (5.3–5.8 eV) [16], high dielectric constant (~25) [17], and compatibility with complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor processing. Consequently, the semiconductor industry is devoting efforts to fabricating next-generation semiconductor devices based on ferroelectric (Hf,Zr)O2 owing to its industry-friendly nature.

Although ferroelectric (Hf,Zr)O2 films exhibit stable ferroelectricity in the 10 nm thickness scale, it is necessary to extend this stability to thicknesses below 5 nm to satisfy the performance required for future semiconductor technologies such as fast data storage/processing and high information density [2]. However, scaling down ferroelectric (Hf,Zr)O2 films to sub-5 nm thicknesses poses critical challenges owing to the non-idealities that arise from the electrode/ferroelectric interfaces. Strkalj *et al*. [18] reported that the net polarization could be suppressed in the ultrathin regime of the electrode/ferroelectric interface. The effect of non-idealities is even worse in the case of semiconductor/ferroelectric interfaces. In particular, an unintended low-*k* SiO2 interfacial layer (IL) may inevitably grow during (Hf,Zr)O2 deposition [19−22]. The presence of the low-*k* IL drops the voltage applied to the sample, thereby increasing the overall operation voltage, which deteriorates the energy efficiency and device reliability, such as retention and endurance. Hence, *P*r degrades owing to the high depolarization field, which is induced by the physical separation of *P*r and the lack of compensation charges by the low-*k* IL [23,24]. Furthermore, charge injection caused by high electric fields across the low-*k* SiO2 IL would result in early fatigue during the endurance test [25].

Numerous studies have been conducted to investigate the ferroelectricity in (Hf,Zr)O2 films with thicknesses below 5 nm [26−37]. These studies have shown that in general, *P*r rapidly decreases as the film thickness decreases. However, most of these studies have focused on (Hf,Zr)O2 films with metal–ferroelectric–metal structures rather than metal–ferroelectric–insulator–silicon (MFIS) structures, which are more sensitive to the formation of low-*k* SiO2 IL. Thus, studies are urgently required to address the low-*k* SiO2 IL issues in ultrathin (Hf,Zr)O2 films with MFIS structures to facilitate the design and production of the next-generation semiconductor devices based on ferroelectric (Hf,Zr)O2. Notably, significant progress has been made in achieving the ferroelectricity of (Hf,Zr)O2 films below 5 nm; in particular, Cheema *et al*. [38,39] achieved the ferroelectricity of (Hf,Zr)O2 films with thicknesses of 1 and 0.5 nm, which is equivalent to the unit cell level.

A previous study investigated the effect of different thicknesses of a Ti layer between antiferroelectric-like Hf0.3Zr0.7O2 films and Si substrates [40] and showed that antiferroelectric-like Hf0.3Zr0.7O2 films can exhibit ferroelectric-like *P*r owing to charges trapped in the deep levels at the Hf0.3Zr0.7O2/SiO2 interface [40]. In this study, to reduce the thickness of the unintended low-*k* SiO2 IL and prevent direct contact between the ferroelectric Hf0.5Zr0.5O2 (HZO) and Si, a TiO2 layer was deposited before depositing the HZO film. As a result, *V*c required for the polarization switching of the HZO film decreased by 50.7%. The switchable polarization (2*P*r) increased to 60.2 μC/cm2 for the 5 nm thick HZO film. These results demonstrate that the pre-deposition of Ti to form high-*k* TiO2 IL can be a promising solution for enhancing the ferroelectricity of ultrathin HZO films grown on Si substrates.

## 2 Experimental

Two types of MFIS capacitors were fabricated with Mo/Hf0.5Zr0.5O2/SiO2/Si (MHS) and Mo/Hf0.5Zr0.5O2/TiO2/SiO2/Si (MHTS) stacks. 5% hydrofluoric acid solution was used to first remove the native oxide on heavily doped n-type Si (n+-Si, resistivity of 0.005 Ω·cm). The HZO films (3–5 nm) were deposited directly on the n+-Si substrate by atomic layer deposition (ALD) at a substrate temperature of 280 ℃. [(CH3)(C2H5)N]4Hf (TEMAHf) and [(CH3)(C2H5)N]4Zr (TEMAZr) were used as organometallic precursors, and O3 at concentration of 200 g/cm3 was used as the reactant precursor. For the MHTS capacitor, prior to the HZO deposition, direct current (DC) sputtering was used to deposit a Ti layer at plasma power of 100 W, base pressure of 3×10−6 Torr, and working pressure of 5×10−3 Torr. For both capacitors, after the HZO deposition, Mo was deposited as the top electrode via DC sputtering at base pressure of 3×10−6 Torr, Ar gas flow rate of 11 mL/min, plasma power of 150 W, and working pressure of 8×10−3 Torr. The top Mo electrode with a thickness of 50 nm was patterned using physical masks with a diameter of 200 μm. Annealing was subsequently conducted to crystallize the HZO film under rapid thermal processing (RTP) conditions at temperatures of 500, 600, and 700 ℃ with holding time of 30 s under an N2 atmosphere.

Lab-scale X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements at *θ*–2*θ* coupled (D8 Advanced, Bruker) and grazing incidence (X’pert Pro, PANalytical) geometries were conducted with a Cu Kα X-ray source (*λ* = 1.54 Å). The incidence angle (*θ*) was fixed at 0.5° in the grazing incidence XRD (GIXRD) geometry. A grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurement was conducted at 3C beamline in Pohang light source (PLS)-II with beam energy of 10.35 keV (*λ* = 1.198 Å), incidence angle of ~0.3°, exposure time of 20 s × 3, and sample-to-detector distance (SDD) of ~202 mm. For the GIXRD measurement, a sibling sample was made where a thin Mo electrode of ~10 nm was deposited on the HZO film, followed by the same RTP treatment as that when the MFIS capacitor was fabricated. For the XRD at *θ*–2*θ* coupled geometry and GIWAXS measurements, the top Mo electrode was etched for 5 min at 50 ℃ via modified SC-1 solution (50 : 2 : 1/H2O : 30% H2O2 in H2O : 30% NH4OH in H2O) to expose the top surface of the HZO film in the MFIS capacitor.

A Cs-corrected transmission electron microscope (Themis Z, Thermo Fisher) was used to capture high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and high-angle annular dark field-scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images. The thickness of each layer and the chemical distribution in the samples were measured using energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) in the STEM mode. An X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Versaprobe III, PHI) was used with an Al Kα X-ray source (*λ* = 8.34 Å) to analyze the chemical state of the TiO2 layer and the stoichiometry of HZO. For the XPS measurement, the HZO film in the MHTS stack was RTP-annealed at 600 ℃ before the top Mo electrode deposition. The binding energy of each high-resolution XPS spectrum was calibrated by referencing the C 1s peak of C–C bonding (284.8 eV). The grain size of the HZO film was analyzed by a plan-view scanning electron microscope (SEM; MERLIN Compact, ZEISS).

Electrical characterizations were performed using a semiconductor characterization system (4200A-SCS, Keithley) with an SMU module (Keithley) and a pulse measurement unit (4225-PMU, Keithley). To determine the polarization–voltage (*P*–*V*) curves, bipolar triangular and positive up negative down (PUND) pulses with pulse width and delay time of 500 μs and 20 ns, respectively, were used. The amplitude of the pulse trains was +2.5 and −3.0 MV/cm for the positive and negative pulses in the PUND measurement, respectively. The negative pulse was higher than the positive pulse because a higher electric field was required to switch polarization in HZO under the negative field as the n+-Si substrate is in the depletion mode under the negative field. The endurance test was conducted with bipolar rectangular pulses at frequency of 50 kHz and electric field of +2.5 to −3 MV/cm. The electric field was calculated with Eq. (S1) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

### 3.1 Effect of a TiO<sub>2</sub> interfacial layer on depolarization in HZO films grown on Si substrate

To study the effect of inserting a Ti layer into the MFIS structure, two representative MHS and MHTS capacitors were prepared with 5 nm-thick HZO films and RTP treatment at 600 ℃ and were particularly compared in this section, unless otherwise stated. Figures 1(a) and 1(b) show the HAADF-STEM images and EDS elemental maps of Mo, Hf, Zr, O, Ti, and Si in the MHS and MHTS capacitors, revealing the presence of a chemically sharp Ti (or TiO2) layer between the HZO film and the SiO2/Si substrate for the MHTS capacitor, as confirmed in our previous study [40]. The thicknesses of HZO and SiO2 were measured as 5.0 and 1.2 nm, respectively, for the MHS capacitor, and the thicknesses of HZO, TiO2, and SiO2 were measured as 5.0, 0.8, and 1.2 nm, respectively, for the MHTS capacitor. The HRTEM image of the MHTS capacitor, shown in Fig. S1 in the ESM, was investigated to verify whether the Ti layer has a specific orientation and showed no evidence of crystalline Ti or TiO2 at the interface between Si and HZO thin films.

The XPS measurement was conducted on the MHTS capacitor at as-deposited and RTP-treated states to investigate the oxidation state of the deposited Ti. Figure 1(c) shows high-resolution XPS spectra of the Ti 2p core level for the as-deposited and RTP-treated MHTS capacitors. As shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM, the binding energy of the Ti 2p3/2 peak was measured as 459.3 and 459.0 eV for the as-deposited and RTP-treated MHTS capacitors, respectively. Because the binding energy of Ti 2p3/2 for TiO2 (Ti (IV)) and Ti2O3 (Ti (III)) typically appear around 458.7 [41−43] and 457.3 eV [41,43,44], respectively, it is physically challenging to perform the peak fitting process by Ti (IV) and Ti (III) components in our results where the major Ti 2p3/2 peak is located near 459.0–459.3 eV. Nevertheless, shifting the Ti 2p3/2 peak to lower binding energy would still imply a decrease in the Ti (IV) component and an increase in the Ti (III) component. In other words, Ti at the interface between HZO and Si would reduce to Ti with a lower oxidation state after RTP.

Another XPS measurement was conducted on the MHS and MHTS capacitors to elucidate the effect of TiO2 reduction on the chemical stoichiometry of HZO. Figure 1(d) shows high-resolution XPS spectra of the Hf 4f core level for the MHS and MHTS capacitors. The XPS result shows that the tail at the lower binding energy of the XPS spectrum of the MHS capacitor is clearly broader than that of the MHTS capacitor, as shown in the inset of Fig. 1(d). Detailed peak fitting results of the XPS spectra using stoichiometric HfO2 and non-stoichiometric HfO2−x* components are shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. Specifically, the fraction of the stoichiometric HfO2 increased from 95.3% to 98.2%. In comparison, the non-stoichiometric HfO2−*x* decreased from 4.7% for the MHS capacitor to 1.8% for the MHTS capacitor, which implies that the concentration of oxygen vacancies was smaller in the MHTS capacitor. The decrease in the concentration of oxygen vacancies in the MHTS capacitor resulted from a reduction in the Ti layer owing to the reduction of a small portion of Ti (IV) into Ti (III) during RTP. To summarize, the pre-deposition of a Ti layer before HZO deposition resulted in the formation of a TiO2 IL, and a TiO2 reduction as well as HZO oxidation occurred during RTP, resulting in a decreased concentration of oxygen vacancies in HZO.

Crystallographic characterizations were conducted through various diffraction measurements. Figure 2(a) shows GIXRD patterns of the MHS and MHTS capacitors. In these patterns, the diffraction peaks from the (*hkl*) planes of the *x*-phase are labeled as *hkl**x* reflections, where *x* can be m (monoclinic, *P*21/*c*), o (orthorhombic, *Pca*21), or t (tetragonal, *P*42/*nmc*). Notably, the detailed phase quantification using lab-scale GIXRD patterns is extremely challenging mainly owing to three reasons: (i) The resolution of the measured XRD patterns is insufficient for the phase quantification because the thickness of our sample is too small, the intensity of the incident X-ray beam is too weak, and the detector has limited resolution. (ii) The tetragonal and orthorhombic phases are structurally similar; hence, the 2*θ* position of the reference XRD pattern is difficult to discern. (iii) The 2*θ* position of the fitted peak could be slightly shifted to higher 2*θ* because of the grazing incidence geometry. The 2*θ* position of the diffraction peak has often been reported to shift to higher 2*θ* as the incidence angle decreases [45], which is largely attributable to the refraction effect [46].

*

Nevertheless, the lab-scale GIXRD results reveal two major findings. First, the *−*111m and 111m reflections, corresponding to 2*θ* of 28.5° and 31.5°, respectively, did not appear in the GIXRD patterns of both capacitors, suggesting that the monoclinic phase was effectively suppressed. Second, a clear XRD peak was observed at 2*θ* of ~30.5°, which indicates the 111o or 101t reflections. Although a slight shift in the 111o/101t reflection to lower 2*θ* (i.e., larger *d*-spacing) implies an increase in the orthorhombic phase fraction [47−51], the peak position of the 111o/101t reflection in both GIXRD patterns was measured as 30.6°, which implies that the phase fractions of the two HZO films are difficult to distinguish based on the lab-scale XRD measurements. Furthermore, the XRD patterns measured at the *θ*–2*θ* coupled geometry shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM showed no evidence of preferred orientation in the film, primarily because of a low signal-to-noise ratio.

The GIWAXS measurement was conducted at the 3C beamline at PLS-II to analyze in more detail the orientation distribution and phase fraction of HZO in the MHS and MHTS capacitors. The schematic of the GIWAXS geometry is shown in detail in Fig. 2(b). Figures 2(c) and 2(d) show GIWAXS images of the MHS and MHTS capacitors, respectively. Both images show no visible peaks from the −111m and 111m reflections, which appear near the *q* value of 1.99 and 2.22 Å−1, respectively, along the azimuthal angle; this implies that the monoclinic phase has been successfully suppressed in both capacitors. The 111o/101t reflection, which appears near the *q* value of 2.10 Å−1 on the GIWAXS image, is marked with a white arrow. The 111o/101t reflection with relatively consistent intensity appeared across almost every azimuthal angle, implying that either the (111) plane of the orthorhombic phase, the (101) plane of the tetragonal phase, or both are present almost randomly. In other words, the orthorhombic/tetragonal phase in the HZO film generally exhibits random distribution. However, relatively sharp and strong 111o/101t reflections for both MHS and MHTS capacitors appeared horizontally at the center of the beam (i.e., *q**y*), indicating the minute preferred orientation of the (111) plane of the orthorhombic phase, the (101) plane of the tetragonal phase, or both in the direction perpendicular to the sample. Figure 2(e) shows the GIWAXS pattern of the MHS and MHTS capacitors extracted along the vertical direction (i.e., *q**z*) from the beam center in their GIWAXS images. The definition of *q**z* would be the *z*-axis of the reciprocal space. In other words, *q**z* shows inverse lattice spacings from crystal planes that are parallel to the sample’s surface. However, it should be noted that *q**z* shown in this study is approximately *θ*+≈0.3° off from the sample normal because of the grazing incidence geometry. Nevertheless, since the incidence angle is the same for both capacitors, *q**z* is treated as the conventional definition described above for simplicity. The 111o/101t reflection of the MHTS capacitor appeared at a *q* value lower than that of the MHS capacitor. Specifically, the *q* value of the 111o/101t reflection was fitted as 2.13 and 2.12 Å−1 for the MHS and MHTS capacitors, corresponding to a *d*-spacing of 2.95 and 2.97 Å, respectively. The detailed fitting results are shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM. The peak shift of the 111o/101t reflection to a lower *q* value indicates that the HZO film in the MHTS capacitor exhibits a larger fraction of the orthorhombic phase than in the MHS capacitor.

Furthermore, to investigate the effect of a TiO2 layer on the grain size of HZO, plan-view SEM was conducted for the MHS and MHTS capacitors. The average grain radii of HZO in the MHS and MHTS capacitors were 4.6±3.0 and 4.5±2.8 nm, respectively, as shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM. These results indicate that the TiO2 layer negligibly affects the grain size of the HZO thin film.

Various electrical properties were measured to elucidate the effect of TiO2 IL on the electrical properties in the MFIS structure. Figure 3(a) shows a schematic of pulse sequences applied to the capacitor during the PUND measurement (left) and endurance test (right) to produce the polarization–voltage (*P*–*V*) curves. Two sequential positive (P and U) and two negative (N and D) pulses were applied to the capacitor, followed by an immediate prepoling step. In the ideal case, the leakage current and dielectric response contributions can be removed by subtracting the current response of the U (or D) pulse from that of the P (or N) pulse. The contribution from the ferroelectric switching response could be extracted using the PUND method. Unless otherwise noted, the *P*–*V* curves and 2*P*r shown in this study have been extracted by the PUND method. Figures 3(b) and 3(c) show the *P*–*V* curves of the MHS and MHTS capacitors measured after each fatigue cycle. The *P*–*V* curves measured without using the PUND method are also shown in Fig. S7 in the ESM for reference. Figure 3(d) shows the 2*P*r variation with the number of fatigue cycles applied to the MHS and MHTS capacitors.

For the pristine MHS capacitor, positive and negative *V*c were measured as +3.0 and −3.7 V, respectively, whereas the positive and negative *P*r were +17.5 and −18.0 μC/cm2, respectively. The *P*–*V* curves shown in Fig. 3(b) were strongly pinched, suggesting that the switching current peak was split in the pristine state. As the number of fatigue cycles increased, the magnitude of the negative *V*c decreased significantly, whereas that of the positive *V*c remained unchanged. In particular, the largest change in the magnitude of the negative *V*c was observed between 102 and 103 switching cycles.

The shape of the *P*–*V* curves of the pristine MHTS capacitor shows a complete (i.e., rectangular) hysteresis loop compared to that of the MHS capacitor. The positive and negative *V*c were +1.5 and −1.8 V, respectively, whereas the positive and negative *P*r were +29.9 and −29.5 μC/cm2, respectively. As analyzed from the structural characterization, large 2*P*r in the MHTS capacitor may have originated from a larger fraction of the orthorhombic phase. Similar to the MHS capacitor, the negative-field regime of the *P*–*V* curve was pinched in the pristine state, suggesting that the switching current peak was split in the pristine state. After 104 switching cycles, the positive and negative *V*c were +1.8 and −1.3 V, respectively, whereas the positive and negative *P*r were +31.7 and −32.1 μC/cm2, respectively. As mentioned in Section 2, the n+-Si substrate exhibits a depletion mode when a negative electric field is applied, thereby requiring a larger electric field in the negative electric field for ferroelectric switching. As a consequence, in the pristine state, the peak distance between the split-up switching current peaks was larger in the negative regime than in the positive regime. As the number of fatigue cycles increased, the split-up switching current peaks were merged, and the *P*–*V* curve became more rectangular, proving that the ferroelectric characteristics of the MHTS capacitor were ideally enhanced. Figure S8 in the ESM shows the IL thickness before and after applying 103 cycles of fatigue pulse in MHS and MHTS samples. The thicknesses of SiO2 and TiO2 were identical even after applying fatigue pulse. To further assess endurance characteristics of the MHTS capacitor, the endurance test results with low amplitude of fatigue pulse are shown in Fig. 3(d) and Fig. S9 in the ESM. MHTS capacitors endure up to 108 cycles with a 2*P*r value ~ 30.0 μC/cm2, thereby reducing the amplitude of the fatigue pulse.

Notably, for the MHS capacitor, an abnormal hump in polarization was observed near +2.9 V (Fig. 3(b)). The abnormal hump, which should not appear in the ideal *P*–*V* curves, is caused by a shift of the current peak at the U pulse to the low electric field region after applying the P pulse. The shift of the current peak at the U pulse might have been caused by an electron-trapping phenomenon that occurred during the P pulse. A detailed discussion is provided in Fig. S10 in the ESM. Hence, the amplitude of the abnormal hump weakened with increasing number of fatigue pulses. It has been reported that even antiferroelectric-like Hf0.3Zr0.7O2 can show a ferroelectric-like single hysteresis owing to the high trapped charge concentration when it is directly grown on the Si substrate [40].

The interface trap density (*D*it) was examined in both MHS and MHTS samples. The maximum *D*it values were determined in the depletion region using the conductance method [52,53] with the small-signal capacitance measurement. The capacitance–voltage (*C*–*V*) curves, measured across a range of frequencies from 5 kHz to 1 MHz, are shown in Fig. S11 in the ESM. The maximum *D*it values for MHS and MHTS were (2.94±0.57)×1013 and (1.46±0.80)×1013 eV−1∙cm−2, respectively. The lower *D*it value observed for the MHTS sample suggests that inserting a Ti layer between the Si substrate and the HZO layer would reduce the number of interface trap sites. Notably, because it is physically separated from *P*r by SiO2 or TiO2/SiO2 layers, the interfacial trap concentration at the SiO2/Si does not have a stronger impact on the depolarization field than the charges at the interface with the HZO layer.

The first-order reversal curve (FORC) analysis was utilized to understand the complex polarization switching properties in MHS and MHTS. The FORC analysis involves quantifying the switching behavior of minor domains, which originate from various reversal fields along the switching current–electric field (*J*sw–*E*) curves that make up a major hysteresis loop [54]. Here, *E* was calculated similarly to Table S1 in the ESM, where the capacitance of each dielectric layer was considered without ferroelectric polarization, and the top and bottom electrodes were supposed to be metallic. The measurement started by applying a large electric field (*E*sat) to saturate the polarization in one direction, where −4 and −3.6 V were applied to MHS and MHTS, respectively. Then, to measure the minor loop, the triangle pulse swept to *E*r,n ∈ (−*E*sat*, **E*sat) repeatedly by increasing *E*r,*n* to *E*sat by Δ*E = E*r,*n*+1*−E*r,*n*. Δ*E* was 0.20 and 0.18 V to keep the same minor loop measurements in each *E*sat of MHS and MHTS. For each minor loop, *p*−FORC(*E*r*, E*) can be obtained, and the second derivative for *E*r and *E* is as Eq. (1) [55,56]:

where *ρ*−(*E*r*, E*) is the switching density (or Preisach density), which expresses the additional fraction of domain switched by the sweep from *E*r,*n* to *E*sat. *ρ*−(*E*r*, E*) can be shown in the contour map, and the results are presented in Fig. 4. The increase in * |ρ| implies a certain amount of domains back-switched by the n*th reversal field (*E*r,*n*). *E*r denotes the reversal field and *E*r,*n** *represents the enhanced value of *E*r, which initially stands at −4 V. The enhancement occurs through an increase of 0.2 V for each *n*, where *n *symbolizes the number of increments. Here, the diagonal from the origin of the graph to the left-bottom corner means *E*c = (*E* − *E*r)/2. However, if the increase in * |ρ| is not on the line of E*c, the domain is biased in the amount of *E*bias =(*E *+ *E*r)/2. The MHS capacitor shows two hysteresis with rather high * |Ec| values (~ 2.1 MV/cm) and low E*bias (~−0.3 MV/cm) and one single hysteresis with rather low * |Ec| (~1.1 MV/cm) value and high E*bias (~1.1 MV/cm). The values are extracted from around the maximum values of each density peak. The high * |Ec| parts are believed to originate from the polarization compensated by the trapped charges at the HZO/SiO2 interface, whereas the low  |Ec| part would originate from the polarization uncompensated by the trapped charges because of the insufficient trap site concentration at the HZO/SiO2 interface. Because  |Ec|has the same value as E*bias*,* the entire minor hysteresis loop by this domain family would be shifted to a positive voltage sweep, implying that the insufficient compensating charge is more likely to originate from the negative charge. The current density peak induced by the insufficient compensating charge causes depolarization of domain, as shown in the region 4 in Fig. 4(b), which can be the origin of the polarization switching current of the region 2 in Fig. 4(a). By integrating the switching current responses, the switching polarization estimated from the high and low * |Ec| parts were ~20 and 30 μC/cm2, respectively. Depending on the P*r portion that can be compensated by the injected and trapped charges, the depolarization field affecting on the amount of the back-switching of domains built-in the ferroelectric is determined [57], and the estimated value can include the leakage current and the current originating from the dielectric response. The MHTS capacitor shows two hysteresis with almost the same * |Ec| values (~1.00 MV/cm) and E*bias values of opposite signs (~−0.25 and 0.25 MV/cm). The observed *J*–*E* curve and FORC are similar to the typical results for the pristine state metal/HZO/metal ferroelectric capacitors before wake-up field cycling, which implies that sufficient charge compensation can be achieved in MHTS [56]. The energy band diagram and the field on HZO in each domain state are considered in Fig. S12 in the ESM to verify the above speculations based on the FORC measurement results and *J*–*E* curves, suggesting that the explanation is well described by classical electromagnetism. Also, the results of Fig. S12 in the ESM imply that *P*r larger in MHTS than in MHS is attributed to the higher concentration of compensating charges that can be trapped at the potential well or shallow trap level by TiO2.

The depolarization fields in the MHS and MHTS capacitors were calculated using Eq. (S2) in the ESM, as shown in Fig. S13 in the ESM. As previously mentioned, owing to the presence of significant compensating charges, the calculated depolarization field in the MHTS capacitor was found lower than that in the MHS capacitor. This lower depolarization field in the MHTS capacitor contributed to an enhanced 2*P*r value compared to that in the MHS capacitor.

In conclusion, three major enhancements result from inserting TiO2 IL between HZO and SiO2/Si substrates:

i) The MHTS capacitor demonstrated larger *P*r and lower *V*c compared to the MHS capacitor. The sufficient charge compensation of *P*r could result in enhanced *P*r and lower *V*c in the MHTS capacitor, where the possible origin of the compensation charge is the TiO2 layer acting as a charge well or shallow trap site. *D*it could be decreased by increasing the number of fatigue pulses during the endurance test, which was confirmed by the decreased intensity of the abnormal hump.

ii) The endurance properties were improved in the MHTS capacitor. Notably, high-*k* TiO2 was formed by injection of O3 during the HZO deposition. Owing to the voltage drop at high-*k* TiO2 IL, early hard breakdown might have been prevented in the MHTS capacitor.

iii) The MHS and MHTS capacitors exhibited stable retention properties up to 104 s of delay time at room temperature, as demonstrated in Fig. S14 in the ESM.

### 3.2 Effect of the HZO film thickness and annealing temperature

The physical, chemical, and electrical analyses performed in this study confirmed that TiO2 IL between ferroelectric HZO and SiO2/Si interface effectively decreases the depolarization field owing to TiO2 acting as a potential well or trapping layer with a high concentration of shallow levels. The effective performance of TiO2 IL prompted us to undertake a comprehensive study to investigate the impact of the film thickness and annealing temperature on the ferroelectric properties of MHTS capacitors. Three thicknesses (3, 4, and 5 nm) of HZO films and three annealing temperatures (500, 600, and 700 ℃) were selected for the study. The crystallization temperature of ultrathin films increases as the film thicknesses decrease, which justifies the selection of high annealing temperatures in this analysis [58−60]. Although this temperature region is unsuitable for back-end-of-line processes, such as the gate stacks of field-effect transistors, the annealing temperature range is reasonable. Film thicknesses lower than 5 nm are important for further scaled FeFET devices. However, it has been reported that the ferroelectricity is sensitively affected by the film thickness owing to the size effect in this thickness regime. The free surface or interfacial energy difference explains the positive correlation between polymorphism and film thickness. Moreover, a low film thickness is physically related to the depolarization effect; the relative ratio of the ferroelectric capacitor to the serial non-ferroelectric parasitic capacitance is the dominant factor in the depolarization field, which is derived from various factors [2,23,61,62].

Figures 5(a)–5(c) show EDS elemental maps of MHTS capacitors with HZO film thicknesses of 3, 4, and 5 nm, respectively. The detailed EDS elemental maps of the MHTS capacitors with various HZO thicknesses are also shown in Fig. S15 in the ESM. As shown in Figs. 5(a)–5(c), a thin TiO2 layer was deposited on the SiO2 layer for all MHTS capacitors, and the thickness of the HZO layer was measured as 3, 4, and 5 nm, respectively. Figures 5(d)–5(f) show GIXRD patterns of the MHTS capacitors with various HZO film thicknesses of 3, 4, and 5 nm, respectively, annealed at different temperatures. Notably, the peak at 2*θ* = 50°–55° originates likely from multiple diffraction of Si 311 reflection [49,63]. In the case of the 3 nm MHTS capacitor, the diffraction peaks from various HZO phases were not visible, suggesting that the 3 nm-thick HZO films would not be crystallized even up to 700 ℃. As shown in Figs. 5(e) and 5(f), both 4 and 5nm-thick HZO films crystallized above the annealing temperature of 600 ℃. The diffraction peak at 2*θ* of 30.5°, corresponding to the 111o/101t reflection, was observed in both 4 and 5 nm MHTS capacitors annealed at 600 and 700 ℃, respectively. Thus, an annealing temperature higher than 600 ℃ is required to crystallize 4 and 5 nm thick HZO films.

For the 4 nm thick HZO films, ferroelectricity with 2*P*r values of 5.5, 31.7, and 38.4 μC/cm2 was observed at annealing temperatures of 500, 600, and 700 ℃, respectively, which aligns with the crystallization trend shown in Fig. 5(b). A large gap exists between the initial and terminal points of the *P*–*V* curves for the 4 nm thick HZO films, and it originates from the depolarization effect or the formation of the antiferroelectric-like tetragonal phase after the removal of the electric field. However, there is no efficient characterization method to clearly distinguish the depolarization and antiferroelectric-like contributions. Even in the FORC measurement, these two mechanisms cannot be distinguished. Assuming that the charge quantity can be stored at the TiO2 potential well or shallow traps, it would be plausible to consider the antiferroelectric-like contribution from forming an increasing fraction of the tetragonal phase because of the surface energy effect, as experimentally demonstrated in previous works [59,64]. The 2*V*c values are important parameters because they determine the memory window of FeFETs when the gate stacks in this work are integrated into FeFETs. The 2*V*c values of the MHTS capacitors with 5 and 4 nm thick HZO films were 3.8 and 2.4 V, respectively, suggesting that a large memory window could be achieved with HZO films as thin as 5 and 4 nm.

For the 5 nm thick HZO films, the nominal ferroelectric-like *P*–*V* curves were observed at annealing temperatures of 600 and 700 ℃ with 2*P*r values of 56.8 and 60.2 μC/cm2, respectively. Notably, the observed 2*P*r values were higher than those of the 10 nm thick HZO films frequently reported in the literature (30–40 μC/cm2) [12]. By inserting TiO2 IL on the Si substrate, high *P*r values can be maintained at a film thickness of 5 nm. The enhancement of the *P*r values by inserting TiO2 IL can be attributed to the fact that the sufficient trapped charges at the potential well or shallow trap level at the HZO/TiO2 interface could have stabilized *P*r more sufficiently than the charges at the HZO/SiO2 interface and more effectively than *D*it.

Figures 7(b) and 7(c) show a comparison between observations obtained in this study and superlative results obtained in previous studies on the sub-5 nm ferroelectric (Hf,Zr)O2 or doped HfO2 thin films; *P*r–thickness and endurance–thickness plots were constructed based on the literature. Retention and endurance test results for MHTS capacitors with 3–5 nm-thick HZO films are demonstrated in Figs. S14 and S16 in the ESM. Figure 7(b) shows that the *P*r values as high as 2.7 and 9.4 μC/cm2 were achieved for the 3 and 4 nm thick HZO films in the MHTS capacitor, respectively. In the case of 5 nm MHTS, the delay time of 10 μs and 10 s was employed to mitigate the risk of overestimating the *P*r value. The *P*r values were 24.7 and 27.2 μC/cm2 for the delay time of 10 μs and *P*r values from P–U and N–D, respectively. In the case of delay time of 10 s, the *P*r values from P–U and N–D were 12.9 and 18.9 μC/cm2, respectively. Detailed PUND measurement results, spanning the delay time from 20 ns to 10 s, are shown in Fig. S17 in the ESM. This approach enables us to investigate the influence of the delay time on PUND measurements. Notably, the theoretical *P*r values of the *Pca*21 orthorhombic HfO2 or ZrO2, calculated from computer simulation, are 50–55 μC/cm2, and the expected ideal *P*r of randomly oriented polycrystalline films is 25.0–27.5 μC/cm2 (50% of the value along the polar axis). Therefore, large *P*r values exceeding 30 μC/cm2 in this study, particularly at the sub-5 nm level, seem to be a great achievement. Figure 7(c) shows that outstanding endurance results were obtained for the 3 and 4 nm thick HZO films in the MHTS capacitor, where the endurance of 106–107 switching cycles was achieved before the occurrence of the hard breakdown. These are considered the best results obtained to date for films of equivalent thickness. Although the endurance of the 5 nm thick HZO film in the MHTS capacitor is about 104 cycles and is lower than the previous results, its high *P*r value suggests that the endurance could be increased by decreasing the applied field and the resulting *P*r.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, two HZO capacitors, MHS and MHTS, were fabricated to study the impact of TiO2 IL on the ferroelectric property and reliability of the HZO-based MFIS capacitor. The insertion of TiO2 IL was found to enhance *P*r of HZO, whereas *V*c decreased. TiO2 IL effectively increased the endurable switching cycles for the ferroelectric HZO thin films before a hard breakdown occurred. In addition, structural and electrical characterizations were conducted on the MHTS capacitor at HZO thicknesses below 5 nm to facilitate the physical scaling down of the ferroelectric HZO thin films. The depolarization field and crystallization temperature were found to be strongly influenced by the film thickness. Specifically, 4 and 5 nm thick HZO films were sufficiently crystallized at RTP temperatures of 600 and 700 ℃, respectively, whereas the 3 nm thick HZO films were not fully crystallized at high RTP temperatures. A 2*P*r value higher than 60 μC/cm2 was achieved in 5 nm thick HZO thin films; however, these could not endure more than 105 cycles owing to hard breakdown. The 4 nm thick HZO films endured over 105 switching cycles when annealed at 600 ℃ while retaining 2*P*r value as high as 45.2 μC/cm2. Although the 3 nm thick HZO films were not fully crystallized at 700 ℃, 2*P*r values of 4.0–8.0 μC/cm2 were achieved, which could be sufficient to control the conductance of the Si channel. Therefore, inserting TiO2 IL between the ferroelectric HZO film and the Si substrate appears to be an effective method for enhancing ferroelectricity and reliability of the HZO-based MFIS capacitor. This study provides a novel interfacial engineering approach for inducing sufficient 2*P*r values in ultrathin HZO films directly formed on Si substrates, which is crucial for the next-generation semiconductor devices.
