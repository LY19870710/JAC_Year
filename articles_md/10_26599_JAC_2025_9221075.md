## 1 Introduction

Near-infrared (NIR) broadband light sources play crucial roles in noninvasive point-of-care diagnostic technologies such as food analysis [1−3], health monitoring [4,5], and molecular fingerprint identification [6,7]. As the most promising NIR light source, NIR phosphor-converted light-emitting diodes (NIR pc-LEDs) have attracted widespread attention because of their comprehensive advantages, such as compact structure, long lifetime, and energy conservation [1−5]. However, traditional NIR pc-LEDs employ a pseudotransmissive configuration (Fig. 1(a)), where microscale NIR phosphors (typically 5–50 μm) are embedded in silicone composites and directly deposited on high-power blue LED chips [6−8]. However, this design paradigm suffers from two fundamental limitations that constrain both optical output and operational reliability. First, the significant refractive index contrast between phosphors (*n* ≈ 1.8–2.4) and silicone composites (*n* ≈ 1.4–1.5) induces pronounced scattering at phosphor-silicone interfaces [7]. This optical mismatch results in severe photon trapping within the composite layer, yielding low NIR transmittance (8,9]. Moreover, the inherently low thermal conductivity of phosphor-silicone composites (0.15–0.25 W/m·K) leads to substantial Joule heat at the chip‒phosphor interface, which results in an efficiency droop of the NIR pc-LED (> 50% at 150°C) and reduces the device lifetime by > 70% compared with the theoretical predictions [10,11]. To address these challenges, it is imperative to develop true-transmission NIR broadband light sources, in which the converted material exhibits high optical transmittance, ideal quantum efficiency and excellent thermal conductivity.

As an emerging class of hybrid photonic materials, glass-ceramics (GCs) combine the structural advantages of crystalline phases (low phonon energy, high activator solubility and superior quantum efficiency) with the functional merits of glass (high optical transparency, high thermal conductivity, and exceptional chemical durability) [10−12]. This unique dual-phase architecture positions GCs as new converter materials for next-generation high-power NIR pc-LEDs. As illustrated in Fig. 1(b), the blue emission from the LED chip undergoes efficient absorption and subsequent conversion to NIR radiation within the GC converter. Owing to the exceptional optical transmittance of GCs, the majority of generated NIR photons traverse the GCs unimpeded through a true-transmission mechanism. Compared with the conventional pc-LED configuration, the high optical transparency and large thermal conductivity of GC-converted LEDs (GCc-LEDs) effectively mitigate the challenges of thermal accumulation and performance degradation observed in traditional systems [13,14].

In this study, we designed highly transparent β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GCs through an oxygen-coordination-competition crystallization strategy (Figs. 1(c) and 1(d)). In conventional silicate glass systems with low Ga2O3 contents (3+ functions primarily as a network intermediate that coordinates with nonbridging oxygen species to stabilize the glass network (Fig. 1(c)). Our compositional design fundamentally alters this paradigm by implementing high Ga2O3 concentrations (12–22 mol%) coupled with a reduced network modifier (Na+) content. This formulation induces an oxygen-deficient environment, triggering competitive oxygen coordination between Ga3+ and Si4+ ions (Fig. 1(d)). Under these conditions, Ga3+ has strong oxygen affinity, preferentially forming [GaO4] tetrahedral units that coexist with [SiO4] networks. This results in a metastable phase-separated microstructure with enhanced crystallization propensity [14,15]. Subsequent controlled thermal treatment facilitates the migration and reorganization of Ga3+ and O2- ions within segregated [GaO4] domains, ultimately driving the localized precipitation of β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ nanocrystals [14,15]. This oxygen-coordination-competition mechanism allows precise regulation of the crystallization kinetics, yielding uniformly dispersed β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ nanocrystals within the GC. The experimental results of β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GCs revealed remarkable performance metrics: high optical transmittance (81.2% @0.4 mm), enhanced thermal conductivity (1.2 W·m−1·K−1) and nearly 100% internal quantum efficiency. These superior characteristics enabled the realization of high-power NIR GCc-LEDs with high conversion efficiency (up to 13%) and exceptional NIR output power (568 mW@500 mA).

### 2.1 Materials preparation

The precursor glass was formulated with a nominal composition of 66.5 mol% SiO2–6.5 mol% Al2O3–(9.5 + *x*) mol% Ga2O3–(17.5 − *x*) mol% Na2O–0.1 mol% Cr2O3 (*x* = 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, or 12.5). These coefficients represented the molar ratios of the respective raw materials. The glasses were prepared through the melt quenching technique. The pure reagent grade powders of SiO2 (99.99%), Al2O3 (99.99%), Ga2O3 (99.99%), Na2CO3 (99.9%), and Cr2O3 (99.9%) were accurately weighed and thoroughly mixed via an agate mortar. The homogenized mixture underwent melting in a high-purity corundum crucible via a three-stage thermal protocol: (1) decarbonization at 800 °C (2 h, a heating rate of 5 °C/min), (2) high-temperature melting at 1650 °C (2 h, ambient atmosphere), and (3) rapid quenching by pouring the melt onto a preheated brass mold (300 °C) followed by hydraulic pressing (10 MPa, 30 s) to minimize thermal stress-induced cracking. The resulting glass blanks were annealed at 550 °C (2 h, below the glass transition temperature (*T*g)) to relieve residual stresses.

To fabricate GCs containing β-Ga₂O₃:Cr3+ nanocrystals, controlled crystallization was achieved through isothermal heat treatment in a programmable muffle furnace: precursor glasses were heated to target temperatures (900–1100 °C, 5 °C/min) and held for 5 h under an air atmosphere, followed by natural cooling to room temperature. For comparative analysis, β-Ga₂O₃:Cr3+ phosphor counterparts were synthesized via a conventional solid-state reaction: stoichiometric mixtures of Ga₂O₃ and Cr2O3 were calcined at 1200 °C (6 h, air atmosphere, heating rate of 5 °C/min) in alumina crucibles, followed by mechanical grinding (agate mortar, 30 min) to obtain submicron particle sizes.

### 2.2 Characterizations

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis was conducted via a Jupiter instrument (STA449C, Netzsch, Germany) under an argon atmosphere. The heating rate was set at 10 K/min within a temperature range of 25–1100 °C to determine the optimal heat treatment temperature. The phase compositions of the β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GC were characterized via X-ray diffraction (XRD) via a diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) equipped with a copper target X-ray tube. The XRD analysis was performed over a 2*θ* range of 10°–80° with a step size of 0.02°.

Morphological and elemental analyses of the β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GC were conducted via high-angle dark-field transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-TEM). Elemental distribution maps were acquired via a microscope (Talos F200X, FEI, USA) operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV and a probe current of 50 pA. TEM samples with a nominal thickness of approximately 40 nm were prepared via mechanical polishing followed by ion beam milling. Transmission and diffuse reflectance spectra were measured via a UV/VIS/NIR spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, USA). Photoluminescence (PL) and photoluminescence excitation (PLE) spectra were acquired with a fluorescence spectrophotometer (FLS1000, Edinburgh Instruments Ltd., UK) equipped with visible/NIR PMT detectors and an integrating sphere. Steady-state spectra were obtained using a 450 W ozone-free xenon lamp as the excitation source. High-temperature PL spectra from 300 to 600 K (5 K/min) were acquired with a custom-built high-temperature heater in conjunction with a spectrophotometer (FLS1000, Edinburgh Instruments Ltd., UK). The PL strength measured at different high temperatures represents a steady-state value, which is obtained in an air atmosphere after 30 minutes of holding. The internal quantum efficiencies of β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GC were measured via an integrated sphere on the FLS 1000 instrument, with high-purity BaSO4 powder serving as the reference.

### 2.3 Device fabrication and performance measurements

NIR GCc-LEDs were fabricated by integrating β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GC with commercial blue LEDs (5 W, 450 nm, Shenzhen Looking Long Technology Co., Ltd., China), whereas pc-LEDs were assembled via conventional silicone composites (OE6551, Dow Corning, USA) and β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ phosphors. The performance of the pc-LEDs was enhanced through optimization of the ratio between the silicone composites and the β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ phosphor. The electroluminescence (EL) spectra and photoelectric efficiency of the fabricated NIR GCc-LEDs and pc-LED devices were characterized via an integrated photoelectric measurement system (OHSP, Hangzhou Hongpu Light Color Co., Ltd., China).

## 3 Results and discussion

The heat treatment temperature for β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GCs was optimized within 900–1100°C (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). Figures 2(a) and 2(c) present the bulk characteristics of the GC samples processed at different heat treatment temperatures and thicknesses, with the latter being specifically treated at 1050°C. The SEM image in Fig. 2(b) shows a smooth surface of the β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GCs, which is indicative of favorable machinability. XRD analysis confirmed the effectiveness of our crystallization strategy, as no crystalline peaks were detected in β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GCs containing low Ga2O3 concentrations (9.5 mol%) (Fig. 2(d) and S2 in the ESM). Notably, distinct diffraction peaks emerged at 2*θ* = 31.74°, 35.18°, and 38.41° when the Ga3+ concentration exceeded 12 mol%, all of which exhibited excellent consistency with the β-Ga2O3 reference (PDF No. 41-1103). Quantitative analysis of the XRD patterns revealed a progressive increase in the crystallization ratio from 4.31% to 15.20% as the Ga2O3 content increased from 12 to 19.5 wt%. These findings substantiated the proposed strategy for controlling β-Ga2O3 crystallization in GC through Ga2O3 concentration modulation. The high-frequency peaks at 656 and 775 cm−1, the mid-frequency peak at 491 cm−1 and the low-frequency peak at 206 cm−1 in Fig. 2(e) corresponded to the active vibrational modes of the GaO4 tetrahedron, GaO6 octahedron, and tetrahedron-octahedron chains in β-Ga2O3:Cr3+, respectively [15]. The vibration peaks attributed to Ga–O bonds were also identified in the FT-IR spectra (Fig. 2(f)), providing further evidence of β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ nanocrystal precipitation in the GC. Both the XRD diffraction peak intensities and Raman spectral features monotonically increased with increasing Ga2O3 concentration from 12 to 19.5 mol%, confirming the precipitation of more β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ nanocrystals at higher Ga2O3 concentrations.

GCs heat-treated at 900°C for 5 h demonstrated exceptional NIR transmittance of 89.6% (Fig. 2(g)). Notably, the processed samples (1050 °C, 5 h) retained a high optical transmittance as high as 81.4% in the NIR region. Figure 2(h) presents the thickness-dependent transmission spectra of the β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GCs, revealing a gradual decrease in transmittance from 81.4% to 67.2% in the NIR region as the thickness of the bulk GC increased from 0.4 to 1.2 mm. These results confirmed that β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GCs maintained remarkable optical transparency despite crystal precipitation. Notably, the 4 mm-thick β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GC exhibited a transmittance of 81.4%, exceeding twenty times that of conventional β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ phosphor-silicone composites (4.2%) (Fig. 2(i)). This superior transparency highlights the potential of β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GC for advanced optical device applications. Two distinct absorption bands at 425 and 600 nm were identified in the transmission spectra (Figs. 2(g) and 2(h)), corresponding to the 4A2g →4T1g and 4A2g → 4T2g electronic transitions of β-Ga2O3:Cr3+, respectively [16−18]. Moreover, β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GCs presented a thermal conductivity of 1.2 W·m−1·K−1 (Table S1 in the ESM), representing a sevenfold increase over that of organic resin (0.17 W·m−1·K−1) [19,20].

The TEM image in Fig. 3(a) confirmed the uniform distribution of β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ nanocrystals in the GC. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images under bright-field (Fig. 3(b)) and dark-field (Fig. 3(c)) conditions revealed that the β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ nanocrystals had an average size of ~50 nm (Fig. S4 in the ESM). Elemental mapping analysis revealed distinct spatial distributions: Ga and Cr were strongly correlated with the nanocrystal regions (Figs. 3(h) and 3(i)), whereas Si, Al, and Na presented complementary distribution patterns (Figs. 3(e)–3(g)). This elemental segregation identified the precipitated nanocrystals as the β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ phase, confirming successful crystallization control within GC.

The PLE spectrum of β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GC (Fig. 4(a)) exhibited three broad bands spanning from 260 to 700 nm, corresponding to Cr3+ transitions from the 4A2g ground state to the 4T1g (4P) (288 nm), 4T1g (4F) (440 nm), and 4A2g (4F) (600 nm) excited states [21,22]. Reflectance spectral analysis (Fig. S3 in the ESM) confirmed that these absorption features originated from both host absorption and Cr3+ electronic transitions (4A2g→4T1g (4F) and 4A2g→4A2g (4F)). Notably, the 440 nm absorption band (4A2g→4T1g (4F)) demonstrated a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 95 nm, aligning with the emission characteristics of commercial blue LED chips. Under 440 nm excitation, the β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GCs exhibited broadband NIR emission ranging from 600 to 1200 nm, which was attributed to the 4T2g (4F) → 4A2g (4F) electronic transition. Notably, 0.10 mol% Cr3+-doped GC achieved exceptionally broad NIR emission with FWHM of 187 nm. As shown in Fig. 4(b),increasing the Cr3+ content resulted in a progressive redshift in the emission wavelength, accompanied by an increase in the FWHM band from 155 to 232 nm. The decay lifetime of β-Ga2O3:x*Cr3+ GC decreases with increasing Cr3+ concentration, which is attributed to the increased interaction between adjacent Cr3+ ions (Fig. S5 in the ESM). By varying the ratio of Ga2O3 and Na2O components, we investigated the influence of the GC composition on the PL intensity of β-Ga₂O₃:*x*Cr3+ GC. Through an analysis of the PL intensities corresponding to different glass compositions, the optimal proportion of GC components was determined to be 19.5Ga2O3–7.5Na2O (Fig. 4(c)). As shown in Fig. 4(d), the maximum PL intensity was attained when the heat treatment temperature was maintained at 1050°C (Fig. S6 in the ESM). However, exceeding this threshold of 1050 °C led to uncontrollable crystallization processes.

In Fig. 4(b), β-Ga2O3:*x*Cr3+ GC exhibited a relatively weak crystal field for Cr3+ ions, which can be assessed through the crystal field strength (*D*q) and Racah parameter (*B*). The values of *D*q and *B* were calculated via Eqs. (1)–(3) [23,24]:

(1) 10Dq=E(4A2g→4T2g)−ΔS2

(2) DqB=15(ΔE/Dq−8)(ΔE/Dq)2−10(ΔE/Dq)

(3) ΔE=E(4A2g→4T1g)−E(4A2g→4T2g)

where ∆*E* represents the central wavenumber difference between the 4T2g and 4T1g states, as derived from the corresponding PLE spectrum. The Stokes shift (∆*S*) was determined by calculating the difference between the peak position of the PLE band and the emission in the PL spectrum. Considering *E*(4A2g → 4T2g) = 16,667 cm−1 and *E*(4A2g → 4T1g)= 22,727 cm−1, the values of the *D*q and *B* parameters were calculated to be 1194 and 598 cm−1, respectively. According to the aforementioned parameters, the calculated *D*q/B value is 1.76, which is lower than the intersection of the 4A2g and 2E excited states (*D*q/*B* ≈ 2.3) [23,24]. This suggested that the Cr3+ ions reside in a relatively weak crystal field environment. The primary reason for the spectral broadening with increasing Cr3+ doping concentration in the β-Ga2O3:*x*Cr3+ GC is attributed to the multilattice occupation and enhanced electron‒lattice coupling of the Cr3+ ions [25,26]. By utilizing the optimized glass composition and heat-treatment temperature, we systematically investigated both the internal quantum efficiency (IQE) and absorption efficiency (AE) for various concentrations of Cr3+ in β-Ga2O3:*x*Cr3+ GCs, ultimately determining their external quantum efficiency (EQE). In Fig. 4(e), IQE, AE, and EQE of β-Ga2O3:*x*Cr3+ are given as a function of the Cr3+ doping concentration. Notably, when the doping concentration of Cr3+ reaches 0.10 mol%, IQE and AE achieve remarkable 87% and 57.5%, respectively, leading to high EQE of 50.1% (Fig. 4(e)).

The operational temperature of a high-power NIR pc-LED chip can reach up to 423 K during prolonged operation of the blue LED chip, underscoring the critical requirement for β-Ga2O3:*x*Cr3+ GC. Figure 4(f) presents a contour plot correlating the PL spectral evolution (*x*-axis) with temperature variation (*y*-axis), demonstrating the remarkable thermal stability of the β-Ga2O3:*x*Cr3+ GCs across increasing temperatures. In Fig. 4(g), the integrated PL intensity retained 84% of its initial room-temperature value at 423 K. The inset schematic illustrates the competing processes in Cr3+ ions following 4T2g→4A2g excitation: radiative transitions and thermally activated nonradiative cross-relaxation channels. Elevated temperatures increase the probability of nonradiative recombination of Cr3+ ions, consequently leading to a reduction in emission intensity, a phenomenon referred to as thermal quenching [27]. To quantitatively assess the thermal quenching resistance, we calculated the activation energy (Δ*E*a) through Arrhenius analysis via Eqs. (4) and (5) [27−29]:

(4) I(T)=I01+Cexp(−ΔE/kT)

(5) ln(I0I−1)=−ΔEakBT+lnA

where *k* is the Boltzmann constant, and *A* is a constant; *I*(*T*) represents the PL intensity at a given temperature; *I*0 is the initial PL intensity at room temperature. On the basis of Eq. (5), Δ*E*a of β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GC was calculated to be 0.44 eV (Fig. 4(h)). This high Δ*E* resulted in the robust thermal stability (89%@423 K) of β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GC. As shown in Fig. 4(i), β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GC exhibited exceptional EQE (50.1%) and luminescent thermal stability (89%@423 K), outperforming the majority of Cr3+-doped bulk materials [30−34].

The potential of β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GC was demonstrated through the design and fabrication of NIR GCc-LED using high-power blue chips (5 W) and as-prepared GC without encapsulants. As shown in Fig. 5(a), NIR GCc-LED exhibited nonsaturable absorption properties under high-power blue excitation. The NIR output power of GCc-LED reached as high as 568 mW, which is several-fold higher than that of pc-LEDs [30,31]. This clearly underscores the substantial advantages of β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GCs over their powder-based counterparts. Furthermore, the photoelectric efficiency of GCc-LEDs reached 13% at an operating current of 500 mA, markedly surpassing that of pc-LEDs (Fig. 5(c)). In addition, we investigated the influence of the GC thickness on the performance of the GCc-LED devices, identifying an optimal thickness of 0.8 mm (Fig. 5(d)). As shown in Fig. 5(e), the temperature of a high-power blue LED chip can rise as high as 150.6°C when it is operated at a working current of 150 mA. The illuminance of the β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ GC reached a remarkable value of 2100 lux and exhibited exceptional thermal stability, remaining virtually unchanged even after prolonged operation for 50 h (Fig. 5(f)).

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, through the implementation of an oxygen coordination competitive crystallization strategy, we designed and fabricated transparent β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ glass-ceramics. By precisely controlling the Ga content, we achieved controllable precipitation of β-Ga2O3:Cr3+ nanocrystals, resulting in exceptional optical properties, including a transmittance of 81.2% at a thickness of 0.4 mm, an internal quantum efficiency (IQE ≈ 100%) and high thermal stability (89%@423 K). When operated at an input current of 500 mA, the NIR GCc-LED device demonstrates remarkable performance, achieving a near-infrared light output of 568 mW with a conversion efficiency of 13%. The potential of the NIR GCc-LED has been successfully employed in various imaging applications, demonstrating its exceptional ability to produce high-resolution images of diverse subjects. Specifically, the device yielded remarkably clear images of intricate biological structures, including finger blood vessels, as well as detailed visualizations of industrial components such as circuit boards and metal springs.
