## 1 Introduction

With the continuous development of aeroengine technology, ceramic matrix composites (CMCs), i.e., SiCf/SiC composites, have emerged as the most promising alternatives to replace traditional high-temperature alloys [1−3]. CMCs exhibit superior properties, including excellent high-temperature resistance, low density, and high strength, and their application in hot-end components can effectively improve the thrust-to-weight ratio of engines [4−6]. However, CMCs easily oxidize or suffer from severe recession when exposed to water‒oxygen environments during service [7,8]. Therefore, protective coatings need to be prepared on the surface to increase their durability under harsh engine operating conditions.

Over the past few years, the latest generation of environmental barrier coatings (EBCs), e.g., Yb2SiO5/Yb2Si2O7/Si systems, have demonstrated good adaptability in complex service environments [9−11]. Nevertheless, emerging challenges arise as the inlet temperatures of next-generation aeroengines have been reported to reach 1500 °C [12], posing a pressing demand for enhanced thermal protection in CMCs under such extreme conditions. The performance of current EBCs is critically compromised by the relatively high thermal conductivity of rare-earth silicate materials and the limitation of the melting point of the Si bond layer, which hinders EBCs from further satisfying the demand for coating services at elevated temperatures [11]. Consequently, the concept of thermal/environmental barrier coatings (T/EBCs) systems was proposed, which replaced the top layer of the coating systems with thermal-insulation materials to further improve the service temperature [13,14]. To effectively develop T/EBCs coatings for CMCs applications, the selection of the top layer material must meet certain requirements, including a high melting point, oxidation resistance, low thermal conductivity, chemical inertness, and matching of thermal expansion to the interlayer materials (Yb2SiO5: 7.3×10−6 K−1, Yb2Si2O7: 4.3×10−6 K−1) [2,15,16]. To search for a suitable top-layer material, many efforts have been put forward. In recent years, several T/EBCs multilayer systems with typical TBC materials, such as LaMgAl11O19 [17], Y2O3-doped HfO2 [18], or rare-earth zirconate [19], as the top layer with promising outcomes have been designed and investigated. However, T/EBCs systems still face challenges in long-term, high-temperature applications, primarily because of significant thermal expansion mismatches and inadequate thermal shock resistance.

Among the novel ceramic materials for T/EBCs, rare-earth hafnates have been proposed for demanding application conditions, mainly because of their high melting point and phase stability above 1500 °C [15,20,21]. More importantly, despite having the same crystal structure as rare-earth zirconates (the most extensively studied thermal barrier coating candidates), rare-earth hafnates with the same rare-earth ions exhibit a lower coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) and lower thermal conductivity than zirconates [15,22]. Furthermore, the screening of rare-earth materials in recent years revealed that, among them, δ-Yb4Hf3O12 possesses a CTE that closely matches that of rare-earth silicates [23]. Moreover, δ-Yb4Hf3O12 is reported to have excellent resistance to both water vapor and molten-salt corrosion [24−27]. Therefore, the high-temperature performance of the T/EBC system with Yb4Hf3O12 as the top layer is undoubtedly worth investigating.

Atmospheric plasma spraying (APS) is a commonly employed technique for fabricating T/EBCs multilayer systems [10,28]. The microstructure and properties of a coating system are closely linked to its fabrication method and display thermodynamic characteristics that are distinct from those of bulk materials [29]. Plasma-sprayed coatings exhibit unique microstructural features, such as lamellar structures, interfaces, pores, and microcracks, which can significantly impact the overall performance of the coatings. In this work, a tri-layer T/EBC system Yb4Hf3O12/Yb2Si2O7/Si, with Yb4Hf3O12 as the top layer, was strategically designed and fabricated via plasma-spraying technology to address critical challenges in high-temperature performance. Yb2Si2O7 was chosen as the intermediate layer because of its low SiO2 reactivity and CTE close to that of SiC [30]. Additionally, considering the relatively large difference in the CTE between Yb4Hf3O12 and Yb2Si2O7, Yb2SiO5 was also added as an intermediate layer to fabricate a four-layer system of Yb4Hf3O12/Yb2SiO5/Yb2Si2O7/Si. The isothermal oxidation and thermal shock behaviors of both T/EBCs systems were studied at 1500 °C. Furthermore, to comprehensively investigate the failure mechanisms of multilayer systems under high-temperature thermal shock conditions, finite element analysis (FEA) was implemented to simulate the dynamic stress evolution within the coating architecture during the thermal process. This work may provide a reference for the design and application of T/EBCs systems.

### 2.1 Materials

Yb2SiO5, Yb2Si2O7, and Yb4Hf3O12 powders were synthesized using a solid-state method. The details of the preparation of Yb2SiO5 and Yb2Si2O7 powders can be found in our previous works [31,32]. Yb4Hf3O12 was produced by mixing Yb2O3 and HfO2 raw material powders (99.9%, Shanghai Sheeny Metal Materials Co., Ltd, China) in a 2 : 3 molar ratio and subsequently reacted in a muffle furnace at 1400 °C for 5 h. The obtained powder was spray-granulated to yield spherical particles with a median particle size of approximately 35 μm. The Yb4Hf3O12/Yb2Si2O7/Si and Yb4Hf3O12/Yb2SiO5/Yb2Si2O7/Si multilayer systems were subsequently deposited on the SiC substrate using air plasma spraying technology (A-2000, Sulzer Metco AG, Switzerland), and the spraying parameters are listed in Table 1. Additionally, to measure the thermal properties of the Yb4Hf3O12 coating, a freestanding Yb4Hf3O12 coating was also prepared and cut into appropriate dimensions for subsequent CTE and thermal conductivity tests.

### 2.2 High-temperature tests

The isothermal oxidation experiments were carried out at 1500 °C under atmospheric conditions. The prepared tri-layer and four-layer T/EBCs systems were placed in a muffle furnace and removed at 50 h intervals to observe the state of the samples, which were characterized after a total of 50 and 100 h of oxidation, respectively. Water-quenching thermal shock experiments were also carried out at 1500 °C. The prepared samples were placed in a tube furnace at 1500 °C, held for 15 min, removed, quickly cooled in deionized water at room temperature, and then dried in an oven at 110 °C to complete each cycle. The samples were removed after 20 and 40 cycles for characterization. To eliminate uncertainties in the sample preparation process, three parallel samples were used for each experiment. Each sample after high-temperature testing was sliced using a low-speed diamond wire cutter and then inlaid and polished via the cold inlay method to observe the cross-sectional morphology.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The phase compositions of the materials and samples were identified via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; RAX-10, Rigaku, Japan), with a 2*θ* scan range of 10°–80°. The microstructures and compositions of the polished cross-sections of the as-sprayed samples and samples after high-temperature tests were characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Magellan 400, FEI, USA) with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS; INCA SERIES, Oxford Instrument, UK). The microstructures of the Yb4Hf3O12/Yb2SiO5 interface after oxidation were analyzed using a focused ion beam (FIB; Versa 3D, FEI, USA) and observed by a scanning/transmission electron microscope (SEM/TEM; Talos F200X G2, FEI, USA). The CTE of the Yb4Hf3O12 coating was measured using a high-temperature dilatometer (TMA403F3, Netzsch, Germany) in the range of 100–1350 °C. The thermal diffusivity (*α*) of the Yb4Hf3O12 coating was measured via the laser flash method using a HyperFlash instrument (LFA 467, Netzsch, Germany). The thermal conductivities (*κ*) of the coatings were calculated using Eq. (1):

(1) κ=α×cp×ρ

where the heat capacity (*c**p*) was calculated on the basis of the Neumann–Kopp rule.

#### 2.4.1 Finite element model establishment and meshing

This study employed the ANSYS programming language for FEA calculations. Given the symmetrical characteristics of the model, the analysis was conducted on half of the cross-section of the coated sample, utilizing the axisymmetric properties of the cylindrical sample for a two-dimensional axisymmetric analysis. On the basis of the experimental results, two physical models of T/EBCs systems were established. The first system consists of Yb4Hf3O12 (0.135 mm)/Yb2Si2O7 (0.135 mm)/Si (0.07 mm), and the second system is composed of Yb4Hf3O12 (0.1 mm)/Yb2SiO5 (0.07 mm)/Yb2Si2O7 (0.1 mm)/Si (0.07 mm). To facilitate subsequent analysis, paths were defined within the finite element model, with computational results designating positive values (+) as tensile stress and negative values (−) as compressive stress (Figs. 1(a) and 1(c)). The finite element model was also subjected to mesh generation (Figs. 1(b) and 1(d)).

#### 2.4.2 Model assumptions and material thermophysical property parameters

Considering the complex physicochemical changes involved in the thermal shock process of the coating system, to facilitate a more manageable and focused analysis while maintaining the integrity and relevance of the simulation results, the following critical assumptions were made in the FEA simulation to simplify the models: (1) The coating is initially in a stress-free state at the beginning of the thermal shock experiment. (2) The entire coating system is in an isotropic elastic deformation state. (3) The interface between two adjacent layers is flat, and the bonding between the internal layers of the coating is well established. (4) The ceramic layer is assumed to be linearly elastic, and cracks will only form in the coating when the stress exceeds the yield stress. (5) The influence of cracks in the coating is neglected.

#### 2.4.3 Boundary conditions

The boundary conditions of this simulation were consistent with those of the experimental process. At the initial calculation time (i.e., *t* = 0 s), the temperature of the entire model was assumed to be consistent with the room temperature at 25 °C, and the model was in a stress-free state. To prevent excessive deformation during the calculation, displacement constraints were applied to the symmetric axis and the bottom of the model. The horizontal displacement (*ux* = 0) was fixed along the left symmetric axis to ensure that no displacement occurred outside the symmetric axis, resulting in a zero normal displacement at the nodes. The vertical displacement (*uy* = 0) was fixed at the bottom of the model to simulate the coating sample being placed horizontally on the test platform.

This study focused on calculating the stress generated during the water-quenching thermal shock assessment process. Specifically, the coated sample was uniformly heated from 25 to 1500 °C in a muffle furnace within 30 s, held at the target temperature for 15 min, and then cooled in deionized water for 20 s. Therefore, convective heat transfer loads were applied to the upper, lower, and right surfaces of the models. During the heating process, natural convection was assumed with a convective heat transfer coefficient of 100 W·m−2·K−1. During the cooling process, the convective heat transfer coefficient was set to 3000 W·m−2·K−1. The ambient temperature for convective heat transfer was set to 25 °C. The evolution and distribution of residual stresses over a 950 s air-cooling assessment process were calculated using a simulation.

### 3.1 Characterizations of plasma-sprayed Yb4Hf3O12 coatings

Figure 2(a) shows the XRD patterns of the Yb4Hf3O12 powder and the as-sprayed coating. The prepared Yb4Hf3O12 powders were confirmed to be a pure phase with a δ-phase and did not decompose after spraying. The CTE curve of the plasma-sprayed Yb4Hf3O12 coating is shown in Fig. 2(b). The CTE of the Yb4Hf3O12 coating increased progressively with increasing temperature, with an average value of 8.51×10−6 K−1 between 100 and 1350 °C. This value is notably lower than those of 6–8 wt% yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) and rare-earth zirconates [21,33]; thus, it is considered to be more suitable for T/EBCs applications. Figure 2(c) presents the thermal conductivity of the as-prepared Yb4Hf3O12 coating from room temperature to 1200 °C. The thermal conductivity of the Yb4Hf3O12 coating decreased with increasing temperature below 800 °C, but tended to increase between 1000 and 1200 °C, reaching 0.72 W·m−1·K−1 at 1200 °C.

### 3.2 Microstructure of T/EBCs systems

The cross-sectional micrographs of the tri-layer and four-layer systems are shown in Fig. 3. The two prepared coating systems exhibited good interlayer adhesion and exhibited defects, such as microcracks and pores introduced by plasma spraying, with total thicknesses of approximately 340 and 360 μm, respectively. The thickness of the Si bond layer was approximately 70 μm for both coating systems. The porosities of the Yb4Hf3O12 layers in the tri-layer and four-layer systems were 6.8%, whereas that of the Yb2Si2O7 layers was 3.5%. The porosity of the intermediate Yb2SiO5 layer was 4.3%. Owing to the volatilization of SiO2 during the spraying process, small amounts of Yb2O3 and Yb2SiO5 secondary phases were generated in the as-sprayed Yb2SiO5 and Yb2Si2O7 coatings, respectively (manifested as white regions in the coatings) [31,32,34]. In contrast, no secondary phases were detected in the Yb4Hf3O12 coating, and the Yb4Hf3O12 top layer displayed a lamellar structure typical of APS coatings.

### 3.3 Isothermal oxidation behavior of the T/EBCs systems

Figure 4(a) shows macroscopic photographs of the tri-layer and four-layer T/EBCs systems on SiC substrates before and after the isothermal oxidation test at 1500 °C. No spallation or optically visible damage occurred in either of the two coating systems after oxidation at 1500 °C for 100 h, indicating their outstanding high-temperature resistance. A small number of black regions and Si spheres were observed on the surface and edge of the coatings after oxidation, which may be related to the diffusion of the Si bond layer at 1500 °C. In addition, the XRD analysis shown in Fig. 4(b) demonstrated that no detectable phase transformation occurred in the surface layer of either coating system after oxidation for 100 h, indicating the excellent high-temperature phase stability of the Yb4Hf3O12 coating at 1500 °C.

To further investigate the isothermal oxidation performance of the coating systems, the cross-sectional morphologies of the coatings were investigated, as illustrated in Fig. 5. After 50 h of oxidation at 1500 °C, vertical cracks appeared in the Yb4Hf3O12 top layer of the tri-layer system in Fig. 5(a), and the yellow arrows along the top of the coating indicate these cracks. The vertical cracks terminated at the interface between the top layer and Yb2Si2O7 interlayer and did not extend downward. With increasing oxidation time, after 100 h of isothermal oxidation, an increase in the number of vertical cracks in the top layer was observed, as shown in Fig. 5(b). However, these cracks remained confined to the interface of the top layer without extending further into the underlying layers. After 100 h of oxidation, no significant lateral cracks were observed at the interfaces between the layers of the tri-layer system. In contrast, at 50 h, a reduction in the thickness of the Si bond layer at the bottom of the coating system was noted. With prolonged oxidation time, defect healing was observed in the top layer of the multilayer system, which was attributed to the sintering of Yb4Hf3O12, as illustrated in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

### 3.5 Interface evolution at 1500 °C

Interfacial stability is an important factor in evaluating the performance of multilayer systems. This work primarily analyzed the interfacial evolution between the Yb4Hf3O12/Yb2Si2O7 and Yb4Hf3O12/Yb2SiO5 top layers at 1500 °C. Following oxidation, a continuous diffusion layer was observed at the Yb4Hf3O12/Yb2Si2O7 interface, with a thickness of 9.8 μm after 100 h of oxidation, as shown in Figs. 8(a) and 8(b). The elemental mapping results revealed an enrichment of Yb within the diffusion layer, whereas Hf was not detected in this region. Furthermore, the EDS results (Table 3) of the diffusion phase revealed a Yb/Si ratio approaching 2 : 1 in the diffusion layer (point 1), coupled with the observed reduction in the Yb content within the Yb4Hf3O12 layer close to the diffusion interface (point 2). This suggests that the interlayer diffusion of Yb from Yb4Hf3O12 to Yb2Si2O7 may occur at high temperature (1500 °C) to form the Yb2SiO5 reaction product, as shown in Reaction (2). Interfacial reactions and diffusion may induce the generation of defects (red circles in Figs. 8(a) and 8(b)) and reduce interfacial stability [16,35]. Additionally, with further intensification of diffusion, the reduction in Yb3+ may cause Yb4Hf3O12 near the interface to undergo a phase transition from the δ-phase to the F-phase [36], leading to differences in the CTE and ultimately potentially resulting in crack formation.

### 3.6 TGO growth and diffusion of the Si bond layer

Upon oxidation, a continuous thermally grown oxide (TGO) layer can be observed between the Yb2Si2O7 and Si layers of both coating systems (Fig. 10), whereas no significant failure or penetrating cracks at the TGO layer interfaces were observed. Figure 11 shows a comparison of the TGO growth behavior at the Yb2Si2O7/Si interfaces for the two coating systems. After 50 h of oxidation, the thicknesses of the TGO layers were approximately 4.2 and 2.6 μm for tri-layer and four-layer systems, respectively. An increase in the thickness of the TGO layer was observed for both coating systems with increasing oxidation time. After a prolonged oxidation time of 100 h, the thicknesses of the two coating systems reached 7.7 and 4.6 μm, respectively. As shown in Fig. 11(e), the four-layer system clearly has a thinner TGO layer than the tri-layer system. This may be attributed to the channel cracks fully penetrating the Yb4Hf3O12 layer in the tri-layer coating, which provided a rapid transport path for oxygen within the coating, accelerating the growth of TGO.

After the thermal shock test, owing to the brief high-temperature exposure, only an extremely thin TGO layer formed at the Yb2Si2O7/Si interface, so we did not compare the TGO thicknesses after the thermal shock of the two coatings. However, the diffusion of Si from the bond layer into the outer layer can be clearly observed in the EDS mapping results of both tri-layer and four-layer systems (Fig. 12). With an increasing number of thermal cycles, Si in the bond layer diffused into the outer layer and was oxidized to form SiO2, leading to a reduction in the thickness of the bond layer. Previous research on EBCs [39] has also revealed the migration of silicon toward the surface layer after oxidation at 1426 and 1482 °C (exceeding the melting point of silicon) owing to the melting of silicon. However, in the T/EBCs system, the diffusion phenomenon was not observed in the cross-sectional analysis during isothermal oxidation. It is apparent that the water-quenching thermal shock process accelerated the diffusion rate of the Si bond layer toward the outer layers, which may be attributed to the generation of microcracks or interfacial defects during the thermal shock, which provide direct channels for Si migration and enhance oxygen infiltration. In addition, rapid cooling and heating cycles induce metastable phase transitions in the SiO2 TGO layer (β-cristobalite to α-phase), accompanied by substantial volume changes and localized thermal stress accumulation [40,41], thus further promoting the outward diffusion of SiO2.

For both T/EBCs systems, although the addition of Yb4Hf3O12 improved their service life at 1500 °C (compared with previous research [19,42−44]), the melting-induced failure of the silicon bond layer remained a critical failure mechanism in the coating system. During prolonged isothermal oxidation at 1500 °C (exceeding the melting point of Si, ~1414 °C), melting and exudation of Si bonds occurred within the coating. Owing to the inadequate diffusion pathways in the coating interior, diffusion was preferentially localized along the unconstrained coating edges. This edge-dominant transport mechanism resulted in the formation of solidified Si spheres at the periphery (Fig. 4(a)) and a thickness reduction in the Si bond layer. After thermal shock, the generation of cracks and pores within the coating provided enhanced diffusion pathways, and Si migration occurred via two pathways: diffusion through the outer layer and diffusion along the edges.

### 4.1 Effect of the Yb4Hf3O12 top laye<i>r</i>

After the addition of the Yb4Hf3O12 coating, the T/EBCs coating system achieved an isothermal oxidation life of more than 100 h at 1500 °C, demonstrating a significant improvement over previously reported applications of EBCs at this temperature (almost less than 20 h) [42−44]. The significantly extended oxidation lifetime of the Yb4Hf3O12 T/EBCs system compared with that of conventional EBCs under isothermal conditions at 1500 °C is attributed primarily to the enhanced oxygen barrier capability provided by the Yb4Hf3O12 top layer. Previous studies on EBCs have shown that failure initiated from the TGO layer is one of the most common modes of degradation of EBCs [40,41]. At 1500 °C, accelerated TGO growth within the EBC system induced a sharp increase in the interfacial stress at the TGO/EBC interface [45], which triggered crack initiation and propagation, ultimately triggering coating spallation (Figs. S4 and S5 in the ESM). In contrast, despite the presence of oxygen permeation channels, such as vertical cracks, the incorporation of Yb4Hf3O12 effectively resulted in a much thinner TGO thickness during the oxidation of T/EBCs (Fig. S6 in the ESM), which directly reduced stress accumulation at the TGO interface, thereby suppressing destructive crack networks and prolonging the service life during the isothermal oxidation test at 1500 °C.

For T/EBCs top layers operating under thermal gradients, low thermal conductivity also remains a critical property. The literature indicates that a ~300 μm thick YSZ coating achieves an insulation temperature of 100–200 °C [46]. As shown in Fig. 13(a), the thermal conductivity of the Yb4Hf3O12 coating is reduced by half compared with that of the conventional EBCs top layer Yb2SiO5 [47] and is even significantly lower than that of widely studied TBC materials such as YSZ (approximately 35%), rare-earth zirconates, and lanthanum magnesium aluminates [48,49]. Thus, the incorporation of the Yb4Hf3O12 top layer is expected to provide excellent thermal insulation for T/EBCs systems. As high-temperature oxidation progressed, sintering occurred in the top layer, leading to an increase in the thermal conductivity. As shown in Fig. 13(b), the thermal conductivity of the Yb4Hf3O12 coating after sintering at 1500 °C for 50 h is approximately double that of its as-sprayed state. However, compared with the Yb2SiO5 top layer, Yb4Hf3O12 still exhibited excellent anti-sintering performance. After sintering at 1500 °C, the thermal conductivity of Yb2SiO5 remained significantly lower than that of Yb2SiO5 sintered at a much lower temperature (1350 °C) for the same duration [47]. Moreover, Yb4Hf3O12 had a much smaller grain size than Yb2SiO5 after sintering (Fig. 9(c)). These factors collectively indicate the effectiveness of the Yb4Hf3O12 top layer in T/EBCs applications.

### 4.2 Crack growth of the T/EBCs

Cracking of the top and intermediate layer interfaces represents a critical factor contributing to the failure of the coatings. Figure 14 shows the crack growth schematics of the two coating systems. The formation of vertical cracks within the tri-layer systems observed during isothermal oxidation was driven primarily by the substantial CTE mismatch between the Yb4Hf3O12 top coat (8.5×10−6 K−1) and the underlying Yb2Si2O7 intermediate layer (4.3×10−6 K−1), which generated significant intrinsic thermal stresses during heating and prolonged holding at 1500 °C. The progressive accumulation of these stresses during high-temperature exposure eventually exceeded the strain tolerance limit of the coating system, leading to relaxation via the propagation of vertical cracks along the thickness direction. The generation of vertical cracks driven by CTE mismatch stress accumulation and relaxation of the oxide layers is consistent with channel cracking in several other multilayer systems during isothermal oxidation tests [50−53]. In contrast, the introduction of an intermediate Yb2SiO5 layer, which possesses a CTE (7.25×10−6 K−1) intermediate between those of Yb4Hf3O12 and Yb2Si2O7, effectively mitigated the coefficient mismatch. In addition, in a multilayer system on the surface of SiC substrates, the Yb2Si2O7 and layers are reported to experience compressive stress, which inhibits crack propagation [34]. Consequently, cracks in both coating systems ended at the Yb2Si2O7 layer.

During the water-quench thermal shock test, more severe experimental conditions induce higher thermal stresses within the coating and at the interlayer interfaces during the thermal shock process. Consequently, more pronounced crack propagation was observed in the coating systems under these transient thermal conditions. In the tri-layer system, in addition to vertical channel cracks, penetrating transverse cracks formed at the Yb4Hf3O12/Yb2Si2O7 interface, driving partial delamination. This is because the substantial difference in the CTE between Yb4Hf3O12 and Yb2Si2O7 resulted in superimposed interfacial shear and tensile normal stresses during quenching, creating preferential sites for transverse crack initiation. Furthermore, during thermal shock, vertical cracks propagating from the surface undergo deflection along the stress-concentrated Yb4Hf3O12/Yb2Si2O7 interface owing to abrupt stress transitions and pre-existing defects, promoting transverse crack nucleation and the generation of a crack network [54]. To systematically investigate the failure mechanisms of the two coating systems under thermal shock, FEA was employed to further quantitatively evaluate the interfacial stress concentrations under extreme thermal gradients. The overall stress distributions of the tri-layer and four-layer coating systems after thermal shock are shown in Fig. 15. The radial stress levels and distributions of the two systems are relatively similar, with maximum radial tensile stresses of 573.86 and 575.66 MPa, respectively. A radial tensile stress concentration area can be observed near the center of the top layer (indicated by the dark red region in Figs. 15(a1) and 15(b1), and the radial tensile stress gradually decreases from the center to the edge of the top layer. Notably, compared with the tri-layer system, the four-layer system exhibited a smaller stress concentration area and included a stress buffer region (indicated by the dark green region in Fig. 15(b1)). The presence of this buffer region in the four-layer system effectively restricted the deflection of vertical cracks toward the interface, thus mitigating the transverse crack formation during thermal shock compared with the tri-layer system. In addition, the axial and shear stress distributions of both coating systems are relatively similar, with overall low stress levels and localized stress concentrations only at the edges. Overall, the four-layer coating system, owing to the presence of a stress transition region, mitigated stress gradients, thereby enhancing the thermal shock resistance of the coating.

Combined with the experimental results and the FEA results, during the water-quenching thermal shock process, both the tri-layer and four-layer coating systems experienced radial tensile stresses in the top layers, leading to vertical crack initiation. Although the magnitude and distribution of the radial tensile stresses in the top layers were comparable between the two systems, the four-layer system incorporated a Yb2SiO5 interlayer with a CTE intermediate between those of Yb2Si2O7 and Yb4Hf3O12, which effectively mitigated the stress gradients and reduced the stress concentration zone in the top layer. Consequently, the four-layer system exhibited fewer vertical cracks during thermal shock. In contrast, the tri-layer system developed numerous vertical cracks that tend to propagate horizontally along the Yb4Hf3O12/Yb2Si2O7 interface, forming interfacial cracks that ultimately led to coating failure. Furthermore, the four-layer system demonstrated smaller stress gradients in the radial, axial, and shear stresses at the edge interfaces, along with reduced edge effects, thereby exhibiting superior thermal shock resistance.

## 5 Conclusions

In this work, T/EBCs multilayer systems comprising Yb4Hf3O12 as the top layer, Yb4Hf3O12/Yb2Si2O7/Si and Yb4Hf3O12/Yb2SiO5/Yb2Si2O7/Si were designed and deposited on the SiC substrate through a plasma-spraying method. The isothermal oxidation and thermal shock behaviors of the T/EBCs were studied at 1500 °C. The main conclusions are drawn as follows:

1) The Yb4Hf3O12 top layer significantly improved the thermal insulation of the T/EBCs systems, mitigating the growth of the TGO layer and enabling structural integrity for more than 100 h at 1500 nC. Despite a partial increase in the sintering-induced conductivity, Yb4Hf3O12 retained a lower post-sintering conductivity than traditional rare-earth silicates, highlighting its superior thermal stability.

2) CTE mismatch-induced vertical cracking in tri-layer systems accelerated oxygen diffusion after isothermal oxidation, leading to the formation of a thicker TGO layer. The four-layer system addressed this through a Yb2SiO5 interlayer that bridged the CTE gradient, eliminating vertical cracks. In addition, the compatibility of Yb2SiO5 with Yb4Hf3O12 ensured interfacial stability and prevented harmful reactions and diffusion.

3) Both kinds of T/EBCs systems showed no significant signs of failure after the water-quenching thermal shock test at 1500 °C. Vertical cracks were observed in the cross-sections of both coating systems following thermal shock, but these cracks did not penetrate into the Yb2Si2O7 or Si bond layers. Compared with the tri-layer system, the four-layer system demonstrated superior high-temperature performance. Extended horizontal cracks appeared between the surface and intermediate layers in the tri-layer systems, leading to partial delamination of the top layer, whereas no penetrating cracks were generated in the four-layer system throughout the test. The FEA results indicated that the Yb2SiO5 layer in the four-layer system effectively alleviated stress fluctuations during thermal shock and reduced the stress concentration areas in the surface layer, thus mitigating the formation of cracks in the coating system.
