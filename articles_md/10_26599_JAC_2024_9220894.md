## 1 Introduction

High-entropy materials are an emerging class of multicomponent solid solution materials that were first proposed for alloys in 2004. This concept was subsequently used in high-entropy ceramics (HECs) [1,2]. Compared with traditional ceramics, HECs possess many interesting properties owing to their four core effects, such as a stable phase structure, high corrosion, oxidation resistance, and unique mechanical properties, making them ideal materials for a wide range of applications [3−5].

Sintering is a high-temperature process for consolidating ceramic powders, during which both densification and grain growth occur. A few grains can grow at a very fast rate under certain conditions, which is known as abnormal grain growth (AGG). In the production process of polycrystalline materials, AGG may lead to a heterogeneous grain morphology, which is typically detrimental to the material performance, particularly the mechanical properties. Therefore, controlling grain growth behavior during sintering is a very important topic for obtaining high-performance ceramic materials. According to these studies, the grain growth in high-entropy ceramics at high temperatures is relatively slower than that in the corresponding single-component ceramics owing to the sluggish diffusion effect. Inspired by this, Wang *et al.* [6] and Zhou *et al*. [7] successfully prepared high-entropy (TiZrNbTaMo)C and (La0.2Ce0.2Nd0.2Sm0.2Eu0.2)2Zr2O7 with fine grains respectively. Then, Tan *et al*. [8] used polyacrylamide gel technology to successfully solve the problems of grain coarsening and particle aggregation in high-entropy ceramics during sintering. By using a plasma-assisted ball milling technique, Cheng *et al*. [9] enhanced the sintering characteristics and prepared high-entropy fluorite oxide ceramics with little AGG, good density, and mechanical properties. However, Zhou *et al*. [10] showed that the sluggish diffusion effect did not dominate the grain growth behavior of high-entropy ceramics owing to the defects formed during the synthesis process, resulting in rapid grain growth of high-entropy ceramics at high temperatures. Thus, studying the growth process of high-entropy ceramic grains is an attractive scientific topic.

Pseudobrookite ceramics are used as high-temperature insulation materials, water purification filters, and diesel particulate filters owing to their low thermal conductivity, high melting point, low coefficient of thermal expansion, and excellent thermal shock tolerance [11−13]. Nevertheless, the structure of pseudobrookite is unstable and prone to disintegration, which severely limits its future applications below 1200 °C [14,15]. To solve the abovementioned problems, Wu *et al*. [16] proposed that the properties of pseudobrookite ceramics can be enhanced by forming high-entropy phases. Mg, Co, Ni, and Zn were selected for the M position in the high-entropy pseudobrookite phase, as these metal elements have been proven to have good compatibility and high doping amounts in the lattice of the pseudobrookite phase. However, the phenomenon of AGG is still found during the preparation of high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5, which is characterized by the coexistence of equiaxed grains and rod-like grains. This type of AGG will undoubtedly have a negative impact on its performance. To solve these problems, an in-depth study on the grain growth behavior of high-entropy pseudobrookite should be carried out.

Currently, typical high-entropy ceramics are generated in a single step. However, high-entropy pseudobrookite ceramics are formed through a two-step solid-phase process. Therefore, in addition to the sluggish diffusion effect and slowing grain growth, the grain sizes of the high-entropy intermediate phase and calcined TiO2 also affect the occurrence of AGG in high-entropy pseudobrookite ceramics. To support this finding, three kinds of (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5 powders were successfully prepared from three different starting materials with varying size distributions. The research results indicate that high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5 prepared from micron-sized starting materials has a small grain size and exhibits excellent hardness and thermal conductivity.

## 2 Experimental procedure

High-entropy pseudobrookite (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5 (MT2) and CoTi2O5 were prepared through an *in situ* solid-phase reaction method using the corresponding individual metallic oxides as starting materials. In this study, Mg, Co, Ni, and Zn were selected for the M position in MT2. Micron-sized CoO and rutile were used to prepare CoTi2O5; three starting material components with different particle sizes were selected to synthesize MT2. Component 1 was composed of four micron-sized metallic oxides (μM) and micron-sized rutile (μT2), and the high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5 prepared by component 1 was called μMμT2. Component 2 was composed of four micron-sized metallic oxides (μM) and nanosized rutile (nT2), and the high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5 prepared by component 2 was called μMnT2. Component 3 was composed of four nanosized metallic oxides (nM) and nanosized rutile (nT2), and the high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5 prepared by component 3 was called nMnT2.

MgO (99%, 3 μm), MgO (99.9%, 50 nm), ZnO (99.9%, 3 μm), ZnO (99.5%, 30 nm), TiO2 (rutile, 99%, 2–3 μm), and TiO2 (rutile, 99.5%, 40 nm) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China; CoO (99.9%, 2 μm), Co3O4 (99.5%, 30 nm), NiO (99%, 4 μm), and NiO (99.5%, 30 nm) were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China. The particle sizes of the starting materials are shown in Fig. 1. The average particle sizes of the micron-sized raw materials are between 2 and 5 μm. However, the average particle sizes of the nanosized raw materials are between 30 and 50 nm. All reagents were used as received. All the oxide powders were weighed by stoichiometry (to ensure that the molar ratio of the sum of metallic oxides to titanium dioxide was 1 : 8) and mixed by ball milling at 200 r·min−1 for 10 h with ethanol as the dispersing phase. After drying and sieving, powder mixtures were added with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and then pressed into pellets. The powder compacts were pressed under 40 MPa for 10 min and then sintered at 1400 °C for 2 h in air to prepare the ceramic specimens.

The phase composition of the as-synthesized powders was identified by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advanced, Bruker, Germany) using Cu Kα radiation (λ* = 1.5406 Å) with a step size of 0.02° at a scanning rate of 2 (°)·min−1. The high-resolution microstructure, crystal structure, and element distribution of the high-entropy MT2 specimens were studied by a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Jem-F200, JEOL, Japan) and an energy disperse spectrometer (EDS; X-Max20, Oxford, UK), respectively. The chemical states and chemical environment of the metallic elements in high-entropy MT2 were analyzed by an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; Axis supra, Kratos, UK) with an Al Kα X-ray source. All the data were calibrated to the adventitious C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Zeiss, Sigma 300, Germany) was used to automatically observe the surface microstructures of the samples after sintering at designated temperatures. The electron back scatter diffraction (EBSD) samples were polished by an SEM mill (1061, Fischione Instruments, USA) at 8 kV for 45 min and 4 kV for 30 min. The average area and number of grain boundaries of MT2s were obtained via EBSD analysis. The EBSD measurements were performed on an SEM (JSM-7800F, JEOL, Japan) in an area of 400 μm × 350 μm. Nano-indentation testing (Hysitron TI-Premier, USA) was performed with a Berkovich pyramid-shaped diamond tip to determine the elastic modulus and hardness. A maximum load of 5 mN was applied to the samples within 15 s, and the samples were held for 2 s at the peak load. The thermal conductivity was obtained by the transient plane source (TPS) method (Tps2500s, Hot Disk, Sweden). The reaction temperature was determined by a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC; NETZSCH, Bavaria, Germany) in heat flux DSC mode. The samples (60 mg) were heated in platinum crucibles at a rate of 10 °C·min−1 in an air atmosphere from room temperature to 1400 °C.

### 3.1 Phases and microstructures of as-sintered ceramics

The XRD and SEM analyses of high-entropy μMμT2, μMnT2, and nMnT2 are displayed in Fig. 2. As shown in the XRD pattern (Fig. 2(a)), the existence of harp peaks suggests that a well-crystallized structure is formed in all three samples. The pseudobrookite structure (CoTi2O5, JCPDS Card No. 76-1600, *Cmcm*, *a* = 3.732 Å, *b* = 9.718 Å, and *c* = 10.069 Å) can be clearly detected in these three samples without the formation of any second phase or mesophase. Figures 2(b) and 2(c) show high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images and the corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns of high-entropy μMμT2. As shown in Fig. 2(b), lattice fringes with an interplanar distance of 0.340 nm are obtained by measuring the average distance of 11 parallel fringes, which corresponds to the *d*-spacing of the ( 1¯ 1¯0) plane in the pseudobrookite structure. Furthermore, the related SAED pattern along the [1 1¯0] direction displayed in Fig. 2(c) also confirms that the crystal structure of the synthesized μMμT2 is a pseudobrookite phase. Figures 2(e) and 2(h) show HRTEM images taken along the [3 3¯4] and [ 3¯3 2¯] axes, respectively. A lattice stripe distance of approximately 0.34 nm is found in both images, which corresponds to the interplanar distance of the {110} face in pseudobrookite. Moreover, the elemental distribution in the samples was analyzed by EDS in STEM. The EDS maps in Fig. 2(d), 2(g), and 2(j) show that Mg, Co, Ni, Zn, Ti, and O are uniformly distributed at the microscopic level in the three MT2s without significant chemical clustering or elemental segregation. As a consequence, the results of the XRD analyses, together with the TEM and STEM−EDS analyses, strongly support that the synthesized μMμT2, μMnT2, and nMnT2 are all pseudobrookite-structured and show great chemical uniformity.

### 3.2 Structural analysis of high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5

Figure 3 shows the total spectra and core energy level spectra of the corresponding elements obtained by XPS. Each of the constituent elements in the three MT2s, including Mg, Co, Ni, Zn, Ti, and O, are visible in the resulting XPS data, which is consistent with the EDS elemental mapping results in Fig. 2. Figure 3(a) shows the XPS spectra of Mg 1s, Co 2p, Ni 2p, Zn 2p, and Ti 2p in μMμT2. The binding energy of Mg 1s in high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5 is ~1303.14 eV, which is consistent with the binding energy of Mg2+ in MgTi2O5 [17]. Similarly, the Co 2p spectrum shows four peaks at ~795.57 and ~780.06 eV with satellite peaks at ~803.00 and ~786.95 eV, which are almost comparable to the binding energy and peak shape of Co2+ in CoTi2O5 [18]. For the Ni ions in high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5, the binding energies of Ni 2p1/2 and 2p3/2 are ~872.73 and ~855.19 eV, respectively, which are similar to the binding energies of Ni2+ in typical Ni insulators [19,20]. Furthermore, the strong satellite peaks at ~861.65 and ~879.79 eV correspond to the satellite structure of high-spin Ni2+ in the octahedral position [18]. For the Zn ions in high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5, the binding energies of Zn 2p3/2 and Zn 2p1/2 are ~1021.27 and ~1044.22 eV, respectively, which are consistent with the binding energies of typical Zn2+ [21,22]. The two detected peaks in the Ti 2p spectrum are located at ~458.51  and 464.40 eV, which can be attributed to Ti 2p3/2 and Ti 2p1/2, respectively. Further investigation reveals that the Ti species are present only in the form of Ti4+ rather than in the form of Ti3+ in the obtained samples. Additionally, the binding energies of the constituent elements in Fig. 3(b) (μMnT2) and Fig. 3(c) (nMnT2) are nearly equal to those in Fig. 3(a) (μMμT2). In conclusion, the XPS results indicate that the chemical states and chemical environments of Mg, Co, Ni, Zn, and Ti ions in the three MT2s are identical.

### 3.3 Analysis of grain growth habit of pseudobrookite ceramic

Figure 4 displays the microstructure of the three MT2s and CoTi2O5. Three different particle sizes of starting materials (component 1–component 3) were sintered at 1400 °C for 2 h to form three MT2s. CoTi2O5 is formed by the reactions of micron-sized CoO and TiO2 with each other at 1400 °C for 2 h. The grain size of all MT2s is much smaller than that of its single-phase CoTi2O5 (Fig. 4(d)). This is because the sluggish diffusion effect decreases the thermodynamic driving force for grain growth at high temperatures and prevents grain coarsening [23,24]. According to Fig. 4(a), μMμT2 has a large number of uniformly equiaxed crystals. The grain size of μMμT2 ranges from 3 to 21 μm, with an average of 6.52 μm. In contrast, Figs. 4(b) and 4(c) indicate that the μMnT2 and nMnT2 grains both exhibit the typical morphology of the pseudobrookite phase (the coexistence of rod-like and equiaxed grains). The appearance of rod-like grains leads to an increase in the average grain size and a decrease in the grain size uniformity of high-entropy pseudobrookite ceramics. The average grain size of μMnT2 is 9.34 μm, which is somewhat slightly larger than that of μMμT2, and the length of the rod-like particles is as large as 39.53 μm. Notably, the average grain size of nMnT2 increases to 13.48 μm, and the length of the plate-like particles reaches 100 µm, which is obviously larger than those of μMμT2 and μMnT2. This indicates that the sluggish diffusion effect is not the only key factor controlling the grain growth of high-entropy pseudobrookite ceramics; the grain size of high-entropy pseudobrookite ceramics increases as the particle size of starting materials decreases under the same sintering process.

Rod-like particles are the typical morphology of pseudobrookite ceramics and are caused by AGG at high temperatures [25]. EBSD was used to characterize the grain orientation in ceramics to further examine the abnormal growth of the grains. Inverse pole figure (IPF) maps of MT2 ceramics synthesized from three components are shown in Figs. 5(a)–5(c), where different colors represent different relative crystal orientations, and closer colors reflect closer grain orientations. Generally, grain boundaries with misorientation angles greater than 15° are called large-angle grain boundaries, and grain boundaries with misorientation angles less than 15° are called low-angle grain boundaries, as represented by the black and white lines in Fig. 5(a)–5(c), respectively. In μMμT2, there are many large pseudobrookite domains that are subdivided into very small subgrains with very similar orientations. This is evidenced by the very similar subgrain colors within the pseudobrookite domain (Fig. 5(a)). Compared toμMμT2, the grain size of μMnT2 significantly increases. As a result of grain size coarsening, the number of low-angle boundaries decreases from 35.6% to 22.8%, while the number of high-angle boundaries increases from 64.4° to 77.2°, as shown in Fig. 5(d). In the case of nMnT2 (as illustrated in Fig. 5(c)), the particles studied appear to be composed of pseudobrookite grains with nearly no subdivisions. Furthermore, nMnT2 contains a significant percentage of high-angle boundaries (the majority of misorientation angles are between 80° and 100°). The grain boundary energy of large-angle boundaries is much greater than that of low-angle boundaries, resulting in the rapid migration of grain boundaries, which promotes grain growth. Thus, the grain growth rates of μMnT2 and nMnT2 were faster than that of μMμT2 in this study.

Notably, the crystal orientations of the rod crystal planes in μMnT2 and nMnT2 are basically the same. As shown in Fig. 5(b) and 5(c), the lengths of the (010) and (001) planes are longer than that of the (100) plane. To further comprehend this phenomenon, density functional theory (DFT) and Bravais Friedel Donnary Harker (BFDH) techniques were utilized to analyze the growth of each crystal plane. The crystal structure determines the growth behavior of grains. The previously mentioned investigations support that single-phase CoTi2O5 and high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5 are pseudobrookite phases. Therefore, CoTi2O5 was used in this study to calculate the theoretical growth habit of high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5.

The CoTi2O5 cells were cut along the (100), (010), and (001) planes, and the vacuum slab thickness was set to 15 Å. A 2 × 2 supercell was created perpendicular to the z*-axis, and the models are shown in Fig. 6. Equation (1) was then used to calculate the surface energy of the low-index (100), (010), and (001) plane surfaces, which are determined to be 8.36, 6.47, and 5.49 eV/nm2, respectively. According to the calculated results, the (100) plane has a greater surface energy than the (010) and (001) planes, which causes the cell to grow significantly along the vertical direction of the (100) plane. Because of the difference in the grain growth rates of the different crystal faces, the slower-growing (010) and (001) planes extend along the *a*-axis during grain growth and are ultimately retained according to the Bravais rule.

where E*slab is the energy of the model; *E*bulk is the energy of an equal number of formula units in the bulk; *A* is the surface area of one face in the slab model [26,27].

However, it is interesting to note that the elongated crystal planes in Figs. 5(b) and 5(c) are not totally composed of the (010) and (001) planes mentioned above. AGG is also observed in additional planes with orientations similar to those of the (021) and (023) planes (the crystal planes are shown in purple and pink). To illustrate this phenomenon, the BFDH model was used to simulate the crystal morphology, as shown in Fig. 5(e). The crystal morphology shows that the (021) and (023) planes expand along the *a*-axis with the growth of the (010) and (001) planes. Therefore, it can be concluded that during the grain growth process of the pseudobrookite ceramic, the (100) plane grows rapidly, causing the (010), (001), (021), and (023) planes to elongate and eventually form columnar crystals.

### 3.4 Study of grain growth process

A formation process analysis was conducted to explain the differences in the grain morphology among the three MT2s. Figures 7(a)–7(c) show the *in situ* XRD patterns of the three components sintered from room temperature to 1400 °C. High-entropy pseudobrookite ceramics are formed through a two-step solid phase process. First, TiO2 and metallic oxides react with each other to form (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)TiO3 (MTiO3), a high-entropy intermediate phase, below 1100 °C. As the temperature increased to 1400 °C, MTiO3 and calcined TiO2 continued to react and finally formed high-entropy pseudobrookite ceramics. The chemical reaction equations are shown in Eqs. (2) and (3). As shown in Fig. 7, μMnTiO3 and nMnTiO3 formed at approximately 1000 °C, while μMμTiO3 formed at approximately 1100 °C. A similar conclusion is obtained through DSC analysis, as shown in Fig. 8. To investigate the difference in grain morphology among these three samples during the intermediate stage of sintering, three starting material components with different grain sizes were sintered at 1100 °C for 2 h. The intermediate phases synthesized from components 1–3 were named μMμTiO3, μMnTiO3, and nMnTiO3, respectively. The XRD results (Fig. 7(d)) indicate that the metallic oxides completely disappear after sintering at 1100 °C for 2 h, and all three samples are composed of MTiO3 and TiO2-1100 °C, with no other phases.

As shown in the DSC curves (Fig. 8), μMnTiO3 and nMnTiO3 form before 1000 °C, while μMμTiO3 begins to form at approximately 1071 °C. The formation temperatures of μMnTiO3 and nMnTiO3 are 100 °C lower than that of μMμTiO3, which is consistent with the in situ* XRD pattern results. This is because the sintering temperature of nanopowders is lower than that of micropowders, which is driven by the small size effect and high sintering activity of nanopowders [28,29]. When the three components are sintered at 1100 °C for 2 h, the densification degrees of the component 2 and component 3 are also slightly greater than that of the component 1. The relative densities of the component 2 and component 3 are 95.57% and 96.03%, respectively. However, the relative density of the component 1 is 93.88%. This indicates that the densification process of the three samples has basically completed, and almost no pores are found in the three samples, as shown in Fig. 9. As the sintering temperature increases to 1400 °C, TiO2-1100 °C and MTiO3 in the three samples further react with each other to form MT2. The densities of the three components are similar and are all above 97%.

As shown in Fig. 9, the average grain size of μMμTiO3 is approximately 0.56 μm, while the average grain sizes of μMnTiO3 and nMnTiO3 are approximately 1.31 and 1.50 μm, respectively. The diffusion barrier of nanoceramic powders is low due to the high surface energy. This leads to rapid diffusion of nanopowders as the temperature increases. Therefore, the grain sizes of μMnTiO3 and nMnTiO3 are much larger than that of μMμTiO3. Notably, the average grain sizes of TiO2-1100 °C in the components 2 and 3 (shown in Figs. 9(b) and 9(c)) are almost the same, at 2.29 μm, which is larger than that of the component 1 (1.98 μm, Fig. 9(a)). Moreover, studies have shown that rutile is more likely to form elongated prism morphologies with increasing temperature [30]. Due to the enormous surface energy of nano-sized TiO2, TiO2-1100 °C in the components 2 and 3 grew into rod-like shapes, whereas TiO2-1100 °C in the component 1 maintained its equiaxed shape. The small grain sizes of TiO2-1100 °C and high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)TiO3 ensure that the formed μMμT2 has a small grain size and does not have rod-like particles, which is caused by AGG, as shown in Fig. 4. Thus, it can be concluded that the grain sizes of the high-entropy intermediate phase and TiO2-1100 °C have an important impact on the grain growth behavior of high-entropy pseudobrookite.

### 3.5 Grain growth of heat-treated ceramics

Heat treatment experiments were performed to further study the grain growth behaviors of high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5 at high temperatures. The average grain size of sintered ceramics was used to study the grain development mechanism. However, there are many rod-like grains in μMnT2 and nMnT2 due to AGG, as shown in Fig. 4. In this case, the average grain size cannot fully represent the growth of the grains. Therefore, μMμT2 was used to investigate the grain growth behaviors in this study. SEM images of the μMμT2 grains after sintering at 1200–1400 °C for 5–15 h in air are shown in Fig. 10. No rod-like grains are observed in any of the SEM images. Moreover, when sintering at 1200 and 1300 °C, grain growth is not particularly obvious with prolonged holding time. However, the grain growth of high-entropy μMμT2 significantly accelerates when the sintering temperature increases to 1400 °C.

## 4 Property

The surfaces of the three MT2s were mechanically polished to a mirror finish, and the hardness and elastic modulus of the three samples were tested using nano-indentation. The force versus penetration depth (*P*–*h*) curves for determining the hardness and elastic modulus of all samples under quasistatic nano-indentation mode at a maximum load of 5 mN are shown in Fig. 12(a). During the unloading process, considerable elastic recovery can be observed, and the maximum penetration depths of the three MT2s are 126.30, 148.11, and 171.54 nm. The results indicate that the depth of surface penetration increases with increasing grain size. The hardness and elastic modulus are determined by the unloading segment of the *P*–*h* curves, and the results for all three MT2s are shown in Fig. 12(b). The hardnesses of μMμT2, μMnT2, and nMnT2 are 9.95, 7.51, and 6.05 GPa, respectively, and the elastic moduli are 159.53, 137.28, and 109.08 GPa, respectively. It is well known that the strength of polycrystals always increases with grain refinement (the increase in the number of grain boundaries) [33]. As shown in Fig. 12(c), the average grain size increases with decreasing grain size of the starting material powders. Therefore, compared with μMnT2 and nMnT2, μMμT2 exhibits excellent mechanical properties.

The thermal conductivities of the MT2s are presented in Fig. 12(d). The thermal conductivities of μMμT2, μMnT2, and nMnT2 at room temperature are 1.583±0.006, 1.939±0.029, and 2.091±0.006 W·m−1·K−1, respectively. These results demonstrate a distinct relationship between the grain size and the thermal diffusivity. The random arrangement of atoms or ions at grain borders leads to the dispersion and absorption of phonons while traveling through the grain boundaries [34]. This dispersion and absorption of phonons can block heat transfer, consequently reducing the thermal conductivity of the material. Compared with those of μMnT2 and nMnT2, the thermal conductivity of μMμT2 is lower.

## 5 Conclusions

In this work, high-entropy pseudobrookite ceramics were prepared using powders of three different starting materials with varying size distributions. It is discovered that high-entropy pseudobrookite ceramics are formed through a two-step solid phase process that differs from that of typical high-entropy ceramics. *In situ* XRD indicates that metallic oxides (e.g., MgO, CoO, NiO, and ZnO) first react with TiO2 to form a high-entropy intermediate phase, (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)TiO3. As the sintering temperature increases, the high-entropy intermediate phase further reacts with the calcined TiO2 to form high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5. The study of the grain growth process indicates that in addition to the sluggish diffusion effect, the morphology of the high-entropy intermediate phase and TiO2-1100 °C are also key factors affecting grain growth in the high-entropy pseudobrookite system. The high-entropy intermediate phase and TiO2-1100 °C prepared with micron-sized powders as starting materials exhibit a smaller grain size, finally resulting in the formation of high-entropy (Mg,Co,Ni,Zn)Ti2O5 with a small grain size. According to the nano-indentation and thermal conductivity results, compared with those of μMnT2 and nMnT2, μMμT2 exhibits a hardness of 9.95 GPa and a lower thermal conductivity of 1.583±0.006 W·m−1·K−1. All these characteristics suggest that high-entropy pseudobrookite ceramics with small grain sizes have great potential in the field of high-temperature thermal protection.
