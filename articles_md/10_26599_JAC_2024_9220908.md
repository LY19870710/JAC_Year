## 1 Introduction

In recent years, a groundbreaking low-temperature sintering technique known as the cold sintering process (CSP) was discovered by Randall *et al.* [1] at Pennsylvania State University in 2016 and has emerged as a highly effective method for producing dense ceramic materials. CSP involves homogenizing powdered inorganic compounds with a transient liquid phase. This blending process yields a uniform mixture characterized by the presence of a transient liquid phase, typically constituting 1%–10% of the total volume. By utilizing pressures ranging from 100 to 700 MPa and temperatures below 300 °C, CSP facilitates the evaporation of solvents and the densification of inorganic compounds, resulting in the formation of high-density sintered materials [2]. The core processing variables of CSP, including the transient liquid phase, sintering temperature, pressure, and holding time, play pivotal roles in driving the densification process of materials [3]. Of particular significance, the transient liquid phase substantially contributes to the dissolution-precipitation phenomenon within CSP. This mechanism enables the partial dissolution of sharp particle edges, thereby creating spaces for particle rearrangement. Moreover, the liquid phase readily redistributes itself, filling particle interstitials and fostering grain growth [4]. CSP has been successfully applied to water-soluble materials such as sodium chloride (NaCl), where NaCl particles diffuse throughout the water film, fill voids, and precipitate through the dissolution–precipitation process inherent to CSP. Consequently, CSP can achieve high relative densities exceeding 90% after 24 h at room temperature and 75% relative humidity. Furthermore, CSP has been effectively employed with various metal oxide and dielectric materials, including KH2PO4, NaNO2, and BaTiO3 (BT) [5,6]. Notably, KH2PO4 and NaNO2 exhibit high relative densities (> 98%) comparable to conventionally sintered materials, even when treated at temperatures below 120 °C under 350 MPa without additional treatment. However, for materials with low water solubility, a tailored liquid solution becomes imperative. Similarly, Guo *et al*. [7] employed CSP to fabricate high-density BT ceramics at temperatures below 200 °C. By utilizing a 25 wt% Ba(OH)2/TiO2 solution as a transient liquid phase and consolidating at 180 °C under 430 MPa pressure, they achieved a relative density of 95%. However, a subsequent annealing step at 900 °C, while achieving characteristics comparable to those of conventionally sintered counterparts, negated the advantage of low-temperature manufacturing, preventing the production of new composites below 300 °C. To address this limitation, Tsuji *et al.* [8] successfully fabricated dense and high-quality BT materials via cold sintering at 300 °C in a single step using NaOH–KOH eutectic fluxes. Nevertheless, the severe conditions of strong alkaline fluxes at 300 °C restrict the fabrication of innovative composites with additives such as polymers, 2D materials, and fibers due to the necessity of appropriate rapid kinetics for the congruent dissolution of surface ions of ceramic phase to ensure dense ceramic manufacturing.

Despite these challenges, recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of cosintering various materials through CSP, enabling the fabrication of novel composite materials. Notably, low melting point materials such as thermoplastic [9], thermosetting [10], and two-dimensional (2D) nanostructured materials [11] can serve as transient liquid phases in CSP, modifying matrix material qualities such as density, strength, hardness, and electrical properties [12,13]. For instance, Guo *et al*. [11] conducted CSP to manufacture a cold cosintering process of 2D-MXene and ZnO at 300 °C for 1 h under 250 MPa pressure, resulting in improved electrical conductivity with the addition of up to 5 wt% Ti3C2T*x* MXene. Sada *et al.* [14] successfully cosintered BT and polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) using Ba(OH)2·8H2O flux, bridging the temperature gap between ferroelectric ceramics and polymers, albeit with challenges related to nonhomogeneous polymer distribution. Similarly, Vinoth and Wang [15] effectively prepared BT/poly(vinylidene difluoride) nanocomposites, albeit with issues regarding the homogeneity of polymer distribution and unclear ferroelectric properties attributed to grain boundary microstructure [16]. In our previous work, we found that organic ferroelectric materials, including gamma-glycine (γ-GC), can be fabricated into a composite with bacterial cellulose (BC) powder via CSP and achieved satisfactory ferroelectric effects at 120 °C/24 h [17]. γ-GC is the most thermodynamically stable phase and exhibits piezoelectric effects at room temperature because it has a winding hydrogen bond network, unlike the other two main phases (α and β phases) [18,19]. Moreover, its water solubility qualifies it for use as a transient liquid phase in a CSP [20,21].

The present study proposes the use of γ-GC phase to fabricate ferroelectric composites via CSP. The γ-GC phase is an ideal transient liquid phase that induces excellent dissolution–precipitation phenomena during CSP. Moreover, it exhibits remarkable ferroelectric properties that do not interfere with the properties of the primary phase. The focus of this research is to determine the optimal quantity of γ-GC phase for high-density BT composites through CSP. A comprehensive examination of the time and temperature conditions applied in the CSP was conducted to refine the fabrication process and enhance the overall performance of the resulting composites.

### 2.1 γ-GC preparation

The γ-GC compound was synthesized using a method described in Ref. [17]. First, 56 g of commercial-grade glycine and 15 g of sodium chloride in a 3 : 1 molar ratio were dissolved in 100 mL of deionized (DI) water by stirring for 5 h at room temperature (RT). The resulting mixture was then filtered using vacuum filtration, and the obtained liquid was kept in an hot air oven (UF55, Memmert, Germany) at 35 °C for 25 d. During this aging process, the α-GC crystals were transformed into γ-GC crystals. The γ-GC crystals were then washed with saturated glycine solution, ground for 3 h using a ball mill (M18-5 Grinding Mill, SWECO, USA), and finally placed in the oven for an additional 2 d. This process resulted in the successful synthesis of γ-GC crystals.

### 2.2 BT preparation

Two types of BT powders were employed: (i) as purchased, a commercial product from Inframat Advanced Materials, LLC (BaTiO3 nanopowder, 99.95%); (ii) heat-treated commercial BT powder at 1200 °C for 12 h in air. Both powders (untreated and heat-treated) were separately stirred with 1 M acetic acid solution at 80 °C for 1 h. Then, the pH of the suspension was adjusted to neutral, and the respective BT powders were filtered prior to drying in the oven at 60 °C for 24 h, as schematically shown in Fig. 1(a).

### 2.3 Fabrication of γ-GC/BT composites via CSP

To optimize the packing density, a specific ratio of 73.6/26.4, determined by theoretical predictions for dense random packing, was employed for mixing BT particles of both nanometer (untreated particles) and micrometer (heat-treated particles) sizes. Subsequently, the fabrication process of composites involving γ-GC and BT via CSP was initiated by precisely measuring 2.208 g of heat-treated BT and 0.792 g of untreated BT. These two types of BT were thoroughly combined in a mortar for 5 min to ensure homogeneity. Next, varying proportions (15%, 30%, 50%, and 90% by weight) of the γ-GC powder were incorporated into the BT mixture and meticulously blended manually in the mortar for an additional 5 min. Subsequently, 7 wt% DI water was introduced into the blend, which was then ground for an additional 5 min to facilitate thorough mixing. Next, 0.3 g of the resulting γ-GC/BT mixture was meticulously deposited into a tungsten carbide die. In the context of CSP, the initial mixture undergoes continuous compression via a straightforward uniaxial pressure of 10 MPa. Heating is facilitated by a resistance jacket enveloping the die system within the CS equipment employed. The heating jacket can ramp up the temperature at a steady rate of 3 °C·min−1, reaching a maximum temperature of 120 °C. The specified heating temperature and duration were maintained for 6 h. The CSP apparatus utilized in this investigation was constructed as depicted in Fig. 1(b) (stages I–III). Once the process was completed, the cold-sintered γ-GC/BT composite was allowed to cool before being extracted from the block for subsequent characterization analysis.

### 2.4 Characterizations

Although the Archimedes method is renowned for its precision in determining density, its utility in our investigation was hampered by the solubility of γ-GC in polar solvents. Traditional organic solvents employed in Archimedes’ principle, such as water, ethanol, isopropanol, and acetone, were deemed incompatible with our samples due to their inherent polarity. Moreover, the use of nonpolar solvents poses a risk of altering the phase or stability of γ-GC. Additionally, the metastable nature of γ-GC warrants cautious immersion protocols, as it can lead to phase transitions in glycine. Consequently, we adopted an alternative approach to determine sample density, relying on measurements of mass, thickness, and dimensions. By employing 12 samples, we aimed to account for inherent variations within the sample set, thereby obtaining a more comprehensive representation of density. Densification calculations were performed using Eq. (1), elucidating the ratio of actual density to theoretical density [17,22].

(1) R=4mπd2hρ×100%

where *R* (%) is the relative density; * m (g) is the mass of the pellet;  d (cm) is the diameter of the pellet;  h (cm) is the thickness of the pellet;  ρ is the theoretical density calculated from Eq. (2):

(2) ρ=mmBTρBT+mγ-GCργ-GC

where  mBT and  mγ-GC represent the masses of BT and γ-GC in the composite pellet, respectively, and  ρBT and  ργ-GC represent the theoretical densities of BT and γ-GC, which are 6.02 and 1.59 g·cm−3, respectively [17,23].

The phase purity and crystal structure of the cold-sintered γ-GC/BT composite at various weight ratios from 15% to 90% were identified by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; SmartLab, Rigaku Corporation, Japan) with Cu Kα (λ* = 1.5418 Å) radiation in the range of 2*θ* = 10°–80°. The XRD results were compared to those in the joint committee on powder diffraction standards (JCPDS) database. Moreover, LeBail refinement of the unit cell parameters was performed using the JANA2006 software. The crystallite size was estimated for the corresponding phase by using the Scherrer equation (Eq. (3)):

(3) L=KλFWHM×cosθ

where *L* refers to the crystallite size; *K* is the Scherrer constant (*K* = 0.94); * λ is the wavelength of the radiation;  θ is the diffraction angle of the peak;  FWHM is the full width at half maximum.

BT and γ-GC powders were subjected to Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR; IRTracer-100, SHIMADZU, Japan) in transmission mode from 400 to 4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 to confirm the presence of functional groups. The morphology, particle size, and grain size of the untreated/heat-treated particles and cold-sintered γ-GC/BT composite were observed via a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; MIRA, TESCAN, Czech Republic) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; TECNAI G2 20, FEI, the Netherlands). In addition, energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping was performed to analyze the chemical components of the materials. Particle size and particle size distribution were measured and analyzed through examination and quantitative analysis using ImageJ software. Three-dimensional (3D) X-ray images of the cold-sintered γ-GC/BT composite were obtained using synchrotron radiation X-ray tomographic microscopy (SR-XTM) at beamline 1.2 W, SLRI, Thailand. The X-ray intensity was controlled by attenuating the polychromatic X-rays with a 350 µm-thick aluminum foil. X-ray images were captured using a microscopic lens system and recorded by an sCMOS camera with a 1.44-µm pixel size. The X-ray image dataset was converted into sinograms, which were generated with Octopus Reconstruction software. The reconstructed images were visualized using Drishti software. The dielectric behavior, including the dielectric constant (ε*r) and loss tangent (tan*δ*), of cold-sintered γ-GC/BT composites was studied using an LCR meter (LCR stands for inductance (L), capacitance (C), and resistance (R); E4908A, Agilent, USA) from RT to 200 °C at frequencies ranging from 100 Hz to 2 MHz. The polarization versus electric field hysteresis (*P*–*E*) curves of cold-sintered γ-GC/BT composites were measured at RT by a standard ferroelectric test system (RT66A, Radiant Technologies, USA).

## 3 Results and discussion

The morphology of the commercial nanocrystallite BT powder (untreated particles) is presented in Fig. 2(a). The SEM images show randomly distributed particles, with a smaller subset exhibiting homogeneously spherical-shaped particles of smaller size. The ImageJ analysis reveals a Gaussian distribution of particle sizes, indicating a relatively uniform particle size population. The mean particle size is determined to be 75.85±13.65 nm, with a narrow size distribution. The skewness and kurtosis values are close to zero, suggesting symmetry and a moderate peak, respectively. After heat treatment, agglomerates of varying sizes became apparent, as depicted in Fig. 2(d). The SEM images reveal a bimodal distribution, with peak particle sizes observed at 1.39±0.03 and 4.83±0.11 µm. This distribution suggests the presence of two distinct populations of particles within the sample, possibly indicating different stages of aggregation or variations in particle formation mechanisms [24]. It is noteworthy that the heat-treated particles exhibit sizes within the micron range, which are more than 30 times larger than those of the untreated particles. BT particles of both nanometer and micrometer sizes will be mixed at a specific ratio determined by theoretical predictions for dense random packing. TEM bright-field images, selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns, and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images of the BT powders before and after heat treatment at 1200 °C for 12 h are shown in Figs. 2(b), 2(c), 2(e), and 2(f). For the untreated powder (Fig. 2(b)), the particles appear nearly spherical with an average diameter of 78* .14±19.12 nm. The inset SAED pattern shows a polycrystalline diffraction ring composed of separate diffraction spots, indicating the polycrystalline nature of the nanoparticles [25]. Further fringes in BT were detected by HR-TEM. Figure 2(c) shows a lattice fringe with an interfering distance of 0.287 nm, which corresponds to the crystal plane (110) spacing of the cubic BT phase (JCPDS Card No. 075-0213, d*(110) = 0.283 nm). In contrast, after heat treatment at 1200 °C for 12 h (Fig. 2(e)), the BT powder exhibited particle agglomeration, resulting in a tenfold increase in the average particle size compared to that of the untreated powder. The inset SAED pattern shows bright spots assigned to the lattice atoms that are spaced crystallographically with a perfect plane orientation of BT, suggesting high crystallinity and structural perfection for the tetragonal perovskite structure [26]. Clear lattice fringes are observed (Fig. 2(f)), with a measured interplanar spacing of approximately 0.286 nm corresponding to the crystallographic plane (101) of tetragonal BT (JCPDS Card No. 079-2265, *d*(101) = 0.285) [27,28]. These results confirm that high-temperature calcination results in the cubic-to-tetragonal phase transformation of BT.

The BT powder before and after calcination was characterized by FTIR in transmission mode, as shown in Fig. 2(h). For the BT powder before calcination, absorption bands appeared at 3507 cm−1, which corresponded to the stretching mode of the O–H groups. The band at 1637 cm−1 is characteristic of O–H bending from physically adsorbed water on BT nanoparticles. The strong absorption at 487 cm−1 is assigned to the bending vibrations of the Ti–O bond in the [TiO6]2− octahedron. It is also a characteristic absorption of the BT powder [30]. Notably, the intensities of the O–H stretching vibration bands at 3507 and 1637 cm−1 become weaker as the calcination temperature increases, clearly reflecting metal–oxygen (Ti–O) band stretching [31−33].

The γ-GC powder and the mixed BT powder were mixed at various weight ratios (15, 30, 50, and 90 wt% γ-GC powder) and ground in a mortar, followed by the addition of 7 wt% DI water and grinding. Then, 0.3 g of the γ-GC/BT mixture was loaded into a tungsten carbide block, and a pressure of 10 MPa was applied. CSP was applied for 6 h before heating to 120 °C. At the end of the process, the tungsten carbide block was cooled, and the γ-GC/BT composite pellets were removed.

The white pellets, depicted in the inset of Fig. 3, have a diameter of 1 cm and a thickness of approximately 0.7 mm. In the case of a composite sintered with 15 wt% γ-GC (Fig. 3(a)), the surface predominantly comprised 85 wt% BT, resulting in densely packed BT particles and a smooth surface with a reduced pore count. With an increase in the γ-GC content to 30% (Fig. 3(b)), the surface remains smooth, albeit with small porous areas emerging. Upon further escalation of the γ-GC content to 50 wt% (Fig. 3(c)), the surface exhibits large particle clusters, nanoparticle dispersion throughout the matrix, and some filling of gaps, leading to increased porosity and surface unevenness. The average grain size is 3.92±1.75 µm. Notably, at 90 wt% γ-GC (Fig. 3(d)), irregularly arranged large clumps of γ-GC particles, numerous small grains (average grain size of 6.67±4.17 µm), and substantial interparticle voids are observed. Additionally, nanoparticles are dispersed within the matrix. The SEM results underscore that an optimal γ-GC content of 15 wt% promotes the formation of dense γ-GC/BT composites. Furthermore, SEM mapping and EDS analysis were conducted to explore the chemical composition of the composites, leveraging X-ray emissions pertaining to the constituent elements. The presence of γ-GC and BT was confirmed by the detection of Ba, Ti, N, C, and O. The SEM mappings of 15 wt% γ-GC pellets (Fig. 4) reveal large agglomerations of Ba (Fig. 4(c)) and Ti (Fig. 4(d)), with gaps between them filled with nitrogen (N) (Fig. 4(f)) and carbon (C) (Fig. 4(g)), which are elements characteristic of γ-GC. Furthermore, EDS measurements (Fig. 4(h)) confirm the presence of Ba, Ti, O, N, and C in the composites, with Ba, Ti, and O comprising 41.2%, 18.4%, and 19.6% by weight, respectively. The Ba and Ti signals correspond to the BT content in the 15 wt% γ-GC pellets, confirming the capacity of γ-GC to bind BT and form a ceramic composite via CSP.

XTM images of the composites containing 5, 30, and 90 wt% γ-GC/BT were obtained using SR-XTM. These images provide a macroscopic view of the 3D distribution of the components, distinguishing between two distinct phases represented by orange (γ-GC) and purple (BT). For the composites containing 5 wt% γ-GC (Fig. 5(a)) and 30 wt% γ-GC (Fig. 5(b)), BT acts as the matrix phase, with γ-GC evenly dispersed over the BT surface. Additionally, in certain regions, the γ-GC phase precipitates and fills the gaps between BT particles. However, as the γ-GC content increases to 90 wt% (Fig. 5(c)), BT becomes uniformly distributed within the γ-GC matrix. The XTM image verifies that the γ-GC phase is dispersed throughout the specimen, with instances of agglomeration and precipitation between BT particles, which is consistent with the SEM observations. These XTM findings offer valuable insights into the spatial arrangement and distribution of γ-GC and BT phases within composites, shedding light on their microstructural characteristics and providing essential information for understanding the properties and behaviors of composites.

The relative density of the γ-GC/BT composites, as determined by Eq. (1), is illustrated in Fig. 6(a). As the weight percentage of γ-GC increases from 15% to 90%, a corresponding trend of decreasing relative density is observed, decreasing from 96.7%±1.2% to 82.7%±1.1%. These findings align closely with the observations from SEM imaging. With decreasing γ-GC content, the BT particles exhibit a more compact arrangement compared to that of the composites containing 90 wt% γ-GC, primarily due to the inherently higher density of BT. Furthermore, the presence of γ-GC serves to fill the interstitial spaces between BT particles, thereby reducing pore formation. This phenomenon contributes to the overall reduction in porosity and subsequent increase in relative density. The relative density can be explained by a mechanism as reported in a previous study [17]. It is found that pressure, sintering temperature, holding time, and transient liquid solvent are important factors for effective CSP. BT powder is known to be thermodynamically unstable in aqueous solutions. Ba2+ ions are leached out of the BT powder, producing a TiO2-rich amorphous surface layer [34,35]. The amorphous layer, which is difficult to dissolve in the liquid phase, impedes the dissolution–precipitation process during CSP, thereby preventing the densification of the BT ceramic. Therefore, the surface of the BT powders in this work was treated with acetic acid (CH3COOH) to generate additional −OH groups on the BT particle surfaces (Fig. 6(b)) via a hydroxylation reaction [36]. When the γ-GC powder is mixed with (acid-treated) BT particles and fabricated via CSP, hydrogen bonds will form between the –COOH groups on the γ-GC chains and the –OH groups on the surfaces of the BT particles [37,38]. An appropriate amount of γ-GC can easily dissolve in water and act as the liquid phase, which enhances the dissolution-precipitation process during CSP. Therefore, γ-GC promotes close packing in composites and enhances the relative density of composites [39].

Furthermore, to elucidate the structural behavior, XRD analysis was employed to assess the cold-sintered samples, as depicted in Fig. 6(c). Notably, all sintered samples (15, 30, 50, and 90 wt% γ-GC) exhibited sharp diffraction peaks corresponding to two distinct phases: BaTiO3 (tetragonal; JCPDS No. 075-0213) and γ-GC (JCPDS No. 06-230). At 90 wt% γ-GC, two prominent peaks observed at 25.28° and 31.57° can be unequivocally indexed to the (110) and (101) diffraction planes of γ-GC and BT, respectively. As the γ-GC content decreases, notably, the intensity of the γ-GC peak at 25.28° significantly decreases, which is consistent with expectations. Subsequently, at γ-GC contents of 30 and 15 wt%, the characteristic peaks associated with γ-GC are clearly absent from the diffraction patterns.

The cold-sintered samples were characterized using FTIR in transmission mode, and the results are presented in Fig. 6(d). Across all sintered samples, distinct peaks indicative of γ-GC patterns emerged within the range of 1200–1500 cm−1 [40]. Notably, the highest intensity peak at 1573 cm−1 (peak 1) corresponds to asymmetric (C=O)O stretching, while the peak at 1492 cm−1 (peak 2) signifies the NH bending vibration of  NH3+. Additionally, the peak at 1390 cm−1 (peak 3) corresponds to symmetric (C=O)O stretching, whereas the peak at 1328 cm−1 (peak 4) represents CH₂ wagging with the lowest intensity. These observations collectively confirm the presence of γ-GC within the samples. However, a notable trend emerges as the γ-GC content decreases: the intensity of the four peaks decreases significantly, accompanied by peak overlap within the range of 486–684 cm−1. The overlapping peaks are attributed to the carboxylate ion group COO– of γ-GC and the stretching vibrations of Ti–O of BT, further underscoring the formation of composites between γ-GC and BT. Importantly, no undesired phase transition of γ-GC was observed throughout the analysis, confirming the stability of the composite structure.

The density of the composites, as elucidated in Fig. 7, was determined through a schematic representation of the cold sintering mechanism across various stages. Initially, the composite material particles comprising γ-GC, BT, and water are densely packed (“I” in Fig. 7). In the initial stage, a sufficient quantity of the liquid phase (water) is integrated with the composite particles, ensuring homogeneous moisturization. Here, γ-GC particles act as a transient liquid phase, dissolving and diffusing alongside water and facilitating particle rearrangement (“II” in Fig. 7). Notably, the pink frame in the illustration delineates the intricacies of particle sliding upon the introduction of the liquid phase, creating spaces at the particle‒particle contact area. These spaces enable particle sliding and subsequent rearrangement. Densification primarily occurs under external pressure at this juncture, leading to overall sample shrinkage (S*1). Subsequently, in the second stage, a dissolution–precipitation process occurs. Following particle dissolution, sliding, and rearrangement in the initial stage, mass transfer occurs via the diffusion of γ-GC ions or atoms through water (“III”, Fig. 7(a)), effectively filling the interstitial gaps between grains. Consequently, precipitates (“III”, Fig. 7(a)) form on crystal sites with lower chemical potentials, which are thermodynamically favored and conducive to neck growth. This mass transport process concurrently diminishes the surplus free energy of the surface and decreases the porosity, resulting in the formation of a dense solid and slight sample shrinkage (*S*2). The final stage, characterized by crystal growth (“IV”, Fig. 7(b)), is predominantly governed by a robust dynamic process catalyzed by water evaporation. This evaporation induces a supersaturated state of the liquid phase at temperatures slightly above its boiling point, thereby engendering a significant chemical driving force for the solid and liquid phases to reach equilibrium. Upon the completion of precipitation, the particle‒particle contacts are markedly enhanced, facilitating crystal growth via the coalescence of small crystallites (thickness = *T*0) into larger crystallites (thickness = *T*1). A comprehensive overview of various BT-based ceramics and composites, including their raw materials, the presence of transient liquid phases, CSP conditions, achieved densities, and corresponding references, is presented in Table 1. BT ceramics synthesized through solid-state reactions typically undergo high-temperature sintering, yielding densities between 94% and 96%. Alternatively, employing Ba(OH)2·8H2O as a transient liquid phase at 180 °C under pressure yields a density of 95%. Composite systems such as BT/ZnO and BT/polytetrafluoroethylene exhibit densities ranging from 60%–98% and 93.5%, respectively, utilizing specific liquid phase chemistries and CSP conditions. Notably, the density of the γ-GC/BT composites synthesized at 120 °C and 10 MPa was 96.7%±1.6%. These findings highlight the diverse synthesis strategies and optimization approaches employed in the fabrication of BT-based ceramics and composites for various applications.

The dielectric responses of the γ-GC/BT composites were examined as a function of temperature and frequency. Figure 8(a) shows the temperature dependences of the dielectric constant (ε*r) and dielectric loss (tan*δ*) for all composites from RT to 200 °C at 100 kHz. For the composites with 15–90 wt% γ-GC, the value of *ε*r tends to increase with increasing temperature. The maximum *ε*r value obtained is 3600, and the low tan*δ* is 1.20 for the 15 wt% γ-GC composite. The increased *ε*r is a result of the addition of ferroelectric ceramic powders, which is primarily caused by the dipolar polarization effect and is induced by the permanent dipoles present in the filler. The permanent dipole of BT results from the uneven distribution of the charge density between O, Ba, and Ti atoms [41].

As the frequency increases, *ε*r decreases until it reaches a near-constant value in the high-frequency region. The high value of *ε*r at low frequencies is caused by a combination of polarizations, including atomic, ionic, interfacial, and electronic polarizations. However, only electronic polarization affects *ε*r at high frequencies. The results clearly show that when the amount of γ-GC decreases, the dipole of the interface increases due to the increased BT content, leading to an increase in *ε*r [42]. At 15 wt% γ-GC, *ε*r = 27,000, and tan*δ* (which is the lowest) is 1.50 at 100 Hz.

We also demonstrate the beneficial effect of increasing the proportion of γ-GC by examining other AC properties, as depicted in Fig. 8(c), and the positive impact of γ-GC becomes more apparent. The imaginary part of the complex dielectric permittivity *ε*″ (dashed line in Fig. 8(c), shown for 90 wt% as an example) follows the universal power law [43,44] *ε*″ = *A*·*f* *s*−1, where *A* and *s* are fitting parameters. The parameter *s* represents the effective dimension of the charge carriers [45,46], which is one-dimensional (1D) for *s* ≈ 0.17–0.44. Accordingly, the *s* values of 0.11 (30 wt% g-GC) and 0.22 (50 wt% g-GC) suggest a 1D nature. Moreover, at 90% g-GC, the low *s* = 0.03 is close to zero such that *ε'* follows *f*−1. This is the “low-frequency dispersion” [47,48], which indicates that very high charge densities are stored, consistent with the well-known capacitive nature of BT.

Figure 8(d) shows the *T*-dependence of the imaginary part of the complex electric modulus *M*″×100 [*M*″ = *ε*″/(*ε*′2 + *ε*″2)]. The electric modulus corresponds to the relaxation of an electric field in the material when the electric displacement remains constant [47]. The samples with 30, 50, and 90 wt% g-GC have relaxation peaks at 87, 154, and 163 °C, respectively. The latter two might be compared to the value of 132 °C obtained from the elastic (mechanical) modulus of BT sintered at 1300 °C [48]. The different peak temperatures might be explained by different sintering methods (CSP vs. traditional) and measurement methods (electric relaxation vs. mechanical relaxation). Nevertheless, the shift of the relaxation is clear, presumably to the value of BT. The ferroelectric polarization–electric field (*P*–*E*) hysteresis loops of the γ-GC pellet and the γ-GC/BT composite pellets are shown in Fig. 8(e). The hysteresis loop of the γ-GC/BT composite pellets becomes larger with increasing BT content. Compared with γ-GC, the composite ceramics exhibit good symmetry and a well-saturated shape (see the inset). For 90 wt% γ-GC, the surface has porosity, and the addition of BT makes the ferroelectric phase discontinuous because it hinders the domain orientation. However, as the γ-GC content decreases to 15 wt%, the porosity decreases, and the ferroelectric property is optimized with easy switching of domains within materials. However, it significantly decreases when the γ-GC content decreases further to 50 and 15 wt% (Fig. 8(f)). Moreover, *P*s clearly increases as the BT content increases. These results indicate that γ-GC, as a transient liquid phase in CSP, can be successfully used to sustainably fabricate high-density composites between γ-GC and BT.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, we successfully demonstrated the use of an organic ferroelectric (γ-GC) phase as a transient liquid phase substitute for the fabrication of γ-GC/BT composite ceramics through the cold sintering process (CSP). Our investigation involved incorporating γ-GC contents ranging from 15% to 90% by weight into BT, followed by CSP with a sintering temperature of 120 °C, holding time of 6 h, and an applied uniaxial pressure of 10 MPa. The addition of γ-GC resulted in notable enhancements in both the relative density and electrical properties of the composites. In particular, the optimal γ-GC content of 15 wt% yielded a remarkable relative density of up to 96.7%±1.6%. Our findings were further corroborated by FTIR and XRD analyses, which confirmed the phase stability of γ-GC and its ability to prevent its undesired transformation into the nonpiezoelectric phase (α-GC). SEM‒EDS mapping revealed the precipitation–dissolution process of γ-GC in CSP, and it functioned as a filler diffusing into the space surrounding the BT particles. The XTM images provided a comprehensive 3D distribution of both BT and γ-GC, consistent with the SEM observations. Furthermore, we conducted a thorough examination of the electrical properties of the composites at various temperatures (RT‒200 °C) and frequencies (100 Hz‒2 MHz). The dielectric constant (*ε*r) increased with decreasing γ-GC content. Notably, the 15 wt% γ-GC pellet demonstrated the highest *ε*r of 3600 coupled with a low dielectric loss (tan*δ*) of 1.20. The *P*‒*E* hysteresis loop measurements revealed that the 15 wt% γ-GC composite ceramic displayed the highest values of *P*s = 1.46 µC·cm−2 and *P*r = 0.55 µC·cm−2. Our study underscores the efficacy of γ-GC as a transient liquid phase in CSP for successfully and sustainably fabricating high-density γ-GC/BT composites at relatively low temperatures. These results contribute to the advancement of ceramic processing techniques and hold promise for various applications in the fields of electronics, energy, and beyond.
