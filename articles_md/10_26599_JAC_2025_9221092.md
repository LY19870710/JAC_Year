## 1 Introduction

Accurate detection of formaldehyde (HCHO) is crucial, as HCHO is a common toxic gas released from interior decorations and incomplete combustion of fuel and tobacco leaves [1−4]. Long-term exposure to low concentrations of HCHO can cause chronic poisoning, damage to the body’s respiratory, nervous, and reproductive systems, and other diseases [5,6]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has established a recommended exposure limit for HCHO in indoor air of 0.08 ppm, as outlined in its Air Quality Guidelines, to safeguard public health; thus, HCHO has been identified as a hazardous substance by the WHO due to its carcinogenic and teratogenic effects [7,8]. Therefore, precise monitoring of low-concentration HCHO is highly important for human health [9,10].

Ternary metal oxides, including spinel oxides (AB2O4), perovskite oxides (ABO3), and mullite oxides (A2B4O9), have attracted extensive attention in the field of gas sensors because of their unique advantages, such as excellent selectivity, good compatibility with other materials, and high catalytic activity. Among these ternary oxides, CoFe2O4 has been widely applied as an anti-spinel oxide with the advantages of high stability and synergistic coupling effects [11−13]. The crystal configuration of CoFe2O4 is a cubic crystal system, where Co2+ and Fe3+ occupy two distinct crystallographic sites in a nearly random manner, forming an inverse spinel structure [14]. This disordered distribution endows CoFe2O4 with good magnetic properties, while the continuous valence interconversion between the two ions significantly enhances electron transfer within the materials [15,16]. For example, Wang *et al.* [17] reported a simple synthesis of CoFe2O4 nanocages by ion exchange for the detection of nitrobenzene, which resulted in excellent sensitivity, good repeatability, and long-term stability. Zhang *et al.* [18] synthesized CoFe2O4 nanoparticles via a solution combustion method and reported that the response to 100 ppm ethanol reached 10.7 at 250 °C. Although CoFe2O4 has been reported for some gas-sensing applications, its pristine form still requires improvement due to limitations such as low response and poor selectivity. It is a common and effective strategy to manipulate its morphology and combine it with other oxides to form heterostructures [19].

Two-dimensional metal-organic frameworks (2D MOFs) based on transition metals (Fe, Co, Ni, etc.) are considered to be promising self-template gas sensing materials owing to their larger specific surface area derived from their porous structure [20,21], which can provide multiple active sites for gas sensing reactions. CoFe2O4 can be derived from the calcination of bimetallic Fe–Co (Fe/Co = 2 : 1) MOFs. 2D MOF nanosheets have high activity because of the unsaturated coordination of metal ions on their surface, rapid mass transfer, and charge transfer [22,23]. In particular, various vacancies in 2D MOF nanosheets can increase the concentration of carriers and conductivity [24−27]. Moreover, the composition of the materials can be controlled by adjusting the proportions of Fe and Co in the Fe–Co MOFs. The excess Co ions will form Co3O4 by increasing the content of Co salt, thereby forming a CoFe2O4/Co3O4 heterojunction, which can provide more surface adsorption sites, facilitate charge transfer, and enhance the sensing performance. As a result, the use of 2D Fe–Co MOFs as precursors can achieve the ability to kill two birds with one stone. In addition, the surface modification of noble metal nanoparticles usually results in a significant improvement due to the synergy of multiple effects, such as catalytic properties. Indeed, 2D materials can effectively avoid the agglomeration of noble metals so that they grow uniformly on the surface of nanosheets. On the other hand, the catalysis of noble metals can promote the dissociation of oxygen molecules and increase the concentration of adsorbed oxygen species. The hybridization of noble metal particles and semiconductors alters the Schottky barrier at the metal‒semiconductor interface, thus promoting the transfer of carriers and improving the sensitivity of sensors [28,29].

In this study, Pt nanoparticle-modified 2D CoFe2O4/Co3O4 nanosheets directly derived from 2D bimetallic Fe–Co MOFs were synthesized via magnetic stirring and a subsequent hydrothermal process. The successful synthesis of 2D Pt/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 and the formation of multiple heterojunctions were experimentally demonstrated. As a result, the ternary Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4-based sensor shows greater response (95.5) to 100 ppm HCHO than do the pristine and binary sensors. The ternary sensor exhibits excellent selectivity, repeatability, long-term stability, and a limit of detection (LOD) of 6 ppb. The enhanced gas sensing response originates from two synergistic mechanisms: surface engineering via 2D nanostructured morphology and electronic structure optimization through multi-heterojunction interfaces. Moreover, the adsorption‒desorption of HCHO on the materials was investigated via *in situ* diffuse reflection infrared Fourier transformation spectroscopy (DRIFTS) and temperature-programmed desorption (TPD), and the sensing mechanism was further elucidated by combining density functional theory (DFT) calculations and experiments.

## 2 Experimental

All chemicals and solvents, including cobalt (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O), ferric chloride (FeCl3·6H2O), terephthalic acid (1,4-BDC), dimethylformamide (DMF), ethanol, triethylamine (TEA), acetone, xylenes, HCHO, methanol, methylbenzene, and acetic acid, were obtained from Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China, and were used directly without any purification.

### 2.1 Fabrication of CoFe<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>, Co<sub>3</sub>O<sub>4</sub>, and CoFe<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>/Co<sub>3</sub>O<sub>4</sub>

First, 2D bimetallic Fe–Co MOF nanosheets were prepared. For the Fe–Co (1 : 2) MOF, in a typical synthesis process, 410 mg of Co(NO3)2·6H2O and 190 mg of FeCl3·6H2O were first dissolved in DMF (100 mL) in a 200 mL beaker, and then 240 mg of 1,4-BDC was added to the mixed solution. After stirring for 1 h, 10 mL of distilled water, 10 mL of ethanol, and 5 mL of TEA were added, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 h to obtain a uniform colloidal suspension. The centrifuged precipitate was subsequently placed in acetone and stirred for 6 h, after which the obtained products were collected via centrifugation, washed, and dried. For the synthesis of the Fe–Co (2 : 1) MOF and Co MOF, the amounts of Co(NO3)2·6H2O and FeCl3·6H2O used were 210, 390, 600, and 0 mg, respectively. Finally, the resulting 2D Fe–Co (1 : 2) MOF, Fe–Co (2 : 1) MOF, and Co MOF nanosheets were heated to 700 °C at 2 °C/min and maintained for 6 h in air to obtain CoFe2O4/Co3O4, CoFe2O4, and Co3O4, respectively. The bulk CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composite was obtained by direct calcination after 410 mg of Co(NO3)2·6H2O and 190 mg of FeCl3·6H2O were added to water through stirring and drying.

### 2.2 Fabrication of Pt/CoFe<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>/Co<sub>3</sub>O<sub>4</sub> composite

A total of 16.6 mg of K2PtCl4 was dissolved in 20 mL of distilled water, and then, 7 µL of HCl (37%) was added to this solution and stirred to form the solution A. A total of 176 mg of ascorbic acid was dissolved in 10 mL of distilled water to obtain the solution B. Subsequently, the solutions A and B were mixed and continuously stirred. 40 mg of the abovementioned CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composite was dispersed ultrasonically in 30 mL of deionized water for 3 h to obtain a CoFe2O4/Co3O4 solution. A total of 7.5 mL of A/B mixed solution was injected into the CoFe2O4/Co3O4 solution and ultrasonically treated for 1 h to obtain the final solution. The final solution was heated to 90 °C and maintained for 1 h in a high-pressure Teflon-lined steel autoclave. As a result, Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 was obtained by centrifugation and freeze-drying. For Pt1/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 and Pt3/CoFe2O4/Co3O4, the amounts of A/B mixed solutions were 5 and 10 mL, respectively. For the Pt1/CoFe2O4/Co3O4, Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4, and Pt3/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composites, the nominal molar contents of Pt were 8%, 12%, and 16%, respectively. The detailed synthetic procedure of the 2D Pt/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composite is shown in Fig. 1, and the quantities of all the ingredients are summarized in Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

### 2.3 Fabrication and performance test of gas sensors

The gas sensors were prepared via the drop-casting technique. The gas sensing performance was measured via a dynamic gas sensor test system (SD101, Hua Chuang Rui Ke, China). Detailed descriptions are given in the ESM. To obtain the baseline resistance, the sensors were exposed to an air atmosphere for a few minutes during the test. The concentrations of the target gases were obtained via a static liquid gas distribution method. The gas response was defined by the equation *S* = *R*g/*R*a for reducing gases, where *R*g and *R*a denote the resistances of the materials in the target gas and air, respectively. The details of the injected liquid amount, the whole detection process, and first-principles calculations can be found in the Supporting Information.

### 3.1 Structural characterization

Figure S1 in the ESM shows images of the Fe–Co MOF (2 : 1), Co MOF, and Fe–Co MOF (1 : 2) samples and their elemental mappings, indicating that the content of Fe increased with the addition of FeCl3·6H2O. Since the CoFe2O4, Co3O4, and CoFe2O4/Co3O4 samples are derived from 2D MOFs after calcination, the organic ligands are destroyed by high-temperature conditions, and the metal ions react with oxygen in air to be calcined into metal oxides, while the 2D morphologies of the MOFs are retained [30−32]. Figures 2(a)–2(c) show the microstructures of the CoFe2O4, Co3O4, and CoFe2O4/Co3O4 samples, demonstrating the morphology of nanosheet with some wrinkles. In contrast, bulk CoFe2O4/Co3O4 has an irregular bulk structure (Fig. S2(a) in the ESM). The folds of the nanosheets provide a larger specific surface area for the materials, and CoFe2O4/Co3O4 is thicker than pristine CoFe2O4 and Co3O4 are, which can be attributed to the formation of heterogeneous structures. For the Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composite, many nanoparticles are uniformly dispersed on the surface of the nanosheets (Fig. 2(d)), which can be attributed to the double ultrasonic dispersion before the hydrothermal process. For the Pt1/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composite, only a small number of Pt nanoparticles are distributed on the surface (Fig. S2(b1) in the ESM), whereas many Pt particles are agglomerated on the surface of the Pt3/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composite (Fig. S2(b2) in the ESM). The actual loading of Pt was determined via atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS), as shown in Table S2 in the ESM. The molar ratios of Pt in the three samples were 8.3%, 11.6%, and 15.46%, which were consistent with expectations. The cross-sectional SEM images of the sensing film show that the thickness of the film is ~70 μm (Fig. S2(c) in the ESM). Furthermore, the SEM images of the CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composite synthesized via controlled thermal calcination of the Fe–Co MOFs at 800 °C indicate that the structural degradation of the 2D morphology obtained at higher temperatures is correlated with increasing crystallinity and accelerated organic ligand decomposition (Fig. S2(d) in the ESM).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of the CoFe2O4/Co3O4 and Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composites further revealed the nanosheet structure and uniform deposition of Pt nanoparticles with sizes of ~10–20 nm on the CoFe2O4/Co3O4 nanosheets (Figs. 2(e) and 2(f)), indicating that the Pt nanoparticles were successfully loaded on the CoFe2O4/Co3O4 nanosheets. The high-resolution TEM image of the Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composite shows lattice fringe spacings of 0.253, 0.244, 0.227 and 0.202 nm, corresponding to the (311) plane of CoFe2O4, (400) and (311) planes of Co3O4, and (111) planes of Pt, respectively (Fig. 2(g)), demonstrating the formation of heterojunctions. As shown in Fig. 2(h), the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern shows symmetrical diffraction rings of the (311) and (400) planes of CoFe2O4 and Co3O4, and the weak diffraction spots of Pt corresponding to the (111) planes indicate the formation of elemental Pt. Moreover, the presence and uniform distribution of Co, Fe, O, and Pt on the material surface is further confirmed by the elemental mappings and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) spectra (Figs. 2(i)–2(l)). The N2 adsorption‒desorption isotherms of the samples indicate that Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 has a relatively larger specific surface area than the other materials do (12.16 m2/g) (Fig. S3 in the ESM), increasing the adsorption of gases by increasing the number of active sites available for gas-sensing reactions. The Barrett‒Joyner‒Halenda (BJH) pore size distribution shows that the average pore size on the nanosheets is ~11 nm, indicating mesoporous characteristics (Fig. S3(d) in the ESM).

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the three MOFs are shown in Fig. S4(a) in the ESM, indicating that the characteristic peaks are consistent with the single-crystal data simulation and that the characteristic peaks at 2θ* = 8.8°, 14.1°, 15.7°, and 17.8° correspond to the (200), (001), (201), and (400) planes, respectively [33−35]. With increasing Fe content, the intensity of the diffraction peaks gradually weakens, which is due to the decrease in crystallinity of the materials caused by Fe ions. The XRD patterns of CoFe2O4, Co3O4, and CoFe2O4/Co3O4 in Fig. 3(a) exhibit the characteristic peaks of CoFe2O4 (PDF#22-1086) and Co3O4 (PDF#42-1467) [36]. The characteristic diffraction peaks in the XRD pattern of the CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sample correspond to the crystal planes of CoFe2O4 and Co3O4, indicating the successful synthesis of the CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composite. The XRD results of the bulk CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composite (Fig. S4(b) in the ESM) match well with those of CoFe2O4 (PDF#22-1086) and Co3O4 (PDF#42-1467). Figure S4(c) in the ESM presents the XRD pattern of the thermal decomposition product derived from Fe–Co MOFs through isothermal calcination at 600 °C for 2 h in an air atmosphere. Rietveld refinement analysis revealed the predominant formation of metastable intermediate phases containing residual organic components rather than the expected equilibrium phases of CoFe2O4 and Co3O4. The molecular structures of the four materials can be characterized by their Raman spectra (Fig. 3(b)). The peak of CoFe2O4 at 693 cm−1 (A1g) can be attributed to the symmetrical displacement of oxygen atoms in the tetrahedral void with respect to metal ions [37]. Other peaks at 472 cm−1 (T1g) and 304 cm−1 (Eg) correspond to the symmetrical and antisymmetric stretching of oxygen atoms relative to metal ions in the octahedral void [38]. These results demonstrate the formation of pure CoFe2O4 with a cubic spinel structure [39−41]. The peaks in the Raman spectrum of Co3O4 at 673, 520.6, and 480.4 eV correspond to the A1g, T1g, and Eg modes, respectively [36,42−44]. The peaks of CoFe2O4 and Co3O4 can be observed in the CoFe2O4/Co3O4 and Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 samples, and they present a slight shift to lower frequency compared with the pristine CoFe2O4 and Co3O4. This can be attributed to the transfer of electrons caused by the formation of heterojunctions [45].

All the Co 2p, Fe 2p, O 1s, and Pt 4f peaks can be observed in the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey of the Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sample (Fig. S5(a) in the ESM). The XPS spectra of Co 2p reveal that two peaks at binding energies of 779.8 and 795.3 eV are connected to Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2, respectively (Fig. 3(c)) [46]. The characteristic peaks of Co2+ are located at binding energies of 783.5 and 796.7 eV in CoFe2O4, revealing the presence of only Co2+ in CoFe2O4 [47]. In contrast, the binding energies at 786.1 and 803.2 eV in the Co 2p spectra are ascribed to Co3+, so the Co2+ and Co3+ coexist in the Co3O4, CoFe2O4/Co3O4, and Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 samples [48]. The ratios of Co3+/Co2+ in the CoFe2O4, Co3O4, CoFe2O4/Co3O4, and Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 samples are shown in Fig. S5(b) in the ESM, further confirming the above analysis of Co. The Fe 2p spectra indicate that the peaks at 714.5 and 727.0 eV belong to Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2, respectively (Fig. 3(d)) [49]. The binding energies at 710.5, 712.6, and 723.9 eV correspond to Fe3+, demonstrating the presence of only Fe3+ in CoFe2O4, CoFe2O4/Co3O4, and Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 [50]. As shown in Fig. 3(e), the characteristic peak of O 1s at 529.4 eV corresponds to metal‒oxygen bonds (Co‒O and Fe‒O) [51,52]. The peaks at 531.2 and 532.8 eV can be attributed to the adsorbed oxygen on the surface vacancy (OV) and the chemisorbed oxygen species (OC), respectively [53−55]. The volume ratios of the oxygen species demonstrate an increase in the OV content with the formation of heterojunctions (Table S3 in the ESM), which also indicates an increase in the number of active sites. The observed phenomena can be attributed to work function disparities between the constituent materials, which induce intermaterial electron transfer to establish Fermi-level equilibrium. The migrated electrons, which exhibit elevated reactivity, subsequently interact with atmospheric oxygen to generate reactive oxygen species. This process consequently enhances the OV concentration within the materials. In the Pt 4f spectra (Fig. 3(f)), the two characteristic peaks at 71.0 and 74.4 eV corresponding to Pt 4f5/2 and Pt 4f7/2 can be observed in the Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sample, revealing the formation of elemental Pt [56,57]. Two weak Pt2+ peaks are caused by the presence of a small amount of Pt oxide (PtO) or hydroxides (Pt(OH)2) [58]. The oxygen vacancies in materials can be effectively detected via an electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectrometer (MicroESR V3.0, MicroESR, Bruker, Germany). A significant paramagnetic transition is observed for all four materials at a magnetic field of 3517 Gs (Fig. 3(g)), and a factor (*g*) of 2.002 is attributed to the large number of uncoordinated electrons generated by oxygen vacancies [59]. The EPR intensities of CoFe2O4/Co3O4 and Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 are significantly higher than those of Co3O4 and CoFe2O4, which can be ascribed to the transfer of more electrons in the heterogeneous junction. More oxygen molecules can be adsorbed on the surface of materials because of the increase in the concentration of surface oxygen vacancies. Moreover, transferred active electrons bind with the oxygen molecules on the surface of materials, leading to the formation of additional surface adsorbed oxygen species and thus enhancing the gas sensing performance. The ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy (UV–Vis) spectra of the four samples exhibit significant absorption in the visible range, and the Eg values of CoFe2O4, Co3O4, CoFe2O4/Co3O4, and Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 can be calculated to be 1.90, 1.82, 1.66, and 1.56 eV, respectively (Figs. 3(h), and Fig. S5(c) in the ESM) [60]. The Eg of the Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composite is smaller than that of the other samples, which is beneficial for electron transfer from the valence band to the conduction band. The narrowed band gap facilitates the generation of highly mobile electrons with enhanced reduced capacity, which can effectively drive the single-electron reduction pathway of O2 molecules to yield superoxide radicals through a charge transfer process. Concurrently, this structural modification expands the thermodynamic redox potential range of the material system, enabling the thermodynamic feasibility of oxygen activation by overcoming the inherent energy barrier for O–O bond dissociation. The electrical conductivity of all the samples at room temperature was characterized via an electrochemical impedance spectroscope (EIS; 1287+1260, Solartron, UK) (Fig. 3(i)), which revealed that the conductivity follows the order of Co3O4 > Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 > CoFe2O4/Co3O4 > CoFe2O4, namely, that the conductivity of CoFe2O4 is significantly improved by the combination of Co3O4 and the modification of Pt nanoparticles [61]. The density of states (DOSs) of the CoFe2O4/Co3O4 and Pt/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 samples were calculated via DFT (Fig. S5(d) in the ESM). The DOS of Pt/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 near the Fermi level is significantly stronger than that of the CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sample, indicating higher conductivity in Pt/CoFe2O4/Co3O4. This is caused by the generation of multiple heterojunctions, which promote the transfer of electrons and increase the carrier concentration.

### 3.2 Gas sensing performance

Figure 4(a) shows the sensing responses of the sensors to 100 ppm HCHO gas at different operating temperatures. Compared with pristine CoFe2O4 and Co3O4, CoFe2O4/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 exhibited enhanced HCHO-sensing responses. The response of Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 reached 95.5–100 ppm HCHO at 280 °C. Moreover, the response curves of the sensors to 100 ppm HCHO at 280 °C are given in Fig. 4(b), and Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 has the highest response compared with those of the other three samples, which are 14.7, 6.77, and 2.9 times greater than those of CoFe2O4, Co3O4, and CoFe2O4/Co3O4, respectively. The greater response of CoFe2O4/Co3O4 is attributed mainly to the formation of p‒p junctions. The response of CoFe2O4/Co3O4 (33) to HCHO is significantly greater than that of bulk CoFe2O4/Co3O4 (20.5), which is due to the larger specific surface area and greater number of reactive sites of CoFe2O4/Co3O4 synthesized using 2D MOF as a template (Fig. S6(a) in the ESM). For Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4, the loading of Pt nanoparticles results in the formation of Schottky heterojunctions with CoFe2O4 and Co3O4, further enhancing the gas sensing performance. Furthermore, the introduced small-sized Pt can act as a catalyst for chemical sensitization to dissociate oxygen molecules, increase the amount of oxygen species adsorbed on the surface, and improve the sensitivity of materials. Figure S6(b) in the ESM shows a comparison of the response of CoFe2O4/Co3O4 loaded with different amounts of Pt nanoparticles to 100 ppm HCHO. The sensing performance of the Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sensor is significantly better than that of Pt1/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 because more Pt nanoparticles participate in the gas sensing reaction. However, Pt particles agglomerate on the surface of CoFe2O4/Co3O4 with increasing Pt loading, which can hinder the adsorption of gas on the surface of the material, reduce the utilization rate of Pt nanoparticles, and decrease the response. Figures 4(c) and Fig. S7 in the ESM show the dynamic response curves of sensors exposed to different concentrations of HCHO gas at 280 °C. The sensor response increases with increasing gas concentration, which can be attributed to more HCHO molecules reacting with adsorbed oxygen and more electrons being released into the material, causing a greater change in the resistance of the material. As shown in Fig. 4(d), the response follows the order of Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 > CoFe2O4/Co3O4 > Co3O4 > CoFe2O4, which can be ascribed to the synergies between the electrochemical sensitization and multiple heterojunctions, and detailed explanations are given in the following sensing mechanism. The gas response of Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 exposed to low concentrations (50–500 ppb) of HCHO was measured (Fig. 4(e)). Even for 50 ppb HCHO gas, the sensor still has a response of 1.26, and the theoretical LOD of the sensor is estimated to be 6 ppb. The fabricated sensors achieve LOD of 6 ppb, demonstrating superior gas-sensing performance compared with the WHO-recommended HCHO exposure threshold of 80 ppb for indoor air quality. This 15-fold increase in detection ability validates the practical applicability of the sensor in environmental monitoring scenarios requiring trace-level contaminant quantification. The detailed calculation methods and procedures are given in the ESM. A strong linear correlation between the HCHO concentration and the sensor response is observed, particularly at low HCHO concentrations. The gas-sensing performance of the ternary Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sensor is better than that reported in previous works to varying degrees (Fig. 4(f), and Table S4 in the ESM) [5,20,44,61−67].

The response and recovery times of the sensors are given in Fig. 5(a) and Fig. S8 in the ESM, indicating that the resistances of all the sensors increase with exposure to the HCHO atmosphere and are restored to baseline levels, indicating p-type sensing. The response and recovery times of the Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sensor (17 and 63 s) are significantly shorter than those of the other sensors. This can be attributed to the generation of heterogeneous structures, which can promote the transfer of electrons and improve carrier mobility. Selectivity is another key performance parameter for gas sensors. The responses to various interfering gases (100 ppm), including HCHO, acetone, ethanol, TEA, xylenes, methanol, methylbenzene, acetic acid, and acetaldehyde, indicate that the HCHO sensing response is superior to those of other gases (Fig. 5(b)), which can be attributed to the lower adsorption energy for HCHO gas. The Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sensor has excellent anti-interference ability for other gases due to its excellent selectivity. The repeatability of the Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sensor was examined by repeating seven cycles of the sensing response to 100 ppm HCHO (Fig. 5(c)). After seven consecutive sensing tests, the sensor nearly maintains the same response (95.5±2), with an error of ≤ 2.1%, demonstrating good repeatability. Moreover, the response and baseline resistance of the Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sensor to 100 ppm HCHO over 30 d slightly varied, decreasing by 3%, revealing good long-term stability (Fig. 5(d)). This can be connected to the water molecules that adsorb on the surface of materials after prolonged exposure to an air atmosphere. Indeed, the gas sensing performance is usually affected by water vapor in the air. Furthermore, prolonged exposure to ambient air induces the recombination of oxygen vacancies with atmospheric oxygen species, leading to a significant reduction in the oxygen vacancy concentration within the material. This depletion consequently diminishes the oxygen adsorption capacity and lowers the concentration of surface-adsorbed oxygen species. The dynamic response curves, response, and resistance of the Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sensor to 100 ppm HCHO at different RHs (15%–65%) are shown in Figs. 5(e) and 5(f). More water molecules are adsorbed on the surface of the materials as the RH increases, thus impeding the adsorption of oxygen molecules, extracting fewer electrons, and decreasing the hole concentration. The resistance continues to increase as a p-type material, whereas the response shows the opposite trend. This observation provides conclusive evidence that the time-dependent degradation of responsiveness originates from progressive water molecule adsorption on the material surface. However, the Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sensor maintains the response of nearly 60%, even at RH of 65%, demonstrating its applicability for HCHO detection under moderate humidity conditions.

### 3.3 Sensing mechanism

The sensing mechanism can be explained by oxygen adsorption and energy band theory [68], and the adsorption‒desorption of HCHO gas on the surface of materials was further investigated by TPD and *in situ* DRIFTS. As shown in Fig. 6(a), two distinct desorption peaks can be detected at 110–190 and 260–330 °C, which are attributed to the desorption of HCHO on Co3O4 and CoFe2O4, respectively. The peak at ~160 °C is derived from the desorption of HCHO gas in the weak acid center of the material, and the peak above 300 °C is caused by the desorption of HCHO gas, which corresponds to medium–strong acid sites [69,70]. The peak intensity of Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 is significantly greater than that of the other materials. The rich acidic sites provide a greater HCHO adsorption capacity, which makes the materials respond faster and more sensitive to HCHO gas [71,72]. Additionally, the FTIR spectra of the samples were investigated to understand the bonding of the materials (Fig. 6(b)). Two prominent bands at 580 and 668 cm−1 in the Co3O4 spectrum are attributed to the stretching vibrations of the Co3+–O octahedral sites and Co2+–O tetrahedral sites of the cubic lattice, respectively [73,74]. For CoFe2O4, the peak at 602 cm−1 is associated with the Fe‒O bond vibration and these sites substantially affect the catalytic activity and HCHO sensing performance [41]. Two peaks near 3436 and 1632 cm−1 correspond to the physical adsorption of water molecules. *In situ* DRIFTS experiments further revealed the adsorption‒desorption of HCHO gas on Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 (Fig. 6(c)). After exposure to HCHO gas for 1 h, the characteristic peaks appear at 930, 964, 1626, and 3332 cm−1, which are attributed to the adsorption of HCHO [75]. With the continuous purging of N2, the desorption of HCHO from Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 and the peak intensity are weakened. The adsorption peak almost disappears after purging for 30 min, suggesting the complete desorption of HCHO.

When the p-type Pt/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sensor is exposed to air, oxygen molecules are adsorbed on the surface of the materials and capture electrons (0.06 e) in the materials to form adsorbed oxygen species (Eq. (1)), producing a hole accumulation layer (HAL). In HCHO gas, HCHO reacts with surface-adsorbed oxygen (O−) to produce CO2 and H2O (Eq. (2)) and releases electrons (0.03 e) back to the materials to recombine with holes [65]. The concentration of holes decreases as the major carriers increase because the resistance of materials increases significantly. The oxygen molecules reoccupy the adsorption sites on the surface of the materials after they drain the HCHO gas, so the resistance of the materials returns to the original level. The response of the CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composite to HCHO is much greater than that of pristine Co3O4 and CoFe2O4, which is caused by the generation of p–p junctions [76]. Owing to the difference in work function between Co3O4 (6.5 eV) and CoFe2O4 (5 eV), electrons flow from CoFe2O4 to Co3O4 to reach the Fermi level equilibrium, and the energy band bends. Electrons with higher activity react with oxygen to produce higher concentrations of surface-adsorbed oxygen, allowing more gas molecules to participate in the gas-sensing reaction, thereby enhancing the gas-sensing properties of materials [77]. Pt nanoparticles play a key role in both electronic and chemical sensitization. For electronic sensitization, Pt nanoparticles form Schottky heterojunctions with Co3O4 and CoFe2O4, facilitating further electron transfer (Fig. 7(b)) [78,79], which is evidenced by the difference in charge density shown in Fig. 7(c). Moreover, two contact modes (Pt/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 (I) and Pt/Co3O4/CoFe2O4 (II)) are formed in the materials since Pt nanoparticles grow on both sides of the CoFe2O4/Co3O4 nanosheets. According to the DFT calculations in Fig. 7(d), the binding energy of the type I structure (−25.67 eV) is lower than that of the type II structure (−25.01 eV), and more Pt nanoparticles tend to grow on the side of CoFe2O4. The adsorption energy of several structures for HCHO indicates that the type I structure has the strongest adsorption capacity for HCHO, namely, that the type I structure plays a major role in the gas sensing reaction. For chemical sensitization, Pt nanoparticles promote the dissociation of oxygen molecules to form adsorbed oxygen. The Schottky heterojunction substantially elevates the activation energy barriers for both carrier transport and chemical reactions through three interrelated mechanisms: interfacial charge potential barriers, oxygen vacancy dynamics, and energy band reconstruction. Specifically, the elevated Schottky barrier height impedes rapid carrier migration by creating a substantial energy offset that exceeds the carrier transport energy threshold in ohmic contacts. This interfacial barrier simultaneously increases the oxygen vacancy formation energy through modified defect thermodynamics. Concomitant energy band reconstruction induces multidomain structural evolution, thus increasing the activation energy of charge transport. However, chemical sensitization can reduce the activation energy of gas molecules on the material surface by changing the energy distribution and molecular interaction mode through reaction path optimization and interface effect coordination. Pt nanoparticles move down the center of the d-band to optimize the molecular adsorption configuration and provide highly active sites to diffuse active oxygen species into the surface of the material. The reaction path can also be changed by orbital hybridization to form a low-energy barrier intermediate state. The activation energy can be calculated from the relationship between the response and operating temperature (Eq. (3)) [80]:

(3) S=exp⁡(−Ea/kT)

where S*, *E*a, *k*, and *T* denote the gas response, the activation energy, the Boltzmann constant, and the working temperature, respectively. The activation energy of the materials follows the order of different temperatures: Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 2O4/Co3O4 3O4 2O4 (Fig. 6(d)), suggesting that chemical sensitization is the main sensitization mechanism of Pt. Furthermore, the specific surface area disparity represents a critical determinant influencing the gas-sensing properties of sensing materials. Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 has a relatively large specific surface area due to the increased degree of folding of the nanosheets and the modification of the nanoparticles, facilitating enhanced surface adsorption through increased availability of active sites. This structural advantage enables more efficient surface redox reactions between adsorbed oxygen species and target gas molecules, thereby significantly improving the material’s gas-sensing response characteristics. The expanded reaction interface directly correlates with improved sensitivity.

To further explore the selectivity of the CoFe2O4, Co3O4, CoFe2O4/Co3O4, and Pt/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 samples, the adsorption energies for four gases (HCHO, acetone, ethanol, and TEA) were calculated via DFT (Fig. 6(e)), and the corresponding adsorption models are shown in Fig. S10 in the ESM. Compared with other sensors, the Pt/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sensor results in lower adsorption energies for all the gases due to the formation of multiple heterojunctions, which can promote the transfer of electrons, increase the concentration of adsorbed oxygen, and facilitate the adsorption of gases [81]. The adsorption energy of all the models for HCHO is lower than that for other gases, which means that the ternary composite has excellent selectivity for HCHO. The calculation results for the adsorption energy are in good agreement with the experimental results. Pt nanoparticle functionalization not only significantly reduces the adsorption energy of HCHO molecules but also modulates interfacial electron transfer dynamics through surface energy regulation. This modification enhances chemical adsorption via optimized charge redistribution at the Pt–semiconductor interface. Concurrently, the ternary heterojunction configuration results in a superior oxygen vacancy concentration, which directly correlates with the polar molecule capture efficiency. These vacancy sites act as active adsorption centers, preferentially binding HCHO through dipole‒charge interactions, thereby establishing a dual-enhancement mechanism for selective adsorption [82]. The reaction process of HCHO gas adsorbed on the surface of materials with adsorbed oxygen consists of five steps, and the reaction models and free energies of each step are shown in Fig. 6(f) and Fig. S11 in the ESM. In the *OCH2 to H + OCH step, a certain energy barrier needs to be overcome, and the potential barrier of Pt/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 (1.3 eV: −0.67 to −1.97 eV) is obviously lower than that of CoFe2O4/Co3O4 (1.58 eV: 0.52 to −1.06 eV), implying that HCHO is more likely to react with adsorbed oxygen on Pt/CoFe2O4/Co3O4. Thus, this sensor has a greater response. This is because noble metal modification can regulate the electronic structure, and the Pt nanoparticles act as electron reservoirs and dynamically release electrons in the reaction to promote target gas dissociation. The catalytic properties of noble metals significantly improve the activation kinetics of oxygen species and promote the dissociation of oxygen molecules. Moreover, Pt can also serve as a specific adsorption site for HCHO and reduce its oxidation initiation potential. The DFT calculation results further confirm that CO2 and H2O are the final products of the gas sensing reaction.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, we synthesized Pt nanoparticle-modified CoFe2O4/Co3O4 nanosheets derived from 2D Fe–Co MOFs via a facile solution method and explored the optimal loading capacity of Pt. The Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 composite exhibited outstanding sensing performance for HCHO, including sensitivity, selectivity, reproducibility, and long-term stability. The Pt2/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 sensor achieves a response of 95.5 to 100 ppm HCHO at 280 °C and a theoretical LOD of 6 ppb. The adsorption and desorption of HCHO on the surface of the sensing materials were investigated via TPD and *in situ* DRIFTs. The DFT calculations demonstrate that Pt/CoFe2O4/Co3O4 is the most stable structure and has the lowest adsorption energy. Moreover, gas sensing mechanisms, including electron transfer at heterogeneous interfaces and the reaction of HCHO on the surface of materials, have been proposed on the basis of band theory and DFT calculations. The excellent sensitivity to HCHO is attributed mainly to the formation of multiple heterojunctions and the catalytic effect of Pt. This work provides a new perspective for fabricating high-performance volatile organic compound (VOC) sensors by constructing multiple heterojunctions decorated by noble metals.
