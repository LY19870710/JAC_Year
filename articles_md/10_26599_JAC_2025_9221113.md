## 1 Introduction

The rapid development of next-generation aerospace vehicles, which are designed for prolonged flight durations and higher speeds, will result in significant aerodynamic heating and intense mechanical stress from vibrations, shocks, and thermal loads [1−6]. Thermal protection systems (TPSs) are crucial for ensuring the structural integrity, operational performance, and safety of both flight and high-precision instruments within hypersonic vehicles [3,4,7−9]. Carbon-bonded carbon fiber composites (CBCFs) are extensively utilized in TPS and can serve as carbonaceous reinforcements for thermal protection materials because of their low density, low thermal insulation properties, high porosity, and dimensional stability [10−14]. The CBCF composite is composed of a network of chopped carbon fibers that are interconnected at their junctions by localized areas of vitreous carbon with an open porosity of 70%–90%, and this distinctive microstructure confers advantages in terms of thermal insulation. Considerable research has been dedicated to enhancing the manufacturability [15−17], mechanical properties [17−19], and oxidation resistance [20−23] of CBCF composites through the design of carbon frameworks or the modification of the matrix. The incorporation of a ceramic matrix into the carbon framework represents a viable approach to enhance the strength, thermal stability, and oxidation resistance of composite materials while simultaneously ensuring that the carbon–ceramic composites retain a low density. SiBCN ceramics demonstrate high-temperature structural stability, resistance to oxidation, low density, high specific strength, and tolerance to high-temperature damage, making them an ideal choice for ceramic matrices [24−30]; however, research on carbon-reinforced SiBCN composite materials requires further development [29,31−34]. The current research on the modification and mature application of CBCFs has focused primarily on SiOC ceramics [35−38], and investigating the performance enhancement of SiBCN ceramic-modified CBCF composites is highly important, warranting further exploration in this research domain.

The comprehensive enhancement of temperature limits and the development of multifunctionality are key directions for advanced thermal protection composite materials [1,4,39−42]. Current thermal protection materials are generally unable to integrate thermal insulation performance with in-plane heat conduction capabilities, thereby limiting the achievement of high-efficiency thermal protection. Dredging thermal protection is a nonablative, semipassive thermal protection approach that leverages high thermal conductivity and efficient heat transfer characteristics of heat transfer elements to rapidly dissipate substantial aerodynamic heat flux near the stagnation point to regions with lower heat flux at the structure’s rear [41,43−45]. The design concept presented in this paper focuses on the development of CBCF composites with enhanced in-plane thermal conductivity, aimed at achieving directional heat leading, which is analogous to dredging thermal protection strategies. This approach effectively directs thermal flow to alleviate localized high temperatures within CBCF composites by integrating high-efficiency in-plane heat conduction pathways within the anisotropic thermal insulation structure, thereby promoting temperature uniformity and facilitating the development of advanced thermal management functions. Additionally, the preparation cycle and the establishment of cost-effective processes that can be scaled for large-size preparation are also important. The developed CBCF composite is anticipated to integrate thermal insulation with in-plane conduction capabilities, thereby enhancing the thermal protection efficiency.

Herein, we report novel SiBCN-modified carbon-bonded carbon fiber (denoted CBCF/SiBCN) composites designed for thermal protection applications, which exhibit significantly enhanced in-plane directional heat-leading functionality. The modified CBCF/SiBCN composites are fabricated with CBCF frameworks impregnated with a SiBCN ceramic matrix via the polymer infiltration and pyrolysis (PIP) method. The fundamental properties of the precursors used in the impregnation process were examined. The micromorphology, physical properties, mechanical response, and thermal performance of the CBCF/SiBCN composites were systematically investigated. The developed composites are lightweight and exhibit favorable compressive strength in both the in-plane and thermal insulation directions. The CBCF/SiBCN composites also exhibited significant differences in thermal conductivity between the heat-leading and thermal insulating directions, demonstrating an ideal thermal management function. This innovation is highly beneficial for the design of thermal protection materials that integrate thermal insulation and improved in-plane heat conduction characteristics, which may effectively meet the essential demands of multifunctional thermal protection in aerospace applications.

### 2.1 Materials

Viscose-based carbon fibers (VCFs; type: 16–18 μm in diameter and 1–2 mm in length) were purchased from Jilin Jiyan Hi-Tech Fiber Co., Ltd. Phenol-formaldehyde resin powder (PR; type: HTC–800) was supplied by Shaanxi Taihang Polymer Co., Ltd. Flexible graphite paper (FGP; type: thickness of 0.1 mm and 0.2 mm) was purchased from Tianjin Sikaifu Energy Saving Technology Co., Ltd. Polyborosilazane (PBSZ; type: liquid 15 V) was obtained from Shandong Qiquan Industry & Trade Co., Ltd. Dicumyl peroxide (DCP) and nonionic polyacrylamide (PAM) were obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd. and used without further purification.

### 2.2 Preparation of CBCF framework

In-plane heat-modified CBCF framework (Cf) composites with a density of 0.24–0.26 g**·**cm−3 were prepared according to a previously reported method [46]. In a typical procedure, VCFs (5.7 g), equal weights of PR powder (5.7 g) and nonionic PAM (0.07 g) were dispersed in deionized water (1000 mL) to obtain a uniform VCF dispersion. The suspension was divided into the required number of aliquots. The VCF dispersion and FGPs were then alternately poured into the filter press mold for a one-step pressure filtration process to obtain a wet preform with a diameter of 60 mm and a thickness of 15 mm, and the FGP was located in the middle of the thickness direction. The wet preform was dried at 100 °C for 5 h and cured at 150 °C for an additional 2 h. The preforms were then carbonized at 1000 °C in an argon atmosphere at a heating rate of 2 °C**·**min−1.

### 2.3 Preparation of CBCF/SiBCN composites

The synthesis procedures of the CBCF/SiBCN composites are illustrated in Fig. 1. DCP (0.3 g, 1.0 wt%) was dissolved in the PBSZ precursor (30 g) and stirred for 30 min in a nitrogen-protected sealed container to obtain the impregnation precursor (Fig. 1(a)). The modified CBCF frameworks, prepared in Section 2.2, were cleaned ultrasonically with ethanol and deionized water in turn and dried for 48 h to mitigate the influence of surface hydroxyl groups. The preformed units were subjected to vacuum impregnation for a duration of one hour (Fig. 1(b)), followed by curing at 100 °C for 1 h, 150 °C for 1 h, 200 °C for 1 h, and 250 °C for 2 h (Fig. 1(c)). The entire curing process was conducted under nitrogen protection conditions. Subsequently, pyrolysis was performed at a heating rate of 5 °C**·**min−1 to 1000 °C and held for 1 h under a flowing argon atmosphere at a rate of 100 sccm (Fig. 1(d)).

### 2.4 Characterizations

The precursors were characterized via Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR; Nicolet iS20, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1 with KBr pellets. The thermogravimetric behavior of the thermal decomposition of the precursor was evaluated via a simultaneous thermal analyzer (TG-DSC; STA 449 F3, Netzsch) combined with a mass spectrometer (TG-MS, QMS 403Q, Netzsch). The phase compositions were examined an x-ray diffractometer (XRD; Rigaku SmartLab SE) with Cu Kα radiation. The chemical bonding states were characterized via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS, K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific). The microstructures of the CBCF/SiBCN composites were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Gemini SEM 300, ZEISS) with an energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) system. The pore size distribution and specific surface area of the CBCF/SiBCN composites were determined via mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP; AutoPore V 9620, Micromeritics). Single axial compression tests were used to measure the compressive strength (*σ*) and modulus (*E*) via an electronic universal testing machine (EM6.204-L TSMT) with a pressure head speed of 1 mm/min. The XY- and Z samples were machined from blocks with dimensions of 10 (length) mm × 10 (width) mm × 10 (height) mm. Five samples were used for each test. The thermal conductivity and thermal diffusion coefficient in the in-plane (IP) direction were measured via a laser thermal conductivity meter (LFA 467, Netzsch). The thermal conductivity in the through-thickness (TTT) direction was measured via a plate heat flow thermal conductivity meter (DRPL-III, Xiangtan).

### 3.1 Pyrolysis behavior of PBSZ via PIP method

To optimize the preparation process for CBCF/SiBCN composites via the PIP method, a comprehensive investigation was conducted on the pyrolysis behavior of the PBSZ precursor and the properties of pyrolyzed ceramics. Figure 2(a) presents the FT-IR spectrum of PBSZ and its pyrolysis products, which were obtained over a temperature range of room temperature to 1000 °C. The primary characteristic peaks observed are as follows: 3395, 1159 cm–1 (N–H); 2965 cm–1 (C–H); 1594 cm–1 (C=C); 1460, 1402 cm–1 (B–N); 1251 cm–1 (Si–CH3); and 912 cm–1 (Si–N–Si). The broad peaks overlapping with the C–H peak at 1460 cm–1 and 1402 cm–1 correspond to the planar B–N hexatomic ring structure [32,33]. In the single-source precursor method, boron is incorporated into the main chain as a hexatomic ring of B–N, ensuring a uniform distribution of elements throughout the ceramic matrix [33]. Notably, the broad peak at 1402 cm–1 significantly decreased after 800 °C, indicating that during precursor pyrolysis, the B elements initially present in the B–N six-membered rings lost structural integrity, indicating the completion of the polymer-to-ceramic conversion. The TGA and TG‒MS results illustrating the pyrolysis characteristics of the PBSZ precursor are presented in Figs. 2(b)–2(d). The labels *m*/*z* in Figs. 2(c) and 2(d) represent the mass‒charge ratio, and the upper black dashed lines represent the weight loss curves. The assessment of ceramic yield is utilized to ascertain the appropriate dosage of crosslinking agent. The TGA curves in Fig. 2(b) show that the variation in ceramic yield at DCP dosages of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 wt% is less than 2%. Notably, the sample with a DCP dosage of 1.0 wt% presented the highest ceramic yield. Consequently, further analysis of the pyrolysis mechanism was conducted. The PBSZ framework primarily consists of –Si–N–B- bonds, with silicon atoms bonded to functional groups, such as –CH=CH2, –CH3, and –NH2. During pyrolysis, these groups undergo structural rearrangements, releasing small molecular gases and forming an amorphous ceramic material. The pyrolysis process of the PBSZ precursor can be categorized into three stages. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the stage I (below 400 °C) results in a 1.7% weight loss starting at 160 °C, primarily due to transamination and dehydrogenative coupling of N–H bonds. A sharp decrease in the TG curve occurs at 423 °C, persisting until 800 °C. The stage II (400–800 °C) involves the cleavage of hydrocarbon chains and elimination of terminal groups, releasing small molecular fragments, such as NH3 and CH4 (Figs. 2(c) and 2(d)). Continuous bond formation and cleavage lead to chain growth during organic transformation [26,29]. Above 800 °C, the mass remains relatively stable. In the stage III (800–1000 °C), the products undergo structural rearrangement, forming an amorphous network structure that stabilizes the system and achieves ceramic conversion. The resulting amorphous ceramics primarily consist of amorphous carbon (graphitic), planar BN phases, and a Si–C–N matrix (comprising SiC*x*N4−*x* units, where *x* = 0, 1, 2, 3) [28,29,31,47]. In summary, the optimal dosage of DCP was determined to be 1.0 wt%, resulting in a final ceramic yield of 66.93%. The TG‒DSC curves of the pyrolytic SiBCN ceramics under argon and air conditions are shown in Figs. S1(a) and S1(b) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). These results indicate that the resultant ceramics exhibit favorable thermal stability at elevated temperatures and have the potential to provide effective substrate protection for the carbon framework.

To further investigate the high-temperature crystallization behavior of the pyrolyzed SiBCN ceramics in an argon atmosphere, powder XRD measurements were performed on the samples. The XRD patterns presented in Fig. 2(e) show that the pyrolyzed ceramics remain amorphous up to 1400 °C. Subtle peaks at 2θ* = 27° and 36° emerged in the 1400 °C sample, indicating the formation of nanocrystalline phases. Crystallization occurred at 1600 °C, resulting in diffraction peaks at approximately 2*θ* = 27°, 36°, and 61°. These peaks are attributed primarily to silicon nitride (Si3N4), boron nitride (BN) and alpha and beta silicon carbide (α/β SiC). The composition of amorphous Si–B–C–N ceramics typically falls within the quaternary system’s four-phase field (SiC/Si3N4/BN/C), comprising mainly tetrahedral SiC*x*N4−*x* (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 4)-type units, BC*x*N3−*x* (0 ≤ *x* 2-hybridized carbon [31]. The presence of a turbostratic BN(C) phase in Si–B–C–N ceramics inhibits crystalline phase formation, thereby maintaining the amorphous structure and enhancing the structural integrity of the composite material [31,48−50]. In addition to the crystalline phases present in the pyrolyzed and annealed samples, the segregated carbon present in the samples was highly disordered [27,51,52]. To elucidate the molecular structure and atomic valence states of pyrolyzed ceramics, XPS was used to analyze the chemical bonding of ceramics pyrolyzed under an argon atmosphere at 1000 °C. The spectra of silicon, boron, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen are shown in Figs. 2(g1)–2(g4). Semiquantitative analysis via XPS yielded an atomic composition represented by the formula Si1B0.5C1.7N0.7O0.5. The detected oxygen is attributed to the unavoidable hydrolysis of the PBSZ precursors during storage and transfer. The C 1s signals at 284.6 and 283.4 eV (Fig. 2(g2)) are attributed to C–C and Si–C bonds, respectively. Furthermore, the Si–C bond is characterized by Si 2p binding energy of 100.4 eV (Fig. 2(g1)). A Si–N bond is formed, corresponding to Si 2p binding energy of 101.1 eV (Fig. 2(g1)) and N 1s binding energy of 397.1 eV (Fig. 2(g3)). The broad, weak Si 2p peak observed at binding energy of 101.9 eV corresponds to the Si–O bond (Fig. 2(g1)). In Fig. 2(g4), the peaks at 190.2 and 193.1 eV are assigned to the B–N bond, which is consistent with the FTIR analysis. In addition, the other peak centered at 193.1 eV is attributed to the B–O bond. In addition, a B–O–N bond with N 1s binding energy of 397.9 eV (Fig. 2(g3)) can be detected. In Fig. 2(g2), the peak at 285.1 eV is assigned to the C–N bond, which is related to the formation of the BN(C) structure within the pyrolytic ceramic, and the weak peak at 289.3 eV is assigned to the C=O bond, which is due to the presence of a residual metastable precursor and carbon dioxide that is absorbed on the sample surface [53]. The optimal addition of the curing agent DCP was established at 1.0 wt%. On the basis of the investigation of the pyrolysis conversion process and the resulting pyrolytic products, it is appropriate to set the pyrolysis conditions for impregnated-cured composites at 1000 °C under an argon atmosphere. This temperature falls within the thermal stability range of the carbon framework and facilitates the formation of a typical amorphous Si–B–C–N ceramic matrix, thereby shortening the preparation cycle and enhancing the process efficiency.

### 3.2 Preparation, microstructure, and characterizations of CBCF/SiBCN composites

A composite material with in-plane heat-conducting modifications was prepared utilizing the CBCF framework as insulating layers (ILs) with a carbon fiber-bonded quasilayered structure and flexible graphite paper as in-plane heat-leading layers (HLs). The modified CBCF framework was synthesized via a one-step coforming pressure filtration method, followed by ambient pressure drying and subsequent pyrolysis. Integration of the SiBCN ceramic matrix into the CBCF framework was achieved through the PIP method. To minimize the effects of moisture and oxygen, the preforms underwent drying treatment, and nitrogen gas was used for protection during both impregnation solution preparation and curing. The process of pyrolysis was conducted in a flowing argon atmosphere. A carbon fiber-reinforced ceramic composite material, denoted CBCF/SiBCN, was obtained. Additionally, the numerical values in the subscript of the sample name correspond to the “number of thermal layers-thickness”; for example, “C1–0.1” denotes frameworks incorporating a single heat-leading layer with a thickness of 0.1 mm, “C1–0.2” denotes frameworks incorporating a single heat-leading layer with a thickness of 0.2 mm, and “CVCF” denotes frameworks without heat-leading layers. The “HL combination” notation in the figures refers to various combinations in the CBCF/SiBCN composites mentioned above. TEM was used to examine the nanomorphology of the polymer-derived SiBCN ceramics. As shown in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b1)–3(b2), no periodically ordered structure was observed in the samples pyrolyzed at 1000 °C, and no diffraction points were present in the electron diffraction pattern, indicating the ceramic’s amorphous state. Small, turbostratic carbon layers formed, demonstrating agglomeration and mutual stacking [28,47,50]. The microstructure of the CBCF/SiBCN composite was examined via SEM, as shown in Figs. 3(c)–3(f). In the IP direction, the original fibrous framework maintained inherent random directional distribution characteristics, as illustrated in Fig. 3(c). The CBCF/SiBCN composite exhibited a uniform microstructure with no macroscopic defects in either direction, characterized by a ceramic matrix that encapsulated carbon frameworks and occupied interstitial spaces, whereas the surface of the ceramic displayed a smooth texture. EDS point scanning analysis of the ceramic matrix confirmed the presence of Si, B, C, N, and O (Fig. 3(d)), which is consistent with the results of the XPS analysis. The incorporation of the ceramic matrix into the CBCF framework composites enabled retention of a distinctive quasilayered structure in the TTT direction, whereas the ceramics also encapsulate carbon fibers and occupy interstitial spaces, as illustrated in Fig. 3(e).

Furthermore, the combination of thermal insulation and heat-leading layers in CBCF/SiBCN has garnered significant attention, warranting further characterization in this area. The morphology at the IL/HL/IL binding regions in the TTT direction was examined (Fig. 3(f)), and the EDS elemental maps of CBCF/SiBCN in the IP and TTT directions are depicted in Figs. S2 and S3 in the ESM. The microscopic morphology indicates that the self-assembly between the layers is both complete and compact. This is primarily facilitated by the connections formed through pyrolytic carbon and porous ceramic, and the analysis of the elemental distribution further corroborates that the porous ceramic effectively encapsulates the carbon fibers and fills the voids within the carbon framework, exhibiting a uniform distribution in both the IP and TTT directions.

MIP measurements yielded cumulative intrusion, pore size distribution, and pore area data, as depicted in Fig. 4(a). The pore diameter primarily ranged from 10 to 100 nm, with an average pore diameter of 26.17 μm, indicating that the CBCF/SiBCN composite predominantly had a mesoporous structure. This observation is consistent with the SEM image results. MIP analysis of CBCF/SiBCN and the corresponding CBCF framework related to the MIP measurements are displayed in Fig. S4 and Table S1 in the ESM. TG-DSC was used to assess the weight loss of the CBCF/SiBCN composite under argon and air conditions, as shown in Fig. 4(b). Under Ar conditions, the residual weight reached 96.47% at 1000 °C. This value decreased by 1.71% at 1200 °C and further decreased by 1.65% at 1400 °C, resulting in a final residual weight of 93.11%. As shown in Fig. S1(a) in the ESM, the weight loss of SiBCN under argon conditions indicates that the ceramics exhibit favorable thermal stability, with a weight loss of approximately 4.61% at 1200 °C. The carbon fiber framework also demonstrates significant high-temperature stability under inert conditions. The weight loss observed in the CBCF/SiBCN composites is attributed primarily to the SiBCN matrix, which involves the dissociation of the amorphous SiN network, leading to the release of nitrogen, as well as the cleavage of metastable organic groups remaining from the precursor conversion process, resulting in the emission of small molecules [25,54,55]. The composite exhibited ideal high-temperature stability under oxygen-free conditions. The changes in thermal weight loss are more pronounced under atmospheric conditions. The residual weight decreased to 66.93% at 1000 °C. This value decreased by 0.6% at 1200 °C and further decreased by 0.64% at 1400 °C, resulting in a final residual weight of 65.69%. The primary cause of weight loss in the range of 500–800 °C is attributed to the oxidation of the carbon fibers. The oxidative resistance of pyrolytic ceramics in air is highly favorable. The changes in density before and after pyrolysis are illustrated in Fig. 4(c). The introduction of a ceramic matrix increased the density of the CBCF framework from 0.25 to approximately 0.5 g·cm−3. The comparative optical images before and after pyrolysis, along with the corresponding changes in density, are presented in Figs. S5 and S6 in the ESM, respectively. As shown in the right image in Fig. 4(d), the lightweight nature of a typical sample was demonstrated by its ability to be placed on a wheat ear.

### 3.3 Compressive mechanical properties of CBCF/SiBCN composites

The mechanical response of the CBCF/SiBCN composites is characterized by a distinct transverse anisotropy, which is attributed primarily to their inherent microstructural features. To facilitate a clear presentation of the experimental findings, the CBCF/SiBCN samples were categorized into two groups. Figure 5(a) shows the fiber orientation distribution and specific orientations. The nomenclature for the IP and TTT direction samples is based on the relationship between the fiber orientation and loading direction, as shown in Figs. 5(b1) and 5(b2). The *XY* sample designation indicates loading parallel to the IP direction, whereas the *Z* sample follows a similar rationale. Consequently, the *XY* sample corresponds to the in-plane heat-leading direction, whereas the *Z* sample is associated with the thermal insulation direction.

*
Fig. 5(a) Schematic illustration of orientation distribution characteristics of fibers in CBCF/SiBCN samples. (b1–b2) Relationships between loading direction and typical fiber orientations in XY* and *Z* samples.

A comparison of the representative stress‒strain curves for the *XY* sample (green dotted line) and *Z* sample (blue short dotted dashed line) of C1-0.1/SiBCN under compressive loading is shown in Fig. 6(a), alongside the corresponding curves of the C1-0.1 framework (gray dotted lines for the *XY* sample and gray short dotted dashed lines for the *Z* sample). Figures 6(b) and 6(c) show comparisons of the representative stress‒strain curves for the other categories of *XY* and *Z* samples, respectively. The compressive strengths (*σ*) and moduli (*E*) were derived from the average values of the curves, and the results are presented in Figs. 6(d) and 6(e). Previous studies have indicated that the random fiber distribution in the IP direction, combined with the quasilayered structure formed in the TTT direction, results in variations in the mechanical behavior between the two directions[12,15,19,56,57]. The compressive behaviors of the CBCF frameworks also demonstrate distinct differences in both the IP and TTT directions, as shown by the gray curves in Fig. 7(a). In the TTT direction, the fibers undergo lateral bending and compression between layers, initially resulting in elastic deformation. As the strain increases, damage indicates the onset of a pseudoplastic phase. Further strain increases lead to fiber fracture, void filling, and structural densification. In the IP direction, the stress‒strain relationship initially displays linear elasticity, with fibers experiencing axial compression and carbon bonding points constraining fiber sliding and rotation. As the strain increases, damage and crack propagation occur, resulting in nonlinear brittle fracture characteristics. A comparison of the curves in Fig. 6(a) suggests that the compressive mechanical behavior of the CBCF/SiBCN composites resembles that of the carbon frameworks. The SEM images presented above (Figs. 3(d) and 3(e)) indicate that the ceramic matrix effectively encapsulates the fibers, fills gaps and interfaces, and enhances fiber bonding and adhesion to the matrix. This structural arrangement significantly improves the stability of the frameworks. Consequently, the integration of the ceramic matrix is anticipated to substantially increase the compressive mechanical properties of the materials.

The following discussion focuses on the compressive mechanical behavior of CBCF/SiBCN composites in the IP direction. As shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b), the stress‒strain curves of the XY* samples exhibit a constant tangent slope during the initial stage, indicating linear elastic deformation. The fiber orientation is parallel to the plane of the applied load, and shear forces at the ceramic-encased bonding points limit fiber deflection and slippage. Simultaneously, some of the portion of the coated fibers experience axial buckling deformation, with their strength in this direction being further enhanced synergistically by the ceramic matrix. The *XY* samples primarily demonstrate linear elastic deformation before failure. As the strain increases, yielding behavior is observed, characterized by a brief plateau in the stress‒strain curve. Once the axial pressure limit of the fibers and the shear limit of the bonding points are exceeded, the microstructure experiences failure. Localized defects develop within the samples, resulting in brittle fracture and a reduction in stress. The maximum stress value preceding the initial decline (in Fig. 6(a), marked as “drop”) is defined as the compressive strength (*σ**XY*), and the initial tangent slope is designated the compressive modulus (*E**XY*). Further increases in strain promote the propagation of internal cracks, causing certain fibers to exceed their bending limits and fracture, whereas the carbon bonding points also fail. The stress curve exhibits nonlinear degradation. As illustrated in the upper right section of Fig. 6(a), when the limits of fracture are exceeded, the sample disintegrates into particles. This disintegration reveals a thin central silver region that represents the compressed graphite paper within the sample, which is surrounded by black CBCF-SiBCN particles. During the IP compression process, there was no sudden drop in stress corresponding to lateral sliding caused by interlayer failure, and both CBCF-SiBCN and GP fragmented into particles of similar size. GP plays a certain reinforcing role in restricting fiber slip during the initial stage of the collaborative deformation process. As the strain increased, in situ compression occurred without interlayer shear failure, indicating that the interface between CBCF-SiBCN and GP was reliable. As summarized in Figs. 6(d) and 6(e), the incorporation of continuous graphite paper as a heat-leading layer can significantly enhance the compressive strength in the IP direction. This phenomenon is attributed primarily to the ability of the heat-leading layer to restrict the slip orientation of specific coated fibers and bonding points during the initial stages of IP compression. The presence of both pyrolytic carbon and ceramic at the interface results in a pinning effect, whereby the shear deformation at the bonding points can absorb compressive energy. Simultaneously, the sliding is constrained by the graphite paper, which facilitates the establishment of a rigid-flexible coupling mechanism in conjunction with the carbon-ceramic load-bearing structure. This interaction significantly enhances the interlaminar shear strength and improves the in-plane compressive performance. Consequently, in the IP direction, both the compressive strength and modulus of the CBCF/SiBCN samples increased, with the incorporation of 0.1 mm graphite paper resulting in a more significant enhancement due to its better flexibility.

In the TTT direction, the stress‒strain curves of the *Z* samples also exhibit a constant tangent slope during the initial stage, indicating linear elastic deformation, as shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(c). During this phase, the ceramic matrix forms a strong bond with the framework, reinforcing the bonding points, bearing shear loads, and preventing changes in orientation between the matrix and the fibers. This leads to elastic behavior and an increase in the strength of the *Z* samples, as illustrated in Fig. 6(a). The material subsequently enters a pseudoplastic phase, characterized by a significant decrease in the tangent of the curves. The yielding of fibers and the matrix occurs in *Z* samples, followed by failure when the ultimate limit of the coated fibers and bonding points is exceeded, resulting in localized damage. This type of failure persists after its initial occurrence, exhibiting an oscillatory pattern in the curves of CBCF/SiBCN. As the compressive strain increases, the unrestricted deflection of the fibers and matrix ceramics continues, leading to crack propagation. The composite material undergoes compaction, ultimately resulting in irreversible deformation. As shown in Fig. 6(a), the *Z* sample disintegrated into fragments that subsequently stacked together after compression. For the carbon framework samples, the compressive strength and moduli were determined from the intersection point of the tangents of the elastic and pseudoplastic stages [15,46,57]. However, on the basis of the preceding analysis, incorporation of a ceramic matrix resulted in the CBCF/SiBCN samples exhibiting characteristic features of brittle fracture. Consequently, the compressive strength (*σ**Z*) and modulus (*E**Z*) are determined at the point of initial stress drop. As shown in Figs. 7(d) and 7(e), the introduction of HLs has a minimal impact on *σ**Z* and *E**Z*, primarily due to their positioning between layers, which allows them to transmit stress without providing significant reinforcement. The incorporation of a SiBCN ceramic matrix effectively strengthened the modified CBCF framework. The compressive strengths of CBCF/SiBCN with incorporated HLs in the IP direction and TTT direction reach 4.05–4.36 and 1.30–1.36 MPa, respectively, whereas the compressive moduli reach 58.6–69.3 and 22.2–25.9 MPa, respectively.

### 3.4 In-plane heat leading and thermal insulation properties of CBCF/SiBCN composites

CBCFs are regarded as optimal lightweight thermal protection materials because of their unique porous structure, which is formed by interlocking carbon fibers [11,58,59]. Unlike long-fiber-reinforced composites, the overlapping arrangement of chopped carbon fibers effectively mitigates the heat-bridge effect. Graphite paper has excellent in-plane thermal conductivity and low thermal resistance, along with commendable chemical stability and mechanical flexibility[60,61], making it an exceptional material for thermal management applications. Integrating graphite as a heat-leading layer within CBCF composite materials aims to enhance in-plane heat conduction. Figure 7(a) shows the thermal conductivity of CBCF/SiBCN in the IP direction (*λ*IP) from room temperature to 1000 °C. The results of the thermal diffusion coefficient and specific heat capacity of the XY samples, as measured via a laser thermal conductivity meter, are presented in Figs. S7a and S7b in the ESM. Infrared detection focused primarily on the center of the samples, corresponding to the feedback position of the entire heat-leading layer and a portion of the insulation layer in single-layer samples. The thermal conductivity of *XY* samples with a single heat-leading layer reached 60–65 W∙m−1∙K−1 at room temperature, indicating effective thermal management. Single-layer sample curves initially decreased before increasing, mirroring the trend of graphite, indicating the dominant role of graphite in composite thermal conduction behavior [56,60,62]. This phenomenon primarily results from low-temperature phonon transport in graphite, which facilitates heat conduction. Increasing temperatures increase the phonon energy, but lattice distortions and impurities impede phonon transport, reducing the thermal conductivity. Additionally, as the temperature continues to rise, the modes of heat conduction convert to being dominated by electron conduction. This shift is accompanied by an increase in the thermal motion of electrons and an increase in their mobility, resulting in a slight increase in thermal conductivity. The modified composites demonstrate commendable in-plane thermal conductivities at high temperatures. The *λ*IP values of the C1-0.1/SiBCN and C1-0.2/SiBCN composites and their corresponding Cf carbon frameworks are compared in Figs. 7(b) and 7(c). Compared with that of the corresponding CBCF framework, the in-plane thermal conductivity of the SiBCN-modified CBCF composite significantly increased, with an average increase exceeding 300%. This finding indicates that encapsulated fibers and ceramic filling between the frameworks enhance the heat transfer efficiency, intensify solid-phase heat conduction, and significantly enhance the thermal conductivity in the IP direction. The thermal conductivities of the CBCF/SiBCN samples in the TTT direction (*λ*TTT) at room temperature are summarized in Table 1 combined with *λ*IP. The incorporation of heat-leading layers did not compromise the thermal insulation capacity of the CBCF/SiBCN composites. The differences in thermal conductivity anisotropy exceed 750-fold at room temperature. The rapid dissipation of heat in the IP direction serves to prevent localized overheating and mitigate thermal stress, thereby enhancing exceptional thermal management capabilities.

Figure 9 shows the CBCF/SiBCN heat transfer mechanism. In the TTT direction, the thermal insulation properties primarily result from the SiBCN-impregnated CBCFs. The increase in the thermal conductivity of the insulating layer, compared with that of the original carbon framework material, is expected to be due to the increased density and reduced porosity resulting from ceramic infiltration. The introduction of the ceramic matrix fills the voids in the carbon framework, creating a more continuous and interconnected network for thermal conduction. This reduces phonon scattering at bonding points and establishes robust thermal bridges that enhance the pathways of solid‒solid heat transfer. While the insulating layers primarily restrict heat flow in the TTT direction, the heat-leading layer promotes effective in-plane heat transfer. In the IP direction, graphite paper, which serves as the heat-leading layer, is an anisotropic material characterized by its exceptionally high thermal conductivity. This property is attributed to its highly oriented and densely packed graphene layers, which facilitate efficient phonon transport with minimal scattering. Furthermore, the flexible nature of graphite paper ensures effective integration with adjacent insulating layers, thereby enabling efficient thermal interfacing without introducing significant thermal resistance. The heat-leading layer functions as a dedicated pathway for in-plane heat conduction, optimizing the thermal management of the composite system. As part of a layered structure, graphite paper acts as a thermal bridge between adjacent layers, effectively reducing the in-plane thermal gradient and ensuring uniform heat dissipation. For insulating layers, the ceramic matrix effectively encapsulates the carbon fibers, improving the directional heat flow along the fiber arrangement direction, and the axial heat transfer through the coated carbon fiber framework becomes more concentrated. The enhanced axial heat transfer operates in conjunction with the heat-leading layer, resulting in improved in-plane heat conduction of the CBCF/SiBCN composites.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, lightweight carbon-ceramic composites exhibiting thermal insulation and enhanced in-plane heat-leading properties were successfully fabricated via the PIP method. Systematic investigations of liquid PBSZ precursors have focused on their curing and pyrolysis processes, as well as their pyrolysis product characteristics. The modified CBCF/SiBCN composite exhibited a low density of 0.5 g∙cm−3. Notably, the compressive mechanical properties significantly improved compared with those of the carbon frameworks, with compressive strengths of 4.05–4.36 and 1.30–1.36 MPa in the IP and TTT directions, respectively. The modified CBCF/SiBCN exhibited substantial anisotropy in thermal conductivity between the insulating and in-plane heat leading directions. The incorporation of heat-leading layers did not adversely affect the insulation performance, as evidenced by a thermal conductivity of approximately 0.08 W∙m−1∙K−1 at room temperature. In contrast, the thermal conductivity in the IP direction reached 60.9–61.5 W∙m−1∙K−1, indicating significant anisotropy and significant potential for effective thermal management. This creative design highlights the potential of composites for advanced thermal management applications. These carbon‒ceramic composites are expected to increase the temperature resistance limits of thermal protection materials, thereby improving the high-temperature thermal protection efficiency. Moreover, this work establishes a new paradigm for multifunctional thermal management materials, providing critical solutions for stagnation point heat flux redistribution in next-generation aerospace systems. Our future research endeavors will focus on enhancing the in-plane heat-leading and high-temperature resistance, with particular emphasis on microstructural construction and design of composite materials, as well as the application of coatings or surface encapsulation to further improve oxidation resistance.
