## 1 Introduction

The semiconductor manufacturing and defense sectors have witnessed a substantial increase in demand for advanced ceramic components with large dimensions and intricate geometries [1−4]. Among the various stages involved in ceramic fabrication, forming is particularly critical because it directly influences the structural reliability of ceramic components and contributes to reducing production costs [5−8]. The choice of forming method fundamentally governs the powder packing density and microstructural homogeneity of the green body, thereby affecting the final ceramic density, mechanical properties, and overall performance [9−11].

Spontaneous coagulation casting (SCC), an emerging forming technique, was first introduced by Yang *et al*. [12]. In this method, a copolymer of isobutylene and maleic anhydride (PIBM; Isobam) functions simultaneously as a dispersant and a gelling agent, allowing for *in situ* solidification of ceramic slurries to form green bodies. SCC offers several notable advantages, including minimal organic additive content (typically less than 0.5 wt%), high solid loading capacity, excellent uniformity in the formed bodies, and optimal green body strength. Furthermore, the absence of metal ions in PIBM makes it particularly suitable for producing high–purity or transparent ceramics. On the basis of functional groups and molecular weight, PIBM variants include Isobam 104, Isobam 600AF, and Isobam 04, among others.

SCC has attracted considerable research attention [13−16] and has been successfully applied to the fabrication of a wide range of ceramics, such as Al2O3 [17], AlN [18], SiC [19], fused silica [20], silicon nitride [21], ZTA (zirconia toughened alumina) composites [22], translucent Al2O3 ceramics [23,24] and YAG [25,26], MgAl2O4 [27], Y2O3 [28], and AlON transparent ceramics [29,30].

High solids loading and low apparent viscosity of the slurry are essential for successful *in situ* solidification. Yang *et al*. [12] employed 0.45 μm alumina powder and 0.3 wt% Isobam 104 (molecular weight: 55,000–65,000) to prepare a slurry with 50 vol% (80 wt%) solids loading—the maximum attainable with Isobam 104 as the dispersant. The resulting green body exhibited a relative density of 58.1%. Subsequently, Sun *et al*. [31] partially substituted Isobam 104 with Isobam 600AF (molecular weight: 5500–5600) while using the same alumina powder and successfully formulated a slurry with 56 vol% (84 wt%) solid loading. This approach reduced both drying and sintering shrinkage of the green body, ultimately yielding alumina ceramics with a flexural strength of 534 MPa.

To extend the applicability of SCC and enhance its sustainability, this study investigated the use of alternative organic additives beyond the PIBM system as dispersants in conjunction with Isobam 104. The goal is to formulate slurries with higher solids loading and lower apparent viscosity, thereby improving the density of the sintered ceramics.

According to Derjaguin–Landau–Verwey–Overbeek (DLVO) theory [32,33], the stability of particle dispersion in a slurry is determined by the total potential energy resulting from van der Waals forces and electrical double–layer repulsion. Near the isoelectric point, the energy barrier becomes low, and the absolute value of the zeta potential decreases, promoting particle agglomeration and sedimentation. In contrast, a larger absolute zeta potential enhances interparticle repulsion, facilitating stable dispersion in the slurry. To achieve uniform dispersion of alumina particles, anionic dispersants, such as Isobam 600AF [17,24,31] and polyacrylic acid [34−36], are employed. These agents adsorb onto alumina particle surfaces and stabilize the slurry through electrostatic repulsion. As organic additives occupy space within the slurry, minimizing their volume allows for a higher proportion of alumina powder, thereby increasing the density and compactness of the green body. Furthermore, their small dimensions make them particularly suitable for nanosized ceramic powders, thereby extending the applicability of SCC to a broader range of powder particle sizes [34].

This study explored the use of polyacrylic acid (PA; AC–10SL) as a dispersant in combination with Isobam 104 for SCC. PA is a commercially available dispersant with a molecular weight of 3000, which is lower than that of Isobam 600AF. The use of PA with the same alumina powder was expected to increase the solid loading of the slurry. Furthermore, PA was modified through pH adjustment with ammonia (NH3·H2O) to form ammonium polyacrylate (denoted as M–PA), and its effectiveness as a dispersant for extending the applicability of the SCC method in combination with Isobam 104 was demonstrated.

### 2.1 Materials

The starting materials include alumina powder, gelling agents and dispersants. The alumina powder (AES–11, Sumitomo, Japan) had an average particle size of 0.45 μm, a purity of 99.7%, and a phase composition of 100% α–alumina. As the gelling agent, a copolymer of isobutylene and maleic anhydride (1 : 1 ratio) with an average molecular weight of 55,000–65,000 (Isobam 104, Kuraray, Japan; hereafter referred to as Ib104) was utilized. Three dispersants were evaluated. The first one is Isobam 600AF (molecular weight: 5500–5600; Kuraray, Japan; hereafter referred to as Ib600). The second is polyacrylic acid (PA; AC–10SL; molecular weight: 3000; Toagosei, Japan). The third is a modified version. Ammonia (AR; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) was added to AC–10SL until the solution reached a pH of approximately 7 to formulate ammonium polyacrylate (M–PA). The dispersion efficiency and gelation behavior of alumina slurries prepared with these three dispersants were systematically compared. For consistency and comparability, the same weight fractions of dispersant and Ib104 reported in Sun *et al*. [31] were adopted for green body preparation.

### 2.2 Sample preparation

Deionized water and Ib104 were mixed via a magnetic stirrer for 30 min to ensure complete dissolution and to facilitate the unfolding of the molecular bundles. Each dispersant (Ib600, PA, or M–PA) was subsequently added to the Ib104 solution. The alumina powder was then incorporated and homogenized via planetary ball milling for 1 h to ensure uniform dispersion of the components. The slurry consisted of 56 vol% alumina powder, 0.1 wt% Ib104, and 0.2 wt% dispersant (relative to the weight of alumina powder). Before casting, slurries were degassed in a vacuum chamber to eliminate entrapped air bubbles. Gelation was carried out in sealed molds (65 mm × 40 mm × 5 mm and  ϕ40 mm × 5 mm) for 24 h. The resulting wet green bodies were dried in a constant temperature (35 °C) and humidity (80%) chamber for 24 h. After demolding, the bodies were heated at 800 °C for 2 h in air at a heating rate of 1 °C.min−1 to remove organic additives. Finally, the samples were sintered at 1550 °C for 3 h in air to achieve the desired densification and mechanical properties. The sintering profile was optimized on the basis of the thermal shrinkage of the alumina green body, which was obtained via a high–temperature deformation visual analyzer (TA–Z16AB–2G, Tianjin Zhonghuan Electric Furnace Co., Ltd., China), as shown in Fig. 1.

### 2.3 Test methods

Zeta potential measurements of alumina slurries were conducted via electrophoretic mobility via a zeta potential analyzer (90Plus PALS, Brookhaven, USA), and the pH values were adjusted with HCl and NaOH solutions. The pH of the slurries was measured via a pH meter (PHS–25, INASE Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China), with all samples being stored under sealed conditions at room temperature. Rheological behavior was assessed via a stress–controlled rheometer (Haake Viscotester iQ Air, Thermo Electron GmbH, Germany) equipped with a 25 mm diameter parallel plate. The apparent viscosity was measured under continuous shear at rates ranging from 1 to 200 s−1 at 25 °C. Gelling behavior was evaluated in oscillatory mode at a frequency of 1.0 Hz and a constant strain of 0.4%. For sintering shrinkage testing, four samples with dimensions of 65 mm × 40 mm × 5 mm were prepared. The cumulative pore area of the green bodies was characterized via mercury porosimetry (AutoPore IV 9520, Micromeritics, USA). On this basis, the relative density of the green bodies was calculated. The bulk densities of the sintered ceramics were determined via Archimedes’ principle. The microstructure of the sintered ceramics was determined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; SU8220, HITACHI, Japan). Flexural strength was assessed via three–point bending tests on bar samples with dimensions of 36 mm × 4 mm × 3 mm, which were polished and chamfered at 45° on all edges, with a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm∙min−1 (Instron–5566, Instron, USA). The sample numbers of Ceramic Ib, Ceramic PA and Ceramic M–PA were 12, 17 and 18 pieces, respectively. Vickers hardness was measured via the indentation method with a Vickers hardness tester (Tukon2100B, Wilson & Wolpert CPAs, USA). For each ceramic, five measurement points were tested. The fracture toughness was calculated on the basis of crack length measurements according to Eq. (1):

(1) KIC=F×9.81×1000(3.14d)2/3×tan(68×3.14180)

where *K*IC is the fracture toughness (MPa.m1/2), *F* is the applied load (N), and *d* is the average crack length (mm).

### 3.1 Zeta potential

Figure 2(a) illustrates the influence of different dispersants on the zeta potential of alumina slurries. The slurry without any additive exhibited a zeta potential with an absolute value below 8 mV within the pH range of 8.1–8.5 (i.e., the pH of the slurry). Notably, the addition of dispersants shifted the isoelectric point (IEP) of alumina without any additive from pH 8.8 to below pH 5, indicating that all dispersants effectively disperse particles in the alumina slurry [37]. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the slurries presented different pH values when different dispersants were added. The pH values of all three slurries increased slightly with time and stabilized after 16 h. In detail, slurries Ib, PA and M–PA presented initial pH values of 8.3, 7.7, and 8.1, respectively, and finally stabilized at 8.5, 8.1, and 8.4 after 24 h. In the stabilized stage, slurry Ib presented a zeta potential of −55.1 mV at pH 8.5, as shown in Fig. 2(a), whereas slurries PA and M–PA presented values of −58.6 mV (pH = 8.1) and −61.8 mV (pH = 8.4), respectively. Therefore, slurry M–PA exhibited a greater absolute zeta potential than slurry Ib and slurry PA did, suggesting that M–PA provides superior dispersing performance relative to Ib600 and PA.

Figure 2(c) shows the change in the apparent viscosity of the slurries with pH. As the pH increased, the apparent viscosity of the slurries first decreased but then increased. ### 3.2 Rheological and gelling behavior of slurries

The rheological behavior of slurries with 56 vol% solid loading and different dispersants—Ib600, PA, and M–PA—was investigated at 25 °C (Fig. 3(a)). All three slurries exhibited shear–thinning characteristics, which is consistent with the zeta potential results discussed in Section 3.1. The measured IEP of alumina without additive was pH 8.8, which shifted below pH 5 upon dispersant addition (Fig. 2(a)), confirming that PA, M–PA, and Ib104 are compatible with slurry casting. Among the three systems, slurry M–PA presented the lowest apparent viscosity (0.54 Pa·s@100 s−1), whereas slurries Ib and PA presented similar viscosities of 0.69 Pa·s and 0.59 Pa·s@100 s−1, respectively. The lower apparent viscosity of slurry M–PA than that of slurry PA may be attributed to enhanced electrical double–layer repulsion. The primary constituent of PA (AC–10SL) is polyacrylic acid, whose –COOH groups partially ionize to –COO– and adsorb onto particle surfaces, thereby inducing electrostatic repulsion between particles. The addition of ammonia (NH3·H2O) transformed –COOH groups into –COONH4, which yielded a greater concentration of –COO– ions, thereby intensifying the double–layer repulsion and therefore dispersing the slurry more effectively. This mechanism aligns with the zeta potential trends in Fig. 2(a), where slurry M–PA has a more negative potential than does slurry Ib. Additionally, the low molecular weight of M–PA minimizes steric hindrance, allowing more space for particle packing in the slurry and enhancing particle dispersion.

The storage modulus (*G*’) of slurries was also measured at 25 °C (Fig. 3(b)). For all slurries containing three distinct dispersants, the storage modulus gradually increased with time, indicating the gradual development of a microgel structure that evolved into a monolithic gel structure. Assuming a target storage modulus (*G*’) of 10,000 Pa, slurry Ib presented a shorter gelation time (1020 s) than did slurries PA (10,020 s) and M–PA (3000 s), whereas slurry M–PA gelled faster than did slurry PA. A shorter gelation time reflects a stronger solidification capability of the slurry. As shown in Fig. 3(a), slurry Ib had a higher apparent viscosity, which is helpful for gelation. Moreover, Ib600 has a longer molecular chain, which enables it to better interact with Ib104 to form a gel network. Compared with slurry PA, the higher alkalinity of slurry M–PA promotes enhanced hydrolysis of Isobam 104, generating additional –COO– groups. This increase in ionic strength within the slurry strengthens intermolecular interactions between Isobam 104 chains, leading to the formation of polymeric clusters. Therefore, the storage modulus of slurry M–PA is greater than that of slurry PA. All three systems ultimately underwent complete gelation within 24 h, demonstrating their suitability for gel casting processes.

Owing to the superior dispersing performance exhibited by M–PA, attempts have been made to prepare alumina slurries with higher solid loadings via M–PA. As shown in Fig. 4(a), the 58 vol% (85 wt%) slurry prepared with the original formulation of 0.2 wt% M–PA and 0.1 wt% Ib104 exhibited higher apparent viscosity, making debubbling and pouring the slurry into the mold difficult. Therefore, the dosage of Ib104 was reduced with increasing M–PA content, maintaining the total organic content at 0.3 wt%. As shown in Fig. 4(b), after adjusting the ratios of M–PA and Ib104, the alumina slurry exhibited shear–thinning behavior with a lower apparent viscosity (0.54 Pa·s@100 s−1). Thus, M–PA enables the preparation of higher solid loading alumina slurries, whereas the use of Ib600 only allows for a maximum solid loading of 56 vol% [31].

### 3.3 Characteristics of ceramics

Figure 5(a) presents the cumulative pore area vs. pore size of three green bodies. The measured average pore diameters were 78.8, 74.9, and 77.4 nm for Gb–Ib, Gb–PA, and Gb–M–PA, respectively, with porosities of 38.9%, 37.2%, and 38.0%, respectively. Notably, Gb–PA has the smallest porosity, but its pore size is the smallest, so the number of pores is greater. Figure 5(b) presents the relative density and porosity of the green bodies on the basis of mercury intrusion data. Samples Gb–Ib, Gb–PA, and Gb–M–PA presented similar relative densities of 61.1%, 62.8%, and 62.0%, respectively. The higher relative density was attributed to the higher solids loading slurry. In comparison, Sun *et al*. [31] reported a lower relative density of only 58.1% for a 50 vol% slurry prepared solely with Ib104 as the dispersant with the same powder. Even at the same solid loading of 56 vol%, Sun *et al* [31]. reported a relatively low density of 60.4% when both Ib104 and Ib600 were used as dispersants. The discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the processing procedures, potentially resulting in higher apparent viscosity of the slurry and incomplete debubbling in their study. The effects of various dispersants on the linear shrinkage of ceramics are presented in Fig. 5(d). Ceramic M–PA demonstrated the greatest linear shrinkage (14.2%), which was slightly greater than that of ceramic PA (13.6%) and ceramic Ib (13.9%) under the same solid loading and Ib104 conditions. This greater shrinkage indicates a denser sintered structure upon sintering. Figure 5(c) shows the relative density and porosity of the sintered ceramics, which were determined via the Archimedes drainage method. Among the samples, ceramic M–PA presented the highest relative density (98.7%), whereas ceramic Ib presented the lowest (98.1%). The enhanced densification in ceramic M–PA is likely attributed to the lower apparent viscosity of slurry M–PA, which facilitates better particle arrangement within the slurry and complete debubbling, thereby minimizing residual porosity in the final sintered ceramics.

Figure 6 compares the mechanical properties of ceramics prepared with different dispersants (solid loading, 56 vol%). Among the tested materials, Ceramic M–PA showed superior performance, with a Vickers hardness of 17.3±0.3 GPa, fracture toughness of 2.8±0.1 MPa.m1/2, and flexural strength of 583±84 MPa. Figure 7 presents both polished–surface and cross–sectional microstructures of the ceramics (from 56 vol% solid loading slurry). The polished surface was etched by thermal corrosion treatment at 1400 °C. As shown in Figs. 7(a)–7(c1), both ceramic M–PA and ceramic Ib have larger pore sizes than does ceramic PA. Figure 5(c) reveals that Ceramic Ib and PA have similar closed porosity levels (1.7% and 1.6%, respectively), whereas Ceramic Ib has larger closed pore sizes—a finding further supported by Fig. 5(b). Thus, although their close porosities are comparable, the smaller pore size of ceramic PA results in a greater number of pores, ultimately degrading their mechanical properties. Figures 7(a2)−7(c2) show that ceramic M–PA exhibited mainly transgranular fracture behavior, whereas ceramic Ib and PA exhibited both transgranular and intergranular fracture. This indicates that the grain boundaries of the ceramic M–PA were strengthened, consequently leading to its superior flexural strength (Fig. 6). The mean grain sizes of ceramics Ib, PA, and M–PA were 1.96, 1.76, and 1.73 μm, respectively, indicating similar grain sizes across samples. For Ceramic Ib, PA and M–PA, 6%, 5% and 2% of the grains were larger than 4 μm, respectively. Ceramics PA and M–PA presented more uniform grain size distributions, whereas ceramic Ib presented a greater proportion of large grains (Figs. 7(a3)–7(c3)). Given that mechanical strength is influenced by both porosity and grain size, the superior strength of ceramic M–PA can be attributed to its lower porosity and more homogeneous microstructure. In contrast, the heterogeneity and prevalence of larger grains in ceramic Ib likely contributed to its reduced strength.

## 4 Conclusions

Spontaneous coagulation casting is known as a novel *in situ* slurry solidification technique that employs Isobam 104 (molecular weight: 55,000–65,000) as both a dispersant and a gelling agent. Partial replacement of Isobam 104 with Isobam 600AF, which has a relatively low molecular weight (5500–5600), has previously enabled the preparation of high-solid–loading slurries. In the present study, polyacrylic acid (PA; molecular weight: 3000) was employed as a replacement for Isobam 600AF in combination with Isobam 104 to prepare alumina ceramics. Following modification with NH3·H2O, PA was converted to ammonium polyacrylate (M–PA). The resulting slurry exhibited high solid loading (56 vol%), lower apparent viscosity, and was capable of spontaneous gelling. The resultant alumina ceramics sintered at 1550 °C achieved a relative density of 98.7%, a Vickers hardness of 17.3 GPa, a flexural strength of 583 MPa, and a fracture toughness of 2.8 MPa·m1/2, all of which exceeded those obtained from ceramics prepared with Isobam 600AF or PA. Furthermore, when the ratio of M–PA to Isobam 104 was varied, the solid loading was increased from 56 to 58 vol%. The green body from 58 vol% solid loading can be sintered at a low temperature (1500 °C) to obtain a relatively high relative density (98.9%) and high Vickers hardness (17.8 GPa). These findings indicate that modified PA not only serves as a viable replacement for Isobam 600AF but also enhances the final ceramic properties. The use of modified PA in SCC thus offers enhanced material performance and broadens the applicability of the SCC technique. Furthermore, the combination of SCC with lower-molecular-weight dispersants is expected to facilitate the fabrication of high–performance ceramics under low sintering temperatures.
