## 1 Introduction

Advanced wireless communication and portable electronics are continuously promoting social and technological development. However, the generated electromagnetic (EM) radiation has posed severe hazards to human health, information security, and the stable operation of high-precision electronic devices [1]. These growing issues highlight the urgent demand for highly efficient EM wave absorption materials with broad bandwidths, strong intensities, light weights, and thin thicknesses [2].

To date, traditional microwave absorption materials, including ceramics, magnetic metals, and metal oxides, have achieved good microwave absorption properties, but excessive mass density, high filler loading, and poor stability are unfavorable for their practical application [3−5]. Fortunately, the discovery of two-dimensional (2D) MXenes has driven the prosperity of the EM absorption field because of their superior electrical properties, large specific surface area, and low density. Various MXene-based EM absorbents have been reported [6,7]. In particular, three-dimensional (3D) macroporous aerogels assembled from 2D MXenes show great promise for enhancing the microwave absorption capability because of the internal multireflecting and EM field-induced currents generated on the pore walls and/or struts [8]. Considering the unsatisfactory interfacial interactions of single MXenes, other nano-building blocks, such as zero-dimensional (0D), one-dimensional (1D), and 2D nanostructures, have been incorporated into MXene aerogels to further reinforce their comprehensive properties because of the synergistic merits of multiple components/scales [9]. Compared with low-dimensional nanostructures, the introduction of 2D structures with high surface areas to construct 2D/2D heterostructures can afford remarkable heterointerfaces for optimizing the dielectric polarization of macroscopic aerogels. For example, Li *et al*. [10] wrapped reduced graphene oxide (rGO) on the outer surface of a Ti3C2TX sphere-core, forming a unique heterostructure. The achieved rGO/MXene hybrid aerogel shows excellent microwave absorption with a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −20.0 dB and a wide EAB of 4.2 GHz in the X-band. Li *et al*. [11] integrated 2D graphene oxide and MXene to form an MXene@GO composite aerogel, delivering an RLmin of −49.1 dB at a filler loading of 10.0 wt%. Shu *et al*. [12] combined nitrogen-doped rGO with MXene and obtained a unique binary aerogel, achieving an RLmin of −62.1 dB and a wide EAB of 5.0 GHz at a filler content of 10 wt%. Examples of such studies have demonstrated that assembling graphene onto an MXene surface to construct 3D aerogels could achieve lightweight and high-performance microwave absorption because of the synergistic advantages of unique 2D/2D combinations and 3D porous connected structures. However, the disadvantages of the high cost and complex preparation process for pure graphene limit its commercial application [13]. As substitutes, 2D graphene-like carbon nanosheets (CNs) derived from renewable biomass are able to overcome this deficiency while preserving the advantages of graphene [14]. Hence, biomass-derived carbon nanosheet/MXene (CNMX) composite aerogels are another promising alternative for microwave absorption applications because of their cost, sustainability, large-scale production, and performance.

Recently, significant progress has been made in enhancing the microwave absorption performance of 3D macroporous MXene aerogels. The construction of an oriented and ordered 3D porous connected network could not only increase the loss efficiency for EM energies but also easily meet the impedance matching conditions [15]. This may pave the way for the exploitation of biomass-derived carbon nanosheet/MXene composite aerogels as ultralight, broadband, and high-performance microwave absorbers. In this study, we innovatively fabricated a sustainable MXene (Ti3C2T*x*)-based composite aerogel by introducing pomelo peel cellulose (PPC)-derived porous carbon nanosheets for efficient microwave absorption. The 2D CNs and MXenes are tightly combined and regularly arranged into oriented and ordered 3D cellular structures via a bidirectional freezing process. By regulating the content of PPC, the parallel layer spacing between nanosheets can be effectively tuned, thus affecting the complex permittivity and microwave absorption properties. At an ultralow filler content of 7 wt%, CNMX3 has a strong RL intensity of −48.5 dB and an EAB of 5.2 GHz. Moreover, CNMX4 also realizes a broad EAB of 5.8 GHz at a thickness of only 2.1 mm. The radar cross section (RCS) simulation results show that the RCS reduction value of CNMX4 aerogels can reach 33 dB·m2. This work provides a new perspective for designing low-cost MXene-based aerogels for lightweight and broadband microwave absorption.

### 2.1 Fabrication of PPC

First, a certain amount of dried pomelo peel powder was added to the mixed acetic acid (HAc) and H2O2 solution and stirred for 4 h at room temperature. Then, the suspension was poured into Teﬂon-lined stainless steel and heated at 120 °C for 3 h. Finally, the PPC was collected via centrifugation.

### 2.2 Fabrication of MXene Ti3C2T<i>x</i> nanosheets

Few-layer MXene was obtained by HF etching of Ti3AlC2. Typically, 3.2 g of LiF and HCl were mixed in a Teflon vessel and allowed to react at 40 °C for 15 min. Then, 2 g of Ti3AlC2 powder was slowly added to the above mixture with further stirring for 48 h. The exfoliation product was collected via centrifugation at 3500 r/min until the pH of the supernatant liquid was 6. The product was subsequently dispersed in 50 mL of deionized (DI) water in a two-necked flask and ultrasonicated for 3 h with Ar protection. The resultant product was centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 30 min. Finally, the upper dispersion was centrifuged at 8000 r/min to collect the few-layer MXene.

### 2.3 Preparation of CNMX aerogels

First, different amounts of PPC (150, 200, 250, 300 mg) and 150 mg of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) were dispersed into 20 mL of MXene solution with stirring for 48 h and ultrasonication for 1 h. Then, the dispersion was loaded into a tailor-made mold and placed on a copper bridge for bidirectional freezing, of which one side was in liquid nitrogen and the other side was in water, to induce a temperature gradient. There are two temperature gradients: one from liquid nitrogen to water (horizontal) and the other from copper to air (vertical). When the component assembly was finished, the PPC/MXene aerogel was annealed at 800 °C for 3 h at a ramp rate of 5 °C/min under Ar protection to obtain the final CNMX aerogel samples. To distinguish the samples, they are denoted as CNMX*s* (*s* = 1, 2, 3, 4), where *s* represents the different amounts of added PPC (150, 200, 250, 300 mg).

### 2.4 Characterization

The phase structure was identified through an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Bruker D8 ADVANCE). The morphology was observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Hitachi S4800) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2 F30 S-TWIN). The surface functional groups were detected via a Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer (FT-IR; Spectrum 1, Perkin-Elmer). A Raman spectrometer (Renishaw inVia) was used to detect the carbon state. An X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; PHI 5000 Versaprobe II) was used to identify the surface chemical state. The porous structure was measured via a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analyzer (Micromeritics, ASAP 2020). The complex permittivity and permeability were tested through a vector network analyzer (Agilent PNA N5244A). Prior to the test, molten paraffin was introduced into the internal structure of the CNMX aerogel. Following solidification, the obtained composite was shaped into a concentric ring with outer diameter (*Φ*out) of 7.00 mm and inner diameter (*Φ*in) of 3.04 mm. On the basis of the gravimetric method, the mass ratio of sample to wax is estimated to be ca. 7 : 93. To increase the reproducibility of the simulation results, additional EM modeling details have been incorporated. CST software was employed to perform (RCS and EM absorption simulations. The detailed simulation methods can be found in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1 shows a schematic diagram of the synthesis process of the CNMX aerogel. First, PPC derived from biomass pomelo peel and 2D MXene derived from Ti3AlC2 served as the structural units to construct aerogels. Owing to their similar 2D morphology and hydrogen-bond interactions (between –OH, –O on cellulose and –F, –OH on MXene), cellulose and MXene tend to stack together. Then, the uniform and stable PPC/MXene suspension was loaded on a copper bridge to induce spatially oriented assembly through the directional freezing method, as shown in Fig. S1 in the ESM. Under the action of a bidirectional temperature gradient in the vertical and horizontal directions, the cellulose/MXene nanosheets are well assembled into a parallel-aligned framework, forming a 3D oriented porous aerogel. Finally, the high-temperature treatment transforms cellulose to CN and strengthens the structural stability of the aerogel. Finally, a lightweight CNMX aerogel with a 3D ordered porous network structure is successfully fabricated.

The surface chemistry is key for constructing CNMX aerogels. The FT-IR spectra (Fig. 3(a)) show that the Ti3C2T*x* MXene has two apparent peaks at 1387 and 584 cm−1, corresponding to the surface terminal groups of C–F and –OH, respectively [18]. For PPC, the peaks at 1639 and 3465 cm−1 are attributed to the stretching vibrations of C=O and –OH, respectively. Three peaks at 663, 1095, and 2920 cm−1 are attributed to the vibration of C–H [19]. These peaks are well retained in the PPC/MXene aerogel, which is favorable for the good combination of PPC and MXene nanosheets via hydrogen bonding between surface functional groups. After high-temperature treatment, most of the peaks disappeared in CNMX (Fig. S2 in the ESM). The peaks centered at 1568, 852, and 833 cm−1 are attributed to the stretching vibration of C=O and the bending vibration of C–H in the benzene ring, indicating that cellulose has been converted to a graphitized carbon [20]. Moreover, the peak at 3423 cm−1 for CN redshifts to 3360 cm−1 for CNMX, and the peak at 620 cm−1 for MXene redshifts to 557 cm−1 for CNMX. The obvious redshifts should be attributed to the strong hydrogen bonding effect between CN and MXene [21,22]. The XRD pattern (Fig. 3(b)) shows that all the composites present three peaks at 9.0°, 26.0°, and 40.0°, corresponding to the MXene and graphitized carbon [23]. Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 3(c)) revealed that all the composites presented D-band and G-band characteristics typical of carbon materials. The D-band indicates disordered carbon, and the G-band indicates graphitized carbon. The value of *I*D/*I*G represents the graphitization degree of the carbon composition [24]. The *I*D/*I*G values of all the composites are similar, suggesting the parallel graphitization state of biomass-derived CN. The low value indicates that the carbon composition possesses a favorable degree of graphitization, which ensures considerable dielectric loss of CN [25]. An XPS survey (Fig. 3(d)) revealed that the CNMX aerogel consists of four elements, C, N, Ti, and O. The C 1s spectra (Fig. 3(e)) can be deconvoluted into four peaks at 283.9, 284.8, 286.0, and 288.7 eV, corresponding to C–O–Ti, C–C, C=O, and O–C=O, respectively [26]. The Ti 2p spectra (Fig. 3(f)) can be fitted into three peaks at 458.2, 461.6, and 464.4 eV, which are ascribed to Ti–O 2p3/2, Ti–C 2p1/2, and Ti–O 2p1/2, respectively [27]. These functional groups trigger dipolar polarization by interacting with EM waves. The nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm (Fig. S3 in the ESM) shows that the BET surface area of CNMX3 is approximately 37.25 m2∙g−1, and the pore size distribution curve reveals the existence of pores with an average pore size of approximately 17 nm. These abundant mesopores provide many solid–air interfaces for enhancing polarization loss.

The morphology and microstructure of the CNMX aerogel were observed through SEM and TEM, as shown in Fig. 4. All the aerogels exhibit a long-range oriented and ordered 3D porous cellular architecture and are composed of many parallelly aligned lamellae linked with perpendicular synapses (Figs. 4(a)–4(h) and Fig. S4 in the ESM). The fascinating framework could promote the multiple scattering and reflection of EM waves, extending their transmission paths and increasing absorption losses [28]. By changing the CN content in the CNMX aerogel, both the structural integrity and interval spacing of the aerogel could be easily regulated. When the PPC content increases from 150 to 300 mg, the interval spacing of the nanosheets gradually decreases from 71 to 29 μm (CNMX1: 71 μm, CNMX2: 50 μm, CNMX3: 36 μm, and CNMX4: 29 μm). This should be attributed to the increased PPC reinforcing the resistance of ice growth between adjacent nanosheets, leading to a decrease in internal spacing. As a result, CNMX4 possesses a denser 3D interconnected framework constructed by oriented porous cell walls than the other samples do. The magnified SEM (Fig. 4(i)) and TEM images (Fig. 4(j)) illustrate that CN is tightly combined with MXene rather than being dispersed separately. The high-resolution TEM image (Fig. 4(k)) shows a clear lattice fringe, and the interval spacing of 0.35 nm corresponds to the (101) lattice plane of the MXene [29]. Owing to the ordered 3D porous cellular architecture, the aerogel presents ultralight properties and can be easily placed on grass leaves (Fig. 4(l)). Table S1 in the ESM shows that the densities of all the CNMX aerogels are smaller than 0.04 g∙cm−3. Elemental mapping images (Fig. 4(m)) of the composite demonstrate that four elements (C, N, Ti, and O) are evenly distributed, suggesting a uniform distribution of CNs and MXenes.

To investigate the EM wave absorption of the aerogel, the complex permittivity (ɛ*r = *ɛ*′ *−* j*ɛ*″) and the complex permeability (*μ*r = *μ*′ − j*μ*″) were measured to evaluate the response behavior under the EM field. Herein, *ɛ*′ and *μ*′ are real parts, reflecting the ability to store EM energy; *ɛ*″ and *μ*″ denote imaginary parts, representing the ability to dissipate EM energy [30]. The loss tangents (tan*δ**ɛ* = *ɛ*″/*ɛ*′ and tan*δ**μ* = *μ*″/*μ*′) are employed to evaluate the dielectric and magnetic loss capability [31]. Since the CNMX aerogels are made up of pure dielectric components without any magnetic substance, the *μ*′ and *μ*″ values are close to 1 and 0, respectively (Fig. S5 in the ESM). Thus, the dielectric loss should play a dominant role in dissipating EM energy. As shown in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b), the values of *ɛ*′ and *ɛ*″ of the aerogels are in the range of 4–11 and 1.8–6, respectively, over 2–18 GHz, and tend to decrease because of their dispersion behavior [32]. Specifically, CNMX4 displays the highest *ɛ*′ values of 10.5–6.2 and *ɛ*″ values of 5.6–3.0, whereas CNMX1 displays the lowest *ɛ*′ values of 6.9–4.8 and *ɛ*″ values of 2.0–1.7. Both *ɛ*′ and *ɛ*″ gradually increase from CNMX1 to CNMX4, which can be attributed to the increased content of cellulose-derived CN within the aerogel. The high content of CN reduces the interlamellar spacing, resulting in a denser 3D interconnected framework for enhancing conductive loss and extending EM wave transmission paths [33]. The tan*δ**ɛ* values of aerogels (Fig. 5(c)) tend to be similar to those of the *ɛ**'* and *ɛ**''* curves, and the values range from 0.3 to 0.6, indicating good dielectric loss ability. The contributions of the polarization and conductive loss are quantified, as displayed in Figs. 5(d) and 5(e), through a nonlinear least square fitting method. The conductive loss is demonstrated to be primary at low frequencies, and the polarization loss is dominant in the high-frequency range. To analyze the dielectric relaxation behavior of the aerogels, Cole–Cole curves were plotted. As shown in Figs. 5(f)–5(i), the presence of multiple semicircles followed by a linear tail indicates that both polarization loss and conductive loss simultaneously contribute to dielectric dissipation. The distorted semicircles demonstrate that multiple relaxation processes, particularly dipolar polarization and interfacial polarization, occur within the aerogel. This relaxation behavior primarily originates from the abundant defects (carbon defects and N-doping) and polar functional groups (C=N and C–O) in the carbon framework, which significantly increase dipolar polarization loss [35]. In addition, the CN/MXene heterointerfaces promote the accumulation of charge carriers at the phase boundaries, resulting in pronounced interfacial polarization [36]. Meanwhile, the linear tail in the Cole–Cole curves is attributed to conductive loss. Notably, the gradual extension of this tail with increasing CN content suggests the enhancement of conductive pathways, which arises from the formation of a denser 3D interconnected network [37].

The EM absorption performance is calculated on the basis of the EM parameters, as shown in Fig. 6. The aerogels presented excellent absorption properties (Figs. 6(a)–6(d)). Specifically, CNMX3 shows superior performance, with strong RL intensities of −48.5 dB and 5.2 GHz for the EAB at a thickness of 2.5 mm. CNMX4 displays a broad EAB of 5.8 GHz when the thickness is 2.1 mm. CNMX1 and CNMX2 show weaker performance at a thickness of 2.5 mm because of their inferior dielectric loss ability. The performance is highly dependent on impedance matching and attenuation ability [38,39]. As shown in Figs. 6(e)–6(h), the contour map (*Z*in/*Z*0 > 0.8) of impedance matching is similar to the region of RL below −10 dB, indicating a close correlation between impedance matching and absorption performance [40]. The rational space structure and component design endows aerogels with excellent impedance matching, which explains their outstanding performance. Furthermore, the attenuation ability (Fig. 6(i)) shows the incremental order from CNMX1 to CNMX4. On the basis of good impedance matching, the high attenuation ability is beneficial for achieving strong intensity and wide EAB. Thus, CNMX3 and CNMX4 exhibit superior absorption performance [41]. Compared with recent studies on MXene-based microwave absorbers, the samples in this work display obvious advantages in terms of the RL intensity, EAB, and thinness (Fig. 6(j), Table S2 in the ESM).

As illustrated in Fig. 6(k), the microwave absorption mechanism of the CNMX aerogels arises from the synergistic contribution of multiple loss processes, including conductive loss, dipolar polarization, interfacial polarization, and structural multiple scattering. The 3D interconnected CN/MXene network provides continuous pathways for electron transport, which enhances ohmic dissipation and contributes substantially to conductive loss. The abundant polar functional groups and defects on both CNs and MXenes (such as C=N, C–O, and Ti–O) can act as dipole centers that respond to alternating EM fields, resulting in pronounced dipolar polarization. Moreover, the extended 2D/2D heterointerfaces between the CN and MXene facilitate charge accumulation at the boundaries, resulting in strong interfacial polarization. In addition, the oriented cellular architecture promotes repeated internal multiple reflections and scattering of EM waves, effectively prolonging the propagation path and increasing attenuation within the aerogel. The regulation of interlamellar spacing improves impedance matching, which enables incident EM waves to more effectively penetrate the materials rather than reflect at the surface. Collectively, the synergistic effect of strong conductive loss, dipolar polarization, interfacial polarization, and superior impedance matching endows CNMX4 with the best overall microwave absorption performance among the aerogel samples.

To evaluate the far-field EM stealth feature of aerogels, a RCS simulation model is established via the CST Microwave Studio platform [42]. In the simulation, a perfect electric conductor (PEC, 100 mm × 100 mm × 0.5 mm) is employed as the substrate to ensure total reflection of the incident microwaves. The aerogels are uniformly coated on the PEC substrate as an absorption layer with a fixed thickness of 2.0 mm. The scattering characteristics are systematically analyzed across −90° to 90° at 15.12 GHz (Fig. 7(a)). As illustrated in Figs. 7(b)–7(f), the aerogel coatings effectively suppressed the scattering signal compared with the bare PEC plate, among which CNMX4 exhibited the best attenuation capability. Its maximum RCS reduction reaches 33 dB·m2 (Fig. 7(g)), which is greater than that of the other samples (CNMX1: 7.52 dB·m2, CNMX2: 12.41 dB·m2, CNMX3: 21.17 dB·m2) (Fig. S6 in the ESM). Moreover, CNMX4 maintains remarkable RCS suppression over a wide angular range of 0°–90° (Fig. 7(h)), providing a theoretical foundation for its application in complex far-field EM environments [43,44].

To elucidate the dissipation mechanism, the energy loss distributions are simulated, as shown in Fig. 7(i) and Fig. S7 in the ESM. The RCS simulation results further reveal frequency-dependent differences in energy dissipation behavior. At high frequencies (15.12 and 18 GHz), CNMX4 exhibits strongly surface-confined energy dissipation, indicating that most of the incident EM waves are absorbed at the coating interface [45]. In contrast, at the lower frequency of 2 GHz, the energy dissipation becomes broadly distributed throughout the entire bulk region. Compared with other aerogels, CNMX4 consistently demonstrated the highest degree of surface-localized energy loss across the examined frequencies, confirming its superior microwave absorption performance [46]. Furthermore, the impedance matching properties of aerogels are assessed via Smith charts, which can be used to estimate whether the absorber achieves impedance matching with free space (chart center) through observing the impedance trajectory [47]. As shown in Figs. 7(j)–7(m), the impedance trajectory of CNMX4 is closest to the free-space center, indicating excellent impedance matching. The simulation reveals the differences in far-field EM stealth behavior and highlights CNMX4 as the most effective microwave absorber, providing solid theoretical support for its potential application in advanced EM shielding and stealth technologies.

In addition, we further investigated the influence of heat treatment temperature (600, 700, and 900 °C) on the structure and microwave absorption performance of the aerogels. Taking the representative CNMX3 as an example, the samples treated at different temperatures were denoted as CNMX3-600, CNMX3-700, and CNMX3-900. As shown in Figs. 8(a) and 8(b), the aerogels treated at 600 and 700 °C maintain a well-defined 3D oriented, interconnected network, in which the lamellae are aligned in parallel and interconnected by vertical bridges to form a robust cellular framework. However, when the pyrolysis temperature increased to 900 °C, the ordered architecture significantly collapsed (Fig. 8(c)), indicating that excessive heat treatment severely disrupted the structural integrity. The XRD patterns (Fig. 8(d)) reveal that all samples retain the characteristic peaks of the MXene and carbon components, whereas new TiO2 peaks appear in the CNMX3 sample, suggesting partial oxidation of the MXene at this higher temperature [48]. The degree of oxidation was characterized by XPS (Figs. S8 and S9 in the ESM), and the corresponding analyses of the Ti 2p XPS peak fitting data are given in the ESM. The Raman spectra (Fig. 8(e)) indicate a slight increase in the degree of graphitization with increasing pyrolysis temperature. Changes in graphitization and microstructure affect the dielectric behavior. Both the ε*′ and *ε*″ values of CNMX3-600 and CNMX3-700 are lower than those of CNMX3 (Figs. 8(f) and 8(g)). This may be due to the lower graphitization degree of CNs and greater number of defects in MXenes. However, CNMX3-900, with a higher degree of graphitization, has lower *ε*′ and *ε*″ values than does CNMX3. The abnormal decrease in the complex permittivity may be due to its broken connected framework structure, which hinders the induced current transmission and weakens the conductive loss and dielectric loss. Thus, the absorption performances of three samples (CNMX3-600, CNMX3-700, and CNMX3-900) are inferior to that of CNMX3 (Figs. 8(h)–8(k)). Overall, 800 °C represents the optimal pyrolysis temperature for achieving a balanced combination of structural integrity, appropriate graphitization degree, well-developed conductive pathways, and efficient dielectric loss, thereby enabling the CNMX aerogels to exhibit superior microwave absorption capability.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, we developed an ultralight biomass-derived carbon nanosheet/MXene composite aerogel with a 3D ordered connected network for microwave absorption via an ice template method. The bidirectional freezing process results in the extrusion of freely dispersed 2D CN/MXene hybrid nanosheets, which form regularly ordered cellular structures. As the CN content increases, the interlamellar spacing gradually decreases, thereby optimizing the 3D interconnected architecture. Owing to the effective components and ordered spatial structure, the aerogels present excellent microwave absorption performance with a strong RL intensity of −48.5 dB and a broad EAB of 5.2 GHz at a thickness of 2.5 mm at a filling ratio of 7 wt%. Precise regulation of the pyrolyzing temperature proved that the 3D ordered cellular network is critical for enhancing the conductive loss network and achieving satisfactory microwave absorption performance. RCS simulations validated the outstanding RCS reduction, revealing the promising application of the CNMX aerogel in advanced EM absorption and stealth technologies.
