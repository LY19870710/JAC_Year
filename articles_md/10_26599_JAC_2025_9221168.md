## 1 Introduction

Electromagnetic wave-absorbing (EMA) materials are experiencing rapid transformative innovations, driven by their dual pivotal roles in military stealth technologies and civilian electromagnetic wave (EM) radiation shielding applications [1]. Doping engineering has emerged as a prominent modification strategy for EMA materials due to its ability to tailor the atomic coordination structure and intrinsic physical properties of materials. The atomic doping strategy has played a pivotal role in enhancing the EMA performance of various materials, including carbon-based materials [2], metal oxides [3], and metal sulfides [4]. Owing to the vast combinatorial possibilities of elemental composition and doping ratio, doping engineering presents a theoretically infinite design space for tailoring the intrinsic properties and EMA characteristics of materials. However, elucidating the underlying mechanisms connecting diverse doping atomic configurations, complex doping processes, and interatomic interactions with electromagnetic properties and microwave dissipation capabilities remains a critical scientific challenge.

Heteroatom doping has been proven to be a promising and efficient technique that can be utilized to activate the inert in-plane domains of MoS2 and produce charge transfer, modulating the properties of MoS2 [5]. The characteristic two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals layered structure of MoS2 provides an exceptional platform for heteroatom doping. Compared with those of bulk materials, the weak van der Waals interactions between MoS2 nanosheets facilitate access to internal atomic planes, significantly shortening dopant diffusion pathways and lowering incorporation energy barriers. This effect is particularly pronounced in metallic-phase MoS2 (1T-MoS2) because of its expanded interlayer spacing [6]. Furthermore, the substantially enlarged specific surface area enables MoS2 nanosheets to offer abundant active sites, such as vacancies and edge defects, which facilitate efficient atomic doping. This unique architecture facilitates uniform dopant distribution while effectively preventing phase segregation.

Previous work has confirmed that atomic doping enables precise regulation over charge transfer, bandgap tuning, and electrical and dielectric properties in MoS2 [7], leading to remarkable improvements in EM attenuation. To date, MoS2 doping research has covered a wide range of systems, including single-element cationic (e.g., W, Co) [8,9] and anionic (e.g., N, Se) [10−12] dopants, as well as codoping configurations [13]. However, the emerging and promising high-entropy doping (HED) strategy [14], which leverages multiple but not limited to near-stoichiometric ratio element doping to achieve synergistic effects, remains unexplored in transition metal dichalcogenide (TMD)-based EMA materials, despite its demonstrated success in energy applications [15,16]. Multicomponent doping introduces synergistic effects, such as localized charge redistribution, pronounced lattice strain, and defect generation, which collectively increase EM dissipation. In particular, high-entropy doping leverages the “cocktail effect”, where the complex interplay of multiple principal elements creates unique electronic and structural configurations [14]. These configurations exhibit significantly improved EMA properties compared with those of conventional single-element or low-entropy doped TMDs owing to their tailored dielectric loss mechanisms and optimized impedance matching.

Despite its potential, the advancement of the HED strategy in TMD-based absorbers is critically impeded by the absence of robust doping protocols. Traditional hydrothermal approaches struggle to achieve effective multi-element integration, while side reactions such as dopant hydrolysis further degrade the doping efficiency. Moreover, these methods often induce undesirable structural changes in the host material [17], exacerbating performance inconsistencies.

Herein, we present tailored solvothermal doping techniques for TMDs in both aqueous and nonaqueous systems. By integrating modular *in situ* doping with post treatment steps, we systematically transitioned from single-element configurations to medium- and HED configurations. This optimized approach ensured the doping efficacy while preserving the delicate phase balance in the MoS2 matrix. The incorporation of multiple dopants introduced pronounced lattice distortions, charge interactions, and redistribution, leading to a dramatic improvement in dipole polarization losses. The effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) was expanded by more than twofold, demonstrating the promise of this HED strategy for developing high-performance microwave absorbers.

### 2.1 Materials

Ammonium molybdate tetrahydrate (H24Mo7N6O24·4H2O), thiourea (CH4N2S), ammonium metatungstate ((NH4)6H2W12O40·*x*H2O), ammonium metavanadate (NH4VO3), niobium chloride (NbCl5), tantalum chloride (TaCl5), ruthenium chloride hydrate (RuCl3·*x*H2O), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), and ethyl alcohol were purchased from Aladdin, Inc. (China). All chemicals were of analytical grade (AR) and were used without further purification. The deionized water (DI water) used in the experiment was homemade in the laboratory.

### 2.2 Preparation of pristine-MoS<sub>2</sub>

Pristine-MoS2 with a high 1T phase fraction was obtained through a modification of a published procedure [18]. Typically, 2.119 g H24Mo7N6O24·4H2O and 4.567 g CH4N2S were fully dissolved in 60 mL of DI water and magnetically stirred for 20 min. During this process, the color of the solution changed from colorless to light blue and transparent. The solution was then transferred to a stainless steel hydrothermal reaction still with a 100 mL polytetrafluoroethylene inner liner and heated to 200 °C for 24 h. Once the system had cooled to ambient temperature, the resulting black powder was centrifuged and thoroughly rinsed three times each with both DI water and ethanol. Following the final ethanol washing step, the powder was air-dried at room temperature for 12 h, yielding pristine-MoS2.

### 2.3 <i>In situ</i> doping process for W/VMoS<sub>2</sub>

During the aforementioned doping process, a certain amount of (NH4)6H2W12O40·*x*H2O was added after the solution changed to light blue and transparent while keeping all other procedures unchanged. W-MoS2 was obtained through an *in situ* doping process.

V-MoS2 was obtained through a similar process, except that (NH4)6H2W12O40·*x*H2O was replaced by a certain amount of NH4VO3.

WV-MoS2 was obtained through a similar process, except that NH4VO3 and (NH4)6H2W12O40·*x*H2O were added to the light blue solution in sequence.

### 2.4 Post-doping process for Nb/Ta-MoS<sub>2</sub>

Pristine-MoS2 was redispersed in 60 mL of DMF and sonicated for 15 min, accompanied by magnetic stirring for 30 min. After the MoS2 powder was uniformly dispersed, a certain amount of NbCl5 was added, and the mixture was stirred for 20 min. The mixture was transferred to a 100-mL solvothermal reaction vessel with a polytetrafluoroethylene inner lining and heated to 140 °C for 2 h. Once the system had cooled to ambient temperature, the resulting black powder was centrifuged and thoroughly rinsed three times each with both DI water and ethanol. Following the final ethanol washing step, the powder was air-dried at room temperature for 12 h and collected as Nb-MoS2.

Ta-MoS2 was obtained through a similar process, except that NbCl5 was replaced by a certain amount of TaCl5.

NbTa-MoS2 was obtained through a similar process, except that NbCl5 and TaCl5 were added into the uniform dispersion in sequence.

### 2.5 <i>In situ</i>/post-doping process for WNbTa/WVNb/WVNbTaRu-MoS<sub>2</sub>

W-MoS2 was redispersed in 60 mL of DMF and sonicated for 15 min, followed by magnetic stirring for 30 min. After the MoS2 powder was uniformly dispersed, a certain amount of NbCl5 or TaCl5 was added, and the mixture was stirred for 20 min. The mixture was transferred to a 100-mL solvothermal reaction vessel with a polytetrafluoroethylene inner lining and heated to 140 °C for 2 h. Once the system had cooled to ambient temperature, the resulting black powder was centrifuged and thoroughly rinsed three times each with both DI water and ethanol. Following the final ethanol washing step, the powder was air-dried at room temperature for 12 h and collected as WNbTa-MoS2.

WVNb-MoS2 was obtained through a similar process, except that the Nb post-doping process was employed on the already obtained *in situ* doped WV-MoS2.

WVNbTaRu-MoS2 was obtained through a similar process, except that the NbTaRu co-post-doping process was employed on the already obtained *in situ* doped WV-MoS2.

### 2.6 Characterization

The morphology and element mapping of the samples were obtained by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; ZEISS Merlin Compact, Zeiss, Germany). The lattice structure and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern were observed via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200X, Thermo Scientific, USA). High-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) images of the lattice structure were obtained on an aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscope (JEM ARM 200F, JEOL, Japan) equipped with a cold field emission gun operated at 200 kV. The phase compositions of the samples were obtained via a powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Empyrean, Malvern Panalytical, UK) with Cu Kα radiation. The stage information of the elements in all the samples was obtained via a X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher, USA). The electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra were recorded from a Bruker A300-10/12 spectrometer (Germany). The specific surface area and pore size information of the samples were obtained via a BSD-PS1/2 Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis meter (Best Instrument, China).

## 3 Results and discussion

Our previous studies demonstrated the facile one-step hydrothermal synthesis of 1T-phase dominated MoS2 microflowers [18]. Building upon this foundation, we have developed a modular *in situ*/post-doping integration strategy to achieve HED engineering in MoS2. A schematic illustration of this process is presented in Fig. 1(a). This innovative approach combines atomic-level precision with scalable synthesis, enabling the controlled incorporation of multiple dopant species while maintaining phase structural integrity. The modular design permits independent optimization of each doping step, offering unprecedented flexibility in tailoring the electronic and dielectric properties of the resulting materials. Notably, the inherent metastability of the 1T phase necessitates careful doping design to avoid phase ratio distortions, which is particularly significant for doping engineering in TMDs with multiphases [19]. Furthermore, many commercial transition metal dopants are prone to hydrolysis (e.g., NbCl5), which hinders further improvement of the doping efficiency, as shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Therefore, we classified the common dopants based on their aqueous properties and adopted different doping strategies for each category. For W and V, water-soluble ammonium metatungstate and ammonium metavanadate were used for *in situ* doping, which was chosen because of their structural and property similarities to those of Mo precursors. As shown in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b), the *in situ* doping of W and V preserved the phase structure owing to their ammonium molybdate-like coordination chemistry. In contrast, for Nb, Ta, and Ru, which lack commercially available water-soluble analogs, a post-doping solvothermal approach was developed. Here, the polar aprotic solvent DMF was selected to disperse MoS2 and dissolve MCl*x* (M = Nb, Ta, Ru) dopants. An optimal solvothermal temperature of 140 °C could effectively maintain the pristine MoS2 phase structure, which was empirically determined, as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. As illustrated in Fig. 2(a), this phase-preserving post-doping strategy is pivotal for HED engineering, as even minor phase fluctuations can significantly impact the functional properties of MoS2. By integrating the aforementioned *in situ* and post-doping pathways, we constructed a modular doping platform for MoS2, which spans low-entropy (i.e., single/double element) doping, medium-entropy (i.e., triple element) doping, and HED. By systematically exploring combinations of the five commercially available transition metal (TM) dopants in this work, we identified 31 rationally designed multi-element doping configurations for MoS2 (Table S1 in the ESM), with 9 representative doped variants successfully synthesized and characterized. Extending this combinatorial approach to include 10 commonly used TM elements (specifically those amenable to forming 2D TMDs, as listed in Table S2 in the ESM), the potential doped MoS2 library expands exponentially to 1023 possible configurations. This dramatic scaling effect vividly illustrates the vast and largely untapped exploration space enabled by HED strategies.

EDS mapping and XRD patterns revealed that this modular approach preserves the 1T-phase inheritance while accommodating multiple dopants and avoiding element segregation (Figs. 1(b) and 2(a)). As illustrated in the magnified XRD pattern (Fig. 2(b)), the (002) diffraction peak of 1T-MoS2, located at approximately 9.4°, shifts systematically upon doping. As clearly demonstrated in Fig. 2(c), V doping induces a pronounced contraction of the (002) interplanar spacing, while Nb and Ta doping leads to significant expansion. These observations are in perfect agreement with the atomic radii of the dopants, where the size relationship follows the order V (122 pm) Fig. 2(d), the high-resolution W 4f spectrum could be deconvoluted into distinct peaks W 4f5/2 at 38.6 eV and W 4f7/2 at 36.5 eV [20]. Comparative analysis of the W 4f signals from the doped samples and the noise signal from the pristine sample revealed two key findings: (1) Both single and dual element *in-situ* doping ensured effective W substitution, and (2) subsequent post-doping did not selectively displace pre-incorporated W but instead multi-element compatibly replaced the host Mo atoms. The mutual compatibility of multiple elemental dopants, coupled with the inherent independence of doping procedures, enables seamless modular integration between *in situ* and post-doping processes. This synergistic approach preserves exceptional design flexibility while maintaining precise control over material properties. Remarkably, this modular *in-situ*/post-doping strategy exhibited similar compatibility and universality for V, Nb, and Ta, as evidenced by their respective high-resolution spectra. Similarly, the Nb 3d spectrum exhibited peaks at 210.8 eV (Nb 3d3/2) and 208.1 eV (Nb 3d5/2) [21], while the Ta 4f spectrum displayed peaks at 28.8 eV (Ta 4f5/2) and 26.9 eV (Ta 4f7/2) [22] (Figs. 2(f) and 2(g)). Notably, the high-resolution V 2p spectrum could be deconvoluted into four distinct peaks as V(III) 2p3/2 at 514.9 eV, V(IV) 2p3/2 at 515.8 eV, V(III) 2p1/2 at 522.1 eV, and V(IV) 2p1/2 at 523.3 eV [23,24], as illustrated in Fig. 2(e). This polyvalent characteristic demonstrates that V doping could result in more complex charge transfer, which is crucial for greater polarization loss.

Typically, the Mo 3d spectrum of the host matrix was deconvoluted into four components: semiconductor-phase (2H)-Mo 3d3/2, 1T-Mo 3d3/2, 2H-Mo 3d5/2, 1T-Mo 3d5/2, and two interference signals, Mo6+ and S 2s [25] (Fig. 2(h) and Fig. S3 in the ESM). Through the decomposition and integration of the Mo 3d signal, the phase ratios of 1T-MoS2 and 2H-MoS2 in each sample were quantitatively determined and are summarized in Fig. 2(j) and Table S3 in the ESM. Despite minor experimental variations, the 1T-phase fraction remained well preserved during doping, which is consistent with the XRD results in Fig. 2(a). Combined with the morphological consistency confirmed by BET measurements (Figs. S4 and S5 in the ESM) and SEM observations (Fig. 1(b)), these findings enabled us to focus exclusively on the effects of atomic substitution on electronic properties, dipole polarization loss, and EMA performance, minimizing interference from phase ratios, morphology, and interfaces.

The high-resolution S 2p XPS spectra could be deconvoluted into 2H-S 2p1/2, 1T-S 2p1/2, 2H-S 2p3/2, and 1T-S 2p3/2 signals (Fig. 2(i) and Fig. S6 in the ESM), whose positions slightly shifted depending on the chemical environment and charge transfer effects [26]. Compared with that of pristine-MoS2, the 1T-S 2p3/2 peak shifted ~0.1 eV toward a higher binding energy, whereas the 2H-S 2p1/2 peak exhibited a more pronounced shift of ~0.46 eV, indicating stronger charge transfer and localized charge redistribution induced by V doping. This is consistent with the polyvalent characteristics of the V 2p XPS spectrum in Fig. 2(e). The electron loss behavior around TM atoms during the bonding process with S atoms in doped MoS2 was investigated through DFT calculations, as illustrated in Fig. 2(m). Notably, compared with the uniform electron loss distribution around Mo atoms in the host matrix, the isosurfaces around dopant atoms exhibit significant variations. In particular, the electron depletion cloud surrounding V atoms is the smallest, indicating a pronounced disruption of the pristine charge balance and localized charge redistribution. This localized charge redistribution introduces additional dipole polarization loss, which manifests as an enhanced dielectric constant and stronger dielectric loss (tan*δ**ɛ*) in the V-MoS2 permittivity curves (Fig. 2(k)). Notably, this strong polarization effect was preserved in the WVNb-MoS2 sample, as evidenced by similar 2H-S 2p1/2 peak shifts and increased dielectric parameters and dielectric loss (Figs. 2(i) and 2(k)). By fitting the dielectric constant curves with a modified Debye polarization relaxation model [27], we deconvoluted the imaginary part of the permittivity into contributions from conduction loss (*ε*c″) and polarization loss (*ε*p″) (Fig. 2(l)). The results clearly demonstrate that the strong charge interactions induced by doping significantly enhance polarization loss, which has been proven to markedly improve EMA performance through optimized impedance matching and enhanced attenuation capacity [28]. Multi-element doping further diversified bonding configurations and charge transfer dynamics, altering the polarization relaxation strength and frequency, as shown by the Cole–Cole circles in Fig. 2(n). This charge transfer–polarization loss correlation profoundly influenced the EMA properties. As shown in Fig. 2(o), under identical 2-mm thickness, the loss peaks of low- and medium-entropy doped samples intensified progressively and exhibited a trend toward lower frequencies, which demonstrates the effectiveness of the doping strategy in enhancing the EMA performance. Although the EMA performance of the doped samples has not yet reached the ideal level, the significant improvements in polarization loss and EMA properties observed in low- and medium-entropy doped systems have inspired us to explore more sophisticated HED strategies. These preliminary results demonstrate that carefully designed multi-element doping can effectively modulate dielectric polarization behavior, providing a promising pathway for achieving superior EMA performance through doping element complexity engineering.

In addition to inducing additional charge transfer, unique atomic-scale doping also generates localized lattice strain concentration regions, leading to charge redistribution at a larger scale than the atomic level. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to characterize the crystal structure of the doped MoS2. Figures 3(a) and 3(b) reveal the preserved nanosheet self-assembly morphology of WVNbTaRu-MoS2, which is consistent with the SEM observations in Fig. 1(b). The SAED pattern in Fig. 3(c) shows only polycrystalline MoS2 diffraction rings, confirming the absence of impurity phases, which aligns with the XRD results. In the 2H phase of MoS2, owing to the overlapping enhancement of electron signals from the aligned S atomic planes along the *z*-axis, the high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image (Fig. 3(h)) displays discernible S atomic contrast despite their significantly lower atomic number than Mo does, resulting in a honeycomb structure [29]. Conversely, in the 1T phase, the absence of such signal enhancement renders S atoms nearly invisible, leaving only isolated triangular patterns of Mo atomic columns, as depicted in Fig. 3(g). Leveraging the distinct contrast characteristics observed in the HRTEM images, we successfully differentiate between the 1T and 2H phases of MoS2, as clearly demonstrated in Figs. 3(d1)–3(f1). At the phase interfaces, electrons preferentially transfer from the metallic 1T phase to the semiconducting 2H phase, establishing well-defined interfacial charge polarization [18]. This phenomenon significantly enhances the material’s EM polarization loss capability, providing an effective mechanism for improved EMA performance. The filtered HRTEM image of pristine-MoS2 in Fig. 3(d1) shows a perfect 1T-MoS2 lattice, corroborating the XRD findings. Geometric phase analysis (GPA) of the HRTEM images was performed to map the strain fields in the *xx* and *yy* directions. While pristine-MoS2 exhibits a uniform lattice fringe distribution with no obvious lattice strain, medium-entropy-doped WVNb-MoS2 shows pronounced strain concentration regions, particularly at phase boundaries and lattice distortion zones, as shown in Figs. 3(d1)–3(e3). Lattice strain enhancement was also observed in the low-entropy doped samples (Figs. S7 and S8 in the ESM), which is consistent with the shift in the diffraction positions observed in the enlarged XRD image in Fig. 2(c). This effect is further amplified in HED samples, where denser strain concentration regions emerge alongside increased lattice distortions and discontinuous lattice fringes (Figs. 3(f) and 3(j)). These strain and distortion effects, arising from multi-element substitution, create diverse electronic carrier transport pathways, thereby contributing to the dielectric dissipation of the EM [30].

Through the integration of multi-element *in-situ* doping and post doping processes, five-element co-doped MoS2 nanosheets were obtained. For the HED WVNbTaRu-MoS2 sample, nanoscale elemental mapping via HAADF-STEM and corresponding EDS analysis further confirmed the effectiveness and homogeneity of HED engineering, as shown in Fig. 3(i). Remarkably, the HED system results in not only increased lattice strain but also characteristic structural defects, including well-defined grain boundaries, lattice distortions, atomic vacancies, and discontinuous lattice fringes (Fig. 3(j)). These structural defects disrupt the charge distribution in pristine lattices, triggering charge redistribution and aggregation to form various EM dissipation centers [31]. Moreover, these crystal defects, particularly significant lattice strain, increased the number of grain boundaries and discontinuous lattice fringe regions, weakening the structural integrity of the pristine crystal lattice. This facilitates the escape of additional anions, leading to increased formation of S vacancies [32]. The enhanced electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) signal at *g* = 2.003 provides clear evidence for this phenomenon (Fig. S9 in the ESM). The higher concentration of S vacancies further disrupts the local charge distribution within the system, promoting the generation of more defect dipoles. These dipoles significantly enhance polarization loss, which has been proven to be more beneficial for impedance matching and broadband EMA performance than excessive conduction loss. The aberration-corrected (AC)-HAADF-STEM image of WVNbTaRu-MoS2 in Fig. 3(k) predominantly shows the 1T-phase atomic configuration, with certain atoms showing significantly higher contrast than the others. Corresponding to representative dopant configuration regions (Fig. 3(m) and Fig. S10 in the ESM), we constructed a partial atomic substitution model of doped MoS2 (Fig. 3(l)) and simulated its HAADF-STEM image (Fig. 3(n)). The excellent agreement between the experimental and simulated intensity profiles along the marked paths in Fig. 3(n) validates the effectiveness of atomic-scale doping.

Additional information regarding the doping elements can be obtained from the XPS spectra of the HED sample (Figs. 4(a)–4(f)). In addition to the characteristic Mo 3d signals from the host matrix, distinct peaks corresponding to W, V, Nb, Ta, and Ru are clearly observed, further confirming the effectiveness of the modular atomic-level doping strategy. Figure 4(g) and Table S4 in the ESM present the normalized contents of metallic elements in various low-, medium-, and high-entropy-doped MoS2 systems. Notably, the dopant concentration in the co-doped systems is consistently lower than that in the corresponding single-element doped samples, as evidenced by the comparative analysis of W-MoS2, WV-MoS2, and WVNb-MoS2 samples. This phenomenon might arise from atomic competition effects during the multi-element *in-situ*/post-doping processes. Fortunately, such competitive substitution does not lead to a significant deterioration in doping efficiency. The multi-element atomic doping introduces diverse charge transfer and redistribution mechanisms, as illustrated by the charge density difference maps of the medium-entropy and HED models (Figs. 4(h) and 4(i) and Figs. S11 and S12 in the ESM). These maps reveal that dopant sites serve as localized charge accumulation centers, inducing more doped electric dipole moments, which repeatedly rotate under the action of the alternating electric field and consume more energy. The HED strategy introduces a high density of heteroatoms, which interconnect the originally isolated dopant dipoles. This leads to significant overlap of electron clouds, resulting in the formation of larger-scale dipole systems [33]. These interconnected dipoles exhibit enhanced collective polarization behavior, fundamentally altering the charge distribution landscape in the host material and creating new pathways for charge transfer and dielectric response modulation.

The HED strategy provides a valuable pathway for tailoring electrical properties and dielectric constants, as evidenced by several key observations: (1) V doping consistently enhances the dielectric constant, as demonstrated by comparative dielectric performance analyses between the pristine-MoS2/V-MoS2 and W-MoS2/WV-MoS2 systems (Fig. 4(k)). (2) Multi-element co-doping induces synergistic cocktail effects that cannot be achieved through the simple superposition of single-element doping effects. For example, NbTa-MoS2 exhibits an anomalous increase in the dielectric constant, although individual Nb or Ta doping slightly reduces the dielectric loss capability. This phenomenon is further corroborated by comparisons of the real and imaginary components of the permittivity in W-MoS2/WNbTa-MoS2 systems. The doping-induced increase in attenuation capability is crucial for improving EMA performance. Empirical analysis indicates that for an absorber to achieve effective absorption (reflection loss (RL) the) of 105.9 [34]. As shown in Fig. 4(l), pristine-MoS2 meets this threshold only over a narrow frequency range, while the medium-entropy and HED samples demonstrate significantly improved attenuation capabilities, with more parts exceeding the threshold. (3) Although the attenuation capability is markedly enhanced, not all doped samples achieve ideal absorption performance, primarily due to excessively enhanced conductivity, which is particularly evident in WNbTa-MoS2 (Fig. 4(m)). Although highly conductive materials can efficiently convert EM energy into Joule heat, they simultaneously generate opposing induced currents that enhance surface reflection and compromise impedance matching characteristics [35]. (4) The HED strategy circumvents excessive conductivity enhancement through balanced coordination of donor (W, Ru) [36] and acceptor (V, Nb, Ta) [37−39] doping elements, thereby preventing severe impedance mismatch and paving the way for broader absorption bandwidth. As shown in Fig. 4(j), the density of states (DOS) comparison among pristine/NbTa/WVNBTaRu-MoS2 reveals certain differences. Compared with pristine-MoS2, NbTa-MoS2 results in a remarkable increase in the DOS near the Fermi level, leading to substantially increased electrical conductivity. In contrast, WVNBTaRu-MoS2 shows a slight decrease in the DOS at the Fermi level, which can be attributed to electron/hole recombination and strong electron scattering caused by its high defect density. This controlled reduction in the DOS prevents excessive conductivity, thereby minimizing undesirable EM reflection and exploring favorable microwave absorption performance. Furthermore, the HED strategy introduces higher densities of defect dipoles, inducing stronger polarization losses that compensate for the reduction in conduction losses caused by moderate conductivity (Fig. 4(n)). Through this coordinated balance between polarization and conduction losses, the system maintains the necessary attenuation capability while effectively optimizing impedance characteristics, enabling a greater portion of the frequency to meet effective absorption requirements (Fig. 4(o)).

Owing to the enhanced loss capability and optimized impedance matching discussed above, the HED strategy has remarkable advantages in tailoring the EMA performance of TMD materials. Figure 5(a) illustrates the regulatory effects of cation substitution doping on the absorption performance. Single-element-doped MoS2 systems generally fail to substantially broaden the EAB, except for V-MoS2, which enhances charge transfer and dielectric loss. In contrast, multi-element co-doping systems exhibit a distinct advantage in broadband absorption. Specifically, compared with the 3.21 GHz EAB of pristine-MoS2, WV-MoS2 achieves an EAB of 3.81 GHz at 2.65 mm, NbTa-MoS2 reaches 5.24 GHz at 1.66 mm, WNbTa-MoS2 reaches 5.41 GHz at 1.64 mm, and WVNb-MoS2 extends the absorption bandwidth to 5.84 GHz at 1.88 mm. Remarkably, the HED-engineered MoS2 sample achieves a broadband EAB of 7.65 GHz at a thickness of 2.09 mm, which is more than double that of pristine-MoS2. The absorption performance metrics of all the samples are summarized in Fig. 5(e), clearly demonstrating that doping engineering is an effective approach for modulating the microwave absorption properties. The multi-element doping systems outperform their single-element counterparts in terms of bandwidth expansion. The HED strategy results in exceptional broadband absorption performance, establishing it as the leading candidate among doped TMDs for advanced EM attenuation applications.

To comprehensively evaluate the radar stealth performance of HED EMA materials under far-field conditions representative of practical applications, we performed systematic simulations of radar cross-sections (RCSs) for various doped samples via the CST Microwave Studio. A standard metal-backed model was established, wherein the EMA materials were applied as coatings on a 200 mm × 200 mm perfect electric conductor (PEC) plate to assess their RCS reduction capabilities [40] (Fig. 5(c)). The 3D RCS patterns of all the samples (Fig. 5(b)) exhibit periodic angular symmetry, with significant suppression of the front surface RCS signal against the back PEC reflection signals, demonstrating the superior radar stealth performance of the doped MoS2 materials. In particular, the WVNbTaRu-MoS2 sample achieves nearly undetectable RCS levels at 10.1 GHz. For a clearer cross-comparison, Fig. 5(d) presents the RCS data in 1D polar format. The HED samples exhibit substantially lower RCS values, with a remarkable RCS reduction of 35 dBm2 at normal incidence. The success in reducing RCS established these materials as exceptionally effective for radar stealth applications.

On the basis of the above analysis, the EMA mechanism of MoS2-based materials is systematically summarized in Fig. 5(f). First, the self-assembled 1T-phase MoS2 host matrix provides a favorable platform for conduction loss and multiple reflections, while the introduction of additional charge carriers through doping further optimizes this loss mechanism. This approach ensures sufficient EM dissipation while preventing excessive conductivity, which could lead to impedance mismatch. Second, the inherent polymorphic nature of MoS2 gives rise to an abundant 1T–2H phase interface within the system. These interfaces play crucial roles in generating significant interfacial polarization loss, which effectively dissipates EM energy through rapid charge redistribution and dipole relaxation processes. Third, TM atom substitution doping significantly disrupts the balanced charge distribution in the pristine MoS2 structure, inducing charge transfer and localized charge redistribution, which generates additional dipole polarization loss. Finally, HED engineering introduces a greater density of lattice strains, distortions, and vacancies, which amplify the charge imbalance within the system, thereby significantly increasing the dielectric loss for EM energy.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a modular *in situ*/post solvothermal doping strategy to achieve HED engineering in a 1T-MoS2 host framework. By carefully optimizing the synergistic combination of *in situ* and post-doping processes, we successfully preserved the intrinsic 1T–2H phase ratio of MoS2, thereby minimizing phase-induced interference with the dielectric properties. The incorporation of heteroatoms significantly enhances defect dipole polarization, facilitating stronger interatomic charge transfer and interactions, which collectively modulate electrical conductivity and amplify dielectric polarization loss. The HED approach further increases the density of lattice strain concentration zones, introducing pronounced lattice distortions, discontinuous lattice fringes, and point defects. These structural modifications induce localized charge accumulation and redistribution, effectively separating positive and negative charge centers to form high-density defect dipoles that substantially increase polarization loss. Benefiting from the HED-engineered amplification of polarization loss and suppression of excessive conductivity, the optimized impedance matching yields a broadband EAB of 7.65 GHz, which is more than double that of the pristine-MoS2 sample, along with a minimal reflection loss of −61.2 dB. Our work establishes a novel framework for multielement doping in EMA materials, opening new avenues for the development of next-generation TMD-based absorbers.
