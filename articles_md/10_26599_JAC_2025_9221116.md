## 1 Introduction

Luminescent materials exhibit broad application prospects in various fields, including white light-emitting diodes (WLEDs), scintillators, biological imaging, laser devices, security, and anti-counterfeiting [1−6]. Among luminescent materials, glass is well known for its easy synthesis, low cost, adjustable components, humidity resistance, and excellent physicochemical stability [7−11]. These inherent advantages enable them to meet the stringent requirements of various applications, such as WLED, scintillators, and laser devices. In recent years, Cu+, an economical, non-toxic, and non-rare-earth activator, has been widely employed in luminescent materials and has received significant attention [12,13]. The 3d94s1 → 3d10 (s → d) transition of Cu+ provides efficient and adaptable wideband emission in the blue-cyan region [13−15], which positions Cu+-doped glass as a promising candidate for multi-scenario applications. However, as the complexity and specific demands of multi-scenario applications increase, the current properties of Cu+-doped glass are insufficient.

Since the multi-scenario applications of Cu+-doped glass demand increased luminescence intensity and thermal stability, a series of strategies were implemented here. First, an oxyfluoride glass was selected as the glass host. Oxyfluoride glass integrates the superiorities of oxide and fluoride glass, featuring high mechanical strength, optical transparency, chemical stability, thermal stability, and low phonon energy [11,16−19]. These advantages help achieve the desired luminescent efficiency in a rigorous environment. Second, the heavy element barium (Ba) was added to the composition of the glass host. The relative atomic mass of Ba is 137.33, which contributes to the X-ray energy absorption [20−23]. Third, the reducing agent Al is incorporated to inhibit the oxidation of Cu+ during the melting process [24]. Unlike Sb2O3, Si3N4, and C, Al only generates Al2O3 and does not introduce impurities or bubbles in the glass. Al2O3 enters the network structure of glass in the form of [AlO4]− tetrahedrons, which improves the physicochemical stability of the glass [25]. Finally, energy transfer from traps to Cu+ was utilized to obtain the desired thermal stability of the Cu+-doped glass. The thermal compensation effect via the release of electrons from traps is expected to greatly compensate for thermal quenching and improve the luminescence intensity at high temperatures [26].

In the fields of radiation detection and X-ray imaging, oil exploration poses more stringent and specific requirements for the performance of scintillators [27]. Scintillators must be operated at temperatures higher than 200 °C, and sometimes, they are used in water or high-humidity environments [28]. However, most existing commercial scintillators, such as Bi4Ge3O12 (BGO) and CsI : Tl, exhibit poor thermal stability [29]. The luminescent intensities of these materials decrease conspicuously as the temperature increases due to the increased probability of non-radiative quenching and structural degradation [30,31]. Perovskite scintillators are highly prized for their exceptional luminescent efficiency, but they usually need to be composited with organics to form scintillator films, which hinders their application in complex environments. In comparison, Cu+-doped glass, with preferable thermal stability and comprehensive performance, is a better choice for bulk scintillators used in extreme conditions.

In the field of WLED, the blue-cyan luminescence of Cu+ could effectively compensate for the cyan trap in the spectral distribution [32,33], thereby enhancing the color rendering index (CRI) of WLED and providing a natural and comfortable lighting experience. For example, the (TEA)2Cu2Br2Cl2-based WLED achieves a high CRI of 95 [34]. Motivated by these properties, the development of a trichromatic full-spectrum WLED using Cu+-doped glass with enhanced thermal stability and luminescent efficiency is highly promising, especially for educational and medical lighting.

In this work, a highly transparent Cu+-doped oxyfluoride glass with anti-thermal-quenching luminescence is designed. In addition, its multi-scenario applications are systematically investigated. For glass scintillators, the X-ray excited luminescence (XEL) intensity of Cu+-doped glass is 311% of that of BGO. The XEL intensities at 423 and 573 K are 155% and 63% of that at 303 K, respectively. In the application of high-temperature X-ray imaging and underwater X-ray imaging, its imaging resolution can reach 24 lp/mm. For full-spectrum WLED, the Cu+-doped glass has an extremely high external quantum efficiency (EQE = 81.0%). When the temperature increases to 573 K, the photoluminescent (PL) intensity still remains at 76% of that at 303 K. The WLED fabricated from Cu+-doped glass has an impressively high color rendering index of 96.1. These results adequately indicate that Cu+-doped oxyfluoride glass has great potential for high-temperature X-ray imaging, underwater X-ray imaging, and full-spectrum WLED.

### 2.1 Preparation of glass samples

Glass samples with chemical compositions of 55SiO2–20Al2O3–15BaF2–10NaF–*x*CuO1/2–*y*Al (*x* = 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4; *y* = 0, 0.9, 1, 1.2, and 1.4 in mol% ratio) were prepared via the melt quenching method. The synthesis process for the Cu+-doped glass samples is shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). First, the ingredients were completely milled in an agate mortar for approximately 30 min. The mixed powder was put into an Al2O3 crucible. Then, it was melted in air at 1520 °C for 1.5 h. ### 2.2 Fabrication of phosphors and light-emitting diode (LED) devices

The yellow-emitting Y3Al5O12:Ce3+ phosphor (purchased from Intematix Co., USA) was uniformly mixed with epoxy resin and then coated on the surface of a 445 nm LED chip to manufacture a commercial WLED (labeled as w-LED-445).

The GH–0.2Cu–1Al glass was ground in an agate mortar for approximately 30 min to obtain glass powder. The GH–0.2Cu–1Al glass powder was uniformly mixed with epoxy resin and then coated on the surface of a 300 nm ultraviolet (UV) LED chip to manufacture a blue LED device (labeled as b-LED-300).

The GH–0.2Cu–1Al glass powder, (Sr,Ba)2SiO4:Eu2+ and (Sr,Ca)AlSiN3:Eu2+ (purchased from Shenzhen Looking Long Technology Co., Ltd., China) was mixed in proportion, uniformly mixed with epoxy resin, and finally coated on the surface of a 300 nm LED chip to manufacture a full-spectra-light WLED (labeled as FW-LED-300).

### 2.3 Characterization of structural, luminescent, and scintillating properties

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; MiniFlex/600, Rigaku, Japan) was used to measure the XRD patterns of glass samples. The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the GH-host and GH–0.2Cu–1Al mixed with KBr powder (1 : 100 ratio) were obtained using a spectrophotometer (NEXUS 670, USA). Transmittance spectra at 200–800 nm were obtained with a ultraviolet‒visible spectrophotometer (U-3900, Hitachi, Japan). A Raman microscope (Via-Reflex, Renishaw, UK) was used to acquire the Raman spectra. A PL spectrometer (FS5 Fluorescence, Edinburgh, UK) equipped with a Xe lamp as the pumping excitation source was used to record the PL spectra, photoluminescence excitation (PLE) spectra, internal quantum efficiency (IQE), EQE, absorption efficiency (Abs), and fluorescence decay curves. A thermoluminescence spectrometer (TL; SL08 TL, RongFan, China) was used to produce curves. An X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCLALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used. The photoelectric properties of the WLED devices were investigated using an automatic temperature control photoelectric analysis and measurement system (ATA-1000 LED, Everfine, China). The refractive index (*n*) at 589 nm was measured using a digital refractometer (GI-RDB, China). The density (*ρ*) was measured according to Archimedes’ principle using deionized water as an immersion liquid.

An XEL spectrometer (OmniFluo960, Zolix, China) was used to collect the XEL spectra, and X-ray images were acquired with a camera (EOS 600D, Canon, Japan) with an image acquisition time of 5 s. Temperature-dependent XEL spectra were obtained with a fiber spectrometer (TAIZI, Godzilla, China) with a copper heater. For the above measurements, the X-ray source was an X-ray tube (TUB00154-9I-W06, Moxtek, USA). BGO and CsI:Tl scintillators with a thickness of 2 mm were used for comparison. BGO crystals were purchased from the Shanghai Institute of Ceramics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China. CsI:Tl crystals were purchased from Shanghai EPIC Crystal Co., Ltd., China. The specific parameters are listed in Table S1 in the ESM.

### 3.1 Structural properties

The XRD patterns of the prepared glass samples are shown in Fig. 1(a) and Fig. S2 in the ESM. The diffuse diffraction peaks indicate the amorphous phase of all glass samples. Figure 1(b) presents the FTIR spectra of the GH-host and GH–0.2Cu–1Al. Specifically, the 456 cm−1 peak originates from the bridging oxygen vibrations of Si–O–Si and Si–O–Al linkages, whereas the 720 cm−1 peak arises from the asymmetric stretching of [AlO4]− tetrahedra [35]. The prominent absorption at 1002 cm−1 is produced by antisymmetric stretching vibrations of Si–O bonds in [SiO4] tetrahedra [36]. The similar shapes of the FTIR spectra and peak positions suggest an approximate glass structure of the GH-host and GH–0.2Cu–1Al.

The Raman spectra are shown in Fig. 1(c). Two prominent vibration bands in the ranges of 400–650 and 900–1250 cm−1 are related to Si‒O asymmetrical vibrations of tetrahedral [SiO4] and Si‒O‒Si and Si‒O‒Al bending vibrations, respectively [37], which is consistent with the FTIR spectra. The maximum phonon energy of the Cu+-doped glass is 1076 cm−1, which is consistent with the characteristics of oxyfluoride glass [38]. The low phonon energy efficiently contributes to the luminescence of Cu+.

The transmittance spectra of the typical glass samples are shown in Fig. 1(d). The transmittance of the GH-host, GH–0.2Cu–0Al, GH–0.2Cu–1Al, and GH–0.4Cu–1Al exceeds 80% at 441 nm. The absorption band at 600‒800 nm is attributed to the characteristic absorption of Cu2+ [39]. The energy gap (*E*g) of the GH-host is 4.38 eV (Fig. S3(c) in the ESM), which is beneficial for PL and XEL performance.

Compared with that of GH–0.2Cu–0Al, the transmittance of GH–0.2Cu–1Al at 441 nm increases, and the absorption of Cu2+ disappears due to the effective reduction of Cu2+ to Cu+ by Al. The absorption edge undergoes a blue shift because of the increased *E*g when the content of Cu2+ decreases. Compared with that of GH–0.2Cu–1Al, the transmittance of GH–0.4Cu–1Al at 441 nm decreases, and the absorption of Cu2+ becomes stronger. The absorption edge exhibits a redshift because of the reduced *E*g when the content of Cu2+ increases [40,41]. The highest transmittance is obtained in GH–0.2Cu–1Al (85.4% at 441 nm). The excellent transparency contributes to the efficient output of light during the scintillation process. The detailed transmittance and *E*g of the glass samples are shown in Figs. S3(a)–S3(d) in the ESM. The refractive index (*n*) of the glass samples is also determined, as shown in the Part E in the ESM. These glass samples exhibit an appropriate refractive index.

The change in the transmittance spectra is related to the reduction of Cu2+ to Cu+. To further demonstrate the reduction efficiency of Al, Figure S4 in the ESM presents the XPS spectra of the GH–0.2Cu–0Al and GH–0.2Cu–1Al samples. When the concentration of Al increases, the proportion of Cu+ increases substantially, and that of Cu2+ decreases rapidly. The Cu2+ present in the glass sample is effectively reduced to Cu+ by the reducing agent Al.

### 3.2 PL properties

The PLE spectra (*λ*em = 441 nm) and PL spectra (*λ*ex = 291 nm) (*λ* represents for wavelength) of the GH–*x*Cu–1Al samples are shown in Figs. S5(a) and S5(b) in the ESM. When the concentration of CuO1/2 increases, the PLE and PL intensities of the GH–*x*Cu–1Al glass samples first increase because of the increasing content of Cu+ and then decrease due to concentration quenching. The PLE spectra (*λ*em = 441 nm) and PL spectra (*λ*ex = 291 nm) of the GH–0.2Cu–*y*Al samples are shown in Figs. S5(c) and S5(d) in the ESM. When the concentration of Al increases, the PLE and PL intensities first increase because of the effective reduction by Al and then decrease because of concentration quenching. After optimization of the CuO1/2 and Al concentrations, GH–0.2Cu–1Al is the optimal sample for PL. The PLE (*λ*em = 441 nm) and PL (*λ*ex = 291 nm) spectra of GH–0.2Cu–1Al are displayed in Fig. 2(a). GH–0.2Cu–1Al has a large Stokes shift and negligible self-absorption. The excitation band at 291 nm corresponds to the 3d10 → 3d94s1 (d → s) transition of Cu+. The broadband emission at 441 nm is ascribed to the s → d transition of Cu+. The emission of Cu+ is located in the blue-cyan range, reflecting its potential as a blue-cyan-emitting phosphor in full-spectrum WLED.

where *I*(*t*) signifies the emission intensity corresponding to the time *t*. The *τ* values of GH–*x*Cu–1Al (*x* = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4) are 42.8, 42.5, 42.4, and 42.4 μs, respectively. As shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM, the *τ* values of GH–0.2Cu–*y*Al (*y* = 0.9, 1, 1.2, and 1.4) are 42.8, 42.5, 42.2, and 42.1 μs, respectively. The *τ* values of the d9s state of Cu+ are in the microsecond range, which is consistent with the lifetimes of Cu+ in inorganic glass, nanocrystals, and perovskites. The relatively high *τ* values suggest that Cu+ may have excellent luminescent efficiency in glass because of the high probability of radiative transitions. When the concentrations of Cu+ increase, the distance of Cu+–Cu+ decreases, which increases the possibility of non-radiative relaxation. Therefore, the lifetimes of the d9s of the Cu+ glass samples decrease slightly when the concentration of CuO1/2 or Al increases.

To further explore the luminescent efficiency of the optimal sample, the IQE, Abs, and EQE of GH–0.2Cu–1Al were examined upon excitation at 291 nm, as shown in Fig. 2(c). The IQE, Abs, and EQE values of the GH–*x*Cu–*y*Al samples are calculated and shown in Part I in the ESM. The values of IQE, Abs, and EQE of GH–0.2Cu–1Al are 96.3%, 84.1%, and 81.0%, respectively. Benefiting from an appropriate glass host, GH–0.2Cu–1Al has an outstanding IQE, Abs, and EQE, which indicates that GH–0.2Cu–1Al may be a competitive candidate for blue-cyan-emitting phosphors for WLED and scintillators.

The thermal stability of GH–0.2Cu–1Al in PL determines its application at high temperatures. The temperature-related PL spectra and intensities are depicted in Fig. S8 in the ESM and Fig. 2(d), respectively. The PL intensity decreases slightly as the temperature gradually increases. The PL intensity of GH–0.2Cu–1Al at 573 K is 76% of that at 303 K, reflecting its excellent thermal stability during PL. The good PL thermal stability indicates that GH–0.2Cu–1Al can be utilized for high-temperature X-ray imaging and WLED.

### 3.3 Scintillating properties

GH–0.2Cu–1Al is the optimal sample for PL. To demonstrate its scintillating performance, a series of characterizations was performed. When scintillators are used for X-ray imaging or X-ray detection, their properties are influenced by prolonged irradiation. Therefore, GH–0.2Cu–1Al was irradiated for 120 min to investigate its radiation resistance. The XEL spectra and intensity of GH–0.2Cu–1Al were recorded every 5 min, as shown in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b), respectively. The typical “bright burn effect” can be clearly observed in the GH–0.2Cu–1Al sample. During the first 40 min of irradiation, the XEL intensity increased rapidly. The XEL intensity at 40 min was 163% of that at 0 min. Subsequently, the XEL intensity remained stable under X-ray irradiation until 120 min. The rapid increase in the XEL intensity is attributed to the progressive filling of the traps. When traps are gradually filled, more charge carriers are captured by luminescent centers instead of traps, thus leading to an increase in the XEL intensity in the first 40 min. After 40 min of irradiation, the traps approached saturation. Therefore, the XEL intensity gradually stabilized with further irradiation.

After heat treatment, the transmittance could be recovered to that before irradiation, which indicates that the X-ray-induced damage to the GH–0.2Cu–1Al sample is repairable. On the basis of the temperature-dependent XEL intensity and TL analysis of the GH–0.2Cu–1Al sample, an XEL mechanism is proposed as Fig. 4(e). Excited by X-rays, numerous high-energy electrons and holes are produced by Compton scattering and the photoelectric effect. After thermalization, high-energy electrons and holes are converted into secondary electrons and holes. Some of the secondary electrons are moved to the 3d94s1 energy level of the luminescent center Cu+, and then these electrons radiatively relax to the ground state 3d10 of Cu+ with a characteristic blue-cyan emission. The others are caught by traps and stored in traps rather than undergoing non-radiative transitions.

When the temperature increases gradually, the electrons captured by the traps are released under thermal activation, and these electrons are subsequently transported to the 3d94s1 level of Cu+. The energy transfer from traps to Cu+ compensates for thermal quenching and enhances the XEL intensity of Cu+. Therefore, the anti-thermal-quenching luminescence (XEL intensity at 423 K reaches 155% of that at 303 K) and excellent thermal stability (XEL intensity at 573 K remains 63% of that at 303 K) of GH–0.2Cu–1Al are obtained. The changes in the XEL intensity at high temperatures are consistent with the TL curve in Fig. 4(d).

The stability of GH–0.2Cu–1Al in water was investigated, as displayed in Figs. S13(a) and S13(b) in the ESM. The brightness and XEL intensity of GH–0.2Cu–1Al present negligible changes, which illustrates that GH–0.2Cu–1Al possesses exceptional stability and applicability for underwater X-ray imaging.

To further confirm the X-ray imaging ability of the GH–0.2Cu–1Al scintillator for objects, the following X-ray images were obtained. As presented in Figs. 5(b) and 5(c), an X-ray image of a three-dimensional object (a capsule containing a nail) is collected. A nail in the capsule can be clearly observed. The high spatial resolution, combined with the high XEL intensity, results in the best comprehensive performance among the Cu+-doped and Cu(I)-based scintillators. These results indicate that the GH–0.2Cu–1Al scintillator possesses tremendous capacity for accurate X-ray imaging.

To assess the X-ray imaging quality of GH‒0.2Cu‒1Al at high temperatures, a heating chip is applied to heat glass scintillator and serves as an imaging object. Owing to the limitation of the maximum heating temperature of the heating chip, high-temperature X-ray imaging can only reach 483 K. X-ray images of the heating chip (303‒483 K) are recorded and displayed in Fig. 5(j) and Fig. S16 in the ESM. Profiting from the anti-thermal quenching of GH‒0.2Cu‒1Al, the internal structure of the heating chip becomes more discernible when the temperature increases. Upon further heating, the X-ray images still maintain high brightness. The values of brightness in Fig. 5(j) were read and analyzed, as given in the Part T in the ESM. The resolution test standard line pair card is made of poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) and lead. Owing to the limitation of the melting point of PMMA, a high-temperature X-ray image of the resolution test standard line pair card was taken at 363 K, as presented in Fig. S17(b) in the ESM. GH–0.2Cu–1Al has incredible spatial resolution (24 lp/mm) at 363 K. These demonstrations indicate that GH–0.2Cu–1Al has high thermal stability in XEL and is applicable for high-temperature X-ray imaging.

The good waterproof properties of GH–0.2Cu–1Al make it particularly suitable for underwater exploration. To evaluate its capability for underwater X-ray imaging, a glass scintillator is positioned at the base of flat-bottomed glassware containing deionized water, as shown in Fig. 5(k). Figure 5(l) shows the underwater X-ray images of a Chinese-character metal sheet. The X-ray image is still bright and clear after prolonged immersion. To demonstrate practical application, underwater X-ray images of a hammer model in a black box (Figs. 5(m) and 5(n)) and a capsule containing a spring (Fig. 5(o)) are collected. The objects in the containers are discernible. In particular, the GH‒0.2Cu‒1Al glass scintillator exhibited a high spatial resolution of 20 lp/mm in underwater X-ray imaging (Fig. 5(p)). Even after 30 d of immersion, its spatial resolution for underwater X-ray imaging remains at 20 lp/mm (Fig. S17(c) in the ESM). These results illustrate that GH‒0.2Cu‒1Al possesses exceptional applicability in a rigorous environment.

The GH–0.2Cu–1Al glass powder was combined with a 300 nm UV chip to obtain b-LED-300. Figure 6(b) shows the emission spectrum of b-LED-300. The CIE coordinates of b-LED-300 are (0.198, 0.216), which are located in the blue-cyan emission region (Fig. S18 in the ESM). ## 4 Conclusions

Highly transparent Cu+-doped glass with anti-thermal-quenching luminescence was designed by selecting an oxyfluoride glass host, introducing heavy elements, incorporating the reducing agent Al, and utilizing energy transfer from traps to Cu+. As glass scintillators, GH–0.2Cu–1Al has impressive XEL intensity (311% of that of BGO) and exceptional resolution for X-ray imaging (24 lp/mm), which reflects the best comprehensive scintillating performance among Cu+-doped and Cu(I)-based scintillators. GH–0.2Cu–1Al glass exhibits anti-thermal quenching luminescence (the XEL intensity at 423 K is 155% of that at 303 K) in XEL. Its XEL intensity at 573 K is 63% of that at 303 K, which is much better than those of CsI:Tl (0.6%) and BGO (1.5%). The spatial resolution is 24 and 20 lp/mm in high-temperature X-ray imaging and underwater X-ray imaging, respectively. As a blue-cyan-emitting phosphor for WLED, GH–0.2Cu–1Al glass has a high EQE (80.1%) and extraordinary thermal stability (the PL intensity at 573 K is 76% of that at 303 K). The full-spectra WLED (fw-LED-300) fabricated using GH–0.2Cu–1Al has a CRI of 96.1, which makes it a superior choice for educational lighting and medical lighting. Our findings illustrate the potential of Cu+-doped glass for multiple-scenario applications, including high-temperature X-ray imaging, underwater X-ray imaging and full-spectra WLED.
