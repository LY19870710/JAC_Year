## 1 Introduction

To achieve high performance and low production costs for advanced ceramics, particularly large products, it is essential to create a green body of ceramics with a uniform microstructure, moderate mechanical properties, and dimensions that are close to the dimensions of the final product. Gel casting, a type of colloidal forming process, has garnered significant attention since the 1990s [1]. Compared to conventional methods such as dry pressing and isostatic pressing, the gel-casting process offers a cost-effective and efficient means of producing near-net-shaped green bodies with favorable mechanical properties (20‒40 MPa). However, traditional gel systems that rely on free radical polymerization [2] or nucleophilic addition reactions [3] produce a dense organic network due to the addition of 4‒5 wt% organic compounds, which impedes water transport and hinders the release of internal stress during drying. Consequently, green bodies are prone to deformation and cracking. Some natural polymers, such as agarose systems (4,5]. Other colloidal forming methods, such as direct coagulation casting (DCC), can be applied to form products with homogeneous structures and complex shapes [6]. However, the low strength of wet bodies (tens of kPa) and the complexity of the molding process constrain their wide application.

Recently, a new large-scale forming method called spontaneous coagulation casting (SCC) has been introduced that utilizes a copolymer of isobutylene and maleic anhydride (PIBM), which is commercially known as Isobam [7]. The PIBM gel system offers several advantages, including a high bulk density and nontoxicity, as well as the ability to achieve dispersion and coagulation with very low additions (8]. This gel system has been successfully employed to shape a wide range of oxide and nonoxide ceramics, including Al2O3, MgAl2O4, Y2O3, YAG, AlN, and fused silica [9−15]. The PIBM molecule comprises three functional groups, –COO–NH4+, –CONH2, and –CO–O–CO–. The PIBM chains are adsorbed on adjacent ceramic particles, resulting in collaboration between the ceramic particles and PIBM molecules during the curing process [16]. Studies have revealed that the SCC system primarily establishes a gel network through intermolecular interactions such as hydrophobic associations and hydrogen bonds between PIBM polymer chains. This process forms wet green bodies by *in situ* curing of ceramic particles. The SCC gel system is characterized as a physical gel with low organic network density and an absence of chemical connections [17,18]. Consequently, the green body produced using this method is brittle and becomes more fragile as the particle size of the powder decreases. This brittleness leads to easy cracking during the storage, transportation, and machining of green bodies.

Relieving the brittleness and improving the plasticity of ceramic bodies are key factors in increasing the yield of ceramic products and making them more machinable. In the plastic industry, plasticizers are commonly used to improve the flexibility and processability of polymers [19]. Plasticizers have been effectively employed in tape casting and gel casting in the ceramic industry. In tape casting, plasticizers can enhance the flexibility of green tape, resulting in a smooth and defect-free surface and facilitating subsequent drying and processing operations [20]. In gel casting, plasticizers can decrease the elastic modulus of gel networks, alleviate the internal stress in green bodies, and minimize crack generation during the debinding process [21,22]. Shang *et al*. [23] utilized polyethylene glycol (PEG) as a plasticizer in combination with SCC to tape-cast aluminum nitride ceramic sheets, drawing upon the work of Yang and Wu [24], who tape-casted transparent alumina ceramic wafers. However, there are no reports of systematic studies examining the selection of plasticizers for ceramic powder with varying particle sizes to be shaped using SCC.

The objective of this study was to employ plasticizers in a PIBM gel system to enhance the plasticity of green bodies of oxide ceramics with different powder particle sizes. The water-soluble plasticizers glycerol and PEG were selected because of their inclusion of polar groups (–OH) and nonpolar groups. The plasticizing effect was assessed by combining the flexural stress‒strain curve with impact toughness. Ceramic green bodies with promising strength and plasticity were successfully prepared. Additionally, the effects of plasticizers on the rheological properties of slurries and on the sintering behavior of green bodies were also explored.

### 2.1 Materials and sample preparation

In this study, two plasticizers were employed to enhance the plasticity of ceramic green bodies composed of three kinds of ceramic powder with particle sizes ranging from the submicron scale to the nanoscale. Specifically, the water-soluble plasticizers glycerol (molecular weight: 92, purity: 99%; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd., China) and PEG (molecular weight: 380–420, purity: 99.9%; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd., China) were added in amounts of 1, 2, and 5 wt%, respectively, relative to the weight of the ceramic powder. AES-11 (purity: 99.7%; Sumitomo Chemical, Japan) and TM-DAR (purity: 99.99%; Taimei Chemicals Co., Ltd., Japan) are two types of submicron commercial Al2O3 ceramic powder with mean particle sizes of 0.45 and 0.18 μm, respectively. S30CR (purity: 99.99%; Baikowski, France) was commercial MgAl2O4 ceramic powder with a mean particle size of 50 nm. PIBM molecules, including Isobam104 and Isobam600AF (molecular weights: 55,000–65,000 and 5500–6500, respectively; Kuraray, Japan), were applied as dispersants and gelling agents. The solid loadings (ratio of powder volume to water and powder volume) and PIBM additions are listed in Table 1.

The experimental procedure for the SCC process is illustrated in Fig. 1. First, PIBM was dissolved in ultrapure water to prepare a PIBM solution, and ceramic powder was gradually added. After attrition milling for 0.5 h at a speed of 300 r/min, a plasticizer was added to prepare the slurry. The slurry was uniformly dispersed after an additional hour of attrition milling at a speed of 300 r/min. After degassing, the slurry was cast into polytetrafluoroethylene molds (9 mm × 12 mm × 50 mm and *ϕ*40 mm × 10 mm). The wet green bodies were formed after coagulation for either 20 or 24 h (using Al2O3 powder for 20 h or MgAl2O4 powder for 24 h). Finally, drying was conducted in a constant temperature (25 ℃) and humidity (80%) chamber for 1–6 d until the weights remained unchanged to obtain green bodies. After debinding at 800 ℃ for 6 h, the green bodies were sintered at 1550 ℃ (using AES-11 and S30CR powder), 1300 ℃ (using TM-DAR powder), and 1500 ℃ (using S30CR powder).

### 2.2 Characterization methods

The rheological properties of the slurries were evaluated using a rotational rheometer (HAAKE Viscotester IQ Air, Thermo Electron LED GmbH, Germany) equipped with a parallel plate (20 mm in diameter) at a temperature of 25 ℃. The test specimens were made from 9 mm × 12 mm × 50 mm molds to assess the linear shrinkage ratio, flexural strength, impact toughness, and compressive strength. The linear shrinkage ratio * γ of the green bodies after drying was determined according to Eq. (1):

(1) γ=1−Ld/Lw

where  Lw and  Ld are the lengths of the green bodies before (50 mm) and after drying, respectively. Each value was the average of the three test results. After the sanding process, the dimensions of the test specimens were measured precisely. These measurements were performed to assess the plasticity of green bodies made using various types and amounts of plasticizers as well as different particle sizes. The flexural strength was determined using a three-point bending test with a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min. The compressive strength was determined with a crosshead speed of 0.2 mm/min using a universal testing machine (Instron-1195, Instron Co., USA). The impact toughness was determined using a pendulum impact testing machine with an impact velocity of 3.5 m/s (Dynatup POE 2000, Instron Co., USA). The impact toughness ( acU) of the green bodies was based on Eq. (2):

(2) acU=ECh⋅a×103

where  EC is the impact energy, and  a and  h are the width and the thickness of the test bar, respectively. The flexural strength, compressive strength, and impact toughness values of each sample were determined by averaging the results of five testing bars. To obtain the flexural stress‒strain curve, the stress and strain were calculated from the loading and displacement data using Eqs. (3) and (4) [25]:

(3) σ=5FL4ah2

(4) ϵ=8hlL2

where  F is the applied load;  L is the outer span (30 mm);  a and  h are the width and the thickness of the test bar, respectively;  l is the crosshead displacement. Drilling tests were performed on round green bodies from ϕ*40 mm × 10 mm molds to evaluate their plasticity enhancement effect. The diameter of the drill is 6 mm.

Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectra were measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (V-770, Jasco, Japan) to analyze the interaction between PIBM and plasticizers in the range of 200‒800 nm at a speed of 400 nm/min. The fracture surfaces of the green bodies and the ceramics were examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU8220, Hitachi, Japan). The average density of three bars from each sample was utilized to calculate the densities of the green bodies and ceramics using Archimedes’ principle. The densities of the green bodies were measured after debinding at 800 ℃ for 6 h. The relative density of the ceramics was calculated by dividing the measured density by the theoretical density (Al2O3: 3.987 g/cm3, MgAl2O4: 3.580 g/cm3).

### 3.1 Rheological behavior of slurries

As PIBM molecules adsorb onto the surface of particles, the negative charge of the particles increases, resulting in a more negative Zeta potential [26]. Consequently, the repulsion between the particles increases, contributing to a dispersion effect. Given that the specific surface area of ceramic particles increases with decreasing particle size, more PIBM with a low molecular weight is required to disperse the particles [27]. Subsequently, plasticizers were added to interact with the PIBM molecules and adjust the gel network. After attrition milling, the viscosities of slurries made from different ceramic powder with varying amounts of plasticizers were evaluated (Fig. 2).

As depicted in Fig. 2, all the slurries displayed a shear-thinning behavior, and the viscosity decreased as the shear rate increased. Specifically, for slurries with 54 vol% solid loadings and AES-11 powder, the viscosity of the slurries decreased from 0.22 to 0.12 Pa·s at 100 s−1 when the glycerol content increased from 1 to 5 wt% (Fig. 2(a)). In contrast, the viscosity slightly increased from 0.22 to 0.29 Pa·s as the content of PEG increased from 1 to 5 wt% (Fig. 2(b)). Notably, all the slurries had viscosities of less than 1 Pa·s at 100 s−1, making them suitable for pouring. Because the AES-11 powder had an average particle size of 0.45 μm, it can be effectively dispersed using less PIBM (0.3 wt%). The PEG molecules have a long chain structure, and excess PEG molecules (> 1 wt%) will entangle each other, which leads to an increase in the viscosity of the slurries. Therefore, an appropriate amount of glycerol was beneficial for both degassing and slurry pouring when using AES-11 powder.

For slurries with a solid loading of 45 vol% and TM-DAR powder, the addition of glycerol resulted in a decrease in viscosity from 0.39 to 0.15 Pa·s at 100 s−1 (Fig. 2(c)). In contrast, the addition of PEG led to a slight decrease in viscosity from 0.39 to 0.28 Pa·s (Fig. 2(d)). Similarly, for slurries with a solid loading of 40 vol% and S30CR powder, the addition of glycerol resulted in a decrease in viscosity from 0.51 to 0.26 Pa·s at a shear rate of 100 s−1 (Fig. 2(e)). Moreover, the addition of PEG led to a slight decrease in the viscosity from 0.51 to 0.36 Pa·s (Fig. 2(f)). Overall, the addition of both glycerol and PEG decreased the viscosity of the slurries at a shear rate of 100 s−1. Increasing the amount of glycerol had a significant effect on the viscosity of the slurry. This can be attributed to the presence of the polar –OH group in glycerol, which may interact with other polar groups on the PIBM chains, decreasing the interaction between PIBM chains and increasing the mobility of polymer chains. This decreased the viscosity of the slurry. Notably, glycerol contains three –OH groups per molecule, whereas PEG has only one –OH group at each end of its molecular chain. Therefore, the effect of glycerol on the viscosity of the slurry was more pronounced at the same concentration.

For the drying shrinkage ratio, we believed that two key factors jointly determined the linear shrinkage ratio during drying. On one hand, the use of plasticizers slightly decreased the solid loadings of the slurries. According to the research conducted by Tarì and Ferreira [28], a decrease in solid loading is often accompanied by an increase in the shrinkage ratio of the green body. On the other hand, PEG has a long-chain structure and interacts with PIBM between particles in the slurry state, increasing the distance between them. During the drying process, the gel network contracts as water evaporates, resulting in greater shrinkage of the green bodies. The chain length of glycerol is relatively short and thus has a limited effect on the distance between the particles. Consequently, for the same amount of plasticizer, the effect of PEG on shrinkage can be slightly greater than that of glycerol.

The trend of bulk density for bodies derived from the AES-11 and S30CR powder demonstrated a downward pattern with a decrease in solid loading and an increase in particle distance (Figs. 3(a) and 3(c)). On the other hand, for bodies derived from TM-DAR powder, a slight increase in bulk density was observed upon the addition of 1 or 2 wt% glycerol and 1 wt% PEG. Specifically, the bulk density increased from 2.386 to 2.407, 2.395, and 2.406 g/cm3, respectively (Fig. 3(b)). This suggested that the ‒OH groups in the plasticizers and polar groups on the PIBM chains may form a new intermolecular connection during the gelation process. This intermolecular force was enhanced by the evaporation of water, resulting in a decrease in the distance between the particles after drying and a slight increase in the bulk density. The decrease in bulk density was relatively small because glycerol is a smaller molecule with more –OH groups.

### 3.3 Plasticity of green bodies

Figure 4 shows the flexural stress–strain curves of the green bodies prepared using AES-11 powder with varying amounts of glycerol and PEG as plasticizers. The addition of glycerol and PEG had different effects on the peak heights and widths of the curves. With glycerol, the peak heights of the flexural stress–strain curves slightly decreased, while the peak width of the curves broadened, which was an important indicator of plasticity improvement. Notably, 1 wt% glycerol had a beneficial effect on broadening the peak width, which was approximately twice that without plasticizers (Fig. 4(a)). In contrast, compared with glycerol, PEG resulted in a relatively large decrease in the peak height and was less effective at broadening the peak width (Fig. 4(b)).

*
Fig. 4Flexural stress‒strain curves of green bodies prepared using AES-11 powder with different amounts of (a) glycerol and (b) PEG.

Figure 5 illustrates the impact toughness and flexural strength of the green bodies prepared using AES-11 powder with different amounts of glycerol and PEG. As depicted in Fig. 5(a), both glycerol and PEG enhanced the impact toughness values of the green bodies, which was consistent with the peak width broadening results (Fig. 4). Specifically, when 1 wt% glycerol was added, the impact toughness increased from 0.62 to 1.06 kJ/m2, representing an improvement of approximately 71% compared to that of the green bodies without any plasticizer. However, as the amount of glycerol increased, the impact toughness decreased slightly, and the standard deviation increased. This was because the increase in glycerol content resulted in a marked reduction in the viscosity of the slurry (Fig. 2(a)), which could lead to particle sedimentation during the extended coagulation process. The uniformity of the structural arrangement of the green bodies was compromised, which hindered the enhancement of plasticity. As illustrated in Fig. 5(b), the incorporation of glycerol and PEG led to a marginal reduction in the strength of the green bodies prepared from the AES-11 powder. It can be assumed that the AES-11 powder is submicron and requires less PIBM (0.3 wt%). The number of functional groups that can interact with plasticizers is small. Consequently, the increase in the amount of plasticizers did not increase their effective interaction with the PIBM molecules but only increased the distances between the particles, which was macroscopically manifested as a decrease in flexural strength. However, the addition of 1 wt% glycerol resulted in only a minor decrease in strength of approximately 7%.

### 3.4 Plasticity mechanism

The mechanisms by which glycerol and PEG improved the plasticity of the green bodies were examined by analyzing the interaction between PIBM and plasticizers in H2O by UV–Vis spectroscopy and investigating the microstructure of the green bodies by SEM. UV–Vis spectroscopy is known to be an effective method for investigating the hydrogen bonding behavior [29−31]. We explored the effect of glycerol with different mass ratios on Isobam104 in H2O using UV–Vis, as displayed in Fig. 12. There is no chromophore in the glycerol molecule, so no noticeable absorption in the UV–Vis wavelength range was observed for the glycerol solution. Since Isobam104 contains groups such as –COO–NH4+, –CONH2, and –CO–O–CO–, interactions with water produced a broad absorption peak at 290 nm. Notably, when the amount of glycerol was increased from 5 to 20 parts (in mass), no change in peak shape was observed, but the absorption peaks were slightly blueshifted (*λ*max shifts from 290 to 289 nm (5 parts glycerol) and then to 288 nm (5 parts to 20 parts glycerol)). Koeppe *et al*. [31] used UV–Vis to study the interaction between 2-chloro-4-nitrophenol and acetate anions, and the formation of hydrogen-bonded complexes caused the UV–Vis band to blueshift by approximately 30 nm. This suggests that C=O in Isobam104 molecules can interact with the –OH in glycerol molecules to produce a hydrogen-bonded association. This leads to an increase in the energy required for the electron transition and a shift in *λ*max toward shorter wavelengths. However, these interactions are relatively weak and cannot form complexes, resulting in a relatively small shift in the UV‒Vis band. Therefore, we deduce that the introduced glycerol and PEG molecules interact with the PIBM molecules in ceramic slurries to form hydrogen bonds, influencing the interactions between PIBM molecules and thereby modifying the gel network structure. Consequently, the impact toughness and strength of the green bodies were affected.

The organic network of PIBM is gradually established by the formation of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions along the PIBM chain [7]. Upon drying, this network encapsulates the surface of ceramic particles to form a cocoon-like structure [32]. With a low molecular weight (92), glycerol has a minimal impact on the gel network structure of PIBM during the coagulation stage. Therefore, it has minimal impact on the morphology of the dried green bodies. In contrast, PEG, which has a large molecular weight (380–430) and a long chain structure, interacts with the polar groups of PIBM molecules during coagulation, affecting the gel network structure and ultimately increasing the thickness of the organic film on the surface of the dried green bodies. This increase in the thickness was proportional to the amount of PEG added. It can be concluded that when using ceramic powder with small particle sizes, polar groups (–OH) are needed not only to improve the mobility of PIBM chains but also to cover long chain sections on the surface of the particles to alleviate the hard contact between particles, and PEG can serve both roles. For nano powder, the brittleness problem is more prominent with a large specific surface area, and a relatively large amount of PEG is needed to improve the plasticity of the green bodies.

The green bodies prepared from the TM-DAR powder were sintered at 1300 ℃ for 6 h. Upon the addition of 1 wt% PEG (3.960 g/cm3), there was no decrease in ceramic density compared to ceramics without plasticizers (3.958 g/cm3). Considering the green bodies derived from the S30CR powder and incorporating 5 wt% PEG, the ceramic density slightly decreased from 3.554 to 3.523 g/cm3 after sintering at 1500 ℃ for 6 h. This decrease in the ceramic density can be attributed to the increased use of plasticizers, which led to a decrease in the bulk density (Fig. 3(c)). Upon increasing the sintering temperature to 1550 ℃, the ceramic density with 5 wt% PEG increased to 3.575 g/cm3, which was very close to that of no plasticizers (3.577 g/cm3).

SEM images of ceramic fracture surfaces with and without plasticizers are presented in Fig. 14. For ceramics made from AES-11 powder and TM-DAR powder, there were no obvious differences in fracture microstructure between ceramics with and without plasticizers (Figs. 14(a)–14(d)). For ceramics made from the S30CR powder, 5 wt% PEG resulted in a small number of pores in the fracture surface of the ceramics (Figs. 14(e) and 14(f)). After the sintering temperature increased to 1550 ℃, those pores were eliminated, and the ceramic fracture surfaces with and without plasticizers were significantly similar (Figs. 14(g) and 14(h)).

Consequently, it can be inferred that glycerol and PEG had negligible impacts on the sintering characteristics of green bodies derived from various particle sizes.

## 4 Conclusions

Two plasticizers, glycerol and PEG, were employed to enhance the plasticity of green bodies fabricated from ceramic powder with submicron to nanoscale particle sizes. The improvement in plasticity was quantified by analyzing the flexural stress‒strain curve in conjunction with impact toughness. The particle size of the ceramic powder plays a critical role in determining the appropriate selection of plasticizers. For submicron powder, glycerol with a low addition and shorter molecular chains had a more pronounced plasticizing effect. As the particle size decreased, the PEG with a longer molecular chain enhanced the plasticity. An increase in the amount was required for nanoscale powder. The particle sizes of 0.45 μm, 0.18 μm, and 50 nm showed the most beneficial improvement in the plasticity with the addition of 1 wt% glycerol, 1 wt% PEG, and 5 wt% PEG, respectively. The drilling test revealed that there was nearly no cracking around the holes in the green bodies with plasticizers. The results indicated that suitable plasticizers could affect the interaction between molecules appropriately to reduce the viscosity of slurries and alleviate the hard contact between particles, thereby ameliorating the brittleness of green bodies. Additionally, plasticizers had little effect on the sintering characteristics of the green bodies.
