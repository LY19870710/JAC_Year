## 1 Introduction

The increasing complexity of electromagnetic environments underscores the increasing research importance of advanced microwave absorption materials [1−3]. These materials achieve efficient dissipation of electromagnetic energy within target regions through synergistic regulation of impedance matching characteristics and electromagnetic wave attenuation mechanisms [4−6]. Owing to its ability to precisely modulate the electromagnetic parameters and microstructure of materials, atomic doping technology has emerged as a critical technical pathway for expanding the operational bandwidth of absorbing materials [7−9].

ABO3 perovskite materials exhibit exceptional electromagnetic parameter tunability and structural controllability due to adjustable A/B-site cations, positioning them as ideal candidate systems for high-performance electromagnetic wave absorbers [10,11]. Studies have demonstrated that rational ion doping strategies significantly enhance their absorption performance. Researchers led by Li reported a Nd-doped BiFeO3 system achieving a reflection loss (RL) of −42 dB with 75% X-band effective coverage (RL 11] and Yuan *et al*. [12] developed LaCo1−*x*Fe*x*O3, which exhibited a maximum RL of −38.99 dB and an effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) of 4.48 GHz at a 3 mm thickness. From the perspective of loss mechanisms, the superior absorption efficacy of these perovskite absorbers is predominantly attributed to dielectric loss. However, achieving broader-band microwave absorption necessitates the construction of synergistic effects among multiple loss mechanisms [13,14]. A/B-site codoping in perovskites has emerged as an efficient strategy for the synchronous regulation of electrical/magnetic losses and polarization dissipation, effectively overcoming the performance bottleneck of single-mechanism systems [15]. This approach enables concurrent optimization of the complex permittivity and permeability without complicating the synthesis process while simultaneously inducing defect-mediated polarization [16,17]. The methodology overcomes the “thick-layer-narrow-band” limitation of conventional materials, significantly enhancing their practical application value [18,19].

Notably, the YCoO3 perovskite offers an ideal crystallographic foundation for A/B-site dual doping because of the favorable ionic radius match between Y3+ (0.90 Å) and Co3+ (0.545 Å), enabling the formation of highly stable orthorhombic phase structures [20,21]. Furthermore, YCoO3 demonstrates unique advantages through oxygen vacancy-mediated Co3+/Co4+ redox cycling, which enhances oxygen ion mobility and microwave absorption efficiency [22,23]. Given the current limitations in the loss mechanisms and EAB of perovskite absorbers, systematic investigations of the microwave absorption characteristics of A/B site-codoped YCoO3 systems hold significant value.

This work explored the optimal B-site doping concentration on the basis of our preliminary experiments that determined the optimal A-site Sr doping ratio (Y0.9Sr0.1CoO3). Y0.9Sr0.1Co1−*x*Fe*x*O3 (*x* = 0–0.2) perovskite absorbers were successfully synthesized via the sol–gel method. The influence of precisely controlling the Fe3+ doping concentration on the microstructure, electronic structure, and electromagnetic response characteristics of a material was systematically investigated. Co-doping A/B-site ions effectively increased the polarization loss and magnetic loss performance in the YCoO3 matrix. The synergistic effect of multiple loss mechanisms broadened the EAB while maintaining a thin thickness. First-principles calculations further validated the mechanism through which the Fe3+ doping concentration governs the absorption performance. This study establishes structure‒property relationships for perovskite absorbers, revealing the critical balance between the Fe3+ doping concentration, impedance matching, and attenuation capacity, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for developing ultrathin high-performance microwave absorbing materials.

### 2.1 Preparation of Y0.9Sr0.1Co1−<i>x</i>Fe<i>x</i>O3

The sol–gel synthesis of Y0.9Sr0.1Co1−*x*Fe*x*O3 (*x* = 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, and 0.2) perovskites was conducted as follows. According to the ionic ratio given by the chemical formula Y0.9Sr0.1Co1−*x*Fe*x*O3, analytically pure Y(NO3)3·6H2O, Co(NO3)3·6H2O, Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, Sr(NO3)2, and anhydrous citric acid with a molar ratio of 1:1.5 to metal ions were weighed, where the total molar number of A-site metal ions was 0.05 mol and other ion concentrations were calculated according to the chemical formula ratio. Each group of compounds was dissolved in 50 mL of distilled water and labeled FS-0, FS-1, FS-2, FS-3, or FS-4. Then, the pH of all the solutions was adjusted to 5–5.5 with an ammonia solution. After stirring uniformly at room temperature, the solution was continuously heated and stirred at 90 °C, and the stirring speed was reduced with increasing solution viscosity, resulting in the formation of a gel after approximately 4 h. The gel was dried in a blast oven at 110 °C for 12 h to obtain a dry gel. The dried gel was placed in a muffle furnace, heated to 300 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min, held for 2 h, and then naturally cooled to obtain a fluffy black solid. Next, the black solid was placed in a muffle furnace for a second sintering, heated to 900 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min, and held for 12 h. Finally, perovskite nanocrystals with five different iron doping amounts were obtained.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The morphological structure was characterized via a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; Hitachi S4800, Hitachi, Japan). The thermal decomposition process and thermal stability were analyzed via a thermogravimetric analyzer (TG; NETZSCHSTA449F3, Germany) from 30 to 900 °C under an air atmosphere at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The phase structure was determined via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed with a spectrometer (AXIS Ultra, Kratos, UK) with an Al Kα source gun. The electromagnetic parameters were measured by a vector network analyzer (VNA; PNA N5244A, Agilent, USA) with a paraffin-to-sample ratio of 1 : 3. The magnetic hysteresis loop of the sample was analyzed via a 7404 VSM system (Lake Shore). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images of the samples were acquired via a field-emission HRTEM (FE-HRTEM; Talos F200X G2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

### 2.3 Calculation of the electronic structure

First-principles calculations are performed via the CASTEP program code, which is based on the first-principles plane-wave pseudopotential approach to the density of states. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) is used to calculate the exchange correlation function during the Perdew-wang91 (PW91) implementation. To increase computational efficiency while maintaining high computational accuracy, the overlapping-target-generation ultrasoft pseudopotential (OTFG) was used. Convergence tests were used in the choice of plane-wave truncation energies and K-grid point parameters to minimize the energy values. The calculations are performed using 2*a* × 2*b* × *c* super primary cells.

### 2.4 Radar cross section (RCS) simulation and calculation

The RCS simulation of samples under far-field conditions was carried out with CST Studio Suite 2021. In the simulation process, the simulation model was composed of an upper absorption layer and a bottom perfect electric conductor (PEC) layer, and the thicknesses of the absorption layer and PEC layer were set as 1.8 and 5 mm, respectively. In addition, the dimensions (length and width) of the model were 100 mm × 100 mm. The established models were placed on the plane, and a linearly polarized plane electromagnetic (EM) wave was added, which was incident from the positive direction of *Z*-axis to the negative direction of the *Z*-axis. Moreover, the direction of electric polarization propagated along the *X*-axis. The far-field monitoring frequency was chosen as 13.72 GHz. The RCS values (* σ (dB·m2)) can be calculated via Eq. (1) [24−26]:

(1) σ(dB⋅m2)=10log(4πSλ2|EsEi|)2

where S* is the area of the established model, *λ* represents the wavelength of the EM waves, and *E*s and *E*i are the electric field intensity and incident EM waves of the scattered wave, respectively.

### 3.1 Microscopic morphology and composition of doped perovskite

Figure 1(a) illustrates the process of stirring and heating the solution to form a wet gel, heating and drying the gel to form a dry gel, and finally calcining it to form a chalcogenide powder. Figures 1(b)–1(e) show SEM images of Y0.9Sr0.1Co1−*x*Fe*x*O3 chalcogenide materials. As shown, all Y0.9Sr0.1Co1−*x*Fe*x*O3 chalcogenide materials exhibit similar nanoscale irregular particle morphologies. From the grain size distribution and calculated average grain diameter plot (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) of the samples, the variation in particle size with increasing Fe3+ doping content can be intuitively observed. When the Fe3+ doping content is 5%, the microstructure of the prepared FS-1 sample distinctly agglomerates, with an average particle size of only 0.18 μm. The progressive substitution of Fe3+ in Y0.9Sr0.1Co1−*x*Fe*x*O3 perovskite (*x* = 0.1–0.2) drives the morphological evolution of the system, manifesting as a reduced particle aggregation tendency and enhanced grain growth [27]. When the Fe3+ doping content reaches 0.2, the prepared FS-4 material possesses the largest particle size among all the samples, with an average particle size of 0.35 μm. The observed reduction in particle agglomeration could be attributed to modified interparticle interactions, where an increased dopant concentration alters the surface energy landscape, consequently weakening the cohesive forces between adjacent particles [28,29]. In addition, the addition of dopant ions may change the energy state of the system, and the increase in particle size with Fe3+ doping may be due to the increase in the free energy of nucleation, which makes the crystals more inclined to grow on the existing nuclei; thus, the particle size gradually becomes larger [30,31]. Figure S2 in the ESM shows the SEM image of Y0.9Sr0.1CoO3 before it was doped with Fe3+. The molecular structure diagram shows that more Co3+ is replaced by Fe3+ with increasing doping. To summarize, the perovskite samples prepared by Fe3+ doping have varying agglomerate sizes, basically in the nanoscale range. Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis of the surface elemental composition, as presented in Fig. S3 in the ESM, provides further evidence for the successful incorporation of Fe3+ ions.

### 3.2 Microwave absorption performance

Figure 3 shows the electromagnetic parameters and loss factor plots of the Y0.9Sr0.1Co1−*x*Fe*x*O3 samples. The real (*ε'*) and imaginary (*ε''*) parts of the complex permittivity of the doped chalcogenide are shown in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b), respectively. The *ε'* of the material decreases and then increases as the doping amount increases. Among them, the *ε'* values of FS-2, ranging from 7.77 to 15.85, are the smallest among all the samples, whereas those of FS-4, ranging from 9.66 to 27.34, are the largest among all the samples, indicating that FS-4 possesses the strongest electrical energy storage capacity [37]. In addition, the *ε'* of the material tends to increase with increasing Fe3+ doping. FS-1 and FS-2 exhibit relatively smooth variations in *ε''* with frequency, whereas FS-3 and FS-4 show monotonic decreases in *ε''* values as the frequency increases. The *ε''* values range from 7.25 to 14.32, which is the largest among the four samples, indicating the strongest ability to dissipate electrical energy. The electrical loss tangent of the FS material samples in Fig. 3(c) increases with increasing doping concentration. All the samples present an increasing trend in the tangent of the electrical loss with increasing frequency. When *ε'* decreases rapidly due to weakened polarization, *ε''* decreases more slowly under the influence of conductive loss and interfacial polarization, resulting in an increase in electrical loss tangent with frequency. FS-4 has the highest tangent of electrical loss, which again proves that FS-4 has the strongest electrical loss capability.

Figures 3(d) and 3(e) show real (μ′*) and imaginary (*μ′′*) plots of the complex permeability of the FS samples with different iron doping levels, respectively [38]. The *μ′* values of the four samples are almost the same in the 2–7 GHz band, and the highest *μ′* value is found for FS-4 in the 7–18 GHz band. This may be because when the Fe doping is 0.2, there is a strong superexchange interaction in the chalcogenide, leading to the ordered arrangement of the magnetic moments, and the degree of magnetization increases, which increases *μ'*. Moreover, the *μ''* values of the four samples are also almost the same in the 2–10 GHz band, but the *μ''* values are the largest for FS-1 and FS-2 in the 10–18 GHz band. The *μ''* results show multiple relaxation resonance peaks, and the peaks correspond to the extreme values of the material’s magnetic loss at that frequency, indicating that the alternating magnetic field energy is efficiently converted into thermal energy through natural resonance [39]. Figure S7 in the ESM presents the room-temperature hysteresis loops of samples with varying Fe-doping concentrations, demonstrating the impact of Fe-ion doping on the magnetic properties. All the hysteresis loops in Figs. S7(a)–S7(d) in the ESM exhibit typical ferromagnetic characteristics, including distinct coercivity (*H*c) and remanence. The hysteresis loop of FS-2 shows a steeper slope and more abrupt saturation, indicating easier magnetization saturation. As the Fe doping concentration increases, the magnetization intensity (*M*s) of the FS-series samples initially increases and subsequently decreases, suggesting that an optimal Fe doping level promotes *M*s enhancement. Specifically, Fig. S7(e) in the ESM reveals that FS-2 achieves the highest *M*s value (8.36 emu/g), significantly exceeding those of the other samples. Generally, a higher *M*s implies that greater energy is required for magnetic moment flipping, thereby shifting the natural resonance frequency toward higher frequencies. This correlation validates the contribution of magnetic loss to the broad EAB of FS-2 in the high-frequency range. However, no significant variation in *H*c was observed with increasing doping level, indicating that Fe doping has negligible effects on hysteresis loss (Fig. S7(f) in the ESM). The negative *μ''* values observed in all the samples indicate that the magnetic loss of eSlectromagnetic waves is lower than the magnetic loss required by the medium’s permeability. This phenomenon may arise from increased eddy current losses due to elevated conductivity, thereby affecting electromagnetic energy transmission [40]. The results for the magnetic loss factor are almost identical to those for *μ''* (Fig. 3(f)). Among them, samples FS-1 and FS-2 have higher magnetic loss tangent values, implying that these two samples have optimal magnetic dissipation performance. Comparative analysis revealed that the dielectric loss component is greater than the magnetic loss component across all the samples, suggesting a dominant contribution from electrical polarization mechanisms in electromagnetic wave attenuation [23].

On the basis of the transmission line theory framework, the sample’s RL characteristics were mathematically derived from Eqs. (2) and (3) [41,42]:

(2) Zin=Z0μr/εrtanh(j(2πfdc))μrεr

(3) RL(dB)=20log|Zin−Z0Zin+Z0|

where * Zin denotes the input impedance,  Z0 is the impedance of free air, μr* is the relative complex magnetic permeability of the material, εr is the relative complex dielectric constant of the material, *d* denotes the thickness of the absorber, * f is the EM wave frequency, and c* represents the velocity of light. RL serves as a critical metric for assessing microwave absorption performance, quantifying electromagnetic energy attenuation through material interactions. The negative correlation between the RL values and absorption efficiency demonstrates that more negative values indicate enhanced absorption capability. When the RL value is less than 10 dB, 90% of the energy is absorbed, so the part of the RL less than 10 dB is defined as the effective bandwidth. In our preliminary experiments, Y1−*x*Sr*x*CoO3 samples were prepared with various Sr doping concentrations (*x* = 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3) to identify the optimal doping ratio. Among the samples, Y0.9Sr0.1CoO3 (*x* = 0.1) exhibited the best microwave absorption performance, achieving an EAB of 4.91 GHz at a thickness of only 1.3 mm (Fig. S8 in the ESM). However, further increasing the Sr doping concentration (*x* > 0.1) progressively narrowed the EAB while shifting it toward greater thicknesses. On the basis of these systematic investigations, we successfully determined the optimal A-site doping concentration. Figures 4(a)–4(d) are the visual representation of the variation in RL with thickness and frequency for chalcogenide samples with different Fe3+ doping amounts in the form of 3D color fills. When the doping amount is less than or equal to 0.1, the samples exhibit obvious microwave absorption bands, and the absorption bands broaden with increasing doping amount. When the doping amount is greater than 0.1, the wave-absorbing property of the sample suddenly deteriorates, and the absorption band gradually narrows with increasing doping amount. Figures 4(e)–4(h) show the RL contour plots of the samples, which clearly demonstrate the EAB of different samples. Among them, FS-1 and FS-2 showed significant microwave absorption, whereas FS-3 and FS-4 showed weak absorption efficacy. The sample with the best microwave absorption performance is FS-2, which achieves an EAB of 5.71 GHz (11.36–17.07) at a very thin thickness of 1.8 mm. In addition, the effective absorption region of all four samples shifted to thinner thicknesses as the frequency increased. The RL is a combination of the impedance matching and loss mechanism of the absorbing material. Therefore, the concept of attenuation constants (*α*) is introduced here, and *α* is calculated as Eq. (4) [43]:

(4) α=2πfc×(μ″ε″−μ′ε′)+(μ″ε″−μ′ε′)2+(μ′ε″+μ″ε′)2

where α* integrally reflects the electromagnetic energy dissipation capability of the material (Fig. S9 in the ESM), and the *α* values of all the samples increase with frequency, where FS-4 has the largest *α* value (74.73–423.71). However, high *α* only ensures rapid attenuation of the electromagnetic wave upon entry, and good wave-absorbing performance also requires surface impedance matching to minimize reflections [44]. Figure S10 in the ESM shows the impedance matching characteristics of the samples, with FS-1 and FS-2 exhibiting optimal matching conditions that facilitate deeper electromagnetic wave penetration. In contrast, FS-3 and FS-4 demonstrate significant impedance mismatches despite their elevated attenuation coefficients (*α*), resulting in substantial wave reflection at the air‒material interface, which negates their intrinsic loss capabilities [45]. The microwave absorption performance quantified by RL is presented in Figs. 4(i)–4(l). Notably, FS-1 achieves exceptional absorption (RL = −55.35 dB) at a 2.9 mm thickness, whereas FS-2 maintains competitive performance (RL = −47.18 dB) at a reduced thickness of merely 1.7 mm, demonstrating superior thin-thickness absorption characteristics. Figure S11 in the ESM shows the microwave absorption performance of several reported perovskite-based materials. The doped perovskite materials prepared in this work exhibit excellent microwave absorption performance even at low thicknesses.

### 3.3 Electronic structure and loss mechanism

To determine the changes in the charge distribution in the crystal due to Fe3+ ion doping, Figs. 5(a) and 5(b) show the results of differential charge density calculations before and after Fe doping, respectively. In particular, the red region is the electron aggregation region, and the blue region is the electron dissipation region. The inhomogeneous electron distribution of Fe atomic sites is significantly stronger than that of Co atomic sites. Electron inhomogeneity may induce local electric field gradients within the material, which can synergize with the microwave field to enhance dipole polarization responses. Simultaneously, the local electric fields formed by charge inhomogeneity can also promote dielectric loss [46]. Moreover, Figs. 5(c) and 5(d) show the density of fractional wave states (DOS) of the Co 3d orbitals before and after Fe3+ doping, with a Fermi energy level of 0 eV, and the *y*-axis positive and negative directions represent spin-up and spin-down, respectively. As a result of Fe3+ doping, the DOS of Co 3d is transformed from a relatively symmetric result to a clear asymmetry of the spin-up and spin-down electronic states [47]. The incorporation of Fe3+ ions significantly enhances the magnetic characteristics of the host material. In addition, the DOSs of the system before and after doping are given in Fig. S12 in the ESM. The s, p, and d orbital electron densities of the samples significantly increase after Fe3+ doping, suggesting that doping facilitates intramolecular electron leakage, which improves the electron mobility and enhances the microwave absorption properties [48]. In summary, Fe3+/Sr2+ codoping synergistically influences the microscopic electronic structure of a material, contributing to multiple microwave loss mechanisms. The real and imaginary components of the complex permittivity can be interpreted via Debye theory [49,50]. The semicircles observed in the *ε'*–*ε''* curves may correspond to Cole‒Cole semicircles associated with Debye relaxation processes [51]. Figures 5(e)–5(h) display the Cole‒Cole plots for all the samples, which reveal a decrease in the semicircle quantity with increasing Fe3+ doping concentration. Notably, FS-1 and FS-2 exhibit multiple arcs in their Cole‒Cole plots, possibly due to stronger relaxation-induced polarization losses in these samples. Additionally, straight-line segments appear in all the samples’ curves, confirming the presence of conduction losses [52].

Combined with the above analysis, the feasible electromagnetic wave dissipation mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 6. The electrical loss primarily consists of conductive loss and dielectric loss, with the latter originating from material polarization, including electronic/ionic displacement polarization, dipolar polarization, and interfacial polarization [53]. In the centimeter-wave band (2–18 GHz), electronic/ionic displacement polarizations can be neglected because of their extremely short formation time [54]. First, the effective doping of Fe3+ enhances the electron mobility in the Y0.9Sr0.1Co1−x*Fe*x*O3 perovskite, and the conductive network in the material results in conductivity loss. In addition, owing to the introduction of the inhomogeneous distribution and lattice distortion of many electrons by doping, the dipole polarization loss in the material is greatly increased, dissipating electromagnetic wave in the form of thermal energy. Moreover, the material contains abundant heterogeneous interfaces, such as particle‒particle interfaces and particle‒air interfaces, which increase the interfacial polarization loss of the material. Finally, Fe3+ doping significantly improves the magnetic properties of perovskite, enhances the magnetic loss, and optimizes the impedance matching of the material at a suitable doping amount. In conclusion, Fe3+ doping synergistically improved the electromagnetic wave response properties of the Y0.9Sr0.1Co1−*x*Fe*x*O3 perovskite in terms of microstructure, electron distribution, and magnetic loss, contributing to the loss of the material to electromagnetic waves.

### 3.4 Simulation of the radar cross section

To verify the attenuation performance of Y0.9Sr0.1Co1−*x*Fe*x*O3 in practical applications, the RCS results of the samples are shown in Fig. 7. Simulating the RCS values of coatings at various incident angles via CST software supplements conventional physical testing by effectively reflecting actual far-field microwave absorption performance. Additionally, simulating real electromagnetic environments enhances test reliability [55]. Radar systems determine target parameters by processing electromagnetic wave reflections [56]. Advanced absorption coatings engineered with specific impedance matching properties can suppress these reflections, leading to measurable RCS reduction in radar signature analysis [57]. Figures 7(a)–7(e) show the 3D reflected signal intensities of the PEC blocks and the 1.8 mm-coated blocks with four samples, namely, FS-1, FS-2, FS-3, and FS-4, respectively. Relative to the PEC reference, the simulated reflection signals exhibit notable attenuation across all the samples, which is consistent with the electromagnetic absorption characteristics. The RCS spatial distribution in the *x*–O–*z* plane (Fig. 7(f)) reveals analogous variation patterns among samples, with FS-2 demonstrating markedly reduced values. In the Cartesian plot of the RCS values, the FS-2 sample demonstrates not only strong electromagnetic wave dissipation capability at larger incident angles but also excellent absorption performance at smaller angles. At the 0° incidence angle, the measured RCS value is −8.79 dB·m2, which is 25.34 dB·m2 lower than that of PEC, demonstrating significantly superior performance compared with other samples. These observations confirm the synergistic attenuation mechanism involving conductive dissipation, dielectric polarization, and magnetic loss components, leading to effective electromagnetic energy conversion and a diminished radar signature.

## 4 Conclusions

This work systematically elucidates the modulatory effects of Fe3+ doping on the microwave absorption properties of Y0.9Sr0.1Co1−*x*Fe*x*O3 perovskites. The attenuation performance is synergistically tuned through three interconnected pathways: enhanced electron mobility, optimized lattice polarization and magnetic loss, and modified interfacial states. Crucially, superior microwave attenuation alone is insufficient and requires complementary impedance matching characteristics. Under the concerted regulation of these mechanisms, the FS-1 sample (*x* = 0.1) demonstrates exceptional performance, achieving an RL of −47.18 dB with an EAB of 5.71 GHz at a minimal thickness below 1.8 mm. This study not only addresses the research gap regarding YCoO3-based materials in microwave absorption but also provides pivotal theoretical foundations and practical guidelines for developing novel absorbers that integrate ultrathin configurations, broadband coverage, and environmental robustness.
