## 1 Introduction

Energy is foundational to human existence, and as societal development and industrial production advance, the demand for energy continues to grow. In this context, advancing a clean, low-carbon, and environmentally friendly renewable energy system is one of the developmental goals of our country [1]. Solar energy, as a clean and sustainable resource, has emerged as an excellent solution for addressing energy needs. However, in addition to inherent efficiency limitations, the high manufacturing costs associated with traditional solar cells highlight the urgent need for the development of materials that can effectively harness multiple energy sources. These materials should not only maintain consistent conversion efficiencies across various energy types but also demonstrate synergistic enhancements in specific applications [2].

Ferroelectric materials, recognized for their reversible spontaneous polarization, play crucial roles in various fields, such as microelectronics, optoelectronics, integrated optics, and micro-electromechanical systems. As a result, they have become a focal point of global research on new functional materials [3−5]. Recent research has demonstrated that the photovoltaic effect in ferroelectric ceramics enables the generation of open-circuit voltages exceeding the bandgap energy of the material, thereby overcoming traditional limitations imposed by the bandgap. This discovery highlights the substantial potential of ferroelectric materials in solar energy conversion, reigniting interest in the long-standing topic of “ferroelectric photovoltaics” [6]. Perovskite materials based on bismuth sodium titanate show strong ferroelectric properties; however, research on their photovoltaic performance has emerged more recently, highlighting their potential for further investigation. While the high bandgap of these materials significantly restricts solar conversion efficiency [7,8], doping modifications have proven effective in enhancing their ferroelectric photovoltaic performance [9]. Recent experiments suggest that B-site doping in perovskites is a promising approach for achieving narrower bandgaps, with first-principles calculations elucidating the critical role of doping ions in reducing the bandgap [10]. With engineered bandgap modulation, these systems exhibit excellent visible light absorption and improved short-circuit current density (*J*sc) in photovoltaic applications. Ferroelectric semiconductors, characterized by ABO3 perovskite structure, uniquely combine ferroelectricity and semiconductivity, have attracted significant interest from researchers because of their distinct electrical and photovoltaic properties. Typically, semiconductor materials, when subjected to external fields (such as light, sound, heat, or electricity), experience varying degrees of valence band electron transitions, leaving vacancies in their original positions and forming electron–hole pairs. Ferroelectric semiconductors are no exception. The lower bandgap of these materials facilitates the generation of a greater number of electrons–hole pairs following bandgap tuning [11]. Notably, the carriers generated in ferroelectric semiconductors can be efficiently separated and transported under the influence of the intrinsic directional electric field established by self-polarization, resulting in a photovoltaic response. The presence of a depolarization field significantly enhances the efficiency and lifetime of charge separation. Furthermore, the effective field in ferroelectric semiconductors is an order of magnitude larger than that found in conventional semiconductor p-n junctions, with the photovoltaic voltage being 2–4 orders of magnitude greater. This makes ferroelectric semiconductors promising candidates for applications in optical sensors, photodrivers, and solar cells [12].

By substituting K for Na in Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3, the resulting compound Bi0.5Na0.4K0.1TiO3 (BNKT) demonstrates enhanced piezoelectric and ferroelectric properties, which have attracted significant attention in recent research. Consequently, a substrate system designated BNKT was developed by doping Sm/Co ions at the A and B sites. The cation (Sm2+) is introduced at the A-site to provide a compensatory charge for polar distortion, thereby ensuring that the system maintains its ferroelectricity while preventing excessive leakage currents due to increased oxygen vacancies. Conversely, the Co3+ ion decreases the electronegativity disparity in the B–O bond of the perovskite structure, generating electronic states within the bandgap.

The good *P–E* loops and remnant polarization under ±70 kV/cm at room temperature still remained in the (1−*x*)BNKT–*x*SmCoO3 ceramics, where *P* and *E* represent polarization intensity and applied electric field intensity, respectively, as displayed in Fig. 1(a), which is superior to typical semiconducting ferroelectric ceramics such as KBNNO [10]. By Sm/Co doping, the direct bandgaps from 3.09 to 2.20 eV in the ultraviolet–visible spectra are presented in Figs. 1(b) and 1(c) [13]. In this article, the schematic idea and the results are simply illustrated in Fig. 1(d). Figure 1(e) shows the optical *J*sc of 44 nA·cm−2 (*x* = 0.1) for all the compositions under AM1.5G illumination (100 mW·cm−2), 10 V, and 25 °C. By the combination of light, and electric, thermal, mechanical, and magnetic fields, further analysis on the photocurrent density of 0.1SmCo sample reveals a strong multi-field influence on the photovoltaic behavior. Figs. 1(f) and 1(g) demonstrate that as the electric field increases from 10 to 100 V (light field (*L*) ≈ 100 mW·cm−2, thermal field at 25 °C), *J*sc increases from 44 to 160 nA·cm−2, reaching 269 nA·cm−2 at 300 mW·cm−2. Figures 1(h)–1(j) show that *J*sc reaches 924 nA·cm−2 as the thermal field increases to 100 °C under a light field of 300 mW·cm−2 and a bias field of 100 V. Moreover, applying a mechanical force of 5 N reduces *J*sc to 92 nA·cm−2, while exposure to a magnetic field (up to 300 GS, where GS stands for magnetic field strength) decreases it to 165 nA·cm−2. Figure 1(k) illustrates that under combined light and electric fields, *J*sc reaches 269 nA·cm−2 and increases further to 924 nA·cm−2 with the addition of a thermal field, indicating a synergistic enhancement effect. Conversely, mechanical and magnetic fields negatively impact *J*sc, reducing it to 92 and 165 nA·cm−2, respectively. These findings suggest that the synergistic application of multiple fields can significantly modulate the photovoltaic properties of BNKT ferroelectric ceramics.

## 2 Experimental

Weakly magnetic metallic ceramics designated as (1−*x*)BNKT–*x*SmCoO3 can be synthesized through a solid-state method, where the *x* values are 0.00, 0.02, 0.04, 0.06, 0.08, and 0.1. The primary raw materials utilized in the synthesis included Bi2O3 (99.90%, Aladdin, China), Na2CO3 (99.80%, XILONG, China), K2CO3 (99%, XILONG, China), TiO2 (99.00%, Aladdin, China), Sm2O3 (99.99%, Macklin, China), and Co2O3 (99%, Macklin, China). These materials were accurately weighed using a balance with a precision of four decimal places, placed in ethanol-containing samples, dried for 12 h, and then pulverized and sieved to ensure a uniform particle size. The powder was pre-sintered at 900 °C for 12 h, after which 5 wt% polyvinyl alcohol was added, followed by further drying and grinding to create fine powder. The powder was then compacted into discs under a pressure of 10 MPa. The discs were heated at 600 °C for 2 h to remove polyvinyl alcohol and then sintered at approximately 1150 °C to achieve 95% of theoretical density, resulting in the final ceramic structure for testing and characterization.

For electrochemical characterization, the samples were polished to a thickness of 350 µm using a metallurgical polishing machine and subsequently finished with 2000-grit sandpaper. Silver paste was applied to one smooth side of the sample, which was sintered at 580 °C. Subsequently, a layer of indium tin oxide (ITO) film was deposited on the opposite smooth surface using pulsed laser deposition (PLD) technology with an excimer laser (COMPEX Pro 205, Coherent Co., USA). The current (*J*)–voltage (*V)* curve under simulated solar conditions were analyzed using a SourceMeter (Keithley 2410, Tektronix, Inc., USA). For more precise impedance measurements, gold paste (batch number 54H-18030) was applied to both sides of the samples and then sintered. A precision impedance analyzer (4294A, Agilent, USA) was employed to test the complex impedance of the samples across various temperatures, with data fitting performed using the Zview software.

The phase composition and crystallographic space groups were analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Smart-lab 9 kW, Japan), and the XRD data were further refined using FullProf software. A high-resolution laser Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba, Japan) was used to detect the Raman scattering spectra. The surface morphology was examined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; FEI Quanta 450 FEG, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Additionally, polar microregions and finer surface morphology details were investigated using a transmission electron microscope (TEM; FEI Talos 200S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). An ultraviolet (UV)–visible–near infrared spectrometer (UV-3600 Plus, Shimadzu, Japan) was utilized to obtain the UV absorption spectra of the materials, and the band gap values were calculated using a band gap estimation formula. The changes in the metal composition and oxidation states were analyzed via an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; K-Alpha, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

## 3 Results and discussion

In order to examine how Sm/Co co-doping affects the BNKT matrix structure, the XRD patterns of the *x*SmCo ferroelectric ceramics were analyzed, as illustrated in Fig. 2(a). The findings show that the doped samples maintain a consistent perovskite structure, with no secondary impurity phases detected. This observation suggests that a significant amount of doped ions successfully dissolve into the BNKT ceramics. In pure BNKT, only a single peak is noted at approximately 40°, but with the incorporation of Sm/Co ions, this peak gradually shifts to higher angles, and a second peak emerges. A review of the PDF data reveals that a phase transition occurs within the lattice, involving a transformation from a tetragonal phase to coexistence of tetragonal and rhombohedral phases. This transition induces lattice distortion and reduces the lattice dimensions, with the degree of distortion intensifying as the doping level increases [14].

As depicted in Fig. 2(b), the Raman spectra of the *x*SmCo ferroelectric ceramics in the 200–450 cm−1 range exhibit the merging of modes at 260, 279, and 295 cm−1 into a broad peak, indicative of the formation of a solid solution resulting from ion substitution in BNKT [15]. As the doping content increases, the Raman scattering peaks progressively broaden, potentially indicating phase transition from tetragonal to rhombohedral [16]. Notably, the peaks at 260, 279, 295, and 599 cm−1 shift to lower wavenumbers with increased doping, primarily associated with Ti–O bond stretching vibrations (similar to the BO6 octahedron), which suggests lattice distortions resulting from the incorporation of Sm/Co into the BNKT lattice. These findings align with the XRD results.

As shown in Fig. 2(c), when the doping level is below 1.5%, the grain size is larger, and the specific surface area is smaller. For doping levels exceeding 1.5%, the grain size begins to decrease, and owing to the excessive presence of samarium on the surface, the specific surface area decreases as well. Consequently, quantum size effects lead to a shift in the absorption region towards the blue region, reducing the absorption of visible light [17]. Among these samples, the 0.1SmCo sample demonstrates the strongest absorbance in the visible light range. The *x*SmCo (*x* = 0.1) sample exhibits a pronounced absorption peak at approximately 360 nm, attributed to the substitution of Ti at the B-site with transition metal Co ions, which alters the valence states as doping levels increase. This change correlates with the bandgap states influenced by the multivalent nature of Co ions, supporting recent findings regarding transition-metal-doped ferroelectric materials [18−20].

To investigate the behavior of doped ions, XPS analysis was conducted on the *x*SmCo ceramic (*x* = 0.1) samples. The fitted XPS spectra for Co ions are presented in Fig. 2(d). There are two noticeable peaks that match Co 2p1/2 and Co 2p3/2. The binding energies of the first doublet are 779.68 and 795.24 eV, indicating the presence of Co3+ ions. The second doublet, located at 781.91 and 797.65 eV, signifies the presence of Co2+ ions [21,22]. The area ratio of the fitted Co2+ to Co3+ ions is 1 : 2.5, suggesting the occurrence of oxygen vacancies, as elucidated by the Kröger–Vink equation (Reaction (1)):

To gain a deeper understanding of the microstructures of the ceramics, the samples were characterized using SEM, TEM, high-resolution (HR-TEM), and selected area electron diffraction (SAED). SEM images of the *x*SmCo ceramics (*x* = 0.00, 0.02, 0.1), depicted in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b), reveal well-defined grains with minimal intergranular voids. In the HR-TEM image of Fig. 3(f) at *x* = 0.1, the interplanar spacing (*d*) is measured to be approximately 0.14 nm, which is slightly smaller than the tetragonal *P*4*bm* (004) plane spacing. This contraction is likely due to the incorporation of Co and Sm ions, attributed to differences in ionic radii. The SAED pattern in Fig. 3(g) clearly shows diffraction rings, indicating a polycrystalline structure with good crystallinity [26]. The energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectrum in Fig. 3(h) confirms the solid solution of various ions within the BNKT base crystal.

To elucidate the principles of the photovoltaic effect under various fields, a macroscopic photovoltaic schematic has been constructed. In ferroelectric semiconductor ceramics, several sub-bandgaps are illustrated in Fig. 4(b1), with their photovoltaic effects primarily arising from spontaneous polarization (*P*s) and the separation of light-excited carriers across the bandgap (Fig. 4(b2)). In ferroelectric materials, the open-circuit voltage is not restricted by the bandgap, thus allowing the generation of photo-voltages that can exceed the bandgap, thereby enabling the photovoltaic effect [28]. In examining the microscopic photovoltaic mechanism, the ITO/ferroelectric (FE) layer/Ag configuration establishes an energy band diagram characterized by a Schottky barrier, generating a built-in electric field (*E*bi) from Ag to ITO. Figure 4(c) displays the J–V* curves of the sample with *x* = 0.1 under various electric fields (10–100 V), a light intensity of 100 mW·cm−2, and a thermal field of 25 °C. As the applied electric field intensifies, both *J*sc and *V*oc consistently increase, reaching 160 nA·cm−2 and 7.4 V at 100 V, respectively. This indicates that greater energy is required for electrons to surmount the bandgap. As illustrated in Figs. 4(d1) and 4(d2), upon applying a higher electric field, the carrier concentration in the 0.1SmCo sample increases. Under the bulk photovoltaic effect, when a voltage is applied in a direction parallel to the polarization, the dependence of the photocurrent on the voltage is expressed as Eq. (2):

(2) J=Jsc−(σd+σph)V/L

In this equation, the current (*J*)–voltage (*V*) characteristics exhibit linear behavior, where *L* represents the thickness of the sample, indicating that an increase in the external electric field results in an increase in *J*sc [30]. As illustrated in Fig. 4(d3), the application of voltage raises the Fermi level (*E*Fn) of the minority carrier (electrons), while the Fermi level (*E*Fp) of the holes remains unchanged. As a result, the surface dipole decreases since a voltage drop under an applied voltage occurs there. Moreover, the absorption over the bandgap promotes electron–hole pair formation. As shown in Fig. 4(d4), the microscopic separation of generated carrier pairs in the conduction band leads to an excess of electrons. As a result, the generation of minority carriers significantly shifts *E*Fn, thereby generating an open-circuit photovoltage (*V*oc), which increases with increasing electric field strength [31].

Figure 4(e) illustrates the *J–V* curves under varying light intensities (100–300 mW·cm−2) subjected to an electric field of 100 V and a thermal field of 25 °C. As the light intensity increases, *J*sc reaches its maximum at 300 mW·cm−2, reaching a value of 269 nA·cm−2. However, *V*oc gradually decreases as the light intensity increases. As shown in Figs. 4(f1) and 4(f2), when the incident light frequency is held constant and under the maximum electric field strength, increasing the light intensity enhances the absorption of photon energy by the ceramic semiconductor. This intensified light absorption within the electronic bands generates a greater number of electrons–hole pairs, which are separated by the internal electric field, resulting in an increased *J*sc, as depicted in Figs. 4(f3) and 4(f4) [32]. According to Eq. (3):

(3) Voc=(σd+σph)/GαId

where *σ*d and *σ*ph are the dark conductivity and the photoconductivity of ferroelectric materials, respectively. *G*, *α*, and *I*d represent the device geometry and structural factor, the material's light response coefficient, and the dark current, respectively. As the intensity of the light field increases, the increase in *σ*ph becomes more significant, which in turn leads to a decrease in *V*oc [33].

Complex impedance spectroscopy (CIS) can be utilized to analyze the electrical behavior of polycrystalline ceramics over a wide range of temperatures and frequencies. This allows for the separation of contributions from grains, grain boundaries, and interfacial effects. The changes in the real (*Z'*) and imaginary (*Z"*) parts of the impedance for the 0.1SmCo ferroelectric semiconductor as a function of frequency, measured between 100 Hz and 1 MHz at different temperatures, are shown in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b). At lower frequencies, the *Z'* and *Z"* values decrease with increasing temperature, indicating a decrease in resistance and a negative temperature coefficient effect (NTCR effect), similar to what is observed in semiconductors [34]. This NTC behavior arises from the continuous accumulation of charges at the two interfaces between the ceramics and the electrodes under high-frequency fields, resulting in a conduction behavior similar to that of a parallel-plate capacitor, thus facilitating alternating current (AC) flow [35]. At the tested temperatures, the *Z"* value of 0.1SmCo displays a frequency-dependent peak, which diminishes in intensity and shifts toward higher frequencies as the temperature increases. This peak behavior indicates the presence of relaxation time dispersion, suggesting temperature-dependent dielectric relaxation phenomena within the system [36].

As shown in Figs. 5(c) and 5(d), the CIS of the selected 0.1SmCo sample was measured from 240 to 390 °C. The scatter points represent experimental data, while the solid lines indicate data fitted using ZVIEW software; the corresponding equivalent circuit is illustrated in the inset of Fig. 5(c). The presence of two non-overlapping semicircles in the spectra suggests that multiple relaxation processes occur within the ceramics. These relaxations correspond to the grains (g) and grain boundaries (gb) [37]. The grain boundary contribution is represented by the mid-to-low frequency semicircle, whereas the high-frequency semicircle denotes the grain contribution [38]. As the temperature increases, the radii of the semicircles decrease, indicating an increase in the bulk conductivity [39].

Further analysis of the CIS of the 0.1SmCo sample at 510 °C in both N2 and air environments is presented in Fig. 5(e). Notably, the impedance radius in N2 is considerably larger than that observed in air. This finding suggests that at elevated temperatures, the 0.1SmCo ferroelectric ceramics primarily exhibit a p-type semiconducting behavior, highlighting the critical role of oxygen partial pressure in the conductivity of the ceramics [40,41]. This is because the high oxygen partial pressure inhibits the generation of oxygen vacancies, leading to a decrease in carrier concentration. However, a nitrogen environment with low oxygen partial pressure favors the generation of oxygen vacancies and the formation of intrinsic defect states associated with oxygen vacancies, which enhances the conductivity [42]. The temperature dependence of the conductivity for grains and grain boundaries is illustrated in the Arrhenius plot of Fig. 5(f), which was calculated using Eq. (4):

(4) σ=σpexp⁡(−Ea/kT)

where *σ*p represents the conductivity; *k* is the Boltzmann constant; *E*a is the activation energy. Using this equation, the activation energies (*E*a) for grains and grain boundaries can be calculated. The graph shows two distinct slopes within the temperature range of 240–590 °C, each representing an activation energy specific to that range, with fitted values of 0.41 and 0.52 eV, respectively. This suggests that the conduction mechanism for both grains and grain boundaries involves a combined process of primary and secondary ionization [43], and the activation energy values are due to the effect of electrons on conductivity [44]; lower activation energies are favorable for photovoltaic performance.

As depicted in Fig. 6(a), under comparable external conditions, BNKT-based ferroelectric ceramics exhibit significantly superior photovoltaic performance compared with BaTiO3-based ferroelectric ceramics, which provides a solid experimental basis for further modifying the photovoltaic effect by means of multi-field coupling [45−47]. Additionally, as shown in Figs. 6(b) and 6(c), the 0.1SmCo sample exhibits both ferromagnetism and piezoelectricity, which provides a physical basis for modulating its photovoltaic effect under applied mechanical and magnetic field conditions. Figure 6(d) presents *J–V* curves of the 0.1SmCo sample under thermal conditions ranging from 25 to 100 °C, with a light intensity of 300 mW·cm−2 and an electric field of 100 V. Notably, the *J*sc triples to 924 nA·cm−2 at 100 °C, whereas the *V*oc decreases to 0.45 V, demonstrating significant thermal effects on the photovoltaic efficiency. Figure 6(e) depicts the *J–V* curves under varying mechanical forces (0–5 N), revealing an unexpected decrease in *J*sc to 92 nA ·cm−2 coupled with an increase in *V*oc to 2.9 V when a 5-N force is applied. Figure 6(f) illustrates the *J–V* curves under magnetic fields ranging from 0 to 300 GS, highlighting a decrease in *J*sc to 165 nA·cm−2 and a drop in *V*oc to 0.85 V at 300 GS, highlighting the significant impact of magnetic fields on photovoltaic responses.

As shown in Figs. 7(a1) and 7(a2), applying a thermal field alongside optical and electric fields to the semiconductor enables a deeper investigation into its intrinsic properties. The heating induces a modification of spontaneous polarization (Δ*P*s) due to the temperature change (Δ*T*), resulting in surface polarized charges oriented in a specific direction within the sample. This phenomenon, characterized by charge release, manifests as a voltage or current at the material ends, driven by temperature variations [48]. As shown in Fig. 7(a3), the increase in temperature introduces thermal energy to the states within the bandgap. Figure 7(a4) shows that the electrons in the bandgap absorb this thermal energy, transitioning to excited states as hot electrons. The increased mobility and release of more electrons into the conduction process lead to a continuous rise in *J*sc. The observed reduction in *V*oc can be attributed to increases in both dark conductivity (*σ*d) and photoconductivity (*σ*ph), as indicated in Eq. (2), with an increase in photoconductivity resulting in a decrease in *V*oc.

Figure 7(b1) illustrates the uniform application of mechanical force to the ceramic sample, demonstrating the effects of a specific mechanical load. As depicted in Fig. 7(b2), the application of external stress causes the internal ions to shift toward the center of the unit cell, resulting in a reduction in polarization and a decrease in the barrier height (micro-coercive field). The applied stress also induces a reversal in the direction of ferroelastic polarization, thereby reducing the residual polarization in the direction of the stress [49]. On a microscopic level, the significant stress distorts the lattice structure of the sample, disrupting the formation of built-in electric fields and altering band structures, ultimately leading to a considerable decrease in *J*sc, as shown in Figs. 7(b3) and 7(b4) [50]. In addition, 0.1SmCo has certain piezoelectricity, and the piezoelectric field (*E*pi) it generates is in the opposite direction of the self-built electric field (*E*bi), which leads to weakening of the self-built electric field, which is unfavorable for the separation of photogenerated carriers and thus reduces the photovoltaic performance [51,52]. The increase in *V*oc may be attributed to the piezoresistive effect in the semiconductor, and the reduced conductivity under pressure results in an elevated *V*oc.

As shown in Figs. 7(c1) and 7(c2), the application of a magnetic field beside the existing light and electric fields results in a uniform distribution of the magnetic field around the sample. The 180° ferroelectric domains within the ceramics allow the magnetic field to penetrate the material perpendicular to the direction of the photocurrent in the domains [53], which in turn produces the Hall effect. As a result, when the semiconductor is subjected to a magnetic field while current is applied, the carriers experience deflection due to the Lorentz force, generating a transverse voltage, known as the Hall voltage [54]. The Hall electric field, as shown in Fig. 7(c3), is oriented opposite to the *E*bi. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 7(c4), the transitions of carriers within the energy band are impeded by the Lorentz force, thereby suppressing the photovoltaic performance [55]. In addition, as shown in Fig. 7(d), after the magnetic field is applied, the magnetoelectric coupling effect disturbs the ordered ferroelectric domain structure induced by the electric field, leading to disordering of the domain structure, thus weakening the built-in electric field on which the photogenerated carrier separation depends and ultimately inducing degradation of the photovoltaic performance [56,57]. Following the application of the magnetic field, *V*oc decreases due to a reduction in the Hall conductivity. However, abnormal Hall conductance often exhibits a nonlinear behavior under the influence of exchange fields and magnetic effects [58], thus resulting in an increase in *V*oc with increasing magnetic field strength.

## 4 Conclusions

To advance the understanding of oxide semiconductors, a comprehensive study was conducted on the photovoltaic effects within these materials, considering the interplay of multiple fields. Bi0.5Na0.4K0.1TiO3–SmCoO3 ferroelectric ceramics, synthesized via solid-state methods, exhibited stable ferroelectric characteristics and a reduced optical bandgap (*E*g ≈ 2.20–3.09 eV). Under high-intensity electric and light fields, the *J*sc value significantly increased to 269 nA·cm−2. When subjected to high-intensity electric, light, and thermal fields, *J*sc rose further to 924 nA·cm−2. In contrast, under the combined influence of high-intensity electric, light, and mechanical or magnetic fields, the *J*sc values decreased to 165 and 92 nA·cm−2, respectively. Furthermore, both the *V*oc and *P*s varied considerably with the application of multiple fields. Electrical performance tests conducted across a temperature range of 25–300 °C revealed a p-type semiconductor behavior and NTC characteristics. These findings highlight the potential of oxide semiconductors in hybrid energy absorption and collection devices.
