## 1 Introduction

Hydroxyapatite (HA) is a primary inorganic component of hard tissues, such as bone and teeth, and is widely used in repairing bone defects [1,2]. Despite advances in three-dimensional (3D) printing technology, which enables rapid and controllable fabrication of HA bioceramics, these materials are not suitable for load-bearing implants because of their poor mechanical performance, particularly insufficient fracture toughness [3,4]. Achieving a balance of high strength and toughness in ceramics presents a significant challenge. Many organisms, such as nacre, bones, and carapaces, exhibit exceptional mechanical properties due to their unique hierarchical structures, which have evolved over time. This finding indicates that strong and tough biomimetic ceramics may be engineered by mimicking these natural structures [5,6].

In natural bone, the *c*-axis of HA is oriented parallel to collagen fibers, which are primarily composed of type I collagen molecules. These components constitute 95% of the dry weight of bone, providing a combination of strength, toughness, and lightness [7]. The exoskeleton of arthropods protects their soft tissues and is characterized by Bouligand structures that impart excellent mechanical strength and toughness, allowing them to withstand impact loads [8]. These structures consist of angle-ply laminates formed by layers of chitin or collagen fibers embedded in a proteinaceous matrix [8,9]. Inspired by these natural structures, the strength and toughness of HA can be significantly enhanced through the construction of bio-inspired structures, such as oriented or Bouligand architectures. Techniques such as freeze-casting [6], layer-by-layer deposition [10], and pressure-assisted sintering have been utilized to fabricate such biomimetic structural materials [11]. 3D printing can facilitate the production of oriented structures through shear-induced alignment [12−14]. For example, carbide whiskers (0.65 μm in diameter, 12 μm in length) and carbon fibers (10 μm in diameter, 220 μm in length) have been aligned in epoxy-based inks via 3D printing, resulting in a ninefold increase in the Young’s modulus compared with that of unmodified epoxy resin [12]. Oriented hexagonal boron nitride-filled polyurethane composites were also produced via the shearing effect in fused deposition modeling [13]. Although oriented structures can be prepared through shear effects, achieving a uniform alignment of reinforcing particles at the center of the rod remains challenging, necessitating strict rheological properties of the matrix [15,16].

Field-assisted 3D printing techniques, including electric field (EF)- and magnetic field (MF)-assisted methods, offer new opportunities for creating bio-inspired oriented structures [17]. Yang *et al*. [18] reported the alignment of graphene nanosheets (GNs) in a polymer matrix via an electric field during 3D printing, resulting in nacre-like structures with strength and toughness comparable to those of natural nacre. However, the processes involved are complex and unsuitable for large-scale fabrication. Although magnetic fields are advantageous in 3D printing because of their ease of control and compatibility with printing equipment, effective alignment of printed particles typically requires directed magnetic responses [17].

HA is a nonmagnetic material that can be aligned under a 3 T magnetic field; however, once the magnetic field is removed, the aligned HA grains tend to revert to a disordered state [19]. Baba *et al*. [20] successfully fabricated *c*-axis oriented HA ceramics through colloidal processing in a high magnetic field (10 T) followed by sintering. Typical reinforcing particles such as HA whiskers, graphene, and carbon fibers are generally diamagnetic and can be aligned under high magnetic fields (over 3 T). Nevertheless, 3D printing under such conditions poses challenges due to high costs, specialized equipment, and complex procedures. To enhance the magnetic responsiveness of diamagnetic particles, they can be coated with superparamagnetic nanoparticles (12 nm iron oxide) via electrostatic adsorption. The coated particles can be aligned under a reduced magnetic field (0.8 mT). Fortunately, Fe3O4 has good biocompatibility and can be used in 3D printing of bioceramics [21]. For example, aligned alumina sheet-reinforced polyurethane components prepared under an 80 mT magnetic field exhibited a 2.8-fold increase in elastic modulus and a 3–4-fold increase in fracture toughness compared with those of components without aligned particles [22]. Magnetic-assisted droplet-on-demand 3D printing technology has also been developed to prepare microstructured microplate composites using magnetically responsive microplatelets dispersed in a 1 wt% polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) aqueous solution. The resulting ceramics demonstrated tunable properties, with Young’s modulus increasing from 22 GPa for horizontally aligned ceramics to 60 GPa for vertically aligned ceramics, and the Vickers hardness increasing from 0.26 to 0.68 GPa [23].

Although microplatelets coated with superparamagnetic nanoparticles can be easily aligned under low magnetic field strengths, nonmagnetic particles such as HA in high-viscosity inks are difficult to orient. Additionally, the oriented structures produced via 3D printing are not easily tunable, and specific performance may not be achieved after postprocessing such as sintering.

Bouligand structures are composed of unidirectional nanofiber lamellae that are helicoidally in biomaterials (e.g., nacre and crustacean exoskeletons), demonstrating exceptional fracture resistance [24]. The aligned configuration constitutes a specialized form of Bouligand structure. Guan *et al*. [25] reported peak impact resistance at a 40° helical angle in epoxy/glass-fiber Bouligand composites, whereas calcium silicate nanofibrous systems achieved maximum dissipation energy (28.8 MJ·m3) at a 20° helical angle [26]. Chen *et al*. [27] reported that the compressive strength of Bouligand-structured ceramics reached 350 MPa at an interlayer helical angle of 0°. When the angle increased to 10°, although the strength decreased to 260 MPa, the fracture toughness improved by approximately 30%. Additionally, Bouligand-structured ceramics with a 20° helical angle exhibited an ~20% increase in fracture toughness. The Bouligand structure with an interlayer helical angle of 5°, which is close to the interlayer helical angle in Mantis shrimp, demonstrated superior crack deflection capability [28]. Nevertheless, the construction of Bouligand architectures with small angles remains challenging because it is difficult to obtain the precise orientation of grains.

This study designed and manufactured a magnetic field-assisted direct ink writing (MF-DIW) 3D printer capable of orienting particles with a high aspect ratio in a high-viscosity ceramic slurry. The particles are aligned to form oriented structures under low magnetic field strengths. The orientation effects in printed rods can be adjusted by varying magnetic field strengths, and the tunable microstructure can be optimized by adjusting the 3D printing parameters to achieve specific performance in oriented ceramics. This study investigated the impact of magnetic field strength on the orientation of HA whiskers and compared the mechanical performance of oriented and nonoriented HA bioceramics. The oriented and Bouligand-structured HA bioceramics exhibit not only excellent mechanical performance but also considerable absorption capacity in synthetic blood. This research offers a novel and effective approach for customizing biomimetic materials with balanced strength and toughness.

### 2.1 HA encapsulated with Fe<sub>3</sub>O<sub>4</sub>

Calcium nitrate tetrahydrate (Ca(NO3)2·4H2O), ammonium hydrogen phosphate ((NH4)2HPO4), and acetamide were sourced from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. We prepared a 132 mmol/L Ca(NO3)2·4H2O solution, a 39.52 mmol/L (NH4)2HPO4 solution, and a 2 mol/L acetamide solution by dissolving these compounds in a 0.03–0.05 mol/L HNO3 solution. The Ca(NO3)2·4H2O and acetamide solutions were mixed at a 1 : 1 ratio. The (NH4)2HPO4 solution was added slowly to this mixture at a 1 : 1 ratio. We adjusted the pH of the solution to 4.5 using 2.6 mol/L NH3·H2O. HA whiskers were synthesized in this solution at 180 °C for 12 h through a hydrothermal synthesis method. After synthesis, the HA whiskers were washed three times with deionized water and then dried at 80 °C for 12 h.

HA whisker suspensions (10 g/L) and 0.8 g/L Fe3O4 (5 nm, Kete New Material Co., Ltd., China) suspensions were prepared by dispersing them in deionized water. The pH of both suspensions was adjusted to 8–9 using 2.6 mol/L NH3·H2O. Fe3O4 suspension was subsequently mixed with the HA whisker suspension at a volumetric ratio of 40 : 1. This mixture was ultrasonically stirred for 30 min and incubated for at least 12 h. The HA@Fe3O4 composite was then collected and dried for 12 h.

Synthetic blood (PH1899) was purchased from Phygene Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China, for the permeability test. The samples (3 mm × 4 mm × 25 mm) were placed in a Petri dish containing synthetic blood for 20 min.

### 2.2 Preparation of HA@Fe<sub>3</sub>O<sub>4</sub> ink and HA bioceramics

Deionized water and glycerin (C3H8O3, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were mixed to obtain a 10 wt% glycerol solution. A 1.5% polyacrylamide solution was created by dissolving the reagent (Macklin Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) in a 10 wt% glycerin solution. The pH value of the polyacrylamide solution was adjusted to 9 using an NH3·H2O solution. HA (HA@Fe3O4) powder and polyacrylamide solution at a 15 : 85 ratio (v/v) were used to calculate the amounts of HA (HA@Fe3O4) powder and polyacrylamide solution, respectively. HA (HA@Fe3O4) powder and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) were mixed at a 6 : 100 ratio (wt/wt), and the mixture was then added to the polyacrylamide solution to produce a HA (HA@Fe3O4) suspension. The suspension was stirred ultrasonically for 30 min. Subsequently, the resulting suspension was vacuumized for 30 min and centrifuged for 10 min three times to remove air bubbles. HA (HA@Fe3O4) ink was produced and used to print the HA (HA@Fe3O4) green body.

The rheological properties of ceramic inks have a great influence on 3D printing. When the ink concentration is 16 vol%, the low flowability of the ink can result in difficulty with extrusion during 3D printing. The extruded HA could also maintain the shape formed by the nozzle when the pressure was removed. When the HA content is 10–14 vol%, the printed rods are prone to collapse. Accordingly, 15 vol% HA ink exhibiting shear-thinning properties was used to print HA bioceramics. The ink was extruded through a printing nozzle onto the platform employing pneumatic-driven extrusion under 0.5 MPa pressure to fabricate customized geometries. After one layer of filaments was printed, a subsequent layer of filaments was deposited on them. A self-written G-code for the green body was used to print a sample with a 1100 μm space between the printed filaments and a layer thickness of 1000 μm. The printing speed was adjusted to 500 mm/min. Annular NdFeB magnets with different dimensions were purchased from Yiwu Zhimagnetic Hardware Co., Ltd., China. Dimensions (outer diameter × inner diameter × height) of three variants are 15 mm × 10 mm × 3 mm, 20 mm × 10 mm × 3 mm, and 25 mm × 10 mm × 5 mm, respectively. Magnetic field strength was measured via a digital Teslameter (Dongguan Jingruyiming Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., China). Different numbers and types of magnets can be assembled under magnetic force to obtain magnetic field strengths of 0, 2, 58, and 116 mT. The obtained magnets were fixed on the outer side of the nozzle. The HA and HA@Fe3O4 green bodies were printed and subjected to a pressure of 60 MPa for 15 min. The samples were then heated to 400 °C for 3 h to eliminate organic matter and sintered at 1300 °C for 2 h. The sintering process is shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

A magnetic-assisted DIW 3D printer (Fig. 1) was used to obtain oriented HA@Fe3O4 green bodies under a weak magnetic field. A magnetic-assisted DIW 3D printer and an auxiliary rotating platform were used to obtain HA@Fe3O4 green bodies with a Bouligand structure. The rotating platform was rotated according to the pitch angle of the Bouligand structure, and then the next layer was printed after the first. The printed HA and HA@Fe3O4 green bodies were dried and pressed under 60 MPa for 15 min. The pressed green bodies were heated to 400 °C for 3 h to eliminate organic matter and sintered at 1300 °C for 2 h.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The rheological properties of the HA@Fe3O4 inks were evaluated via a Discovery Hybrid Rheometer (HR-1, TA Instruments, USA) with a plate–plate geometry (50 mm in diameter). Viscosity measurements were taken over a shear rate range of 0.01–100 s−1 at room temperature. The porosity of the sintered HA ceramics was determined via Archimedes’ method in deionized water. Morphological and elemental analyses of HA and HA@Fe3O4 were conducted via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos-F200X, Thermo Fisher, USA), a selected area electron diffractometer (SAED), a convergent beam electron diffractometer, and an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS; Super-XTM, Thermo Fisher, USA). The crystal structure of the samples was characterized further by fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis. The magnetic properties of HA@Fe3O4 were assessed via a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM; Lakeshore 7404, Lake Shore Corporation, USA). Phase analysis of the HA whiskers and bioceramics was conducted via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Smart Lab 9kW, Rigaku Corporation, Japan) in a 2*θ* range of 5°–70°.

### 2.4 Mechanical testing

Compressive strength, flexural strength, and fracture toughness of HA bioceramics—specifically, the printing direction (Pd), thickness direction (Td), and random arrangement (Rd)—were evaluated via a universal testing machine (XWW-20A, Shanghai JZ Instrument and Equipment Co., Ltd., China). The compressive strength of cubic ceramics measuring 5 mm × 5 mm × 5 mm was tested at a speed of 0.5 mm/min. A three-point bending test was conducted on HA bioceramics with dimensions of 35 mm × 3 mm × 4 mm at 0.05 mm/min. Fracture toughness was calculated according to the GB/T 23806-2009 standard for the single edge-notched beam (SENB) method. The formula is demonstrated as Eqs. (1) and (2). The sample (30 mm × 3 mm × 5 mm) with an incision depth of 2.5 mm was tested at 0.05 mm/min to obtain the fracture toughness.

(2) Y(lw)=32×(lw)12×1.99−lw(1−lw)[2.15−3.93lw+2.7(lw)2](1+2lw)(1−lw)32

where *K*Ipb refers to the critical stress intensity factor, with the unit of MPa·m; *Y*(*l*/*w*) refers to the geometric correction factor; *P*f refers to the critical failure load, with the unit of N; *t* and *w* are the thickness and height of the sample, respectively, with the unit of mm; *d*1 is the span, with the unit of mm; *l* is the incision depth, with the unit of mm.

To explore the initiation and propagation of cracks, the samples were loaded at 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, and 100 N with a dwell time of 15 s by a Vickers hardness tester (pyramidal Berkovich indenter HV-6, Shanghai Taoxin Industrial Co., Ltd., China). Obvious cracks can hardly be observed in the samples loaded at 50, 60, 70, 80, and 90 N, and their images are not presented. At a load of 100 N, a scanning electron microscope (SEM, X-MaxN, Oxford Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., UK) was used to observe cracks in the samples. The modulus and hardness of HA bioceramics (Pd, Td, and Rd) and HA single grains were assessed via nanoindentation tests with a TI 950 TriboIndenter (Hysitron, USA). Loading and unloading rates were set at 0.3 mN/s, with peak loads of 10 mN for 10 s.

### 3.1 Magnetic field-assisted 3D printer for oriented ceramics and oriented HA bioceramics

Schematic and structural diagrams of the MF-DIW 3D printer are illustrated in Figs. 1(a) and 1(b). The printing process of MF-DIW for oriented ceramics is shown in Fig. 1(b). The nozzle of the MF-DIW 3D printer is depicted in Fig. 1(b). In traditional direct ink writing 3D printers, grains cannot be well oriented because of the drawing force during the printing process. To address this, the nozzle angle was adjusted in relation to the printing substrate to maintain particle orientation. When the angle between the nozzle and the printing substrate was 90°, a large bending deformation of the printed rod was produced, which affected the orientation of HA. Interference can occur at a small angle (0°) during printing. Therefore, an angle of 30° was adopted to ensure the orientation of the grains and printing [29]. The printing speed was 500 mm/min.

The viscosity against shear rate of the inks containing 15 vol% HA and HA@Fe3O4 is shown in Fig. 1(d). As the shear rate increases, the viscosity of the ink decreases, which might be due to the arrangement of grains. The ink can be easily extruded from the nozzles owing to the shear-thinning behavior, whereas structural recovery can retain shape fidelity. These findings indicate that HA and HA@Fe3O4 inks are suitable for 3D printing. The oriented HA@Fe3O4 green body comprising 22 layers of filaments was printed. After the printed HA@Fe3O4 was dried at 80 °C, a HA@Fe3O4 green body with a diameter of 40 mm and a thickness of 20.0±2.0 mm was obtained and then prepressed under 60 MPa in a mold. Diameter and thickness of the prepressed bioceramic green body were 40 mm and 6.0±0.5 mm, respectively. Diameter and thickness of the bioceramics sintered at 1300 °C were 39 mm and 5.7±0.5 mm, respectively. Figure S3 in the ESM shows photographs of HA@Fe3O4 and HA inks, green body of HA, sintered HA, green body of HA@Fe3O4, and sintered HA@Fe3O4.

### 3.2 HA and HA@Fe<sub>3</sub>O<sub>4</sub> whiskers

The formation of HA whiskers is influenced by pH, which increases due to the hydrolysis of AA in the solution. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the XRD patterns of HA whiskers confirm alignment with the HA peaks (JCPDS PDF#72-1243). The sharp peaks indicate high crystallinity of HA, with the most intense peaks at the (300) lattice plane suggesting preferential growth of HA whiskers along the *c*-axis [30,31].

The elemental composition of the HA whiskers was analyzed via TEM-EDS, and the resulting spectra are presented in Fig. 2(b). The Ca/P atomic ratio of the synthesized HA is 1.64, indicating that it is a calcium-deficient variant. TEM images and SAED patterns of the HA whiskers are displayed in Figs. 2(c)–2(k). The whiskers exhibit both plate-like and columnar morphologies, featuring a high aspect ratio of approximately 100–150, as shown in Figs. 2(c)–2(f).

TEM-EDS elemental mapping for Ca (purple) and P (orange) in both columnar and plate-like HA whiskers is presented in Figs. 2(g) and 2(j), respectively. A high-magnification TEM image near the edge of the HA crystal reveals a uniform atomic arrangement, with Ca, P, and O evenly distributed throughout the HA whisker. Figures 2(f)–2(i) show that the longitudinal axis of the HA whiskers aligns with the (001) direction [32]. FFT analysis and diffraction spots confirm that the crystal has a single-crystal structure with a hexagonal system [33]. The whiskers are single crystals with consistent structures, exhibiting no obvious dislocations or defects. FFT analysis (insets of Figs. 2(h) and 2(k)) revealed diffraction spots characteristic of the HA structure. Both plate-like and columnar grains demonstrate a uniform growth direction, even distribution of elements, and orderly atomic arrangement. Furthermore, FFT analysis reveals that HA grains display sharp diffraction spots typical of single crystals, confirming the presence of a hexagonal crystal system.

The content of Fe3O4 is crucial to the magnetic properties of HA@Fe3O4. Fe3O4 is sparsely distributed on the surface of HA whiskers when the content of Fe3O4 is 1%, resulting in low magnetic properties. Although HA whiskers with 3%–4% Fe3O4 have good magnetic properties, some agglomerations occur. Therefore, HA whiskers with 2% Fe3O4 were used for the preparation of HA@Fe3O4 ink and HA bioceramics because of the proper magnetic properties and uniform distribution of Fe3O4 on the surface of HA whiskers in this study.

TEM images of HA@Fe3O4 are presented in Figs. 3(a)–3(c), demonstrating that nano-Fe3O4 coats the surface of HA whiskers. A high-resolution TEM image (Fig. 3(f)) displays lattice fringes, with the inset showing single-crystal diffraction spots, indicative of a tetragonal crystal structure. The element distributions of Fe (yellow), Ca (orange), and O (purple) in HA@Fe3O4 are displayed in TEM–EDS maps (Figs. 3(g)–3(i)). Figure 3(j) shows the magnetic properties of HA, HA@Fe3O4, and Fe3O4. The magnetic behavior of HA@Fe3O4 is demonstrated by the hysteresis loops, with the coercivity being influenced primarily by Fe3O4 characteristics since HA whiskers exhibit negligible magnetic properties.

According to the electric field theory, Fe3O4 nanoparticles and HA whiskers with different charges are attracted to each other [34]. Fe3O4 nanoparticles in the suspension have a positive charge, while the HA whiskers carry a negative charge under alkaline conditions (pH = 7–9). When they are in close proximity, Fe3O4 nanoparticles are adsorbed on HA whiskers by electrostatic adsorption, forming the magnetic HA@Fe3O4 in the suspension. After the formation of magnetic HA@Fe3O4, ultrasonic treatment was conducted so that weakly adsorbed Fe3O4 peeled off. Although the quantitative assessment of bonding strength remains challenging for HA@Fe3O4, the Ca, P, O, and Fe element distributions of HA@Fe3O4 in Fig. 3(k) indicate good binding strength between them [35].

### 3.3 Orientation of HA whiskers

This study applies a lower magnetic field strength to align magnetic HA whiskers with a high aspect ratio. Figure 4(a) displays the center region of the printed rod under varying magnetic field strengths. In the absence of a magnetic field, as shown in Fig. 4(b), HA whiskers are randomly arranged. Applying a weak magnetic field of 2 mT results in most HA whiskers remaining in random orientations during magnetic field-assisted 3D printing. This may be due to the magnetic field being too weak to generate sufficient torque for alignment (Fig. 4(c)). When the magnetic field strength increases to 28 mT, some HA whiskers away from the center exhibit a disordered arrangement, whereas those in the center are aligned (Fig. 4(d)). This alignment is likely due to magnetic HA whiskers with stronger magnetic properties being more easily aligned. As the magnetic field strength increases to 58 mT (Fig. 4(e)), most HA whiskers align along the magnetic field direction. A similar alignment effect continues as the magnetic field strength increases to 116 mT (Fig. 4(f)). When an appropriate magnetic field (58–116 mT) is applied in magnetic field-assisted 3D printing, the magnetic torque aligns HA whiskers, as illustrated in Fig. 4(g). HA grains with an angle of ±10° between the grain and the magnetic field direction are described as “oriented grains”, and the statistical histogram is shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM.

*

Excessively small particles (~0.1–1 μm) exhibit intensified Brownian motion, and large particles (~100–1000 μm) experience dominant gravitational forces, necessitating a higher magnetic field strength for alignment [22]. HA grains, with appropriate dimensions and aspect ratios, were coated with Fe3O4, and the coated HA (HA@Fe3O4) was oriented under weak magnetic field strengths owing to its magnetic responsiveness. The viscosity of the ink decreases by approximately 10,000 times during the printing process. The magnetic field can strongly act on HA@Fe3O4 in the printed ink compared with that in the ink without a shear-thinning effect before the printing process. Accordingly, alignment can be achieved under weak magnetic field strengths. HA grains with lengths of 1–200 μm and aspect ratios greater than 10 can be aligned under weak magnetic fields (Fig. 4(h)), an angle (θ*) forms between the long axis (2*b*) of the HA whisker and the magnetic field direction (*H*0). Once the magnetic field strength exceeds a threshold value, HA whiskers align with the magnetic field. Under a magnetic field, the shear stress and torque experienced by the grains can be predicted, which in turn allows inference of their movement trends. The alignment of these whiskers is affected by both magnetic torque (*T*m) and viscous torque (*T*η). In high-viscosity inks where *T*η greatly exceeds *T*m, HA whiskers tend to align due to shear stress from the ink [36,37]. In low-viscosity inks or in inks with shear-thinning effects in the process of printing, the alignment of HA whiskers depends on both the shear stress and the torque supplied by the magnetic field.

The XRD patterns of HA bioceramics prepared under a magnetic field strength of 116 mT, both before and after sintering, are presented in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b). These spectra provide insights into the arrangement of HA whiskers. The (300) and (211) diffraction peaks correspond to the *c*-axis and *a*-axis of the oriented HA whiskers, respectively [38]. The differences in peak intensities indicate the degree of orientation of the HA whiskers within the bioceramics; a higher intensity suggests a higher degree of orientation [13]. In the XRD pattern of HA bioceramics perpendicular to the thickness direction (Td, orange), the (300) peak is relatively high, indicating that most HA whiskers are oriented. Conversely, along the printing direction (Pd, green), the (211) peak is stronger, and the (300) peak is significantly lower than that in the thickness direction. This suggests that more HA whiskers are oriented perpendicular to the printing direction. For non-oriented HA bioceramics, where HA whiskers align randomly (Rd, blue), the (300) peak is higher than the (211) peak, likely due to the high aspect ratio of the HA whiskers.

The XRD patterns of the three sintered samples are shown in Fig. 5(b). The presence of characteristic diffraction peaks of β-TCP indicates the decomposition of HA during the sintering process [39]. Notably, β-TCP has biocompatibility and osseointegration capabilities comparable to those of pure HA. Its unique combination of osteoconductivity, osteoinductivity, and cell-mediated resorption enables complete bone defect regeneration [40]. With a solubility similar to that of bone minerals, β-TCP resists dissolution in physiological environments but undergoes cellular resorption—primarily via osteoclasts [40]. The decomposed product (β-TCP) can enhance the biological performance of sintered bioceramics.

The XRD patterns of the three samples suggest that the arrangement of HA whiskers in the HA bioceramics remains largely unchanged after sintering. SEM images of the printed rod before (Fig. 5(c)) and after sintering (Fig. 5(d)) revealed that HA whiskers were aligned along the printing direction (the magnetic field direction). To further illustrate the arrangement of HA whiskers in the printed rod, SEM analysis was conducted on regions Ⅰ, Ⅱ, and Ⅲ of the printed rods with and without a magnetic field (116 mT) before sintering (Fig. 5(e)). The results show that HA whiskers align along the magnetic field direction. In the absence of a magnetic field (Figs. 5(i)−5(k)), the HA whiskers are randomly arranged. However, in region II (Fig. 5(j)) of the printing rod near the nozzle wall, HA whiskers without a magnetic field are aligned along the printing direction, whereas those farther from the nozzle wall remain randomly arranged. Without a magnetic field during printing, HA whiskers close to the nozzle wall align along the printing direction because of the shearing effect caused by differing ink velocities at varying distances from the nozzle wall [41]. As the distance from the nozzle wall increases, the degree of alignment of HA whiskers decreases.

A photograph of the printed rod is shown in Fig. 6(a). The printed rod is flexible, allowing it to bend and deform significantly (Fig. 6(b)). Additionally, it is strong enough to withstand substantial tensile forces. The porosity of the sintered HA bioceramics was 30%±2%. These bioceramics also demonstrate considerable absorption in synthetic blood, reaching full saturation in approximately 20 min due to their high porosity. Pore size in HA bioceramics with ~30% porosity is 1–10 μm. The orientation of high-aspect-ratio HA whiskers results in the formation of an aligned pore wall. Hence, channels in HA bioceramics can be formed by the aligned pore wall. The channel size, which is determined by the pore size in HA bioceramics, can create a capillary effect in a liquid. When exposed to synthetic blood, preferential fluid propagation occurred along the aligned microchannels due to capillary forces, while backward flow was significantly suppressed [42]. This unique structure significantly enhances capillarity, which is a critical factor for post-implantation transportation of metabolic wastes and nutrients [43].

### 3.4 Mechanical properties of HA bioceramics

Both compressive and flexural strengths of the samples are highly dependent on the orientation of HA whiskers. As illustrated in Fig. 7(a), the maximum load (the load at failure) for Rd HA bioceramics indicates a compressive strength of 40.4 MPa. In contrast, Td HA bioceramics have a lower compressive strength of 25.6 MPa. Notably, by altering the orientation of the HA whiskers, Pd HA bioceramics achieve a compressive strength of 93.4 MPa, representing a 1.3-fold improvement over non-oriented HA bioceramics and significantly greater than that of Td HA bioceramics [44] and other HA bioceramics with similar porosities [45,46]. The stress–strain curves depicted in Fig. 7(b) reveal a 1.6-fold increase in strain and a 3.4-fold increase in compression deformation, defined as the energy required to fracture the ceramics during compression testing, compared with non-oriented HA bioceramics. When oriented HA bioceramics are compressed parallel to the HA whiskers, the stress–strain curve fluctuates continuously without a distinct maximum load value. Although this maximum load is lower than that of non-oriented HA bioceramics, the compression deformation work is greater. As shown in Fig. 7(c), the elastic moduli of oriented (Td and Pd) HA bioceramics are lower than those of non-oriented bioceramics. This suggests that the oriented HA bioceramics can endure greater strains before fracturing. Although Rd HA bioceramics have a higher modulus than Td and Pd bioceramics, their compressive strain and strength are significantly lower than those of Pd HA bioceramics (Fig. 7(c)). The grain alignment of Rd- and Pd-HA bioceramics can affect micropore shape and distribution. Although a relatively uniform pore distribution in Rd HA bioceramics can increase their stiffness [47], cracks propagate more easily under an external load, resulting in reduced strength. Notably, the strength–modulus ratio of Pd HA bioceramics is approximately 3 times greater than that of Rd bioceramics, which might be caused by the microstructure of oriented materials [48]. An analysis of the compression stress–strain curves of Pd HA bioceramics indicated that the process can be divided into three stages. During the first stage, intergranular fractures may occur because intergranular strength is lower than intragranular strength. In the second stage, grains can displace, increasing the strain of the HA bioceramics. As strain continues to increase, the HA whiskers become immobile, leading to fracturing of the HA bioceramics in the third stage [49].

During the compression process of Td HA bioceramics, intergranular fractures occur, leading to a decrease in the spacing of HA whiskers. This reduction means that HA whiskers cannot provide substantial support. As a result, compressive strength decreases, whereas compressive strain increases (Fig. 7(d)). During compression, both intergranular and intragranular fractures can occur simultaneously in non-oriented HA bioceramics, with few grains slipping. This simultaneous fracturing increases the likelihood of catastrophic failure [19]. Consequently, the maximum strain in non-oriented HA bioceramics is lower than that in oriented HA bioceramics.

The results of the flexural tests are presented in Fig. 7(e). Compared with the Pd HA bioceramics, Td HA bioceramics had a greater flexural strength of 29.7 MPa at 18.4 MPa, and the flexural strength of the non-oriented HA bioceramics was 11.5 MPa. Photographs of Rd, Td, and Pd HA bioceramics after flexural test are shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. The flexural stress–strain curves of Td HA bioceramics indicate greater flexural strain than that of non-oriented HA bioceramics (Fig. 7(f)). When a sample experiences an external bending load, compressive stress forms in the upper layer, whereas tensile stress occurs in the lower layer. Cracks primarily develop in the lower section of the sample. The bending strength of oriented ceramics increases because HA whiskers can hinder crack propagation [6,19]. Additionally, transgranular and intergranular strengths differ, requiring more energy and displacement to fracture oriented HA bioceramics [49,50]. When oriented HA bioceramics are subjected to torque (M) (Fig. 7(g)), the HA whiskers break, causing grain dislocation. This increases both flexural stress and strain. In contrast, non-oriented bioceramics, where HA whiskers are randomly arranged, allow cracks to propagate easily, requiring less fracture energy and resulting in lower flexural stress than Td HA bioceramics. Furthermore, the flexural stress of Pd HA bioceramics is greater than that of non-oriented bioceramics [51]. When subjected to torque (M), cracks in Pd HA bioceramics deflect along the direction of HA whiskers, generating secondary cracks that consume additional energy. This contributes to the overall higher strength of these bioceramics.

Fracture toughness measures a material’s ability to resist crack propagation and is a crucial mechanical property [52]. Materials with low fracture toughness are prone to crack propagation. This property is vital for ensuring the structural integrity and reliability of ceramics [3,53]. As shown in Fig. 8(a), the fracture toughness of Td-1 HA bioceramics exceeds that of Rd and Td-2 HA bioceramics; however, its fracture work is less than that of Td-2 HA bioceramics. This difference can be analyzed through the load–displacement curves and the micromorphology of the fractures. For all HA bioceramics, the load increases linearly with displacement. The load–displacement curve for non-oriented HA bioceramics, shown in Fig. 8(c), reaches a maximum displacement of 26.2 μm. Both transversely and longitudinally fractured HA whiskers appear in Fig. 8(d), indicating that cracks propagate randomly due to whisker alignment, leading to catastrophic failure. However, the cracks propagate rapidly (Fig. 8(e)).

### 3.5 Strengthening and toughening mechanisms of HA bioceramics

To investigate the micromechanical behavior of HA bioceramics, a nano-indentation test was performed. Figures 9(a)−9(c) show the load–displacement curves at peak load for HA bioceramics. The load–displacement curves for the Pd HA bioceramics are shown in Fig. 9(a). The indentation loading direction is aligned with the *c*-axis of most HA whiskers. These curves can be classified into three groups. The characteristics of type I curves help identify the indentation locations. In type I curves, the load is applied along the *c*-axis of the HA whiskers, resulting in smooth curves without noticeable fluctuations. The elastic modulus is approximately 75 GPa. For type II curves, the HA whiskers at the load position are randomly arranged, leading to fluctuations likely caused by grain dislocations, with an elastic modulus of approximately 41 GPa. Type III curves, which represent the areas between HA whiskers, have an elastic modulus of approximately 34 GPa.

The load–displacement curves for Td HA bioceramics are presented in Fig. 9(b). Similar to earlier results, these curves are also divided into three groups (type I, type II, and type III curves) based on the loading location during the nanoindentation test. Figure 9(d) presents the corresponding elastic moduli for different indentation locations. The elastic modulus in the (300) direction is greater than that in the (211) direction, which is consistent with the results of the theoretical prediction [54]. The elastic moduli for loads applied at the end surface and the longitudinal surface of HA grains are approximately 74, 64, 43, and 24 GPa, respectively.

The failure of ceramics is often associated with their inherent defects. Moreover, ceramics lack the slip systems found in metals, making crack propagation essential for improving their mechanical properties [55]. Figure 10 shows the microstructures of cracks in the Rd, Td, and Pd HA bioceramics after an indentation test using a four-sided diamond indenter with a load of 100 N for 15 s. Td and Pd HA bioceramics were loaded perpendicularly and parallelly to the printing direction, respectively. Although cracks initiate at the indentation points across all the samples, their morphologies differ significantly. In the Rd HA bioceramics, the cracks are irregular and propagate in a tortuous manner, with a crack length of approximately 70 μm, as depicted in Figs. 10(a)–10(c). This irregularity is likely due to the random arrangement of HA whiskers [49,56]. In contrast, the Td HA bioceramics display longer cracks, exceeding 1000 μm, as shown in Figs. 10(d)–10(f). This may result from varying directions of crack propagation along the whisker orientation [19,49]. Pd HA bioceramics show cracks that also follow a tortuous path and exhibit multiple secondary cracks, as illustrated in Figs. 10(g)–10(i). These cracks preferentially grow along grain boundaries, where the whisker strength exceeds the intergranular strength [49,51].

Given the inherent characteristics of HA bioceramics, completely preventing crack formation and controlling crack initiation sites are challenging. Therefore, an effective approach to enhance the mechanical properties of ceramics involves inhibiting crack propagation, reducing propagation speed, and limiting further crack growth. Altering the grain orientation can change the crack growth path, thereby improving the overall mechanical performance. This strategy offers a viable method to enhance the mechanical properties of HA bioceramics.

### 3.6 Mechanical properties of HA bioceramics with Bouligand structure

Bouligand-structural materials are recognized for their exceptional mechanical properties, which are closely related to the angles between their layers. The Bouligand structure has a wide angular range for interlayer helical angles, with significant variations in mechanical properties across different angles. A previous study indicated that a Bouligand structure with an interlayer helical angle of 5° demonstrated superior crack deflection capability [28]. Consequently, HA with a helical angle of 5° was prepared in this work. The Bouligand structure of the HA bioceramics is illustrated in Fig. 11(a). Each layer in the Bouligand-structural HA billet consists of printed rods, with a helical angle of 5° between neighboring layers. The HA billet was then pressed and sintered to produce Bouligand-structural HA bioceramics with a layer thickness of approximately 170 μm.

The compressive behavior of the Bouligand-structural HA bioceramics differs notably from that of the oriented HA bioceramics. As shown in Figs. 11(b) and 11(c), the compressive strength along direction I is 40.3 MPa, indicating the onset of a pseudoplastic deformation stage that occurs at a compressive strain of 5% [48]. This pseudoplastic stage, which spans a strain range of 5% to 10% (indicated by the red region in Fig. 11(b)), is significantly longer than typical for ceramics, suggesting a substantial increase in toughness [57]. During this stage, the compressive strength does not increase with increasing strain. Although the strength is lower than that of Pd HA bioceramics, the compressive deformation work is 5.6 MJ/m3, approximately twice that of Td HA bioceramics.

The natural Bouligand structure (Mantis shrimp) features a chitinous organic matrix beneath the mineral phase, characterized by a distinctive helical organization where locally parallel chitin fiber lamellae are stacked layer by layer. Each layer has an incremental rotation angle relative to the adjacent layer. Crack propagation manifests as helical fracture patterns progressing through the interlayers, and the rotating crack front can be consistently aligned with the direction parallel to the whiskers. When a crack deviates from its helical trajectory and propagates into neighboring layers, energy dissipation increases because of the anisotropy of the Bouligand structure. The helicoidal architecture helps dissipate the energy released by propagating microcracks [58]. The compressive response of Bouligand-structural HA bioceramics typically includes three stages. In the first stage, the stress increases linearly until it reaches a peak, followed by a stress plateau stage (the second stage). The plateau region is characterized by small stress fluctuations, which can be referred to as “pseudoplastic deformation”, where progressive interlayer sliding and microcrack propagation might occur [28,59]. This phenomenon enhances energy dissipation through frictional resistance and ordered crack extension. In the third stage, the densification of the Bouligand-structural HA bioceramics induced a stress increase, as shown in the stress–strain curve (Fig. 11(b)).

In contrast, the compressive strength in direction II reaches 105.5 MPa, with a compression deformation work of 3.1 MJ/m3. This value is lower than that observed in direction I and is comparable to the compression deformation work of Pd HA bioceramics. Additionally, the flexural strength of Bouligand-structural HA bioceramics is 2.8 times that of non-oriented HA bioceramics. The flexural strength and stress–strain curves of the Bouligand-structural bioceramics are similar to those of the Td HA bioceramics.

The fracture toughness of the Bouligand-structural bioceramics was 16.0 MPa·m1/2, which lies between those of the Pd and Td HA bioceramics, as illustrated in Fig. 11(e). The Bouligand-helical structure effectively guided crack growth and inhibited further crack propagation. The displacement of Bouligand-structural HA bioceramics reaches 70 μm, which is 2.5 times that of Td HA bioceramics. This finding demonstrates the ability of Bouligand-structural HA bioceramics to manage crack propagation, resulting in slower crack growth. Although Td HA bioceramics can impede crack progression, they tend to exhibit faster fracture extension.

Compared with HA bioceramics in other studies [27,60−62], as shown in Fig. 12, the Bouligand-structural HA bioceramics prepared in this study exhibited excellent mechanical properties. The Bouligand-structural HA bioceramics presented significantly greater compressive strength, flexural strength, and fracture toughness than the pure HA ceramics. The compressive strength is comparable to that of CF/HA composites [61] and whisker-reinforced HA composites [60]. Furthermore, the fracture toughness is greater than that of Bouligand-structured HA composed of HA microfibers (10° interlayer angle) [62] and CF/HA composites [27]. Typically, the mechanical properties decrease, and the biological properties of biomaterials improve with increasing porosity. Nevertheless, the compressive strength of Pd HA bioceramics and Bouligand HA bioceramics with 30% porosity is much greater than that of Rd HA bioceramics and CaP bioceramics with the same porosity [45,46]. To satisfy the requirements of implants in load-bearing bone sites, Bouligand HA bioceramics should have high porosity for improved biological properties on the premise that they achieve mechanical properties similar to those of cortical bone. Ultimately, Bouligand-structural HA bioceramics represent an ideal balance of strength and toughness. The fracture toughness and compressive strength of these materials align with the characteristics of cortical bone, wherein the fracture toughness ranges from 2 to 12 MPa·m1/2 and the compressive strength varies between 100 and 230 MPa [63,64].

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, biomimetic HA bioceramics were fabricated via a self-developed magnetic field-assisted 3D printer. Under weak magnetic fields (58–116 mT), HA whiskers align with the magnetic field, resulting in a favorable orientation effect. The printed rods, consisting of oriented HA whiskers, exhibit both flexibility and strength. These oriented HA bioceramics demonstrate varying mechanical properties depending on the direction of the HA whisker. The compressive strength of the Pd HA bioceramics reached 93.4 MPa, which was 2.3 times that of the Rd HA bioceramics. Additionally, the bending strength of the Td-1 HA bioceramics was 2.6 times that of the Rd HA bioceramics. The measured fracture toughness is 17.3 MPa·m1/2, which is 1.44 times that of Rd HA bioceramics. During the failure process, cracks in the oriented HA bioceramics primarily propagate along the printing direction, preferentially following the grain boundaries. However, crack propagation along the thickness direction is hindered by the HA grains, resulting in increased fracture toughness. The compressive strength, bending strength, and fracture toughness of HA bioceramics with the Bouligand structure were 2.6 times, 2.8 times, and 1.2 times those of the Rd HA bioceramics, respectively, and pseudoplastic deformation during compression was observed. Bouligand-structural HA bioceramics offer an excellent combination of strength and toughness, comparable to that of cortical bone. The developed magnetic field-assisted 3D printing technique will facilitate the fabrication of biomimetic ceramics with outstanding mechanical properties. This approach effectively balances the strength and toughness of brittle ceramics and establishes a basis for 3D printing biomimetic materials with fine microstructures and tunable mechanical performance.
