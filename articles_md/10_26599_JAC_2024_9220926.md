## 1 Introduction

Aircraft radomes or antenna windows are constructed with wave-transparent material to guarantee the effective transmission of electromagnetic waves. It shields the antenna from extremely harsh external environments and maintains normal communication and precise guidance, and it is one of the most important components of hypersonic aircraft. With the increased speed of aircraft, the radome will suffer from increased temperature caused by aerodynamic heating. Therefore, the high-performance wave-transparent material with excellent high-temperature capability, good oxidation resistance, low dielectric constant, and low dielectric loss are urgently needed [1−4]. Al–Cr phosphate composites have been widely used as the wave-transparent material due to their excellent dielectric properties, low cost, and simple molding process [5]. Cao *et al*. [6] prepared Al–Cr phosphate composites, which were found to have excellent high-temperature capability and could be used as radome material. However, Al(PO3)3 begins to decompose into AlPO4 and P2O5 gas when the temperature exceeds 1200 °C [7]. A change in chemical composition affects the electromagnetic performance and service life of a radome. Therefore, improving the performance of phosphate-based wave-transparent material with better mechanical properties and higher temperature resistance is highly important.

In recent years, ZrP2O7 has attracted much attention due to its low coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE), low thermal conductivity, and high-temperature capability [8−10]. Researchers [11−14] predicted that it may be a high-temperature wave-transparent material candidate. However, ZrP2O7 undergoes a phase transition at approximately 300 °C [15−18], and the volume change may cause a stress concentration, which limits its practical application, especially at high temperatures. In addition, the dielectric performance of ZrP2O7 as a wave-transparent radome material has seldom been reported.

According to Boltzmann’s hypothesis on the relationship between the entropy and the complexion of a system [19], solid solutions containing three to four metal elements are referred to as medium-entropy ceramics, whose configurational entropy (1.0*R* ≤ Δ*S*conf ≤ 1.5*R*) lies between that of high-entropy ceramics (Δ*S*conf > 1.5*R*) and that of low-entropy ceramics (Δ*S*conf 20−25]. For instance, Rost *et al*. [21] reported that an entropy-stabilized ceramic (MgCoNiCuZn)O can be achieved by a multicomponent solid solution even if the ensemble of binary oxides does not exhibit a uniform crystal structure or extensive solubility. Xu *et al*. [26] reported a novel entropy-driven strategy to stabilize single Pd atoms on high-entropy (CeZrHfTiLa)O*x* with improved reducibility of lattice oxygen compared to traditional support-stabilized catalysts. In addition, manipulating the material’s entropy can also improve its properties. Braun *et al*. [27] measured the structural, mechanical, and thermal properties of several entropy-stabilized oxides. Local ionic charge disorder can effectively reduce the thermal conductivity without compromising the mechanical stiffness. The above investigations suggest that the introduction of unique atomic species to regulate the entropy may improve the stability and properties of ZrP2O7. To suppress the phase transition, the dopant should affect the stretching or rotation of the [P2O7] polyhedrons in ZrP2O7. Ota and Yamai [16] selected several dopants, including Ce, Li, Y, and Ti, to inhibit the phase transition of ZrP2O7, but only Ti can achieve this purpose. Because of the low melting point, large dielectric constant, and poor temperature capability of TiP2O7, synergistic doping of multiple elements is necessary to balance the overall performance. Previously, Zhao *et al*. [28] designed and successfully synthesized a multicomponent equimolar solid solution of (TiZrHf)P2O7. It has good thermal stability. However, the effect of the solid solution on the phase stability and dielectric properties was not presented, which hinders its high-temperature applications. Therefore, it is important to investigate the effects of Ti and Hf introduction on the structure, dielectric properties, and mechanical properties of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7.

In this work, a (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 solid solution was prepared using a pressureless sintering method to regulate the performance of ZrP2O7. Severe lattice distortion and chemical bond variation were introduced at the Zr lattice sites. The lattice constants of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 were obtained by refining the powder X-ray diffraction patterns to reveal the lattice distortion. The thermal conductivity and CTE of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 were measured, and the influences of multicomponent doping were comprehensively analyzed. Nanoindentation and three-point bending tests were applied to explore the mechanical properties. The dielectric properties of this material were characterized in detail to clarify their potential application as radome material. The results highlight (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 as a promising candidate for the wave-transparent material.

### 2.1 Processing

Bulk (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 was synthesized in two steps, as shown in Fig. 1. First, TiO2, ZrO2, and HfO2 powders (purity 99.99%, Maclean Biochemical Co. Ltd., China) were uniformly mixed in equal molar ratios. The mixture was ball-milled with ethanol for 24 h and dried at 80 °C for 12 h. The mixture and H3PO4 (AR, ≥ 85 wt% in H2O, Aladdin Reagent Co. Ltd., China) were mixed at a molar ratio of 1 : 2 and then heated at 300 °C for 1 h. The slurry was washed by centrifugation after cooling. After drying, the sample was sintered at 1000 °C for 1 h to obtain pure (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 powders. Then, 2 wt% MgO (purity 99.99%, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd., China) was added as a sintering aid to improve the density of the (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 bulk ceramics. The pure (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 powders and sintering additives were ball-milled again and subjected to a uniaxial pressing (5 MPa, 5 min) and a cold isostatic pressing (200 MPa, 15 min) to obtain a cylindrical green body. Dense and bulk (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 ceramics were obtained by sintering in a muffle furnace (KSL-1700X-A1, HF-Kejing, China) at 1220 °C for 100 min. To compare the thermomechanical properties of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 with those of single-component pyrophosphates, bulk ceramics of TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, and HfP2O7 were also prepared by pressureless sintering at 1220 °C for 100 min, 1350 °C for 100 min, and 1300 °C for 100 min, respectively.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The phase compositions were determined by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan) with a step size of 0.02° at a scanning rate of 2 (°)/min. The lattice parameters of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 were calculated by the Rietveld refinement method using the General Structure Analysis System II (GSAS II) software. The crystal structure, crystallinity, and defects were characterized by a Raman spectroscope (RS; LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba, Japan). A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Sigma 300, ZEISS, Germany) was used to observe the morphologies of the samples. Microstructures and element compositions were identified by a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HR-TEM; Talos F200X, FEI, USA) along with an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS; Thermo Fisher, USA). The dielectric constant and dielectric loss at room temperature of the samples with dimensions of 2 mm in diameter and 30 mm in thickness were measured by the rectangular resonant cavity perturbation method using a vector network analyzer (VNA; N5227B PNA, Keysight, USA). The high-temperature dielectric constant over a frequency range of 8–18 GHz was measured by a dielectric constant meter (DCM; HQR0718HT1150, Chengdu Enchi Microwave Technology, China) using the high-*Q* cavity method. The dimensions of the sample are 50 mm in diameter and 2 mm in thickness. Thermal analysis was performed using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC; DSC 250, TA, USA). CTE of the samples with dimensions of 3 mm × 4 mm × 12 mm was measured by a pushrod dilatometer (DIL 402, NETZSCH, Germany). The thermal diffusivity (*α*) of the samples was measured by a laser flash analyzer (LFA 467HT, NETZSCH, Germany) with sample dimensions of 10 mm in diameter and 1 mm in thickness. A graphite coating was applied to both sides of the sample before testing to eliminate the effect of thermal radiation. The thermal conductivity (*κ*) was calculated by Eq. (1) [29,30]:

(1) κ=α×ρ×cp

where *ρ* is the density obtained by the Archimedes method, and *c**p* is the heat capacity calculated by the Neumann–Cope law [31]. The thermal conductivity of the dense sample is corrected by Eq. (2) [32]:

(2) κ0=κ1−4φ3

where *κ* denotes the thermal conductivity of a fully dense sample, and *φ* is the porosity. The reduced modulus and hardness were characterized by a nanoindenter (Hysitron TI980, Bruker, Germany); 400 points were tested within a range of 40 μm × 40 μm, the interval between two adjacent points was 2 μm, and the load was 5 mN. Additionally, three-point bending tests (C42.104, New Sans, China) were used to evaluate the flexural strength. The span (*L*) for bending was 30 mm, and the loading rate was 0.5 mm/min. The flexural strength (*σ*) of the samples was calculated using Eq. (3) [33]:

(3) σ=3FL2BH2

where *F* is the maximum load at fracture. *B* and *H* are the width and the thickness of the samples, respectively.

### 3.1 Phase composition and microstructure

Figure 2 shows the powders and bulk XRD patterns of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7, TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, and HfP2O7. The powder samples were pure phase without impurities. Compared with those of ZrP2O7 and HfP2O7, the diffraction peaks of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 slightly shift toward higher diffraction angles. This shift is qualitatively understandable because the ionic radius of Ti is smaller than those of Zr and Hf (Zr and Hf are similar in size), resulting in lattice contraction. Sintering aids were added to promote the densification of the bulk (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7, TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, and HfP2O7 ceramics. Only small amounts of MgZr4(PO4)6, MgHf4(PO4)6, and Mg2P2O7 can be detected in the XRD patterns.

In addition to the grain size, the formation of a second phase by the sintering aid also affects the material properties. TEM was performed to characterize the microstructure and composition of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7. In Fig. 5(a), the bright grains are named the grain A, and the grain B is the dark grain boundary phase. A high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) image is shown in Fig. 5(b). It clearly shows a typical periodic lattice structure, in which a set of fringes is approximately 0.470 nm, corresponding to the (333) planes of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 with a space group of Pa** 3¯. In the high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) image (Fig. 5(c)), grain A is surrounded by the grain B. EDS mappings (Figs. 5(d)–5(i)) further clarify that Ti, Zr, and Hf are uniformly distributed in the (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 grains without segregation. In addition, the grain B is rich in Mg and O, which can be attributed to Mg2P2O7. The SAED patterns in Fig. 5(a) prove the existence of Mg2P2O7 with the B*21/*c* space group, which coincides with the XRD results. Therefore, (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 donates the grain A, and Mg2P2O7 donates the grain B.

MP2O7 (M = transition metals) possesses two polymorphs with the same Pa** 3¯ space group, which are named the X1 phase (Fig. 6(a)) and X2 phase (Fig. 6(b)). ZrP2O7 changes from the X2 phase to the X1 phase when the temperature is increased to approximately 300 °C [34]. In the X1 phase, the P–O–P bond angle is constrained to 180° [34]. In the X2 phase, the same Pa** 3¯ space group with a 3 × 3 × 3 superstructure appears, and 89% of the P–O–P bond angles of such groups can range from 134° to 162° [11,34]. The P–O–P bond angles in the two polyhedrons are the main difference. The as-prepared TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, HfP2O7, and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 are in the X2 phase. As shown in Fig. 6(b), (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 primitive cell consists of six M sites (from M1 to M6), eleven P sites (from P1 to P11), and thirty-three O sites (from O1 to O33). The M site is coordinated by six oxygen, P-centered distorted tetrahedrons, including four O atoms.

The refined XRD patterns are presented in Fig. 7. *The weighted profile *R*-factor (*R*wp) and goodness of fit (GOF) are less than 10% and 2, respectively, indicating that the calculated curve fits well with the experimental curve. Table 1 shows the lattice parameters of ZrP2O7, TiP2O7, HfP2O7, and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 obtained by Rietveld refinement. The lattice constant of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 is smaller than those of ZrP2O7 and HfP2O7. This trend coincides with the shifting of the XRD diffraction peaks toward higher diffraction angles. The average of the sum of the lattice constants of TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, and HfP2O7 are also calculated for comparison. Multicomponent doping can introduce defects into (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7, and Raman spectroscopy is sensitive to local distortion in the crystal. Figure 8 shows the Raman spectra of TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, HfP2O7, and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7. Their peak profiles are similar to each other. The position of the (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 Raman peak shifts to a lower angle than that of the ZrP2O7 and HfP2O7 Raman peaks. This difference mostly originates from the difference in mass and size among Zr, Ti, and Hf atoms [35]. The pyrophosphate anion consists of two [PO3] groups and a [POP] bridge. The Raman peaks can be assigned to different modes in terms of [PO3] and [POP] group vibrations. This has been made on the basis that ν*as[PO3] > *ν*s[PO3] > *ν*as[POP] > *ν*s[POP] > *δ*[OPO] > *δ*[POP] [36,37]. *ν*as and *ν*s refer to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the terminal [PO3] or bridge [POP] bonds, respectively. *δ* refers to the corresponding bond-bending vibrations, which are naturally lower in frequency. Therefore, the intense band of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 at approximately 1045 cm−1 can unambiguously be assigned to *ν*s[PO3]. The [POP] stretching mode generally lies at lower wavenumbers than terminal stretching, and *ν*as[POP] produces a Raman band at approximately 980 cm−1 [38,39]. The band at approximately 733 cm−1 corresponds to *ν*s[POP] vibrations. The *δ*[POP] deformation mode of the [POP] bridges should occur at approximately 330 cm−1. The bands at approximately 645 and 500 cm−1 are attributed to *δ*[OPO], *δ*[OMO], and *δ*[MOP] (M = Ti, Zr, and Hf) [36,38,39]. The Raman peak of the (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 solid solution widened in shape and shifted in wavenumber. Lattice distortion and atom disorder are likely the main reasons for this broadening [35]. (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 maintains the same crystal structure as ZrP2O7, TiP2O7, and HfP2O7. However, the internal vibrations of the atoms change, and these changes in bond angles and distances may lead to the widening and shifting of the Raman bands.

Aircraft radomes or antenna windows are usually operated in thermal–mechanical–electrical coupling environments [41], and good mechanical properties are important for maintaining the integrity of the aerodynamic configurations of the wave-transparent material. The Rietveld refinement of the XRD and Raman spectra illustrates that (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 maintains the original symmetry of the crystal structure, but the chemical bonds, including bond length and tilting angle, are modified. ### 3.3 Thermophysical properties

To ensure that the internal electrical equipment works properly at high temperatures, a wave-transparent radome should have good phase stability and excellent thermal insulation properties. (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 and TiP2O7 were heated to high temperatures to investigate the phase stability, and their XRD patterns are shown in Fig. 12. Although the melting point of TiP2O7 is 1500 °C, it decomposes into Ti4P6O23 at 1400 °C, exhibiting a worse temperature capability. However, (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 does not decompose at 1550 °C for more than 2 h, presenting good high-temperature phase stability. Therefore, the temperature tolerance of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 dramatically increases, proving good prospects for high-temperature applications.

The thermal conductivities of TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, HfP2O7, and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 were measured and are shown in Fig. 13(a). The overall trend of thermal conductivity increases with increasing temperature, exhibiting a glass-like thermal conductivity [46]. Typically, the thermal conductivity of nonmetallic ceramics usually decreases with increasing temperature and tends to stabilize at extremely high temperatures due to phonon scattering. In Eq. (1), the thermal conductivity is related to the temperature-dependent thermal diffusivity and heat capacity. The heat capacities usually increase with increasing temperature, and they tend to be stable at high temperatures (Fig. 13(b)). However, thermal diffusivity is susceptible to the influence of defects, phase transitions, and thermal radiation. As shown in Fig. 13(c), since there is no phase transition in TiP2O7 and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7, their thermal diffusivities tend to change with temperature similarly. The thermal diffusivities of TiP2O7 and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 decrease with increasing temperature below 700 °C, and they increase above 700 °C due to the thermal radiation effect. ZrP2O7 and HfP2O7 exhibit phase transitions at approximately 300 °C. The X2 phase should have a lower thermal diffusivity, so the thermal diffusivities decrease until reaching the phase transition temperature, after which the thermal diffusivities increase. At higher temperatures, the thermal diffusivities of ZrP2O7 and HfP2O7 appear to increase due to the effect of thermal radiation. Therefore, the thermal conductivities of TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, HfP2O7, and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 increase as a function of high temperature. For TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, and HfP2O7, the thermal conductivity decreases with increasing atomic mass, and the mass of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 is in the middle. However, due to the significant lattice distortions and defects, the thermal conductivity of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 decreases to a value close to that of HfP2O7. (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 exhibits very low thermal conductivity in the range of 0.82–1.28 W/(m·K).

For crystalline material with defects, there is a negative correlation between the thermal conductivity and phonon scattering coefficient. The phonon scattering coefficient ( Γi) is dominated by defects (Eq. (6)) [47,48]:

(6) Γi=fi{(ΔMiM)2+2[6.4×13γ1+ν1−ν(Δδiδ)]2}

where i* is a kind of lattice defect, and *f**i* represents the concentration of the defect. *M* and *δ* represent the average atomic mass and the ionic radius of the lattice site, respectively. Δ*M**i* = *M* − *M**i*, Δ*δ**i* = *δ* − *δ**i*, where *M**i* and *δ**i* denote the atomic mass and the ionic radius of a specific defect, respectively. *γ* and *ν* are the Grüneisen parameter and the Poisson’s ratio, respectively. Based on Eq. (6), Ti, Zr, and Hf jointly occupy the M site in the (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 lattice, and size mismatch becomes the dominant factor of strain field fluctuations, which distorts the lattice of the host material. The phonon scattering caused by point defects is identified as the main reason for the reduced thermal conductivity of the solid solution.

As a structural component used in aircraft, the sudden expansion or contraction due to the phase transition of ZrP2O7 will lead to mechanical failure during the high-temperature cycling process [15−18]. Lattice distortion can significantly improve the thermal and mechanical properties of material, and increased entropy can limit the growth of grains. Therefore, the phase transition may be improved by the multicomponent doping method. Figure 14(a) shows the linear thermal expansion curves of TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, HfP2O7, and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 from 25 to 1000 °C. The thermal expansion of ZrP2O7 increases linearly with temperature before 300 °C, and it flattens at high temperatures due to the phase transition. This phenomenon can also be found in HfP2O7, but TiP2O7 exhibits a linear increase without phase transition. DSC is highly sensitive to phase transitions in material. Figure 14(b) shows the DSC curves of TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, HfP2O7, and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7. A sharply defined endothermic peak at 293 °C is observed for ZrP2O7, which is consistent with the sudden increase in temperature. HfP2O7 has a similar phenomenon to that of ZrP2O7. No peaks are detected in the TiP2O7 and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 solid solutions. The introduction of Ti and lattice distortion inhibit the phase transition, which effectively improves the thermal properties. The linear thermal expansion curves of the material vs. temperature are fitted by a function and are shown in Figs. 14(c) and 14(d). CTEs of ZrP2O7 (13.3×10−6 K−1) and HfP2O7 (12.0×10−6 K−1) are relatively large at low temperatures. However, they decrease to 2.8×10−6 and 3.5×10−6 K−1 at high temperatures, respectively. For TiP2O7 and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7, CTEs are stabilized at 14.3×10−6 and 8.0×10−6 K−1, respectively, over the test temperature range.

The thermal expansion of a three-dimensional frame structure is affected by both the bond length and the tilting of polyhedra [49]. When the polyhedra are tilted away from symmetrical orientations, the tilting effects are generally greater, and a change in the tilt angle often culminates in a phase transition. Since the structure of ZrP2O7 is constructed of [ZrO6] octahedrons and [PO4] tetrahedrons, the abrupt expansion of ZrP2O7 should be attributed to the rotation of the [ZrO6] octahedrons and [PO4] tetrahedrons from the X2 phase to the X1 phase [16]. However, the replacement of Zr ions by Ti and Hf changes the tilting angle. When smaller [TiO6] octahedrons replace larger [ZrO6] octahedrons, they are almost stretched without tilting, the degree of total tilting decreases, and the expansion flattens. In brief, multicomponent doping successfully improves the effect of the phase transition on thermal expansion.

The bond lengths and distortion degrees of TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, HfP2O7, and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 were obtained based on the Rietveld refinement of the powder XRD data, and the results are shown in Tables S2–S5 in the ESM. For (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7, the average bond lengths of M–O (M = Ti, Zr, and Hf) are shorter than those of ZrP2O7, except for the M3 site. In contrast, the average bond lengths of most P–O groups tend to increase. In addition, (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 shows the highest degree of lattice distortion. The severe lattice distortion and inhomogeneous bond length of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 change the anharmonicity of low-frequency phonons, increasing the content of phonons with a negative Grüneisen constant [50]. Therefore, (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 has the relatively low CTE.

### 3.4 Dielectric properties

The dielectric property is one of the most important indicators of wave-transparent material. Normally, the dielectric constant should be less than 10, and the dielectric loss should be less than 0.01. Figure 15 compares the dielectric constant and dielectric loss of TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, HfP2O7, and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 at 9.6 and 14.5 GHz. Among them, TiP2O7 has the largest dielectric constant (9.7 (9.6 GHz) and 9.3 (14.5 GHz)) and the lowest dielectric loss (7.9×10−3 (9.6 GHz) and 8.3×10−3 (14.5 GHz)). HfP2O7 has the lowest dielectric constant (5.6 (9.6 GHz) and 5.4 (14.5 GHz)) and a surprisingly high dielectric loss (4.1×10−2 (9.6 GHz) and 3.6×10−2 (14.5 GHz)). The dielectric constant (7.5 (9.6 GHz) and 7.2 (14.5 GHz)) and dielectric loss (9.7×10−3 (9.6 GHz) and 9.7×10−3 (14.5 GHz)) of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 are in the middle of those of TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, and HfP2O7, which illustrates that multicomponent doping maintains good dielectric properties.

The dielectric properties of a material are related to temperature, frequency, crystal structure, composition, and other factors. Among these factors, the structure of the ceramics and their composition are the key factors influencing the dielectric constant [51−53]. (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 maintains the same crystal structure as ZrP2O7, and the introduction of Ti and Hf into ZrP2O7 only causes an increase in lattice distortion and entropy, both of which synergistically limit the crystal structure transition and maintain a low dielectric constant and loss tangent. In addition, Ti, Zr, and Hf belong to the same main group of tuple periodic tables, and they have similar properties. Thus, a change in composition has less effect on the dielectric properties. Therefore, it is inferred that defects and entropy mediating mechanisms are the main reasons that (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 maintains good dielectric properties.

Factors affecting dielectric loss include interfacial polarization, dipolar polarization, defect-induced polarization, and conductive loss [54]. Many studies have shown that oxygen vacancies are prone to form in Hf-containing oxide ceramics, e.g., HfO2, HfZrO4, and HfSiO4 [55−57]. Figure 16 shows the geometric phase analysis of the HRTEM images of TiP2O7, ZrP2O7, HfP2O7, and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7. Compared with those of TiP2O7 and ZrP2O7, the strains of HfP2O7 and (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 are greater, suggesting a high concentration of defects. Therefore, the lower thermal conductivity of HfP2O7 may also be caused by defects in the material, thus enhancing the defect-induced polarization loss. The decrease in the dielectric loss of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 compared to HfP2O7 is because the content of Hf in the (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 material is only 1/3, resulting in fewer defects in the Hf material. Although doping causes lattice distortion, the composition of the material is also different from that of HfP2O7, and the introduction of Zr and Ti reduces the dielectric loss. Thus, the decrease in the dielectric loss of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 is due to a combination of composition and microstructure.

## 4 Conclusions

The thermomechanical and dielectric properties of (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 solid solutions were comprehensively investigated in this work. The doping of Ti, Zr, and Hf introduces a large lattice distortion and increased entropy. The phonon scattering is significantly enhanced, and grain growth is suppressed. A decrease in thermal conductivity and an improvement in mechanical properties are achieved. At the same time, lattice distortions also cause changes in bond length and tilting angle, so that (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 does not undergo sudden expansion as does ZrP2O7. The phase transition of ZrP2O7 is inhibited, resulting in excellent thermal stability at high temperatures. In addition, (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 maintains excellent dielectric properties. The improved thermomechanical and dielectric properties of the (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)P2O7 solid solution suggest that it is a promising high-performance wave-transparent material.
