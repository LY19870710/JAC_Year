## 1 Introduction

To date, transition metal oxides (TMOs) have been extensively investigated as potential anode active materials for lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) owing to their high theoretical capacity, stable chemical/thermal properties, and low cost [1]. Unfortunately, TMO anodes undergo severe structural degradation, generic volume expansion/shrinkage, and low electronic/ionic conductivity during continuous lithiation/delithiation processes, resulting in inferior cycling stability and rate capability [2]. Therefore, many strategies have been introduced to improve the electrochemical properties of TMO anode materials by combining them with carbon-based materials [3], doping them with small amounts of heteroatoms, such as La3+, Sc3+, Al3+, and so on [4−6], and tuning their morphological structure [7]. However, the resulting batteries may not meet practical requirements.

Recently, high-entropy oxides (HEOs), as a new kind of multicomponent and single-phase solid solution, have drawn great interests for a wide range of applications owing to their entropy-stabilized crystal structure, abundant defect sites, synergistic effect of multiple cations, and natural abundance [8−10]. As a result of these advantages, transition-metal-based (TM-based) HEOs have emerged as promising new class of battery active materials for LIBs due to their improved cycling stability and lithium storage capacity [11−14]. Among them, spinel-structured HEOs have three-dimensional channels for Li+ diffusion and two Wyckoff sites (tetrahedral and octahedral sites) for random cation distribution, which further enhances the configurational entropy and the formation of mixed valence states, thereby achieving reversible redox reactions [15]. As expected, spinel-type HEOs have gained considerable research attention as high-performance anode materials for next-generation LIBs [16−18]. For example, Nguyen *et* *al*. [16] prepared spinel-structured (Co0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2Mn0.2Ni0.2**)**3O4 nanoparticles by a surfactant-assisted hydrothermal method, and the as-prepared HEO anode delivered a superior capacity of 1235 mAh·g−1 with 90% capacity retention after 200 cycles at 20 mA·g−1. However, the inferior intrinsic electronic conductivity, which results in a poor rate capability, hinders its practical applications.

To further improve the high-rate Li+ storage performance of HEO anodes, coating other conductive materials to the interface, as a convenient and effective “extrinsic” strategy to regulate the electrical conductivity, has been widely mentioned in the fields of energy storage, microelectronics, supercapacitors, etc. [19,20]. In particular, polyaniline-coated (Co0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2Mn0.2Ni0.2**)**3O4 HEO anode materials prepared by Yen *et* *al*. [19] showed an extremely long cycling stability of 261 mAh·g−1 at 4.0 A·g−1 after 3200 cycles and a high rate performance of 325 mAh·g−1 at 10 A·g−1. Moreover, “intrinsic” approaches, such as heteroatom-doping strategies, have also been proven to play a contributing role in improving electrochemical energy storage [21,22]. For instance, Liu *et* *al*. [21] explored Li+-doped (LiCoCuMgNiZn**)**O anode materials by a wet-chemical molten salt method, and the (LiCoCuMgNiZn**)**O anode yielded a 51.6% increase in the rate capability at 2 A·g−1 (455 mAh·g−1 at 2 A·g−1) compared to that of the (CoCuMgNiZn**)**O anode (300 mAh·g−1 at 2 A·g−1). Nevertheless, the customization of HEO with intrinsic electron conductivity is still lacking.

Rare earth (RE) elements have garnered much attention due to the unique chemical and electronic properties of 4f subshell electrons for modifying the electronic structure and improving the electrochemical performance of spinel-type TMOs to some degree [23,24]. Among RE elements, La is the lightest and second most abundant RE element and is a preferred dopant due to its large ionic radius, strong La**–**O bond energy, and spin coupling [24,25].

Therefore, this work successfully designed and synthesized a series of single-phase spinel-type (La*x*CoCrFeMnNi**)**3/5+*x*O4 (*x* = 0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, and 0.04) HEO electrode materials by a solution combustion synthesis (SCS) method. The effects of large-radius (1.36 Å) La3+ doping on the microstructure, the kinetic transport of electrons, diffusion of Li+, and the electrochemical performance were also studied and discussed in detail.

### 2.1 Synthesis of HEO

Undoped and La-doped spinel-type (La*x*CoCrFeMnNi**)**3/5+*x*O4 (*x* = 0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, and 0.04) HEO nanocrystalline powders were synthesized by the SCS method starting from the corresponding metal nitrates and fuel glycine, and the details can be found in our previous studies [26]. All the chemicals were of analytical grade and obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China, and the ratio of fuel glycine to the oxidizer was 0.5. The molar ratio of La : Co : Cr : Fe : Mn : Ni in the as-synthesized sample was *x* : 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 : 1, which can be formulated as (La*x*CoCrFeMnNi**)**3/5+*x*O4. For a typical synthesis, stoichiometric amounts of metal nitrates and fuel were mixed homogeneously in a minimum amount of deionized water by stirring at room temperature, and then the mixture was evaporated to obtain viscous gel. Subsequently, the gel was calcined at 1023 K for 30 min and quenched to room temperature by direct extraction from the hot zone. For convenience, the samples were denoted as La*x* ((Co0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2Mn0.2Ni0.2)3O4 (La0), La0.01, La0.02, La0.03, and La0.04) in sequence.

### 2.2 Physicochemical characterizations

The crystal structures were detected by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Mini-Flex 600, Rigaku, Japan) and the XRD patterns were analyzed by Rietveld refinement using the software package GSAS. The morphology, microstructure, and composition were determined on a scanning electron microscope (SEM; S-3500N, Hitachi, Japan) equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-2100, JEOL, Japan). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images were also analyzed by using the digital micrograph software package (Gatan) to obtain the interplanar spacing based on inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT) fringe processing algorithms. N2 adsorption**–**desorption isotherms were detected on an analyzer (Tristar Π3020, Micromeritics Instrument Co., USA) at 77 K. The specific surface area and pore size distribution were also calculated by Barrett**–**Emmett**–**Teller (BET) and Barret**–**Joyner**–**Halenda (BJH) methods, respectively.

### 2.3 Cell assembly and electrochemical measurement

The electrochemical performance of the as-synthesized samples was evaluated using CR2025 coin-type half cells, as described in our previous reports [22]. In brief, working electrodes with 14 mm in diameter and 1.5±0.1 mg in mass loading were prepared by coating homogeneous slurry with active materials (70 wt%, as-synthesized powders), super P carbon black (20 wt%), and sodium alginate binder (10 wt%) dissolved in some distilled water on pretreated Cu foil. After that, the coin cells were assembled in an argon-filled glove box (Lab 2000, Etelux, China) with the as-prepared electrode as the anode, a polyethylene membrane (Celgard 2400, Celgard LLC, USA) as the separator, lithium foil as the cathode, and a mixed solvent of 1 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate (EC) : diethyl carbonate (DEC) : dimethyl carbonate (DMC) (1 : 1 : 1 in volume) as the electrolyte.

The galvanostatic discharge/charge performance was evaluated on a neware battery testing system (BST-4008W, Neware Electronic Co., Ltd., China) over a voltage range of 0.01**–**3.00 V (vs*.* Li+/Li) at room temperature. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were all performed on an electrochemical workstation (CHI760e, Shanghai Chenghua Instruments Co., Ltd., China). The CV curves were scanned over a potential range of 0.01–3.00 V (vs*.* Li+/Li) at various scan rates, while EIS analysis was executed over the frequency range between 1.0×105 and 0.01 Hz.

### 3.1 Structural characterizations

Figure 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of the as-synthesized La3+-doped samples, accompanied by (Co0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2Mn0.2Ni0.2)3O4 for comparison with our earlier publication [27]. The diffraction peaks indexed as the (111), (220), (311), (222), (400), (422), (511), and (440) planes confirm that the as-synthesized samples have a single-phase cubic spinel structure with the space group *Fd 3¯m* [26]. No additional phases other than cubic spinel are detected in the XRD patterns, indicating that La3+ is successfully incorporated into the lattice of the spinel. Notably, after La3+ doping, the (311) peak shifts to a lower angle due to the larger ionic radius (1.13 Å) of La3+ than that of the host transition metal ions [25]. Moreover, the crystalline sizes estimated from the most intense XRD peak by Scherrer’s equation are 21.45, 15.00, 15.34, 16.55 and 15.98 nm for La0, La0.01, La0.02, La0.03, and La0.04, respectively. As expected, the crystalline sizes of the La3+-doped samples are smaller than those of the undoped La0 sample.

To investigate the crystal structure and phase purity, Rietveld refinement analysis was performed. The typical Rietveld refinement curves of the as-synthesized samples are shown in Figs. 1(b)–1(f), and the calculated results are listed in Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). As clearly shown in Figs. 1(b)–1(f), the experimental data and the calculated curves demonstrate a reasonable match. Moreover, the values of the statistical fit parameters (profile R*-factor (*R*p), weighted profile *R*-factor (*R*wp), and *χ*2 = (*R*wp/*R*p)2) also demonstrate that the quality of the refined XRD patterns are reliable. Furthermore, the refined cell parameters and the unit cell volume of the La3+-doped samples increase compared to those of the undoped La0 sample, leading to a greater degree of lattice distortion, which is also confirmed by the *R*wp values [9,28]. The structural distortion caused by the doping of La3+ broadens the transfer channels and increases the diffusion routes for Li+, especially at low current density [25,29]. Moreover, lattice distortion can also act as a redox active site to further improve the Li+ storage performance [30]. However, upon further increasing the La3+ content from 0.01 to 0.04, a slight lattice contraction is observed. A shrinking lattice implies the occurrence of additional charge compensation mechanisms, such as oxygen vacancies (OVs) or low-valent transition metal cations in a higher valence state, because the radius of La3+ is marginally larger than the average radius of host transition metal ions [25]. This is also confirmed by the XPS results.

The morphology and elemental distribution of the representative La0.01, La0, and La0.04 samples are presented in Fig. 2 and Figs. S1 and S2 in the ESM. The SEM images at lower and higher magnifications show a porous morphology and foam-like agglomerates constructed of many spherical ultrafine primary particles due to the nature of the combustion synthesis reaction [26]. Furthermore, the EDS mappings confirmed that all the elements were distributed uniformly throughout the whole landscape, indicating the homogeneity of the chemical composition and microstructure. In addition, EDS analysis confirmed that the La0.01 sample was composed only of La, Co, Cr, Fe, Mn, Ni, and oxygen, and the La : Co : Cr : Fe : Mn : Ni molar ratio was 0.01 : 1.0 : 1.0 : 0.95 : 1.08 : 0.97, which is consistent with the designed ratio.

The porous structure and specific surface area of the La3+-doped samples were determined by N2 adsorption–desorption analysis, as illustrated in Figs. 2(e)–2(h). The result of La0 in our earlier work is also presented in Fig. S3 in the ESM for comparison [27]. All the samples exhibit a type-IV with H2-type hysteresis loops, indicating typical mesoporous characteristics [31−33]. The pore size distribution curves are shown in the insets of Figs. 2(e)–2(h) and Fig. S3 in the ESM, and most of the pore sizes are in the range of 2–40 nm, confirming the mesoporous structure. The BET specific surface area (S*BET), BJH adsorption cumulative pore volume (*V*BJH), and average pore diameter (*D*aver.) of the as-synthesized La3+-doped samples are listed in Table 1. These phenomena are attributed to the fact that La3+ doping could refine the crystalline grains, thereby improving the specific surface area [34]. It is well known that the high specific surface area and the rich mesoporous structures not only provide sufficient passageway for Li+ uptake and release during the electrochemical process but also increase the contact area of the active materials/electrolytes, thus improving the lithium storage capability [35].

The elemental composition and surface chemical states of the as-synthesized samples were further determined using XPS, as shown in Figs. 3(a)–3(h) and Fig. S4 in the ESM for comparison [27]. The XPS survey spectra in Fig. 3(a) reveal that all La, Co, Cr, Fe, Mn, Ni, and O are detected in the La3+-doped samples. As seen in the high-resolution XPS spectra (Figs. 3(b)–3(h) and Fig. S4 in the ESM), the La cations in the as-synthesized samples are in trivalent states, the Cr cations are in mixed trivalent/hexavalent states, and the Mn cations are in mixed trivalent/tetravalent states, while the Fe, Co, and Ni cations exhibit mixed divalent/trivalent states [17]. Furthermore, the calculated OV/OT (total oxygen, OT) ratio as well as the relative contents of higher valence ions to lower valence according to the peak area are shown in Table S2 in the ESM. It has been demonstrated that the incorporation of La3+ into the (Co0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2Mn0.2Ni0.2)3O4 lattice causes an increase in the concentration of oxygen vacancies. However, upon further increasing the La3+ doping amount x* to 0.04, the concentration of oxygen vacancies decreases. In fact, the charge balance between the positive La3+/oxygen vacancies and the negative TM cations occurs when a small amount of La3+ is doped in the (Co0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2Mn0.2Ni0.2)3O4 lattice. Table S2 in the ESM also shows that Cr6+ and Fe3+ mainly compensate for the charge, while Co3+ is almost unchanged. However, the content of Ni3+ and Mn4+ decreases due to the charge balance. Hence, the decrease in the concentration of oxygen vacancies can be attributed to the presence of more high-valence Cr6+, Fe3+, and Ni3+ in the La0.04 sample.

Moreover, the electronic conductivities of the undoped and La3+-doped samples were determined via four-point probe resistance measurements, as shown in Fig. 3(i). As indicated, the synergistic effect of lattice distortion and moderate oxygen vacancies could optimize the electronic structure, thus leading to a maximum room-temperature electronic conductivity (0.204 S·cm−1) for the La0.01 sample. Therefore, it can be concluded that La3+ doping not only causes lattice distortion to increase oxygen vacancies but also refines the crystalline grains and increases the specific surface area. Moreover, the synergistic effect of lattice distortion and oxygen vacancies can tailor the electronic structure to improve electron/ion conductivity [37–39].

### 3.2 Electrochemical performance

The electrochemical properties of the as-synthesized HEO electrodes were evaluated in fabricated coin-type half cells between (0.01 and 3.0 V) vs*.* Li+/Li. To better understand the conversion reactions during the charge/discharge processes, the CV curves of the as-synthesized electrodes in the first three cycles at a scanning rate of 0.2 mV·s−1 are shown in Fig. 4(a) and Figs. S6(a1)**–**S6(a4) in the ESM. The CV curves of the La3+-doped electrodes are similar to those of the spinel-type (Co0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2Mn0.2Ni0.2)3O4 electrode reported in our previous study, indicating that the doped La3+ in the as-synthesized electrodes is electrochemically inactive [27]. As shown in Fig. 4(a), the initial cathodic curve exhibited three distinct reduction peaks that were considerably different from those of the subsequent cycles, confirming that irreversible activation occurred during the first cycle. The initial reduction peak at 1.48 V disappeared in the subsequent sweeps, corresponding to the decomposition of the electrolyte together with the formation of solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) films on the electrode surface [40]. This is related to the initial irreversible capacity loss, as indicated in the charge‒discharge curve (Fig. 4(b)). The peaks at 0.15 and 0.51 V are assigned to the reduction of active transition metal cations accompanied by Li2O formation. In the following anodic process, the oxidation peak at approximately 1.58 V can be ascribed to the reversible reoxidation of the reduced species accompanying the decomposition of Li2O [41]. After the first cycle, the main reduction peaks shift to higher potentials of 1.10 and 0.69 V due to pulverization of the electrode active materials and a decrease in the crystal size during the first lithiation process [42]. However, the oxidation peaks remain unchanged during the subsequent cycles. The potential difference between the redox peaks represents the degree of polarization of the electrode materials [43]. In contrast with Figs. S5(a1)**–**S5(a4) in the ESM, the potential difference (Δ*φ* = 0.89 V) of the La0.01 electrode is the smallest among the synthesized electrodes, and the Δ*φ* values of the La0, La0.02, La0.03, and La0.04 electrodes are 0.90, 1.07, 0.95, and 0.93 V, respectively, indicating that the La0.01 electrode has less interfacial polarization [22]. It is also noted that the CV curves of La0.01 in the second to third cycles almost overlap, confirming the favorable stability of the electrode.

The galvanostatic discharge and charge profiles of the synthesized electrodes at 0.2 A·g−1 are shown in Fig. 4(b) and Figs. S5(b1)**–**S5(b4) in the ESM. All electrodes show similar initial charge/discharge voltage profiles, and the initial discharge capacities of the La0, La0.01, La0.02, La0.03, and La0.04 electrodes are 1198, 1419, 1035, 920, and 942 mAh·g−1, respectively, with Coulombic efficiencies (CEs) of 75.40%, 73.69%, 73.05%, 73.33% and 62.49%, respectively. The slight decrease in CE for the three Lax* (*x* = 0.01, 0.02, and 0.03) samples can be attributed to the increase in the specific surface area and decrease in the number of crystalline grains, resulting in more irreversible reactions during initial charging. Nevertheless, the La0.04 electrode shows a higher initial discharge capacity and lower CE than La0.03, which may be due to its smallest crystalline grain. Note that the initial discharge curve is also quite different from that of the following reversible cycles, indicating that structural rearrangement accompanied by different reactions occurred during the cycling process.

Figure 4(c) compares the cycling performance and CE of the as-synthesized HEO electrodes evaluated at a rate of 0.2 A·g−1. The specific capacities of the as-synthesized electrodes decrease gradually over the initial 80 cycles due to the continuous structural transformation, gradual formation of SEI films on the electrode surface, and irreversible conversion reaction [44]. As one cycle progresses, the specific capacity increases continuously due to the gradual activation of the electrode materials and the penetration of electrolyte molecules into the inner pores of the HEO electrode. This phenomenon is also observed in a rock-salt (Co0.2Cu0.2Mg0.2Ni0.2Zn0.2)O HEO anode [11,40]. It is worth mentioning that with cycling, CEs of the anodes all rapidly increase to 100% after the initial charge/discharge cycle. Furthermore, we can also intuitively see that the La0.01 electrode delivers the highest reversible specific capacity during the whole cycle, and the highest discharge/charge specific capacity of 1228/1248 mAh·g−1 is obtained for 400 cycles, compared with discharge/charge specific capacities of 771/778, 369/378, 472/475, and 485/489 mAh·g−1 for the La0, La0.02, La0.03, and La0.04 electrodes, respectively. The theoretical specific capacities calculated by Eq. (1) are 923.2, 919.2, 915.1, 911.2, and 907.3 mAh·g−1 for La0, La0.01, La0.02, La0.03, and La0.04 electrodes, respectively.

(1) Capacity=n⋅F3.6⋅M

where *n* is the number of elections per molecule in the redox reaction; *F* is the Faraday constant (96,485 C·mol−1), and *M* represents the molar mass. Notably, the actual specific capacity of the La0.01 electrode after 400 cycles exceeds its theoretical value. According to the published literature, researchers have shown that TMO anodes always exhibit greater theoretical value, and the increased Li+ storage capacity can potentially be attributed to processes such as spin-polarized electron storage [45], pseudocapacitive effects [43], interfacial charge storage theory [46], or the reversible formation and decomposition of SEI interfaces [47].

The rate capability of the as-synthesized electrodes is displayed in Fig. 4(d), and the reversible capacity of La0.01 at different rates is the highest, indicating that the La0.01 electrode has the fastest Li+ diffusion and reaction kinetics. Specifically, the La0.01 electrode delivers a maximum capacity of 1006 mAh·g−1 at a low rate of 0.1 A·g−1 and can still maintain a discharge capacity of 420 mAh·g−1 even at a high rate of 3 A·g−1, exhibiting a capacity retention of 41.75%. When the rate is restored to 0.1 A·g−1 after 25 cycles, the largest proportion (more than 87%) of specific capacity can be recovered due to the specific capacities decrease over the initial 80 cycles, as we explained earlier in the text. Remarkably, the La0.01 electrode yields an increase of 75% in the rate capability at 3 A·g−1 compared with that of the undoped (Co0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2Mn0.2Ni0.2)3O4 electrode (240 mAh·g−1 at 3 A·g−1). As summarized in Table S3 in the ESM, the high rate capacity achieved by the La0.01 electrode is superior to that of other published spinel-type anode materials and much greater than that of spinel HEO anodes coated with natural conductive materials in Ref. [20]. Therefore, a small amount of doped La3+ is beneficial for improving the reversibility of Li+ insertion/desertion in the electrode, while an increase in the amount of doped La3+ gradually reduces the amount of active metal ions, which decreases the number of active sites available for charge transfer [29].

where *i*(*V*) is the current at a particular potential and *k*1*ν* and *k*2*ν*1/2 represent the pseudocapacitive and diffusion behaviors, respectively. As shown in Figs. 5(c) and 5(d), the pseudocapacitive contribution increases from 64.6% to 89.3% as the scan rate increases from 0.1 to 1.0 V·s−1, which is beneficial for boosting the rate capability, especially at a high rate. As a result, the pseudocapacitive contribution plays a pivotal role in improving the rate performance and increasing the capacity.

EIS measurements were also adopted to investigate the migration resistance of Li+ and electrons in the La0.01 electrode, and compared with undoped La0 anode. As indicated in the EIS spectra of the La0.01 and La0 electrodes before and after 500 cycles in Figs. 6(a) and 6(c), all spectra show a single semicircle in the high- to medium-frequency region (representing the SEI film resistance (*R*SEI) and charge-transfer resistance (*R*ct)), and a sloping line in the low-frequency region (representing Warburg impedance (*Z*w) corresponded to Li+ diffusion coefficient in the electrode, where *Z*w is inversely proportional to the slope of the line) [43]. Obviously, the semicircle diameter and the slope of the anodes after 500 cycles are smaller than those of the fresh anodes, exhibiting an improved charge transfer ability as well as Li+ diffusion ability during charge/discharge cycles [12]. Moreover, although *R*ct of the La0.01 electrode is larger than that of the La0 electrode before cycling due to the lack of La3+ doping, the La0.01 electrode has a significantly smaller *R*ct after 500 cycles, indicating a faster charge transfer ability for the La3+-doped La0.01 electrode during the electrochemical reaction.

*
Fig. 6EIS Nyquist plots where the insets show equivalent circuits and fitting lines of Z*' against *ω*−1/2 for (a, b) La0.01 and (c, d) La0 electrodes before and after 500 cycles.

In addition, plots of the real resistance (*Z*ˊ) and the inverse square root of the frequency (*ω*−1/2) in the low-frequency region are shown in Figs. 6(b) and 6(d), respectively to evaluate the Li+ diffusion performance. The diffusion coefficient of Li+ (* DLi+) can be calculated by  DLi+=R2T2/(2A2n4F4C2σ2), where T* represents the test temperature of 298.15 K; *R* stands for the gas constant of 8.314 J·mol−1·K−1; *A* is the electrode surface area of 1.52183 cm2; *C* means the molar concentration of Li+; and *σ* indicates the Warburg factor, which can be derived from the slope by * Z′=Rs+Rct+σω−1/2[6]. Therefore, the calculated D*Li*+* values of La0.01 electrode before and after 500 cycles are 3.15×10−18 and 7.64×10−17 cm2·s−1, respectively, 9.81 and 1.77 times higher than that of undoped La0 anode before (3.21×10−19 cm2·s−1) and after 500 cycles (4.31×10−17 cm2·s−1), suggesting the doping of La3+ in the form of La0.01 can effectively improve the electrochemical reaction kinetics of Li+ insertion/extraction. We speculate that the superior rate capability and excellent cycling stability of La0.01 can be ascribed to the small amount of La3+ doping, which enhances the charge transfer ability as well as Li+ diffusion during cycling.

To elucidate the reversible lithium storage mechanism during electrochemical cycling, detailed XRD and TEM/HRTEM were performed on the La0.01 powders and electrodes after cycling, as shown in Fig. 7. The *ex situ* XRD patterns depict the structural evolution of the La0.01 electrode under different states of charge during the first lithiation/delithiation process, as shown in Fig. 7(a). The diffraction peaks of the spinel structure almost disappear in the initial discharge to 1.5 V, even after recharging to 3 V. This may be due to the formation of smaller grains of the active materials during the conversion reactions below the XRD detection threshold, which is also identified by the HRTEM results. The TEM image of the as-synthesized La0.01 powders confirmed their homogeneous spherical morphology and porous microstructures with a size concentrated at 15.8 nm (Fig. 7(b1)). Moreover, the equidistant and straight lattice fringes in the HRTEM image suggest high crystallinity and long-range order of atoms within pristine La0.01 HEO, and the lattice stripes are determined to be (311) and (111) planes by measuring the interplanar spacing between the 10 nearest neighbors (Fig. 7(b2)).

*
Fig. 7(a) Ex situ* XRD patterns of La0.01 electrode at different states of discharge/charge. (b1) TEM and (b2) HREM images of La0.01 powders with inset showing IFFT images and profile plots of atomic array, and HRTEM and corresponding magnified HRTEM images with inset showing IFFT images after (c1, c2) the first and (d1, d2) 500 cycles of La0.01 electrode.

As shown in Figs. 7(c1) and 7(d1), the long-range order region disappears, and the active materials La0.01 gradually transform into many smaller nanograins after the redox reaction, even after the initial cycle. Furthermore, as clearly seen in the magnified HRTEM images (Figs. 7(c2) and 7(d2)), some small crystalline domains (marked with rectangles) with well-exposed lattice fringes can be clearly identified even after 500 cycles, indicating that the cycled active materials have a certain degree of crystallinity. Moreover, the inset IFFT images reveal that the locally blurred and distorted lattice strips may originate from the reduced crystallinity induced by repeated Li+ insertion/extraction, thereby creating more internal defects. The refined particles can reduce Li+ diffusion distances and increase the electrode**–**electrolyte interface area, and the enriched defects can facilitate active site exposure and regulate the electronic structure of the active center, thereby synergistically boosting the electrochemical Li+ storage performance during cycling [50].

To further investigate the structural evolution of the active materials during lithiation/delithiation, the interplanar spacing was measured from IFFT images. After the first cycle, the rock-salt (200) plane and spinel (311) planes are identified based on the interplanar spacing in the IFFT images, indicating that the conversion process is not fully reversible. Previous studies also reported that the rock salt phase can be observed in spinel-structured (Co0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2Mn0.2Ni0.2)3O4, (Ni0.2Co0.2Mn0.2Fe0.2Ti0.2)3O4, and (FeCoNiCrMnZnLi)3O4 HEO anodes during the initial several cycles, and their original spinel structure can also be retained [48,51,52]. For example, Sun *et al*. [48] investigated the structural evolution of a spinel-structured (Co0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2Mn0.2Ni0.2)3O4 anode through *in operando* XRD, HRTEM, or even X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectroscopy and confirmed the existence of a rock-salt phase in the spinel structure in the first lithiation process, and the spinel structure was maintained even when the as-synthesized electrode was fully lithiated. However, to date, only a few papers have further studied the structural changes in active materials after long-term discharge/charge cycles. According to the HRTEM image (Fig. 7(d2)) of the La0.01 anode after 500 cycles, the lattice fringes that are well matched to the spinel structure gradually disappear, while the lattice fringes corresponding to the rock-salt (311) plane can also be maintained, and at the same time, new interplanar spacings of 0.178 nm relating to the Co(200) plane can also be detected [53]. During long-term delithiation processes, some of the metal nanograins may not fully react and thus remain in lower oxidation states. Thus, after long-term cycling, the spinel frameworks eventually transform into a rock-salt structure, which is different from the spinel structure of HEO anodes reported by other researchers [48,51,52], and further research on electrodes after long-term cycling is needed. As a result, the synergy of the nanosized rock-salt phase (if only partially) and metallic nanocrystals during discharge/charge processes is considered another critical factor for the excellent high-rate energy storage performance.

## 4 Conclusions

To improve the high-rate Li+ storage performance of HEO anodes, La3+ was doped into model spinel-type materials (Co0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2Mn0.2Ni0.2)3O4 to prepare  La3+-doped samples. The doped samples still maintain a complete spinel structure and porous morphology. On one hand, it is worth mentioning that moderately doped La3+ not only causes lattice distortion to increase the surface oxygen vacancies of the spinel structure but also synergistically enhances the ability of electron transport and Li+ diffusion. On the other hand, La3+ doping could refine the crystalline grains and improve the specific surface area to provide sufficient passageway and active sites for Li+ uptake and release during the electrochemical process. However, excessive La doping hinders the conductivity and Li+ diffusion kinetics, resulting in capacity loss. Compared with the model spinel (Co0.2Cr0.2Fe0.2Mn0.2Ni0.2**)**3O4 anode synthesized under identical experimental conditions, the (La0.01CoCrFeMnNi)3/5.01O4 anode exhibited superior rate performance (1006 mAh·g−1 at 0.1 A·g−1 and 420 mAh·g−1 at 3 A·g−1), with a capacity retention of 41.75%. Moreover, the formation of nanosized rock-salt phases and metallic nanograins during discharge/charge processes is also helpful for increasing the electrode/electrolyte interface, reducing the Li+ diffusion length, and transporting electrons, thus increasing the rate performance. Therefore, this work also provides a facile heteroatom-doping strategy to improve the electrochemical performance of spinel-type HEO anodes for LIBs.
