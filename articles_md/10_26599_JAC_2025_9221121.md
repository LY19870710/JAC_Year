## 1 Introduction

With the rapid development of global energy storage technology, the development of energy storage technology represented by lithium batteries is particularly rapid [1,2]. Data from the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) show that the national production of lithium-ion batteries will be 940 GWh in 2023, a year-on-year increase of 25%. Accompanied by a dramatic increase in ownership, the number of retired lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) has increased annually, causing great pressure on the environment, and improper handling will inevitably cause environmental pollution [3−5]. Retired LIBs contain various valuable resources, such as cobalt and lithium. Therefore, the use of efficient and clean recycling process for the treatment of used LIBs has become the research focus of scholars at home and abroad.

Currently, pyrometallurgy and hydrometallurgy are commonly used methods for the recovery of waste LIBs materials [6]. The traditional pyrometallurgical procedure usually incinerates all the acetylene black, organic electrolyte and binder, which consumes a large amount of energy. In contrast to pyrometallurgy, hydrometallurgy has the advantages of mild reaction conditions and high recovery efficiency. In the hydrometallurgical method, the dismantled electrodes after a series of mechanical pretreatments are first dissolved in an acidic solution. Then, the solution containing the metal ions is retrieved by precipitation separation [7−9]. When a strong acid solution is used, more than 99% of the cobalt and lithium can be extracted. Nevertheless, toxic gases such as Cl2, SO3, and NOₓ are also released during the reaction. In addition, the recovery process becomes more complicated due to a series of separation and purification processes between cobalt, lithium, and other components, which may generate secondary pollution [10−13]. Therefore, organic acids, such as succinic acid, citric acid and malic acid, are considered promising alternatives. However, organic acids face their own set of challenges. For example, additional reducing agents such as hydrogen peroxide, glucose or other substances are needed to accelerate the process [14,15].

Tribocatalysis, as a novel catalytic technology, can utilize tiny amounts of energy in nature for catalysis [16−18]. The outstanding advantages in energy utilization efficiency and the ability of simple and low-power friction to deliver excellent catalytic effects have led many researchers to study it continuously, with applications in dye degradation [19], nitrogen fixation [20], bioremediation [21], hydrogen production [22], and carbon dioxide reduction [23]. To investigate the application of friction energy in other fields, we explored tribocatalysis for the first time in the recovery and reuse of waste LIBs cathode materials by using the weak acid leaching process of hydrometallurgy without adding any reducing agent. The aim is to develop a straightforward, efficient and environmentally friendly recovery process to effectively separate and recover valuable metals from waste LIBs. Specifically, in the process of tribocatalysis, the continuous excitation of friction between the catalyst, Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) disk, and glass beaker is achieved through the magnetic stirring of a magnetic stirrer, thereby generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) continuously via triboelectric effects. Zinc oxide, serving as the catalyst, can be recovered and reused. Given the characteristics of tribocatalysis, which involves recyclable catalysts (original dielectric materials) and mechanically activated reaction zones, we anticipate that this method can provide a green, efficient, and environmentally friendly approach for the recycling of LIBs, addressing the exponentially growing demand for LIBs production.

### 2.1 Materials and reagents

The used lithium cobalt oxide (LCO) (Shenzhen Maikos Energy Technology Co., Ltd, China) and Li-ion ternary batteries (NMCs) (Tianjin Guoan Menguli New Material Technology Co., Ltd, China) used in this study were recovered from the used cell phone market in Guilin City. The catalysts used included ZnO (99.9%, Aladdin, China), TiO2 (99.8%, Aladdin, China), and SiO2 (≥ 99.9%, Bisili, China) (Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). A citric acid (CA, 99.5%, Sinopharm, China) leaching solution was subsequently utilized for the leaching experiments of the cells.

### 2.2 Discharge and disassembly

The recycled spent LIBs need to undergo discharge, disassembly, roasting, and separation processes (Fig. S1 in the ESM). Before disassembly, to ensure safety, the spent LIBs were soaked in a 10% NaCl solution (≥ 99.5%, Xilong, China) for 12 h. Then, they were taken out and dried in an oven (GZX-GF101-1-BS-II, Shanghai Yuejin Medical Equipment Co., China) at 55 °C. After that, scissors were used to cut open the metal casing of the dried LIBs, and the enclosed cathode, anode, and separator materials were obtained. In order to remove acetylene and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) from the cathode material to improve the leaching effect, long strips of the cathode material were cut into small rectangular pieces, placed in a muffle furnace (LHT04-17, Nabertherm, Germany) and held at 600 °C for 2 h. The LCO adhering to the aluminum foil was separated from the aluminum foil after the roasting process. The separated powder was hand ground to make it easier to leach. Finally, it was tested via X-ray diffraction (XRD) and infrared spectroscopy and could be used as a pure LCO for tribocatalytic leaching experiments (Fig. S2 in the ESM).

### 2.3 Metal leaching

All leaching experiments were carried out in a glass beaker to which a PTFE disk (Fig. S3 and S4 in the ESM) had been added and to which the LCO powder that had been recycled and ground was added. Citric acid solutions of known concentration and content were added to the reactor (Discovery-H 0.5/10, Shanghai Lichen Instrument Technology Co., China) by pipetting. The beaker was then placed in a magnetic stirrer (DF-101S, Shanghai Akihisa, China) with a water bath. The temperature of the magnetic stirrer was controlled, and magnetic stirring was used as a source of mechanical energy to reduce and leach various ions from LCO and NMC, which were subsequently recovered via chemical precipitation techniques [24].

During the leaching experiments, samples were collected at predetermined time intervals. When the reaction time was less than 5 h, black residue (unreacted LCO), catalyst and pink solution were present in the reaction mixture. At this time, centrifugation of the reaction mixture separated the residue and pink solution. When the reaction time was more than 5 h, the LCO completely reacted, and the catalyst and pink solution were contained in the reaction mixture. At this time, the reaction mixture was centrifuged to separate the catalyst from the pink solution, and the catalyst was washed with water three times and dried for subsequent use. After normal leaching, the following operational variables were considered to determine the optimal conditions: type of catalyst (ZnO, TiO2, or SiO2), catalyst content (50–200 mg), concentration of CA (0.3–0.9 M), solid–liquid ratio (8–14 g/L), temperature (55–95 °C) and reaction time (4–9 h).

### 2.4 Metal ions precipitation

First, the LCO filtrate was leached under optimal conditions several times. At room temperature, sodium oxalate (≥ 99.8%, Sinopharm, China) was introduced into the filtrate while stirring, and the mixture was allowed to react for 30 min. Then, the mixture was centrifuged and filtered to obtain a pink precipitate. Afterwards, the lithium-rich solution obtained in the previous step was concentrated in an oven at 100 °C. Concentrated hydrochloric acid was added to adjust the pH to 0.5 via a pH tester (pH = 10, LICHEN, China). Subsequently, NaOH (≥ 96.0%, Sinopharm, China) was added to further adjust the pH to 12, reaching the range for the precipitation of lithium carbonate. Finally, a saturated sodium carbonate (≥ 99.38%, Sinopharm, China) solution at 85 °C was introduced into the solution mentioned above. Then, the resulting mixture was centrifuged and filtered in an aqueous environment maintained at 85 °C, yielding a white precipitate [24].

### 2.5 Analytical methods

The crystal structures of the catalysts as well as the metal-precipitating substances were characterized via X-ray diffraction (PANalytical Empyrean, the Netherlands) with Cu-Kα radiation (*λ* = 0.15406 nm) and a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FT-IR; IFS 66v, Germany). Elemental analysis and content determination of Co3O4 were carried out by an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDS; X-Max, Oxford, UK). The surface morphology and microstructure of each catalyst before and after the reaction were observed by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; S-4800, Hitachi, Japan). The particle size and adsorption–desorption distribution of ZnO and Co3O4 were characterized via a nanoparticle size and zeta potential analyzer (Nano ZS, Malvern, UK). The semiconductor type and flat-band valence of ZnO were determined via the Mott–Schottky test on an electrochemical workstation (CHI-660E, Beijing Huake Putian, China). The concentration profiles of the lithium and cobalt ions were determined via an inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometer (Flexar/NexION300X, PerkinElme, USA) (Fig. S5 in the ESM), and the reactive leaching rate was calculated based on the concentration of ions in the leach solution. The leaching efficiency (*η*) of LCO under different conditions was calculated by Eq. (1):

(1) η=CtVtmω%×100%

where *η* is the leaching efficiency of the metal (%); *C*t is the metal concentration of Li or Co ions in the solution (mg·L−1); *V*t is the volume of leaching solution (l); *m* is the mass of LCO powder; *ω*% is the mass fraction of metals in the spent LCO powder. An electron paramagnetic resonance spectrometer (Electron Spin Resonance (ESR), Bruker A300, Japan) was used to test the reactive substances in the tribocatalytic leaching process.

### 2.6 Electrochemical experiments

First, 5 mg of ZnO was introduced into 5 μL of Nafion solution (2.5 wt%). Next, 1 mL of anhydrous ethanol was added to an ultrasonic cleaner (KQ-50DE, Ningbo Xinzhi, China). The mixture was then sonicated for 30 min. Then, the well-mixed solution was coated on the surface of the ITO glass (10 mm × 10 mm) and dried in an oven at 60 °C. The resulting samples were subjected to the Mott–Schottky test via a standard three-electrode system (Pt counter electrode, ITO working electrode coated with the sample, and saturated calomel reference electrode) on an electrochemical workstation (CHI-660E, Beijing Hua Ke Putian, China).

### 2.7 Friction current test

Structural design of the vertical contact separation mode triboelectric nanogenerator (CS-TENG) involving PTFE (30 μm thickness) and ZnO catalyst as CS-TENG friction materials, each prepared in dimension of 3 cm × 3 cm. Double-sided adhesive tape was applied to a 3 cm × 5 cm acrylic sheet substrate, and an electrode layer material (copper foil) of the same size was cut and used. Copper foil electrodes were adhered to the back of the catalysts, with the front side of the catalysts serving as friction layers. The fabricated electrodes and friction layers were attached to double-sided adhesive tape. Wires were connected between the double-sided adhesive tape and the electrode layer to complete the circuit, making the catalyst the positive electrode plate of the CS-TENG. PTFE was selected as the friction material for the negative electrode plate, which was fabricated via the same procedure. The CS-TENG was formed by assembling positive and negative electrode plates as counter electrodes.

### 2.8 Active substance detection

First, the hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and superoxide radicals (·O2−) signal spectra were detected and recorded via an electron paramagnetic resonance spectrometer. 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) was added as a trapping agent to magnetically stirred ZnO deionized water and ZnO solutions, respectively [25]. Similarly, the singlet oxygen (1O2) signals were detected and recorded using 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) as a trapping agent in a magnetically stirred ZnO deionized water solution [26].

In another experiment, 1 mM TBA (tert-butanol, a hydroxyl radical scavenger) [27], BQ (p-benzoquinone, a superoxide radical scavenger) [28], and EDTA (ethylenediamine tetracarboxylic acid, a hole scavenger) [29] were added to a mixture containing deionized water and ZnO to further investigate the effects of h+, ·OH, and ·O2− on tribocatalysis.

To evaluate the stability of ·OH during tribocatalysis, we measured the fluorescence spectra of ZnO under tribocatalytic conditions, where ·OH reacts with terephthalic acid (TA) to form 2-hydroxyterephthalic acid (TAOH), emitting at 425 nm. The measurements were conducted via a fluorescence spectrophotometer (FL-FS5, Edinburgh, UK) at an excitation wavelength of 315 nm [30−32].

### 3.1 Tribocatalytic leaching of LCO ions experiment

The whole recovery process is shown in Fig. 1(a). First, LCO was separated from the LIBs, and then the metal was leached by the tribocatalytic method. After the reaction started, the color of the solution changed from colorless to pink. As the reaction time increased, it further transformed from pink to burgundy, indicating that Co3+ in the cathode material had been leached out and reduced to divalent cobalt ions (Co2+), whose color was burgundy (Fig. S6 in the ESM). The leachate was subsequently subjected to centrifugal filtration to separate the catalyst (ZnO). Next, sodium oxalate and sodium carbonate were added successively to the filtrate through a precipitation process to separate the metals and synthesize the raw materials required for the LIBs. To investigate the piezoelectric properties of the sample, the piezoelectric response of ZnO was examined. The deformation of the optical fiber was detected via a laser interferometer and processed with a lock-in amplifier to obtain representative AFM topography, PFM amplitude, and phase images (Fig. 1(b) and Figs. S7(a)–S7(c) in the ESM). Figures 1(c)and 1(d) display the local piezoelectric hysteresis loops of ZnO, showing a 180° variation in the phase angle that confirms the occurrence of a polarization switching process. The butterfly-shaped strain curve further supports the local ferro-/piezoelectric response of ZnO. In ideal cases, the butterfly curve is symmetric around the zero point; however, the asymmetry observed in Fig. 1(d) indicates the presence of a built-in electric field within ZnO [27].

To investigate the effect of different influencing factors on the leached ions, the exploration of catalyst types was first carried out. To reduce the cost of the reaction and explore the universality of the tribocatalytic leaching method, TiO2 (P25), SiO2, and ZnO catalysts, which are more widely used commercially, were selected. As shown in Fig. 1(e), the Li+ and Co2+ leaching rates with the addition of the three catalysts were higher than the tribocatalytic leaching rates without catalysts. The reason for the ion leaching rate without a catalyst is that we designed a unique PTFE disk to encapsulate the magnet inside the disk to greatly improve the friction of the stirring process, thus generating more active substances to accelerate the leaching process, which is not achievable with ordinary PTFE magnetic rods (Fig. S8 in the ESM). Among the three catalysts, ZnO had the strongest ion leaching ability, so ZnO was chosen as the subsequent catalyst for further use, and its specific surface area versus adsorption-desorption curve is shown in Fig. S9 in the ESM. On the other hand, the other catalysts also showed ion leaching effect, further illustrating the universality of the tribocatalytic leaching method (not only for specific catalysts).

As shown in Fig. 1(f), the effect of the addition of the ZnO catalyst (20–150 mg) on the tribocatalytic leaching rate was explored. The results show that the efficiency of tribocatalytic leaching of lithium and cobalt increases and then decreases with increasing catalyst mass, and the optimum leaching rate is reached at 50 mg. In fact, an excessive amount of catalyst causes agglomeration of the powder, which is not conducive to adequate friction between the catalyst and PTFE disk or the beaker during the tribocatalysis process, leading to a decrease in the leaching rate. Figure 1(g) shows the effect of the reaction time on the leaching efficiency. The reaction time ranged from 4 to 9 h. The other experimental conditions were as follows: citric acid concentration of 0.7 mol/L, ZnO dosage of 50 mg, solid-liquid ratio of 10 g/L, and reaction temperature of 85 °C. The leaching efficiency increased rapidly with time from 4 to 8 h. However, the leaching efficiency was saturated when the reaction time reached 9 h. The leaching of LCO was controlled by surface chemical reaction, and the reaction rate was affected by ion migration and chemical reaction, so the longer the reaction time was, the more the anode active substance was dissolved [33−35].

The effect of the reaction temperature on the leaching efficiency is shown in Fig. 1(h). The concentration of 0.7 mol/L CA, a dosage of 50 mg of ZnO, and a solid-liquid ratio of 10 g/L were used, the reaction time was 8 h, and the reaction temperature ranged from 55 to 95 °C. The leaching rate was relatively slow at first with increasing temperature. The leaching rate was relatively slow with increasing temperature, and then the leaching rate increased rapidly, which can be interpreted as the increase of the reaction temperature accelerating the leaching reaction rate. The leaching process of metal recovery from the cathode materials of used LIBs is usually a heat-absorbing reaction, and the higher the temperature is, the higher the leaching efficiency [36−38]. Therefore, a relatively high temperature is favorable for the leaching reaction.

The effect of solid-liquid ratio on the leaching efficiency is shown in Fig. 1(i) under the following conditions: 8–14 g/L solid–liquid ratio, 0.7 mol/L CA concentration, 50 mg of ZnO, 85 °C reaction temperature and 8 h reaction time. As the solid-liquid ratio increased, the leaching rate first increased but then decreased, and the optimum leaching rate was reached at 10 g/L. When the solid–liquid ratio increased from 10 to 14 g/L, the leaching efficiency decreased linearly. Therefore, too little or too much leaching agent could not obtain a high leaching efficiency, and a proper solid-liquid ratio was needed to ensure a high leaching rate. As shown in Fig. 1(j), the CA concentration had a significant influence on the leaching rate. At 85 °C, an LCO dosage of 0.3 g, a reaction time of 8 h, a solid-liquid ratio of 10 g/L, and a CA concentration ranging from 0.3 to 0.7 mol/L, the leaching rate increased rapidly from 44.5 to 72%. Nevertheless, when the acid concentration continued to increase to 0.9 mol/L, the leaching rate started to decline. As the CA concentration increases, the H+ concentration also increases, which is beneficial for the leaching of LCO. However, a too-high concentration of CA is harmful to leaching. This is because the coordination reaction between metal ions and ligands (such as the different dissociation forms of citric acid, H2Cit−, HCit2−, and Cit3−) plays a crucial role in the leaching of LCO. When the CA concentration is relatively low, H+ can be significantly consumed, and the concentrations of H2Cit−, HCit2−, and Cit3− are sufficient to form complexes with the leached metal ions. Conversely, when high-concentration CA is considered, H+ cannot be significantly consumed. The remaining H+ strongly inhibits the formation of H2Cit−, HCit2−, and Cit3−, preventing the leached Li+ and Co2+ from forming stable complexes with the ligands. This leads to a decrease in the leaching efficiency [39−41]. In addition, a high concentration of CA implies a high consumption of leaching reagents. Therefore, in the CA tribocatalytic leaching system, the optimal concentration of CA was determined to be 0.7 mol/L, which strikes a balance between leaching efficiency and reagent consumption.

### 3.2 Mechanism of tribocatalytic activity

The above results demonstrate that tribocatalysis can be used as an effective approach to speed up the leaching of Li+ and Co(II) from waste LCO under suitable conditions. However, the conversion mechanism of Co(III) to Co(II) needs further exploration. To further understand the mechanism of the tribocatalytic leaching process, the generation of reactive species was investigated. As shown in Figs. 2(a)–2(c), the ESR technique was used to obtain the signal spectra of DMPO-·OH, DMPO-·O2−, and TEMPO-1O2 under tribocatalytic conditions at different leaching times. The results indicated the generation of ·OH, ·O2−, and 1O2. ### 3.3 Structural characterizations of recovered precipitates

At the end of the tribocatalysis process, a leaching solution containing a mixture of Li and Co ions was acquired. These ions needed to be separated and recovered from the solution for further use. To separate the cobalt ions from the leaching solution, the LCO filtrate that had been leached under optimum conditions was collected several times. Then, sodium oxalate (with the number of moles equal to that of the cobalt ions in the solution) was introduced at room temperature while stirring. The mixture was subsequently allowed to react for 30 min, followed by centrifugation and filtration to obtain a pink precipitate. The XRD pattern in Fig. 3(a) shows that this pink precipitate corresponds to the standard card of CoC2O4·2H2O (JCPDS No. 26-0251). This indicates that Co2+ can react with oxalic acid to form a CoC2O4 precipitate. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) revealed distinct CoC2O4 characteristic peaks (Fig. 3(b)), which are consistent with the XRD results. It is noteworthy that the peak at 1615.59 cm−1 is attributed to the asymmetric vibration of the C=O bond, while the closely spaced bands at 1375.10 and 1308.61 cm−1 are attributed to the symmetric vibration of the C–O bond. This indicates that the four oxygen atoms are all coordinated with metal atoms to form oxalates [44]. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images indicated that the cobalt oxalate precipitates had an irregular blocky structure (Fig. 3(c)). Next, after removing cobalt ions from the solution by precipitation, saturated sodium carbonate was added to the lithium. The XRD patterns, FTIR spectra, and SEM images of the precipitate are shown in Figs. 3(d)–3(f). The XRD spectra of the Li precipitates are consistent with Li2CO3. In addition, the FTIR peak at 1089.71 cm−1, attributed to the symmetric C–O vibration [45], and the antisymmetric stretching vibration of the C–O bond in the bands located at 1388.16 and 1548.56 cm−1, attributed to Li2CO3, further confirmed this identification [46]. For further application of the cobalt oxalate precipitate obtained in the first step, we subsequently calcined the cobalt oxalate. The calcination was carried out at 450 °C for 2 h, with 30 min holding at 450 °C, to obtain a new black precipitate. The XRD, IR, and SEM results in Figs. 3(g)–3(i), as well as the SEM energy spectra in Fig. S10 in the ESM, demonstrated the synthesis of particulate Co3O4. Co3O4 is an excellent potential catalyst with wide-ranging applications in several fields. For example, it shows an outstanding effect on the tribocatalytic dye degradation of Rhodamine B (Fig. S11 in the ESM). This synthesis not only realizes the recycling of valuable metals but also provides a promising catalyst for environmental remediation.

### 3.4 Cyclic stability analysis of the catalysts

After tribocatalytic leaching, we evaluated the cyclic stability of the tribocatalytic leaching of LCO with the recoverability of the catalyst. As shown in Fig. 4(a), the recovered ZnO catalyst was washed and dried several times, and the leaching experiments were repeated under optimal conditions. ### 3.5 Mechanistic analysis of tribocatalyzed leaching of lithium ions

The energy band structure of ZnO was further verified via theoretical calculations (Fig. 5(a)), and its energy band gap of 3.11 eV is very close to the actual tested value of 3.19 eV (Fig. 3(i)). The contributions of different atomic orbitals to the energy band gap are represented by the electronic density of states (Fig. 5(b) and Fig. S12(a) and S12(b) in the ESM), and a comparison of the total density of states (TDOS) and projected density of states (PDOS) state densities in the figure reveals that the Zn 3d orbitals contribute not only to the density of states at the top of the valence band but also at the bottom of the conduction band and that the density of states at approximately 8 eV consists of the Zn 4s orbitals. For the oxygen atom, the O 2p orbital, although its contribution is weaker than that of Zn 3d, is similar to that of the Zn 3d orbital, contributing to the density of states at the top of the valence band and at the bottom of the conduction band. At the back of the valence band, due to the low energy near the position of the density of states, it is difficult for this region to be involved in the reaction, and the contribution is very small. Therefore, the 3d orbitals of Zn and the 2p orbitals of O mainly contribute to the forbidden band width of ZnO, which, together with the measured values, verifies that the generation of free radicals during the friction leaching process is consistent with the energy band structure of ZnO.

In the system of tribocatalytic leaching of ions, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of CA and H2O were compared to investigate the contributions of CA and H2O to the leaching system. As shown in Fig. 5(c), the LUMO energy of CA is lower than the LUMO energy of H2O, while the HOMO energy is higher than the HOMO energy of water (−6.252 vs. −6.821 eV), suggesting that CA is more susceptible to redox reactions upon contact friction on the ZnO surface [47]. The electrostatic potential (ESP) (Fig. 5(d)) of CA and H2O molecules was then calculated. Compared with H2O, CA has greater electrostatic polarity, indicating that CA can undergo more adsorption and reaction on the LCO surface through electron transfer. The corresponding adsorption energies were compared via density functional theory (DFT) calculations. As shown in Fig. 5(e), it can be clearly found that the interaction is stronger when CA molecules are adsorbed on the LCO(110) crystal surface (−3.88 vs. −0.15 eV). The interactions between different molecules can also be illustrated by the differential charge densities in Fig. S13 in the ESM. The red and blue clusters in the figure represent the increase and decrease in electron density, respectively, which tends to transfer electrons from CA to LCO during adsorption friction to form enhanced chemisorption, which is consistent with the LUMO and HOMO information. Therefore, the weak acid leaching process of CA is a good solution for ion leaching in tribocatalytic leaching systems.

The possible role of piezocatalysis in the tribocatalytic process was considered. In another set of complementary experiments, a stationary experiment after adding CA, a leaching experiment in which PTFE disks were replaced with glass magnets, and a leaching experiment using ultrasonic vibration were performed. As shown in Fig. S14 in the ESM, there was also a certain ion leaching rate for the stationary condition after 8 h, indicating that CA has a certain ion leaching capacity, but this leaching capacity was not significant with time (Li (23.29%), Co (9.26%)). Glass is a weakly polarized smooth material, and experiments using glass magnets in glass beakers can avoid the catalytic effect of friction produced by the different materials of glass beakers and PTFE magnets. However, after 8 h, the ion leaching rate of the experiment using glass magnets (Li (33.14%), Co (15.36%)) is similar to that of standing (Li (23.29%), Co (9.26%)), which is much lower than the tribocatalytic leaching rate of the disk (Li (95%), Co (84%)), suggesting that the piezoelectricity of purely mechanical stirring or mechanical stirring to generate a piezocatalytic effect is much lower than that of tribocatalysis. On the other hand, the lithium and cobalt leaching rates when ultrasonic vibration was used (Li (70.79%) and Co (65.22%)) were higher than those when glass-magnet stirring was used (Li (33.14%) and Co (15.36%)). It is worth noting that catalytic experiments under ultrasonic conditions induce piezocatalytic effects. The catalyst used in this study is also a piezoelectric material, but piezocatalysis induced by ultrasonic vibration was not as effective as the tribocatalytic leaching rates of lithium and cobalt from the discs (Li (95%), Co (84%)). As shown in Fig. S15 in the ESM, even though the power of ultrasonication (240 W) is much greater than that of magnetic stirring (35 W), under optimal conditions, the ion leaching rates of different catalysts for tribocatalysis are greater than those under ultrasonication, indicating the high efficiency of designing PTFE magnets for tribocatalytic leaching of LCO ions. Finally, the above experimental and theoretical analyses show that for ZnO catalysts, the ion leaching process under magnetic stirring-induced conditions involves both piezoelectric and tribocatalytic effects but is dominated by tribocatalysis.

Finally, the mechanism behind the tribocatalytic leaching of LIBs ions was analyzed. It is worth noting that the tribocatalytic effect is closely related to the triboelectric effect. Through experiments with liquid-solid triboelectric nanogenerator (LS-TENG), electron transfer in water was demonstrated, showing that electrons are indeed transferred between different materials and water. However, due to the tribocatalytic leaching experiments conducted in a pure water system, the leaching rates for lithium and cobalt ions are very low (0.8% and 0.2% for lithium and cobalt ions, respectively, after 8 h under optimal conditions, Fig. S16 in the ESM). Therefore, the effect of friction between water and other materials is not considered in this study, and water as a solvent is mainly responsible for transferring electrons and further synthesizing active species. In the PTFE+ZnO+glass tribocatalytic system, it is highly unlikely that one material will rub against two other materials at the same time. This is because the charge generated by friction between the two materials is consumed almost instantaneously (within 10−10 s) before friction with another material occurs [48]. Therefore, the system for leaching LCO ions can be simplified into three separate friction systems: PTFE+glass, ZnO+PTFE and glass+ZnO (Figs. 5(i)–5(j)). According to the positions of glass, ZnO and PTFE in the triboelectric sequence (Fig. S17 in the ESM) [49−51] and the friction current curve of PTFE-ZnO (Fig. S18 in the ESM), in the PTFE+glass friction system, PTFE (e−) gains electrons, and glass (h+) loses electrons. In the ZnO+PTFE friction system, ZnO (h+) loses electrons, and PTFE (e−) gains electrons.

In the Glass+ZnO friction system, glass (h+) loses electrons, and ZnO (e−) gains electrons. At this time, the electrons transferred to the PTFE surface generate ·O2− with dissolved oxygen in water, while the holes left on the glass surface generate ·OH with OH-. Similarly, the electrons on the ZnO surface generate ·O2− with dissolved oxygen in water, and the holes on the ZnO surface generate ·OH with OH− [31]. During the formation of superoxide radicals with holes can further generate 1O2. Thus, all friction systems contribute to the production of reactive species. These active substances (·OH, ·O2−, and 1O2) successfully accomplish the leaching of LCO ions in the tribocatalytic system.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, a tribocatalytic strategy was used for the first time to recycle and reuse used lithium-ion battery cathode materials. Using nano-ZnO as the tribocatalyst for recycling, 95% Li+ and 84% Co2+ leaching rates were achieved under optimal conditions. Tribocatalytic experiments were conducted using ternary lithium batteries, and the leaching efficiencies of Mn, Ni, Co, and Li were 96.61%, 89.99%, 76.06%, and 61.78%, respectively. The combination of theoretical calculations (electrostatic potential, adsorption energy and electron differential charge density) and experiments verified that the tribocatalytic weak acid leaching process is a good ion leaching scheme. ESR and free radical capture experiments demonstrated that the active species in the friction process is the key to efficiently leaching each ion from LIBs. This method has great potential to reduce metal ions or leach metal ions, which can alleviate the shortage of valuable resources and effectively deal with the waste lithium-ion power batteries, is in line with the national requirements of the circular economy, and is also conducive to the promotion of the sustained and healthy development of the lithium-ion industry.
