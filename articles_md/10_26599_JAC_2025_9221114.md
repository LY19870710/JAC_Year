## 1 Introduction

With the rapid development of urbanization, industrialization, and agricultural production, environmental issues have become increasingly severe. The global economy and human demands have stimulated the production and release of new chemical substances, whereas the pace of managing emerging pollutants lags behind their generation [1]. Antibiotics, as emerging pollutants, are frequently detected in aquatic environments worldwide [2−4]. Environmental contamination by antibiotics can induce the emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) [5,6]. Even when discharged at compliant levels or low doses, antibiotics can accumulate in organisms and gradually magnify through the food chain, ultimately harming both environmental organisms and human health [7,8]. There are various approaches for removing residual antibiotics from water bodies, such as microbial treatment [9], adsorption [10,11], and advanced oxidation technologies [12,13]. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), which can completely decompose target pollutants, represent a novel approach for antibiotic treatment, offering advantages such as rapid reaction rates, high efficiency, and environmental friendliness. In the presence of catalysts, ozone, oxidants, or light radiation, reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydroxyl radicals, sulfate radicals, and singlet oxygen, are generated. These ROS possess strong oxidative capabilities, enabling the continuous degradation of organic pollutants into smaller molecules, ultimately mineralizing them into low-toxicity or non-toxic products such as small organic molecules, carbon dioxide, and water [14,15]. In AOPs, persulfate (PS) oxidation has demonstrated remarkable advantages in degrading antibiotics, such as a high oxidation potential (*E*0 = 2.5–3.1 V), long half-life (30–40 μs), and broad pH applicability range (2.0–8.0) [16]. It is well-known that while PS itself can degrade certain antibiotics by utilizing electron-rich groups, its removal efficiency for antibiotics is very low without activation. Therefore, external energy is required to break the chemical bonds in PS molecules to generate reactive radicals [17]. However, current activation methods are usually time-consuming, have low efficiency, and require plenty of energy [18]. Consequently, designing catalysts with high catalytic activity and environmental adaptability for PS activation is of critical importance.

MOFs are organic–inorganic hybrid materials composed of organic linkers and metal ions or clusters [19]. Owing to their remarkable characteristics, such as high porosity, large specific surface area, tunable and uniform structure, abundant active sites, and excellent chemical stability, MOFs have shown broad application prospects in various fields, including gas adsorption and separation [20], catalysis [21], sensors [22], biomedicine [23], and energy storage [24]. In recent years, MOF-based functional materials have become a research hotspot in water treatment applications [25], encompassing adsorption, photocatalysis, Fenton-like reactions, PS activation, and catalytic ozonation [26−28]. Heterogeneous PS activation technology has been widely adopted for environmental remediation because of its simplicity and high efficiency. In this context, the selection of suitable environmental catalysts has become a critical factor. Given their unique properties, research on MOFs for PS activation has increased significantly [29,30]. However, MOF powder catalysts often face challenges, such as agglomeration, difficulty in separation and recovery, and metal ion leaching in aqueous solutions, which hinder their large-scale practical applications [31]. To address these limitations, researchers have explored the immobilization of MOFs onto carbon materials [32], foams [33], and other substrates [34,35]. For instance, Tian *et* *al*. [36] integrated MIL-88A with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) through *in* *situ* synthesis, resulting in the formation of MIL-88A/PVA composite sponges. The synthesized MIL-88A/PVA demonstrated high removal efficiency for macromolecular organic pollutants, such as tetracycline (TC), oxytetracycline (OTC), and chlorotetracycline (CTC), in sulfate radical-based advanced oxidation processes (SR-AOPs). Similarly, Chen *et* *al*. [37] prepared composite materials in the form of zeolite@ZIF-67 microspheres using an ion exchange method. These microspheres can be easily packed into column beds, enabling the efficient removal of TC in continuous flow processes.

Geopolymers (GP) are a novel class of aluminosilicate materials characterized by a three-dimensional network structure composed of [SiO4]4− and [AlO4]5− tetrahedral units [38]. The raw materials used for geopolymer synthesis can be naturally occurring aluminosilicate minerals, such as metakaolin (MK) and clay [39], as well as industrial solid wastes such as fly ash, slag, mine tailings [40], etc. [41]. Geopolymers are an intriguing class of materials that bridge the gap between traditional cement and ceramics, often referred to as “non-sintered ceramics” because of their ceramic-like properties without requiring high-temperature sintering. Owing to their low energy consumption, relatively simple preparation process, resistance to acid and alkali corrosion, and thermal stability, geopolymers have garnered significant attention in recent years as emerging materials for water treatment applications [42]. In particular, after the geopolymerization process, the alkali metal ions in the system can be easily replaced by other cationic ions (such as NH4+ [43], Cu2+ [44], Mn3+,4+ [45], Co2+, and Ag+ [46]), which can endow or improve the catalytic performance of the materials. Furthermore, porous geopolymers with high specific surface areas and the possibility of releasing alkali ions are suitable as catalytic supports or catalysts. For example, Luukkonen and von Gunten [46] reported the immobilization of Co2+, Cu2+, and Ag+ within a geopolymer matrix to activate peracetic acid for the degradation of phenol. Similarly, Ma *et* *al*. [45] developed OH−-releasing microspheres by incorporating manganese (Mn) into metakaolin-based geopolymers, and proposed a strategy that combines defect engineering with the controlled release of OH−. Spherical porous geopolymers can be prepared through various methods, such as direct molding [47], dispersion-suspension solidification [48], and reversed-phase suspension-dispersion method [49]. Furthermore, spherical porous geopolymers have been applied as catalyst matrices in the field of water treatment in recent years. Cai *et* *al*. [50] used Mn slag as raw materials, synthesized Fe₂O₃-based porous geopolymer spheres by alkali activation, improved the curing method, and enhanced the catalytic activity of the reaction by loading MnO2 on their surface. The removal rate of ciprofloxacin (CIP) reached 99.1% within 40 min.

In this study, a porous geopolymer-based catalytic system (Cu-MOFs/GP) was developed for the efficient degradation of antibiotics. The catalytic system consisted of a porous geopolymer with *in* *situ* grown Cu-MOFs and a peroxymonosulfate (PMS) solution. The porous geopolymer was synthesized using a simple direct molding method with a specific ratio of metakaolin and slag, followed by the growth of Cu-MOFs on its surface. When PMS was used as the oxidant, the system achieved efficient catalytic degradation of tetracycline hydrochloride (TCH). The morphology, structure, and chemical composition of the catalyst were analyzed and characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Additionally, the effects of PMS concentration, TCH concentration, pH, and anion interference on the degradation efficiency were systematically investigated.

### 2.2 Preparation of geopolymer spheres

Similar to the preparation method employed in our previous study, metakaolin/slag-based geopolymer spheres were fabricated using a facile direct molding approach [47]. Specifically, the alkaline activator was prepared by mixing a sodium silicate (water glass) solution and an 8 mol sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution at a mass ratio of 22 : 16. To this solution, 17.92 g of metakaolin and 7.68 g of slag were added incrementally at 20-min intervals into 38 g of the alkaline activator solution, and the mixture was continuously stirred at 500 r/min for 40 min to obtain the geopolymer slurry. Subsequently, 2 g of surfactant (LAO-30) and 4 g of a 3 wt% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) solution were added to the slurry to prepare the foam mixture. Finally, the foam slurry was poured into a mold containing 90 spherical cavities (each with a diameter of 7.5 mm) and cured sequentially at 75 °C for approximately 24 h. After curing, the porous metakaolin/slag-based geopolymer spheres were demolded (Fig. 1(a)).

### 2.3 Preparation of Cu-MOFs/GP

As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), the composite materials were synthesized using a simple solvothermal method. Initially, 4 mmol of copper(II) nitrate trihydrate (Cu(NO3)2·3H2O) and 2 mmol of 1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylic acid (H3BTC) were separately dissolved in 15 mL of a mixed solvent consisting of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), anhydrous ethanol, and deionized water at a volume ratio of 1 : 1 : 1, denoted as solutions A and B, respectively. Solution B was then poured into solution A and thoroughly dispersed. The porous geopolymer spheres were subsequently immersed in the mixed solution, and the resulting mixture was transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave (Anhui Kemi Instrument Co., Ltd., China). The autoclave containing the mixture was placed in an oven (DHG-9023A, Shanghai Jinghong Laboratory Equipment Co., Ltd., China) and heated continuously at 100 °C for 20 h. After the autoclave cooled to room temperature, the obtained porous geopolymer spheres were washed multiple times with DMF, ethanol, and deionized water, followed by drying in an oven at 75 °C for 6 h to yield the Cu-MOFs/GP composite materials.

### 2.5 Catalytic degradation experiment

All TCH degradation experiments were conducted at room temperature using a magnetic stirrer (MMS-4, Qun’an Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., China). In a 300 mL beaker, 250 mL of TCH solution was added, and the initial pH of the solution was measured to be 6.5. Subsequently, 2 g of the Cu-MOFs/GP composite materials was introduced into the solution, followed by the addition of varying amounts of PMS. The mixture was stirred at a speed of 500 r/min. At 10-min intervals, a sample was collected and filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane into a cuvette, and the TCH concentration was measured using a UV spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 360 nm. All experiments were repeated at least twice to minimize errors, and the results were expressed as mean values with standard deviations. After the reaction, the catalyst samples were alternately washed five times with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol, dried, and subsequently used in the catalyst reuse experiments.

To investigate the effects of interfering ions and humic acid (HA), SO4²− solutions were prepared using sodium sulfate (Na₂SO₄), NO3− solutions were prepared using sodium nitrate (NaNO₃), H2PO4− solutions were prepared using sodium dihydrogen phosphate (NaH₂PO₄), and Cl− solutions were prepared using sodium chloride (NaCl). The concentration of all ions used in the experiments was set at 10 mM. Additionally, HA was added to the solutions at concentrations of 10 and 20 mg/L for further analysis.

In the continuous-flow catalytic experiments using simulated wastewater, the Cu-MOFs/GP composite materials were loaded into a custom-designed column reactor, which was packed with quartz wool and glass beads (Fig. 9(a)). A peristaltic pump was used to continuously introduce TCH solutions containing 3 mM PMS at various concentrations into the reactor at a flow rate of 4 mL/min. Effluent samples were collected at hourly intervals, and their absorbance was measured using a UV spectrophotometer.

### 3.1 Characterizations

The crystalline phase assemblages of geopolymer (GP), Cu-MOFs, and Cu-MOFs/GP were investigated using XRD, as illustrated in Fig. 1(b). The GP sample exhibited a broad diffraction peak within the 2*θ* range of 20°–40°, which is indicative of the typical amorphous structure of geopolymers [51]. In contrast, the Cu-MOFs crystals displayed distinct and intense diffraction peaks at 2*θ* = 6.9°, 9.6°, 11.8°, 13.5°, 16.5°, 17.6°, and 19.2°. These peaks can be assigned to the characteristic crystal planes of the Cu-MOFs and agree with previously reported data [52]. This confirmed the successful synthesis of copper metal–organic framework (Cu-MOFs). Notably, the XRD pattern of the synthesized Cu-MOFs/GP composite materials did not significantly change compared to that of the pristine Cu-MOFs crystals. This observation suggests that the crystalline structure of the Cu-MOFs remained intact after being generated on the GP. Furthermore, the characteristic peak of GP was obscured by the complex and strong diffraction peaks of the Cu-MOFs, although the broad peak associated with its amorphous nature was preserved. These results collectively demonstrate that the integration of the Cu-MOFs on the GP did not compromise the structural integrity of either component, with the amorphous framework of the GP being maintained.

The surface functional groups of the samples were analyzed using FTIR, as shown in Fig. 1(c). For the GP sample, a bending vibration peak corresponding to the H–O–H group of water was observed at 1650 cm−1, indicating the presence of free water within the sample [53]. Additionally, the band at approximately 1000 cm−1 is a distinctive feature of the geopolymer structure, originating from the asymmetric stretching and bending vibrations of Si–O–Si and Si–O–Al bonds [54,55]. In the case of the Cu-MOFs, the vibration peak at 730 cm−1 is attributed to the Cu–O bond, which arises from the coordination between copper and the oxygen-containing functional groups in 1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylic acid. Furthermore, the peak at 1384 cm−1 is associated with the stretching vibration of C–O in 1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylic acid, whereas the peak at 1646 cm−1 corresponds to the typical stretching vibration of the aromatic C=C bond [56]. The FTIR spectra of the Cu-MOFs/GP composite materials exhibited characteristic peaks of both the GP and the Cu-MOFs, providing further evidence that the Cu-MOFs were successfully loaded onto the surface of the GP without altering the structural integrity of either component. This result confirms the effective integration of the Cu-MOFs with GP while maintaining the distinct chemical features of both materials. The Raman spectra of the Cu-MOFs and composite materials are shown in Fig. 1(d). The peaks of the Cu-MOFs loaded on GP are nearly identical to those of the pristine Cu-MOFs, except for a slight reduction in peak intensity. Several bands are observed in the range of 730–1800 cm−1, which are associated with the organic components of the MOF structure. For example, the bands at 1612 and 1004 cm−1 are attributed to the *ν*(C=C) modes of the benzene ring, whereas those at 1462 and 1546 cm−1 correspond to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching modes of the carboxylate ions, respectively. The bands at 742 and 826 cm−1 are due to out-of-plane bending vibrations of the (C–H) ring and out-of-plane ring bending, respectively. Furthermore, in the low-frequency region at 501 and 272 cm−1, vibrational bands corresponding to Cu(II) are observed. The test results are in good agreement with those of previous reports [57], further confirming the structural integrity of the Cu-MOFs loaded on GP.

The microstructural morphologies of the prepared materials were analyzed using SEM, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The Cu-MOFs crystals (Fig. S1(a) in the ESM) clearly exhibit a typical octahedral structure characterized by uniform and intact particles with smooth surfaces, consistent with previous reports [58]. Figures S1(b) and S1(c) in the ESM show that the Cu-MOFs grow on the GP substrate, mainly inside the pores and at the pore junctions. Figures 2(a)–2(c) reveal substantial deposition of Cu-MOFs crystallites on the GP surface while largely maintaining their octahedral morphology. However, the crystal surfaces appear slightly rougher, with minor structural damage. This phenomenon may be attributed to the coordination competition between the surface OH− groups on the GP and H3BTC ligands. Previous studies have demonstrated that the intermediate Cu(OH)2 species generated during this process can be completely transformed into Cu-MOFs [59]. This phenomenon is likely attributed to the extensive adsorption of Cu by GP, leading to substantial deposition and stacking of the Cu-MOFs on the surface. This process resembles the growth-assisted adsorption described in the study conducted by Su *et* *al*. [60]. The presence of Cu, O, Si, Al, Na, and N was confirmed via EDS (Fig. 2(d)). Furthermore, the total elemental distribution mapping spectrum reveals that Cu and O are the dominant elements, whereas the other elements collectively account for a significantly smaller proportion (Fig. S1(d) in the ESM). This further indicates that the Cu-MOFs are uniformly coated on the GP surface.

The chemical composition and states of the catalyst surface were analyzed using XPS, as shown in Fig. 3. The full spectrum scan revealed characteristic peaks corresponding to the elements Al 2p (74 eV), Si 2p (101.96 eV), C 1s (284.46 eV), N 1s (399.96 eV), O 1s (531.27 eV), Cu 2p (932.61 eV), and Na 1s (1071.63 eV), further confirming the effective integration between the geopolymer and Cu-MOFs (Fig. 3(a)). In Fig. 3(b), the Cu 2p spectrum comprises two main peaks, corresponding to the Cu 2p3/2 (934.65 eV) and Cu 2p1/2 (954.45 eV) regions. The peaks at 934.78 and 954.44 eV are characteristic of Cu2+, whereas the peaks at 939.68 and 943.48 eV indicate the presence of Cu+ [61]. The Cu 2p3/2 main peak (934.78 eV) exhibits two satellite peaks at 942 and 938.8 eV, which are attributed to Cu2+, whereas the Cu 2p1/2 main peak (954.44 eV) shows a satellite peak at 962.7 eV associated with Cu+ [62]. These findings demonstrate the coexistence of two valence states of copper, namely, Cu2+ and Cu+, in the composite materials.

Figure 3(c) displays the C 1s peaks located at 284.7, 285.9, and 288.3 eV, which correspond to C–C, C–O, and C=O bonds, respectively. The C–O bond, an electron-rich moiety, serves as an active site at the PMS-activation interface [63]. As illustrated in Fig. 3(d), the peaks observed at 531.35, 532.15, and 533.3 eV are attributed to C–O, surface-adsorbed oxygen species, and surface hydroxyl groups, respectively. Notably, surface hydroxyl groups play a crucial role in accelerating PMS activation by facilitating the conversion of Cu+ to Cu2+ [64]. Furthermore, Fig. 3(e) presents the high-resolution N 1s spectra, which comprises metallic N (398.15 eV), pyridinic-N (399.85 eV), pyrrolic N (400.25 eV), and graphitic N (401.36 eV) [65].

The N2 adsorption‒desorption isotherms of the Cu-MOFs, GP, and Cu-MOFs/GP are shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM, and their specific surface areas, pore volumes, and average pore sizes are summarized in Table S1 in the ESM. Cu-MOFs, GP, and Cu-MOFs/GP all exhibit hysteresis loops, indicating that their nitrogen adsorption‒desorption isotherms are type IV, which suggests the presence of mesoporous structures in these catalysts [66]. Specifically, the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface areas of the Cu-MOFs, GP, and Cu-MOFs/GP are 368.93, 27.86, and 169.15 m²/g, respectively. Notably, the BET specific surface area of the Cu-MOFs/GP decreases compared with that of the pristine Cu-MOFs, which can be attributed to the structural disruption of the Cu-MOFs surfaces due to the introduction of GP. The pore size distribution shows that most of the pristine Cu-MOFs are microporous, whereas GP is mesoporous. The corresponding Cu-MOFs/GP is a mixture of mesopores and micropores. Generally, large pore sizes and high specific surface areas are both beneficial for adsorption, yet they inherently form a challenging trade-off. The pore size of the Cu-MOFs/GP composite is more than three times larger than that of the pristine Cu-MOFs, whereas its specific surface area decreases by slightly over half. However, the pore volume remains largely unchanged, implying that the internal pore structure of the Cu-MOFs remains intact. In contrast, the BET specific surface area of Cu-MOFs/GP is significantly larger than that of GP—approximately six times greater—which is expected to positively influence both the adsorption and catalytic performance of the composite materials.

### 3.2 Catalytic performance

The catalytic degradation of TCH was compared across different catalyst systems in this study. In each system, the concentration of PMS was maintained at 3 mM, while the TCH concentration was set at 50 mg/L, with a reaction time of 90 min. As shown in Fig. 4(a), the degradation efficiency of TCH remained stable at 62.71% in the presence of PMS alone, which can be attributed to the inherent oxidative capacity of PMS as an oxidant. However, the degradation efficiency of TCH improved significantly in systems where both PMS and catalysts were present. Specifically, the GP+PMS and Cu-MOFs+PMS systems achieved degradation efficiencies of 69.54% and 73.01%, respectively. Unlike previous works that focused on high OH− sustained-release properties [47], the near-neutral GP exhibited slight catalytic degradation of TCH. This increase is likely attributed to trace metals in the MK within the geopolymer, which can partially activate PMS [67]. The amount of Cu-MOF powder added was determined based on the estimated loading capacity of the geopolymer matrix. The observed low catalytic efficiency could be attributed to the limited MOF loading. Previous studies [68,69] have similarly demonstrated the limited PMS activation efficiency of pristine MOFs. Further investigations are needed to systematically optimize pristine MOF and evaluate their effects on the catalytic performance of PMS. Although the Cu-MOFs+PMS system slightly outperformed the GP+PMS system, both showed only marginal improvements compared to the PMS system alone. In contrast, the introduction of the Cu-MOFs/GP composite led to a remarkable increase in the degradation efficiency, reaching 92.28%. Conversely, in the absence of PMS, the Cu-MOFs/GP composite achieved a TCH removal rate of only 24.06%, primarily because of its limited adsorption capacity. Therefore, the significant increase in TCH degradation efficiency of the Cu-MOFs/GP composite can be attributed to two factors: first, the substantial adsorption of TCH on the surface of the Cu-MOFs/GP composite, which facilitates TCH degradation; second, the synergistic catalytic relationship between the Cu-MOFs and GP. A pseudo-first-order degradation kinetic model was employed to simulate the process, revealing degradation rate constant (*k*) of 0.0117, 0.0129, 0.0137, and 0.0279 min−¹ for the PMS, GP+PMS, Cu-MOFs+PMS, and Cu-MOFs/GP+PMS systems, respectively. These results further corroborate the notable improvement in the catalytic performance. Table S2 in the ESM compares the removal efficiencies of various copper-based catalysts for PMS activation to degrade organic pollutants. Among the evaluated composite catalysts, the Cu-MOFs/GP composite demonstrates remarkable potential for antibiotic degradation. The effects of varying PMS concentrations on TCH removal were investigated, as illustrated in Fig. 4(b). As the PMS dosage increased from 1 to 3 mM, the TCH removal efficiency significantly improved from 69.32% to 91.32%. This increase can be attributed primarily to the increased probability of contact between PMS and the Cu-MOFs/GP catalyst, which facilitated the generation of a greater number of highly reactive radicals and non-radical species with strong oxidative properties. Consequently, this accelerated the reaction rate and enhanced the degradation efficiency. However, when the PMS concentration was further increased to 4 and 5 mM, a slight decrease in TCH degradation efficiency was observed. This phenomenon is commonly explained by the excessive accumulation of catalytically generated reactive radicals, leading to interactions among radicals that produce fewer reactive species. Such radical quenching suppresses redox reactions, thereby negatively impacting TCH removal efficiency [70]. Therefore, based on these findings, the optimal PMS concentration for subsequent experiments was determined to be 3 mM. As illustrated in Fig. 4(c), a negative correlation was observed between the removal efficiency and the initial concentration of TCH, with the efficiency decreasing as the concentration of TCH increased. This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that higher TCH concentrations do not increase the PMS activation rate of the Cu-MOFs/GP. Furthermore, Cu-MOFs/GP cannot provide sufficient active sites for the degradation of high-concentration TCH, and the ROS generated by PMS are unable to effectively convert high concentrations of TCH into smaller molecular compounds [71]. The experimental results revealed that the degradation rates were 96.03% and 64.96% at initial TCH concentrations of 25 and 100 ppm, respectively. Notably, the degradation efficiency at an initial concentration of 50 ppm was comparable to that at 25 ppm, indicating minimal difference. Therefore, for subsequent experiments, an initial TCH concentration of 50 ppm was selected, as it better demonstrates the degradation performance of the Cu-MOFs/GP composite materials for high concentrations of TCH.

Real wastewater contains a variety of anions and organic compounds, which may interfere with the performance of catalytic systems. Figure 4(e) shows the effects of different anions on the Cu-MOFs/GP@PMS catalytic system. According to relevant studies, certain anions can react with ROS to generate less active radicals, thereby inhibiting the oxidative capacity of the catalyst [73]. The experimental results revealed that the degradation efficiency of TCH was slightly suppressed but remained largely unaffected in the presence of 10 mM NO3−, SO4²−, H2PO4−, and Cl−. This suggests that coexisting inorganic anions have minimal interference with the reaction system. On the other hand, organic compounds, which are intermediate products formed during the metabolic cycles of plants and animals, are widely present in natural environments and can pose risks to animals, plants, and humans. HA, a representative model of natural organic matter, was used to investigate the influence of HA concentration on the degradation efficiency. However, over time, HA adsorbs onto the catalyst surface and occupies active sites, preventing the effective activation of PMS and reducing the generation rate of reactive species [74]. Consequently, the final degradation efficiency remained unchanged. These observations validate the stability of the Cu-MOFs/GP@PMS system for TCH removal from real wastewater, highlighting its potential for practical applications.

The stability of a catalyst during recycling is closely related to the practical application cost and serves as a critical indicator for evaluating catalyst performance. Therefore, the cycling stability of the catalyst was tested through four consecutive degradation experiments, as illustrated in Fig. 5(a). The degradation efficiencies of TCH in the first three cycles were 91.26%, 89.02%, and 85.15%, respectively, whereas they decreased to 78.2% after the fourth cycle. The decrease in TCH degradation efficiency may be attributed to the accumulation of residual TCH and its intermediates on the catalyst surface, which reduces the availability of active sites, as well as the inevitable loss of catalyst during the recycling process.

### 3.3 Catalytic mechanism

The changes in the copper species before and after the reaction were analyzed using XPS, as shown in Fig. 5(b). XPS peak area analysis revealed that the peak positions of Cu on the surface of Cu-MOFs/GP remained unchanged before and after the reaction, whereas the valence states and their proportions underwent significant alterations. As shown in Table S3 in the ESM, the relative peak area ratios of Cu+ and Cu2+ in the sample before the reaction were 35.69% and 43.97%, respectively, whereas after the reaction, these values changed to 32.45% and 49.8%, respectively. This shift corresponds to an increase in the Cu2+/Cu+ ratio from 1.232 to 1.534, indicating that Cu+ was oxidized to Cu2+ during the reaction. The Cu2+/Cu+ redox pair in the Cu-MOFs/GP catalyst facilitated the formation of active species through electron transfer under PMS activation, thereby achieving efficient degradation of TCH. These findings provide strong evidence that the Cu2+/Cu+ cycle plays a pivotal role in the catalytic mechanism, highlighting the dynamic interplay between the copper species and PMS activation in driving the degradation process. This insight not only increases our understanding of the catalytic behavior of Cu-MOFs/GP but also underscores its potential for AOPs in environmental remediation.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the activation of PMS can generate ROS with high oxidative potential, including hydroxyl radicals (·OH), sulfate radicals (SO4•−), superoxide radicals (O2•−), singlet oxygen (¹O2), or a combination of them. To identify the specific ROS involved in the reaction and further elucidate the catalytic mechanism of TCH degradation, quenching experiments were conducted using methanol (MeOH), tert-butanol (TBA), and p-benzoquinone (p-BQ) to scavenge SO4•−, ·OH, and O2•−, respectively. Additionally, furfuryl alcohol (FFA) was employed to quench the 1O2.

As illustrated in Fig. 6(a), the addition of 500 mM MeOH and TBA resulted in TCH degradation efficiencies of 63.48% and 75.23%, respectively. When 2 mM p-BQ was introduced, the TCH degradation efficiency decreased to 64.98%, whereas the addition of 50 mM FFA significantly reduced the degradation efficiency to 33.2%. Notably, FFA exhibited the most pronounced inhibitory effect among the tested quenchers. The corresponding degradation rate constants (*k*) in the presence of MeOH, TBA, p-BQ, and FFA were calculated to be 0.014, 0.0147, 0.0098, and 0.0037 min−¹, respectively. Based on these experimental results and subsequent analysis, it can be inferred that the Cu-MOFs/GP@PMS system operates through a combined radical and non-radical pathway, with 1O2 playing a dominant role, whereas ·OH, SO4•−, and O2•− contribute as secondary reactive species. This dual mechanism not only enhances the versatility of the catalytic system but also provides a comprehensive understanding of its oxidative degradation capabilities, offering valuable insights for the design of AOPs for environmental applications.

In summary, we propose a plausible mechanism for the degradation of TCH in the Cu-MOFs/GP@PMS catalytic system (Fig. 7). Initially, TCH is adsorbed onto the surface of Cu-MOFs/GP. Upon the addition of PMS, PMS molecules acquire electrons from Cu+ in the Cu-MOFs, leading to the cleavage of the peroxy bond (O–O) in PMS and the subsequent generation of SO4•− and ·OH. During this process, Cu+ is oxidized to Cu2+ as shown in Reactions (4) and (5). Additionally, PMS can undergo gradual self-decomposition, producing SO4•− and ·OH. Furthermore, Cu²+ accepts electrons and reacts with HSO5− to form SO5•− and Cu+, thereby maintaining the Cu²+/Cu+ redox cycle and ensuring the regeneration of active Cu+ sites as shown in Reactions (6) and (7) [77]. Moreover, a portion of SO4•− reacts with OH− and H2O to generate ·OH as shown in Reactions (8) and (9) [63]. The O2•− in the system primarily originates from three reaction pathways: the reaction between PMS and H2O, the redox reaction of dissolved oxygen, and the reaction of a small fraction of ·OH with OH− to produce a minor amount of O2•−.

Concurrently, as previously reported, due to the relatively low redox potential of O2/O2•− (−0.33 V), Cu²+ reacts with O2•− to convert back to Cu+, resulting in the consumption of a small amount of O2•− as shown in Reactions (10)–(14) [78]. This intricate interplay of radical and non-radical pathways, coupled with the dynamic Cu²+/Cu+ redox cycle, underpins the efficient degradation of TCH in the Cu-MOFs/GP@PMS system, highlighting its potential for AOPs in environmental remediation. Quenching experiments revealed that 1O2 plays the most significant role in the degradation of TCH. The generation of 1O2 likely occurs through multiple pathways: (1) the reaction between Cu²+ and HSO5− produces SO5•−, which subsequently reacts with H2O to directly generate 1O2; (2) O2•− and ·OH further react to form 1O2; (3) PMS can decompose directly into a small amount of 1O2. Ultimately, the generated reactive species (1O2, ·OH, SO4•−, and O2•−) oxidize TCH into intermediate products, which are eventually mineralized into CO2 and H2O as shown in Reactions (15)–(19) [64]. During this process, TCH molecules act as electron donors, as they are oxidized and lose electrons, whereas PMS molecules function as electron acceptors, facilitating the production of various reactive species that oxidize TCH. Cu-MOFs/GP serve as an electron shuttle between PMS and TCH molecules, transferring electrons from TCH to PMS through the Cu²+/Cu+ redox cycle. This electron transfer mechanism enhances the efficiency of the degradation process. In summary, the synergistic interplay of radical and non-radical pathways, mediated by the Cu2+/Cu+ redox cycle and the generation of diverse reactive species, underpins the high-performance degradation of TCH in the Cu-MOFs/GP@PMS system, offering a robust strategy for AOPs in environmental applications. As a catalytic support, GP enables the uniform dispersion of Cu-MOFs and maximizes the exposure of the active sites. Furthermore, owing to its high porosity and mesoporous structure, GP can effectively adsorb TCH molecules through pore confinement effects, creating a localized high-concentration environment that reduces the diffusion distance between TCH and active sites (Cu-MOFs), thereby enhancing the catalytic performance of the composite. This efficient synergistic adsorption-catalysis mechanism plays a pivotal role in optimizing the overall catalytic efficiency of the composite catalyst.

### 3.4 Continuous flow catalytic TCH degradation.

The porous geopolymer spheres, with an open porosity of approximately 63%, demonstrated both practical mechanical stability and handling convenience, exhibiting a compressive strength of ~34.4 N while maintaining a relatively large diameter of ~7.5 mm (Table S5 in the ESM). This optimal balance between structural integrity and macroscopic dimensions enables facile recovery and multiple reuse cycles without generating secondary powder contamination. To evaluate the practical application of the Cu-MOFs/GP catalyst in real wastewater treatment, an in-house-designed fixed-bed reactor was employed for the continuous catalytic degradation of TCH at various concentrations, as illustrated in Fig. 9(a). The degradation efficiencies of TCH at concentrations of 10, 25, and 50 ppm reached 99.97%, 98.70%, and 90.34%, respectively, after 1 h. After 8 h of continuous operation, these efficiencies slightly decreased to 99.01%, 97.4%, and 88.19%, respectively (Fig. 9(b)). This minor decline may be attributed to the subtle loss of the Cu-MOFs/GP composite materials from the catalyst surface during the continuous flow process. Moreover, SEM analysis of the catalyst after 8 h revealed that the morphology of the Cu-MOFs remained intact, although some agglomeration was observed (Fig. S5 in the ESM). These results demonstrate that the constructed catalytic system has high efficiency and robust continuous treatment capability for antibiotic-laden wastewater, even at elevated concentrations. Moreover, geopolymers, as cost-effective and environmentally friendly resources, not only contribute to the degradation of TCH in wastewater but also reduce the consumption of natural resources. By utilizing industrial solid waste, this approach achieves the goal of “treating waste with waste”, significantly lowering the cost-effectiveness of wastewater treatment. The toxicity of TCH after flow-through catalytic degradation was investigated by observing the growth of mung beans. The germination and growth processes of mung beans in deionized water, degraded TCH solution, and pristine TCH solution are shown in Fig. 9(c). The results indicate that the toxicity of the degraded TCH solution was markedly lower than that of the pristine TCH solution, although it still exhibited a slight difference compared to that of deionized water. This finding underscores the effectiveness of the catalytic system in reducing the environmental impact of antibiotic contaminants, further supporting its potential for sustainable wastewater treatment applications.

## 4 Conclusions

This study pioneers the immobilization of Cu-MOFs on the surface of porous geopolymer supports through a facile and scalable synthesis route, creating a novel composite catalyst that synergistically combines the catalytic properties of MOFs with the structural advantages of geopolymers. The Cu-MOFs/GP@PMS system demonstrated exceptional TCH degradation efficiency, achieving over 90% removal of 50 mg/L TCH within 90 min through a combined adsorption-catalysis strategy. The catalyst exhibited remarkable operational stability across a wide pH range (5−11) while maintaining its performance in the presence of interfering anions and organic compounds. Mechanistic studies revealed a mixed radical/non-radical pathway dominated by 1O2, with additional contributions from ·OH, SO4•−, and O2•−. Continuous flow experiments further confirmed the practical potential of the Cu-MOFs/GP@PMS system, which resulted in consistent TCH removal over 8 h of operation. By effectively addressing the powder handling challenges of conventional MOF catalysts, this geopolymer-supported design offers an efficient, cost-effective, and environmentally sustainable solution for PMS-activated wastewater treatment systems.
