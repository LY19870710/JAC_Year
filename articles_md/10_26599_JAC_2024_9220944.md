## 1 Introduction

In recent years, microwave technology has dramatically progressed, marked by the arrival of the 5G era, owing to the advantages of electromagnetic waves in long-distance, wireless, and high-speed transmissions. However, electromagnetic wave pollution problems such as electromagnetic wave interference and electromagnetic wave radiation are becoming increasingly serious [1−5]. Electromagnetic wave pollution not only affects the normal operation of electronic equipment and greatly threatens the information security of the scientific community but also endangers human health and is a possible cause of cancer and sensitized diseases [6−8]. Therefore, electromagnetic wave pollution has caused widespread concern in the scientific community, and researchers have designed and fabricated various electromagnetic wave absorbers to address this issue.

According to the transmission line theory, electromagnetic wave absorbers are divided into dielectric and magnetic loss materials, which include carbon-based [9−11], metal-based [12−14], polymer-based [15−17], and ceramic-based composites [1,3]. Among them, ceramic-based composites, especially polymer-derived SiOC [18−21], SiC [22,23], SiBCN [24], and SiCN [25] ceramics, have many merits, such as corrosion resistance, high-temperature resistance, chemical stability, and oxidation resistance, and are considered promising candidates for electromagnetic wave protection applications in harsh environments. However, the main challenge of polymer-derived ceramic (PDC)-based composites for electromagnetic wave absorption is the single loss mechanism, resulting in inferior electromagnetic wave attenuation ability.

Two strategies are typically used to address this problem. One is the introduction of a second phase (carbon [26], SiC [27], ZrB2 [28], and Fe3Si [20]) to endow the material with additional electromagnetic wave attenuation mechanisms, such as magnetic loss, interfacial polarization loss, and conductive loss. Another strategy is to design special structures, such as nanofibers [25], aerogels [3], and porous structures [26], with high specific surface areas to enrich the heterogeneous interface, increase the interfacial polarization loss and form many pores to increase multiple reflections and scattering loss.

For example, Feng *et al*. [28] fabricated TiB2–SiCnw–SiOC ceramics via the PDC method with the introduction of TiB2. The resulting sample exhibited superior electromagnetic wave absorption performance, with a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −55.1 dB and an effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) of 4.2 GHz. This performance was attributed to the synergistic effects of multiple reflections, scattering, interfacial polarization, and conductive loss mechanisms. Wang *et al*. [25] reported lightweight and flexible SiCN nanowires obtained by electrospinning and pyrolysis treatment. They found that the electromagnetic wave absorption performance of SiCN nanowires was affected by the pyrolysis time and RLmin of this material reached −53.1 dB; EAB covered the whole Ku band. The excellent electromagnetic wave absorption performance of the SiCN nanowires was ascribed to the synergistic effect between carbon defects and the heterogeneous interface between the SiC and Si3N4 nanocrystals, which resulted in defects and interfacial polarization for dissipating the electromagnetic energy. More recently, Yang *et al*. [3] used a simple ambient pressure drying to prepare a superior electromagnetic wave-absorbing C/SiOC composite aerogel with thermal insulation and flame retardant properties. The optimum RL value of the C/SiOC composite aerogel was −67.85 dB, which arose from the excellent impedance matching. Although the electromagnetic wave absorption performance of PDCs has been considerably improved in previous works, the reported manufacturing processes are complex and require expensive equipment. Typically, nanostructures exhibit a high specific surface area, which is beneficial for the reflection and scattering of electromagnetic waves. In addition, the carbon structure plays a vital role in the performance of carbon-containing ceramic-based composites in terms of electromagnetic wave dissipation. Designing SiOC ceramic nanospheres with carbon shells to enhance their electromagnetic wave absorption performance has rarely been reported, and the effects of the phase composition and microstructure of SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres by adjusting the pyrolysis atmosphere on their electromagnetic wave absorption performance still require clarification.

Herein, SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres were obtained using a liquid-phase method combined with the PDC method. The morphology, microstructure, and phase composition of SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres prepared under different heat treatment conditions were investigated in detail. In particular, the SiOC@C pyrolyzed in an Ar atmosphere (SiOC nanospheres) at 1450 °C for 2 h showed RLmin of −67.03 dB; at the same time, the SiOC@C nanospheres pyrolyzed in a N2 atmosphere (SiOC(N) nanospheres) at 1250 °C for 2 h presented an RLmin value of −63.76 dB. The outstanding electromagnetic wave absorption performance of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres was attributed to the synergistic effect between the conductive loss, interfacial polarization loss, and high specific surface area, resulting in multiple reflections and scattering of electromagnetic waves.

### 2.1 Materials

Dimethyl dimethoxy silicane ((CH3)2Si(OCH3)2) was purchased from Zhengzhou Alpha Chemical Co., Ltd. Tetraethoxysilane (Si(OCH2CH3)4, TEOS) was supplied by Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd. Resorcinol (C6H6O2) was provided by Shanghai Maclin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Formaldehyde (HCHO, 37–40 wt%), ammonium hydroxide (NH3·H2O, 25–28 wt%), absolute ethyl alcohol (C2H5OH, analytical reagent (AR) > 99.7%), and deionized water were utilized in this work. All chemical reagents were used without further purification.

### 2.2 Fabrication of SiOC@C nanospheres

First, an alkaline solution consisting of 100 mL of absolute ethyl alcohol, 14 mL of deionized water, and 7 mL of ammonium hydroxide was prepared by mixing individual components and stirring for 30 min. Subsequently, 4.55 mL of tetraethoxysilane and 0.91 mL of dimethyl dimethoxy silane were added dropwise to the alkaline solution, followed by continued stirring for 24 h to obtain SiOC nanospheres. Then, 1 g of resorcinol and 1.4 mL of formaldehyde were added to the above solution and stirred for another 24 h to obtain SiOC@C nanospheres. Finally, the SiOC@C nanospheres were collected by centrifugation at 5000 r/min for 5 min with absolute ethyl ethanol three times and then dried in an oven at 80 °C.

### 2.3 Fabrication of SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres

The collected SiOC@C nanospheres were heat-treated in an Ar atmosphere at 1250, 1350, and 1450 °C for 2 h to obtain SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres. For convenience, these samples were labeled SiOC@C-1, SiOC@C-2, and SiOC@C-3, respectively. Additionally, the collected SiOC@C nanospheres were heat-treated in a N2 atmosphere at 1250, 1350, and 1450 °C for 2 h to obtain SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres. The samples obtained in the N2 atmosphere were labeled SiOC@C-4, SiOC@C-5, and SiOC@C-6, respectively.

### 2.4 Characterizations

The morphologies and microstructures of the as-obtained SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres were analyzed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Helions Nanolab 600i, FEI) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; CM300, Philips). The phase composition of the as-obtained SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres was characterized through an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X’Pert) and a Raman spectroscope (WITec alpha300R, Oxford Instruments). The specific surface area and pore size distribution were determined via a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analyzer (BSD-660S A3, Best Instrument Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd.). The surface elemental compositions of the as-obtained SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres were recorded by X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; K-Alpha+, Thermo Fisher). The electromagnetic parameters were recorded by a vector network analyzer (N5230A, Agilent).

### 2.5 Radar cross section (RCS) simulation

CST Studio Suite 2023 software was used to simulate the far-field electromagnetic wave absorption performance of SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres when the electromagnetic wave frequency was 2–18 GHz. In this simulation, the square area of the perfect electric conductor (PEC) and covered electromagnetic wave absorbing coating was 200 mm × 200 mm, as shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Different thicknesses and monitoring frequencies were set for different samples. The RCS of electromagnetic wave-absorbing SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres was simulated based on far-field response.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1 shows the preparation process of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres. First, tetraethoxysilane and dimethyl dimethoxy silane were subjected to hydrolytic polycondensations to form SiOC nanospheres. Then, the added resorcinol and formaldehyde formed phenolic resin on the surface of the SiOC nanospheres through hydrolytic polycondensations, yielding SiOC@C nanospheres. Finally, the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres were prepared by the pyrolysis of SiOC@C nanospheres in an Ar (N2) atmosphere.

The XRD results were recorded to investigate the phase composition of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained under different temperatures and atmospheres, the results of which are displayed in Figs. 2(a) and 2(d). The diffraction peak at approximately 23° was attributed to the amorphous SiOC structure [29,30], and the diffraction peak at approximately 44° was assigned to amorphous carbon [16]. As shown in Fig. 2(a) (the nanospheres obtained in an Ar atmosphere), with increasing heat-treatment temperature, the intensities of several crystalline peaks located at 35.5°, 41.3°, 59.8°, and 71.6° gradually increased, which corresponded to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) crystal planes of SiC. These results indicated the formation of the SiC crystal phase owing to the phase separation of SiOC and the carbothermal reduction reaction according to Reactions (1) and (2). In addition, the stacking fault (SF) diffraction peak to the left of the (111) crystal plane resulted from the stacking dislocations of SiC [31], which are conducive to the dissipation of electromagnetic waves. The ceramic nanospheres heat treated in the N2 atmosphere (Fig. 2(d)) showed some crystalline peaks corresponding to α-Si3N4 (powder diffraction file (PDF#74-0554) and β-Si3N4 (PDF#33-1160), which were not observed in the ceramic nanospheres heat treated in the Ar atmosphere. As we all know, SiC is a good electromagnetic wave-absorbing material, which is conducive to improving the attenuation coefficient of the material, and S3N4 is a typical electromagnetic wave-transmitting material [10], which is conducive to optimizing the impedance matching of the material. Both play important roles in electromagnetic protection materials.

Similarly, the carbon structure also plays an important role in electromagnetic wave-absorbing materials. Raman and XPS spectra were used to characterize the features of the carbon structure of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres. As displayed in Figs. 2(b) and 2(e), the typical Raman shifts at approximately 1335 and 1590 cm−1 are ascribed to the D and G peaks of carbon materials, respectively. The intensity ratio of peak D to peak C (I*D/*I*G) values of the ceramic nanospheres obtained in both Ar and N2 atmospheres increased with increasing heat treatment temperature, which indicated that the defect content gradually increased. The carbon defects benefit from the formation of a defect center to generate defect polarization, resulting in attenuation of the electromagnetic wave energy. As shown in Figs. 2(c) and 2(f), all the XPS spectra of the nanospheres obtained in the Ar/N2 atmosphere presented Si 2s, Si 2p, O 1s, and C 1s peaks, and the N 1s peak of the nanospheres prepared in the N2 atmosphere is also shown in Fig. 2(f). Moreover, the fine peaks of the C 1s peak of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in the Ar atmosphere (Figs. 3(a)–3(c)) were fitted into three typical peaks at approximately 283, 284, and 287 eV corresponding to C–Si, C–C/C=C, and C–O [32], respectively. The details are shown in Table 1. In the fine C 1s spectrum of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in the N2 atmosphere (Figs. 3(d)–3(f)), the three fitting peaks were C–C/C=C, C–N, and C–O [33]. In their N 1s fitting peaks (Figs. 3(g)–3(i)), two peaks at approximately 297 and 298 eV coincided with N–Si and N–C, respectively. Nitrogen atoms have more extranuclear electrons than carbon atoms. Therefore, the incorporation of nitrogen atoms into carbon materials leads to the breaking of C‒C bonds and the formation of C‒N bonds. Carbon materials have more free electrons in their structure, which results in greater conductance loss to dissipate electromagnetic wave energy. This result is consistent with the Raman spectroscopy results. In the Raman spectra, all the *I*D/*I*G values of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in the N2 atmosphere were lower than those obtained in the Ar atmosphere at the same heat treatment temperature, which indicated that the conductivities of the SiOC@C ceramic spheres obtained in the N2 atmosphere were greater than those obtained in the Ar atmosphere.

The isotherms of all the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres exhibit a type IV curve, as shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM, suggesting a mesoporous structure. According to the BET results, the average pore sizes of SiOC@C-1, SiOC@C-2, SiOC@C-3, SiOC@C-4, SiOC@C-5, and SiOC@C-6 are 8.72, 5.05, 3.30, 2.65, 2.86, and 3.49 nm, respectively. The BET results indicate that the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres possess large specific areas, which not only decrease the effective permittivity of materials but also aid in multiple reflections/scattering [34].

To further investigate the microstructure of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres, TEM images are presented in Figs. 4 and 5. The average diameter of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres was 600 nm, as shown in Fig. 4. With the temperature increase, the nanospheres in Fig. 4(b1) began to appear in a second phase; when the temperature increased to 1450 °C, the content of the second-phase particles increased. Figure 4(c3) displays a high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image of the SiOC ceramic nanosphere. The second phase exhibited a lattice spacing of 0.25 nm, corresponding to the (111) crystal plane of SiC. The energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) results are shown in Figs. 4(c5)–4(c7). The second-phase particles contained abundant Si elements, while other regions were evenly distributed with Si, O, and C elements. Additionally, from the HRTEM images shown in Figs. 4(a3), 4(b3), and 4(c3), the boundary region of the spherical particles contains graphite phase carbon, and the other areas have amorphous structures. Based on the above results, the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in the Ar atmosphere consisted of nanosized SiC crystalline, graphite phase carbon, amorphous carbon, and amorphous SiOC. Graphite phase carbon contributes to generating microcurrents to attenuate electromagnetic wave energy, and the heterogeneous interface formed by the multiphase structure contributes to the formation of interface polarization loss to consume the electromagnetic wave energy.

Figure 5 shows the TEM results of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in the N2 atmosphere. The average diameter of the larger nanospheres was 600 nm, and that of the smaller nanospheres ranged from 150 to 200 nm, which coincided with the SEM results. Interestingly, the smaller nanospheres acted as bridges between bigger nanospheres, which is conducive to forming a more connected conductive network for enhancing conductance loss to consume the electromagnetic wave energy. In addition, graphitic carbon was also present in the boundary region of both nanospheres (Figs. 5(a3), 5(b3), and 5(c3)), which is conducive to improving the conductivity of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres. In the nanosphere, there are crystals with an interplanar spacing of 0.43 nm, corresponding to the (101) crystal of α-Si3N4 (Fig. 5(c3)). The EDS results are shown in Figs. 5(d1)–5(d4). The SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres are evenly distributed with Si, O, C, and N elements. Based on the above XRD, SEM, TEM, and XPS results, the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in the N2 atmosphere consisted of nanosized crystalline Si3N4, graphitic carbon, amorphous carbon, and amorphous SiOCN.

The electromagnetic wave absorption properties of dielectric materials are related to their relatively complex permittivity, and the real and imaginary parts of permittivity (ε*′ and *ε″*) represent the storage and dissipation abilities of electric energy [10,35,36]. Because the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres prepared in this work do not introduce magnetic components, the complex permeability is regarded as 1. The complex permittivity of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in the Ar atmosphere measured from 2 to 18 GHz is displayed in Fig. S4 in the ESM, and the loading of the SiOC@C nanospheres was 15%. The SiOC@C-1 and SiOC@C-2 samples have average *ε*′ values of 4.84 and 4.41, respectively, and average *ε″* values of 0.31 and 0.32 at 2–18 GHz, respectively, indicating that they are almost all electromagnetic wave-transmitting materials. With increasing heat treatment temperature, the SiOC@C-3 sample has an average *ε*′ value of 6.42 and an average *ε″* value of 1.42. The increase in permittivity is due to the interfacial polarization formed by the amount of nanocrystalline SiC, and the defect polarization results from the carbon defects and stacking dislocations of SiC. As shown in Fig. S4(d) in the ESM, the three semicircular curves indicate that three polarization relaxation phenomena occur in the materials. where *μ*0, *ε*0, and *Z*0 are the space permeability, the space permittivity, and the free space impedance, respectively; *Z*in represents the normalized input impedance; *μ*r represents the complex permeability of materials (*μ*r in this work is 1), and *ε*r denotes the complex permittivity of materials; *f* and *c* represent the electromagnetic wave frequency and the velocity, respectively; *d* represents the thickness of electromagnetic wave-absorbing materials.

The three-dimensional (3D) RL plots of the SiOC@C-1, SiOC@C-2, and SiOC@C-3 samples are shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM. Generally, if the RL value is less than −10 dB, the materials can consume 90% of the incident electromagnetic wave energy, which can meet the requirements of civilian electromagnetic wave-absorbing materials. As displayed in Fig. S5(a) and S5(b) in the ESM, the RL values of SiOC@C-1 and SiOC@C-2 barely reached −10 dB when the thickness of the samples was adjusted, indicating their poor electromagnetic wave absorption performance. Additionally, RLmin of the SiOC@C-3 sample reached −67.03 dB with a thickness of 4.25 mm, while its EAB was 2.89 GHz with a thickness of 2.98 mm, revealing that the electromagnetic wave absorption clearly improved with increasing SiC nanocrystals and carbon defects.

As shown in Fig. S5(c) in the ESM, EAB of SiOC@C-3 could cover only part of the X-band and Ku-band by adjusting its thickness. To further broaden the absorption band, the effects of different loadings of SiOC@C-3 on the electromagnetic wave absorption performance were further investigated. The complex permittivity of all the samples with different SiOC@C-3 contents is shown in Fig. 6. The average permittivity gradually increases as the loading of SiOC increases. In particular, SiOC@C-3-50% has a relatively higher average *ε*″ value than the other two SiOC@C-3 materials at 2–18 GHz. In fact, excessive *ε*″ values may result in poor impedance matching, which is not conducive to improving the electromagnetic wave absorption performance [40].

*
Fig. 6(a) ε*′, (b) *ε*″, (c) tan*δ*, and (d–f) *ε*′–*ε″* curves of SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in Ar atmosphere.

The dielectric loss tangent value (tan*δ* = *ε*″/*ε*′) represents the dissipation ability of electromagnetic waves, and a higher tan*δ* value means that more electromagnetic wave energy can be attenuated. As shown in Fig. 6(c), the average tan*δ* values of SiOC@C-3-15%, SiOC@C-3-30%, and SiOC@C-3-50% are 0.20, 0.24, and 0.70, respectively. Therefore, the dissipation ability of both SiOC@C-3-15% and SiOC@C-3-30% is equally strong, and SiOC@C-3-50% has excellent dielectric loss properties. In addition, Debye depolarization is important for improving the electromagnetic wave absorption performance. Several semicircular curves can also be observed in Figs. 6(d)–6(f). In conclusion, all the samples exhibit Debye depolarization owing to the amounts of SiC nanocrystals and carbon defects dispersed in the amorphous SiOC and carbon matrix to form interfacial and defect polarizations.

In addition to the above factors (dielectric loss and Debye polarization) that should be considered when evaluating electromagnetic wave absorption performance, impedance matching (*Z*m = *Z*in/*Z*0) and attenuation constant (*α* = * 2πf[(μ′′ϵ′′−μ′ϵ′)+(μ′′ϵ′′−μ′ϵ′)2+(μ′ϵ′′+μ′′ϵ′)2]12/c*)are also two important factors for electromagnetic wave absorption [41,42]. Impedance matching is used to determine whether the incident electromagnetic wave can successfully enter the inside of the material, and the attenuation coefficient is used to measure the comprehensive attenuation ability of the material to the incident electromagnetic wave. According to previous works [38,43], if a material can be used as a civilian electromagnetic wave-absorbing material, its impedance matching value needs to meet *Z*m = 0.53–1.93. As shown in Figs. 7(a)–7(c), all the *Z*m values of SiOC@C-3-50% are less than 0.53, indicating poor impedance matching. However, the *α* value of SiOC@C-3-50% is far greater than those of both SiOC@C-3-15% and SiOC@C-3-30% at 2–18 GHz. In other words, although SiOC@C-3-50% has excellent attenuation ability, the incident electromagnetic wave cannot enter it. In addition, the *α* value of SiOC@C-3-30% is greater than that of SiOC@C-3-15% at frequencies ranging from 2 to 18 GHz.

The RL plots of different loadings of SiOC@C-3 are shown in Fig. 8. To further investigate the influence of the composition of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres on electromagnetic wave absorption, the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in a N2 atmosphere were tested. On the basis of the above results, the mass fraction of SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres was set at 30%. The complex permittivity of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in the N2 atmosphere is clearly greater than that of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in the Ar atmosphere (Fig. 9). In addition, the complex permittivity of SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres increases with increasing heat-treatment temperature. The average ε*′ value changes from 9.5 to 9.6 and then to 13.67 (Fig. 9(a)), while the average ε″ value increases from 2.96 to 3.38 and then to 7.44. With increasing heat treatment temperature, the average tan*δ* increases from 0.32 to 0.36 and then to 0.55, indicating improved electromagnetic wave dissipation ability. According to the above Raman and XPS results (Figs. 2 and 3), in the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in a N2 atmosphere, N atoms are doped into C atoms to form C‒N bonds, increasing the electrical conductivity, which is the main reason for the increase in permittivity. This result can be further verified by the Debye curves (Fig. 9(d)–9(f)) in which there is a straight line at the end of the curve, which is caused by the conductivity loss of the materials [45]. Moreover, the semicircle curves of all the samples were caused by interfacial and defect polarization.

However, for electromagnetic wave absorption materials, both impedance matching and attenuation constant are significant factors to produce excellent reflection loss, as shown in Fig. 10. The order of *α* value was *α*SiOC@C-6-30% > *α*SiOC@C-4-30% > *α*SiOC@C-5-30%. Higher heat-treated temperatures in a N2 atmosphere lead to greater attenuation ability because of the higher conductivity of N2. The average *α* value of SiOC@C-6-30% obtained at 1450 °C was 211.69, which was higher than that of SiOC@C-3-30% (average *α*SiOC@C-3-30% = 132.88). The higher *α* value for the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in the N2 atmosphere is due to two factors. First, the introduction of N improves the conductivity; second, the smaller SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres bridge the larger ones to form a more connected conductive structure, which is more conducive to carrier transport (Fig. 5). However, SiOC@C-6-30% has worse impedance matching than SiOC@C-4-30% and SiOC@C-5-30%, which does not facilitate the entry of electromagnetic waves.

Through their microstructure and composition, the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres exhibited excellent absorption performance, and their electromagnetic wave absorption mechanism is summarized as follows (Fig. 13). First, the accumulation of the ceramic nanospheres results in the formation of many pore structures, which improves the impedance matching of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres. More electromagnetic waves can enter the interior of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres, producing multiple reflections and scattering in the pores to attenuate electromagnetic waves. Second, the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in an Ar atmosphere consist of nanosized SiC crystalline, graphite phase carbon, amorphous carbon, and amorphous SiOC. When electromagnetic waves enter the interior of SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres, the moving speed of electron is different because of the different conductivities of the different phases. Therefore, it is easy to generate electron accumulation at the heterogeneous interface, and the other side is equivalent to a positive one, forming a capacitor structure. The direction of the electromagnetic field changes at all time, and the capacitor is charged and discharged indefinitely, resulting in the constant consumption of electromagnetic energy. In addition, the carbon defects and stacking dislocations in SiC nanocrystals can produce defect polarization behaviors. The better electromagnetic wave absorption performance of the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in the N2 atmosphere than in the Ar atmosphere should be attributed to the introduction of N atoms enhancing the conductance loss. Although Si3N4 nanocrystals exhibit electromagnetic wave-transmission properties, they can optimize impedance matching. However, the SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in the N2 atmosphere at 1450 °C exhibited excessive permittivity, leading to an increase in the reflection waves. The SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres prepared in this work clearly exhibit excellent electromagnetic wave absorption performance with a thin thickness, strong absorption, wide absorption bandwidth, and low loading.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, a series of SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres containing SiC/Si3N4, free carbon, and amorphous SiOC were successfully obtained through rational control of the heat-treatment temperature and atmosphere. The main conclusions of this study are summarized below.

(1) The SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in an Ar atmosphere consisted of nanocrystallineSiC, graphite phase carbon, amorphous carbon, and amorphous SiOC. The RLmim value of SiOC@C-3-30% was −45.23 dB at a matching thickness of 1.5 mm, and its EAB could cover the entire C, X, and Ku bands by adjusting the sample thickness.

(2) The SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres obtained in the N2 atmosphere consisted of nanocrystalline Si3N4, graphite phase carbon, amorphous carbon, and amorphous SiOCN. The RLmin value of SiOC@C-4-30% was −62.76 dB at a matching thickness of 1.63 mm. The RLmin value of SiOC@C-5-30% was −60.54 dB at a lower matching thickness of 1.57 mm.

(3) The prepared SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres achieved excellent electromagnetic wave absorption performance, which was attributed to the synergistic effects of interfacial and defect polarization, conductance loss, and multiple reflections and scattering.

Therefore, SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres with “light, thin, wide, and strong” features can be candidates for electromagnetic wave-absorbing materials.
