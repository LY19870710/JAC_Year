## 1 Introduction

Microwave dielectric ceramics (MWDCs), pivotal materials for the fabrication of mobile communication devices, are extensively utilized in dielectric resonators, filters, and antennas. With the global deployment of 5G mobile communication networks gaining momentum and the preliminary design and development of 6G mobile communication underway, the advancement of mobile communication toward higher frequency bands is accelerating, increasing signal transmission speed and quality [1−4]. The rapid evolution of mobile communication technology has escalated the demand for reliable devices, underscoring the critical role of MWDCs in meeting diverse requirements and improving communication system quality. To meet current developmental needs, there is an urgent need to develop MWDCs characterized by low signal delay (low permittivity, *ε*r), low loss (high quality factor, *Q×f*), and excellent temperature stability (near-zero temperature coefficient of resonant frequency, *τ*f) [5−10].

In recent years, garnet-type microwave dielectric ceramics have received extensive attention due to their high controllability. By adjusting the chemical composition and designing different cations to replace specific positions in garnet, the properties of ceramics can be effectively adjusted. As a typical garnet structure material, Y3Al5O12 (YAG) ceramics have excellent microwave dielectric properties (S.T. = 1650 °C, 12 h, *ε*r = 10.01, *Q×f* = 65,000 GHz, *τ*f = −45 ppm/°C) [11], but the sintering temperature is high, and the *Q×f* and *τ*f values still have room for further optimization. Researchers have explored the substitution of YAG with various cations and achieved different degrees of progress. For example, Song *et al*. [12] recently reported the synergistic substitution of Mg2+ and Si4+ for YAG to obtain Y3MgAl3SiO12 ceramics. The sintering temperature of the ceramics is reduced to 1550 °C, and *τ*f is closer to zero (*ε*r = 10.1, *Q×f* = 57,340 GHz (@9.5 GHz), *τ*f = −32 ppm/°C). He *et al*. [13] reported Lu2MgAl4SiO12 ceramics with *ε*r = 10.2, *Q×f* = 37,938 GHz (@9.47 GHz), and *τ*f = −31.7 ppm/°C. Through multielement cosubstitution, a low dielectric constant was maintained, and the sintering temperature was reduced to 1490 °C. Compared with YAG, the sintering temperature of this series of Si-based garnet-type microwave dielectric ceramics is slightly lower, but the *Q×f* performance is relatively poor. In addition, Fang *et al*. [14] reported a series of high-performance Ge-based garnet-structured microwave dielectric ceramics. For example, Sr3B2Ge3O12 (B = Yb, Ho) ceramics exhibit *ε*r = 9.30, 9.23, *Q×f* = 129,360, 104,600 GHz, and *τ*f = −42, −26 ppm/°C, respectively. Furthermore, our research group previously reported Ca3Al2(GeO4)3 ceramics (*ε*r = 7.6, *Q×f* = 104,100 GHz, *τ*f = −15 ppm/°C) [15] and Ca3Fe2Ge3O12 ceramics (*ε*r = 10.31, *Q×f* = 82,636 GHz, and *τ*f = −45 ppm/°C) [16], both of which demonstrate outstanding performance. These results indicate that Ge-based microwave dielectric ceramics hold significant research potential.

Research has shown that Ge-based garnet-type microwave dielectric ceramics generally exhibit high *Q×f* values. The substitution of Mg2+ has been found to help reduce the sintering temperature of YAG ceramics to some extent. However, these ceramics still face challenges in simultaneously achieving low dielectric loss, near-zero *τ*f values, and a low *ε*r. Therefore, based on the structural stability of garnet and the excellent performance of YAG-based ceramics, this work adopts the cosubstitution of Mg2+ and Ge4+ in YAG ceramics to prepare Y3MgAl3GeO12 ceramics. The samples were comprehensively characterized via X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and Raman spectroscopy to determine their phase composition, microstructure, and morphological features. Furthermore, factors influencing microwave dielectric properties were analyzed through Phillips–Vechten–Levine (P–V–L) theory, thermal expansion, far-infrared spectroscopy, terahertz (Thz) spectroscopy, and temperature-dependent spectroscopy. From an application perspective, a near-zero *τ*f value is required to ensure the optimal performance of the material. Therefore, to achieve high-temperature stability, compensating additives with opposite *τ*f values were incorporated into Y3MgAl3GeO12 to tailor its temperature coefficient of resonant frequency. Additionally, the design of a dielectric resonator antenna was simulated to explore the potential for device applications of this material.

### 2.1 Material synthesis

Y3MgAl3GeO12 (YMAG) ceramics were synthesized via a solid-state reaction via high-purity oxides: Y2O3 (99.5%, Guangxi Guosheng Rare Earth New Materials Co., Ltd., China), MgO (98.5%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), Al2O3 (99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), GeO2 (99.99%, Yunnan Chihong Zinc & Germanium Co., Ltd., China), and TiO2 (99.98%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China). The stoichiometric powders were ball-milled with zirconia balls in ethanol for 4 h, dried, and calcined at 1500 °C for 4 h. The calcined powders were remilled, granulated with 8 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and uniaxially pressed into cylindrical compacts (10 mm diameter × 5 mm height). After PVA removal at 550 °C for 4 h, the samples were sintered at 1550–1650 °C for 4 h to obtain dense ceramics.

### 2.2 Characterization methods

The phase composition and crystal structure of the ceramic samples were analyzed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD) using Cu Kα radiation (XPertPRO diffractometer, PANalytical, Almelo, the Netherlands). The crystal structure parameters, including the lattice constants, atomic positions, background, peak broadening, and preferred orientation, were refined through Rietveld analysis with GSAS software. The crystal structure was further analyzed via VESTA software. The ceramic surface morphology was characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM6380LVSEM, JEOL, Japan). The phase composition and lattice vibrational characteristics of the molecular structure were investigated via a confocal micro-Raman spectrometer (DXR, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Room-temperature infrared reflectivity spectra were acquired via a Bruker IFS 66v Fourier transform infrared spectrometer at the infrared beamline station (U4) of the National Synchrotron Radiation Laboratory (NSRL), China. The thermal expansion properties were measured via a horizontal thermal dilatometer (DIL 402C, Netzsch Instruments, Germany). The apparent density of the ceramic samples was determined via the Archimedes drainage method. The time-domain spectrum (TDS) data were transformed into frequency-domain spectra (FDSs) via a fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm to extract amplitude and phase information. This information was utilized to calculate the complex permittivity of the ceramic samples. The microwave dielectric properties were characterized via an E5080B vector network analyzer (Keysight, USA; 9 kHz–44 GHz) in conjunction with a DELTA9039 constant-temperature drying oven (LR Environmental Equipment Company Inc., USA). The *τ*f was determined across a temperature range of 25–85 °C and was calculated according to Eq. (1):

(1) τf=(f85−f25)(85−25)f25×106

where *f*25 and *f*85 denote the resonant frequencies at 25 and 85 °C, respectively.

The YMAG ceramic material crystallizes in a cubic garnet structure with a space group *Ia** 3¯d*, belonging to the isometric crystal system [17]. In this structure, Y occupies the dodecahedral site, forming [YO8] dodecahedra (Fig. 2(b)), whereas Mg and a portion of Al occupy the octahedral sites, forming [Mg/AlO6] octahedra (Fig. 2(c)). The vibrational modes A1g, Eg, and T2g correspond to internal vibrations, translational vibrations, and rotational vibrations, respectively. Owing to the limitations in detecting low-intensity and overlapping vibration peaks, the number of observed peaks is fewer than the number of predicted values. Below 250 cm−1, the Raman peaks are attributed to the external translational motion of Ca2+ cations relative to the lattice. In the range of 250–500 cm−1, the Raman peaks arise from the rotational and translational vibrations of the [GeO4] tetrahedra. Between 500 and 800 cm−1, the Raman peaks are associated with the bending vibration modes of Ge–O bonds. Above 807 cm−1, the Raman peaks result from the symmetric stretching mode A1g of Ge–O bonds [20].

The Raman linewidth (half-width) is a critical parameter in Raman spectroscopy, reflecting the phonon vibration mode linewidth in materials, which is influenced by lattice distortion, grain size, stress state, and defects. To investigate the effect of sintering temperature on the lattice vibrations of YMAG ceramics, Gaussian fitting was applied to the Raman spectra. This method is effective for analyzing the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of each vibrational mode. The fitting results for the samples sintered at the optimal temperature are presented in Fig. 3(b). According to classical radiation theory, the damping coefficient (γ*) affects the FWHM, leading to variations in internal loss [21]. The dielectric loss (tan*δ*) is inversely correlated with the FWHM (Eqs. (3) and (4)):

(3) tan⁡δ≈γω0ωT2

(4) FWHM=γγ2+4ω022ω0

where *ω*0 and *ω*T represent the center frequency of the optical mode and the angular frequency of the lattice vibration, respectively. Additionally, the FWHM value of the Raman peak is closely related to cation disorder and crystallinity, and a smaller FWHM corresponds to higher cation order and higher crystallinity. Consequently, a smaller FWHM value implies reduced dielectric loss caused by lattice defects. Furthermore, the FWHM is inversely proportional to the phonon lifetime, meaning that a relatively narrow bandgap corresponds to a longer phonon lifetime and weaker interactions. As the FWHM decreases, anharmonic vibrations are reduced, potentially weakening vibrational consistency and damping behavior, thereby lowering the intrinsic dielectric loss. Conversely, an increase in the FWHM leads to a higher attenuation coefficient for lattice vibrations, resulting in greater intrinsic loss and a reduced *Q×f* value [22,23]. This demonstrates that the FWHM value is one of the key factors influencing the *Q×f* value of the material (Fig. 4(d)).

### 3.2 Microwave dielectric properties

The density and microwave dielectric properties of the ceramic samples sintered at different temperatures were measured. As shown in Fig. 4(a), the bulk density (*ρ*) and relative density (*ρ*re) of the YMAG ceramics initially increased but then slightly decreased with increasing sintering temperature. Notably, *ρ*re is more sensitive to changes in the sintering temperature [24], and its calculation formula is shown in Eq. (5):

(5) ρre=ρρth×100%

where *ρ*th denotes the theoretical density of the ceramic samples. *ρ*re reaches ~94%, indicating a high degree of ceramic densification.

Figure 4(b) shows the variation in the relative permittivity (*ε*ᵣ) and the corrected permittivity (*ε*co) with the sintering temperature. As the sintering temperature increased, *ε*ᵣ of the YMAG ceramics initially increased and then stabilized within the temperature range of 1550–1650 °C, maintaining a stable value between 8 and 10.5. Generally, the factors influencing *ε*ᵣ of ceramics can be categorized into intrinsic and extrinsic factors [25]. The impact of ion polarizability, an intrinsic factor, on theoretical permittivity (*ε*th) is described by the Clausius–Mossotti equation (Eq. (6)):

(6) εth=3Vm+8πα3Vm−4πα

where *V*m is the molecular volume and *α* is the polarizability of the molecules. The molecular polarizability of YMAG is calculated by Eq. (7):

(7) α(Y3MgAl3GeO12)=3α(Y3+)+α(Mg2+)+3α(Al3+)+α(Ge4+)+12α(O2−)

where *α*(Y3+), *α*(Mg2+), *α*(Al3+), *α*(Ge4+), and *α*(O2−) represent the ionic polarizabilities. According to the results of Shannon [26], the ionic polarizability of a ceramic sample is as follows: Y3+ (3.81 Å3), Mg2+ (1.32 Å3), Al3+ (0.79 Å3), Ge4+ (1.63 Å3), and O2− (2.01 Å3). The *ε*th value calculated according to the ionic polarizability is ~10.75, and *ε*r is close to the *ε*th value, indicating that the *ε*r value of YMAG ceramics is less affected by extrinsic factors.

Figure 4(d) shows the variation in the *Q×f* value of the YMAG ceramics with the sintering temperature. The *Q×f* value initially increases and then decreases with increasing sintering temperature, reaching a maximum of ~89,000 GHz at 1600 °C. The *Q×f* value of microwave dielectric ceramics is influenced primarily by intrinsic and extrinsic losses [27]. Intrinsic losses include crystal structure and lattice vibrations [28], among which the packing fraction is a critical factor affecting the crystal structure. A higher packing fraction effectively suppresses lattice vibrations, reduces intrinsic losses, and enhances the *Q×f* value. The packing fraction is calculated using Eq. (8):

(8) Packingfraction=4π3×(3rY3+2rMg/Al3+3rAl/Ge3+12rO3)a3×Z

where *r*Y = 1.019 Å, *r*Mg/Al = 0.6275 Å, *r*Al/Ge = 0.39 Å, and *r*O = 1.4 Å; the calculation results are shown in Fig. 4(d). As the sintering temperature increased, the packing fraction initially increased but then decreased, reaching a maximum value of ~68.8% at 1600 °C, which aligns with the trend observed for the *Q×f* value. This confirms that the packing fraction is one of the key factors influencing the *Q×f* value of ceramics. Extrinsic losses, on the other hand, are associated with defects, pores, secondary phases, and grain size [29]. With respect to extrinsic factors, the XRD patterns indicate the absence of secondary phases in the ceramic samples, ruling out their influence on the *Q×f* value. The SEM and grain size distribution analyses clearly revealed that during the sintering process, particle migration, grain boundary movement, pore elimination, and shrinkage contribute to densification and grain growth. At lower sintering temperatures, the high porosity of the ceramics results in poor dielectric properties. As the sintering temperature increased, these properties gradually improved, reaching an optimal state near 1600 °C. However, further increases in the sintering temperature lead to excessive grain growth, which degrades the dielectric properties.

Figure 4(f) shows the variation in the *τ*f of the YMAG ceramics with the sintering temperature. The *τ*f value initially decreases and then increases as the sintering temperature increases. The sample sintered at 1600 °C has the minimum *τ*f value of −40 ppm/°C. The *τ*f parameter determines the temperature stability of the resonant frequency in practical applications and is influenced primarily by the temperature stability of the permittivity and the thermal expansion coefficient.

The properties of a material are intrinsically linked to its structure, and the structure of crystalline materials can be viewed as a combination of various chemical bonds. Therefore, analyzing the chemical bonds in YMAG ceramics provides insights into the intrinsic factors influencing their microwave dielectric properties [6,30]. YMAG ceramics can be decomposed into the following bond types based on complex crystal chemical bond splitting theory (P–V–L theory) (Eq. (9)):

(9) Y3MgAl3GeO12=Y3/2O31(1)+Y3/2O31(2)+(MgAl1)O31+(Al2Ge)O31

On the basis of the research findings of Batsanov S.S. in the early 1980s [31], the permittivity of dielectric materials significantly correlates with the ionicity (*f*i) of their internal chemical bonds. The specific relationship is expressed as Eq. (10):

(10) εr=n2−11−fi+1

where *n* is the optical frequency coefficient of the dielectric material. Based on the above formula and the calculated splitting results of chemical bonds, the ionicity of different chemical bonds in YMAG ceramics was determined. The specific results are shown in Fig. 4(c), revealing the trend *f*i(Mg/Al−O) > *f*i(Y−O) > *f*i(Al/Ge−O). According to Eq. (10), the Mg/Al−O bond significantly influences the permittivity of YMAG ceramics.

For crystalline materials, higher lattice energy (*U*) corresponds to greater structural stability and reduced anharmonic lattice vibrations. The magnitude of dielectric loss fundamentally depends on the strength of these anharmonic vibrations. Therefore, chemical bonds with higher lattice energy generally result in lower dielectric loss [32].

The variation in the lattice energy with the sintering temperature according to the formula is illustrated in Fig. 4(d) and shows a consistent trend with respect to the *Q×f* value. This confirms that a higher lattice energy corresponds to lower dielectric loss and a higher *Q×f* value. The lattice energies of various chemical bonds in samples sintered at different temperatures are presented in Fig. 4(e), with the *U*Y−O bond at 9174 kJ/mol, the *U*Mg/Al−O bond at 10808 kJ/mol, and the *U*Al/Ge−O bond at 35,135 kJ/mol. Consequently, the exceptionally high lattice energy of the Al/Ge−O bond plays a significant role in achieving a high-quality factor for YMAG ceramics.

*τ*f of microwave dielectric ceramics is influenced by two factors: the temperature coefficient of the permittivity (*τ*ε) and the coefficient of thermal expansion (*α*L) of the ceramic material. The relationship is expressed as Eq. (11):

(11) τf=−(τε2+αL)

The *α*L of YMAG@1600 °C was measured within the temperature range of 100–500 °C. The results, as illustrated in Fig. 4(f), show an *α*L value of 9.13 ppm/°C. Additionally, the *αL* of ceramic materials can be estimated by calculating the average thermal expansion coefficients of all chemical bonds within the material. These thermal expansion coefficients are influenced by the lattice energies of the bonds. Therefore, the *α*L value of the internal chemical bonds in YMAG ceramics is calculated using Eqs. (12)–(15):

(12) βmn=m(m+n)2n

(13) γmn=kZANCAUbΔAβmn

(14) αmn=−3.1685+0.8376γmn

(15) αL=∑Fmnαmn

where *k* = 1.38×10−23; *m* and *n* denote the number of elements between the bond formulas, respectively; *Z*A, *N*CA, and ΔA denote the effective valence electron number of A ion, the adjacent coordination number of A ion, and the period correction factor, respectively. The *α*L values of YMAG ceramic samples sintered at different temperatures, as derived from chemical bond theory, exhibit a consistent trend with the variation in *τ*f values. The difference between the calculated and measured *α*L values is less than 2 ppm/°C, and the trends align well, as shown in Fig. 4(f). However, *αL* is not the primary factor influencing the *τ*f values. Therefore, this study further analyzes the influence of different chemical bonds on *τ*f based on bond energy and valence of electrons.

The bond energy (*E*), also referred to as the chemical bond dissociation energy, represents the energy required to break a chemical bond into independent atoms. Thus, *E* serves as a critical physical parameter for assessing the thermal stability of materials. A higher *E* corresponds to greater thermal stability, and the *τ*f value of ceramics is closely related to their thermal stability [33,34]. Moreover, the bond valence (*V*ij) represents another critical parameter affecting the stability of crystal structures [35,36]. Figure 4(h) shows the *V*ij and *E* values of the sample. It is evident that *E*Al/Ge−O > *E*Mg/Al−O > *E*Y−O, indicating that the Al/Ge−O bond contributes more significantly to the *τ*f value. Additionally, *V*Al/Ge−O ≈ *V*Y−O > *V*Mg/Al−O, suggesting that the bond valences of Al/Ge−O and Y−O play crucial roles in determining the temperature stability of the material.

### 3.3 Dielectric properties in multiple frequency bands

Figure 5(a) shows the trend of the permittivity (*ε*) of the samples as a function of temperature (0–300 °C) at different frequencies (0.1–10 kHz). The *ε* gradually increases with increasing temperature. The temperature coefficient of the dielectric constant (*τ*ε) was calculated by selecting test values of 25 and 85 °C, and the *τ*ε values corresponding to frequencies of 0.1–10 kHz were 62.42, 39.03, and 39.12 ppm/°C, respectively. Consequently, the *τ*f values calculated via Eq. (11) are −40.34, −28.65, and −28.69 ppm/°C, respectively, which align well with the experimentally measured values. Notably, the *ε* decreases with increasing test frequency, and the *ε* (9.86) measured in the microwave frequency range (11.65 GHz) is significantly lower than that measured in the 0.1–10 kHz range. The contribution to *ε* is determined by a combination of electronic displacement polarization, ionic displacement polarization, dipole orientation polarization, and space charge polarization. Under different conditions, such as varying electric field frequencies, the contributions of these polarization mechanisms to *ε* differ. At low frequencies, electronic displacement polarization, ionic displacement polarization, and dipole orientation polarization dominate. However, in the microwave frequency range, as the frequency increases, the electric field changes more rapidly, and dipole orientation polarization cannot keep up with these changes, gradually diminishing its contribution to the *ε*. As a result, electronic and ionic displacement polarizations become the primary contributors, explaining why the *ε* measured in the microwave range is lower than that at lower frequencies [37].

Figure 5(b) shows the trend of dielectric loss as a function of temperature (0–300 °C) at different frequencies (0.1–10 kHz). The dielectric loss remains stable within a low range of 0–0.001, indicating minimal sensitivity to temperature changes. Additionally, the loss decreases with increasing test frequency, which is also related to the varying contributions of the polarization mechanisms at different frequencies. At high frequencies, the large molecular inertia prevents dipole orientation polarization from following rapid changes in the external electric field, reducing its contribution to dielectric loss. Therefore, the loss of the sample decreases as the frequency increases [38].

Since the natural frequencies of lattice vibrations typically fall within the mid- to far-infrared frequency range, vibrational spectroscopy serves as an effective tool for investigating the lattice vibration characteristics of microwave dielectric ceramics. To reveal the intrinsic dielectric properties of YMAG ceramics, far-infrared reflectance spectra were analyzed using the Lorentz oscillator model, with an appropriate number of resonant modes selected for fitting. The results are presented in Fig. 6 and Table 3. As shown in Fig. 6(a), the fitted spectrum (solid line) aligns well with the experimental spectrum (circles), and the corresponding phonon parameters and resonant modes are listed in Table 3. The optical frequency permittivity (ε*∞) of the YMAG@1600 °C ceramics is 2.16, whereas the intrinsic permittivity is 5.98. The *ε*∞ represents the contribution of electronic displacement polarization to *ε*r. In the low-frequency range of 100–800 cm−1, the infrared-active vibrational modes contribute 63.9% to the permittivity, indicating that the primary polarization mechanism in the sample originates from ionic polarization, with limited contributions from electronic polarization [39]. Compared with the experimentally measured value of 9.86 (at 11.65 GHz), the permittivity derived from far-infrared spectroscopy is slightly lower, which may be attributed to imperfections in sample preparation [40]. Extrapolating the far-infrared imaginary part fitting data to the microwave frequency range yields a theoretical *Q×f* value of 94,311 GHz (at 9.86 GHz), which is slightly greater than the experimentally measured value of 89,000 GHz. This suggests that the sample preparation process has a minor effect on the dielectric loss, and the upper limit of the *Q×f* value is primarily determined by intrinsic factors.

### 3.4 Performance regulation and device simulation

For most microwave dielectric ceramics, the *τ*f value is not close to zero, and it is necessary to adjust |*τ*f| to a smaller value (|*τ*f| f of the sample to near zero, TiO2 (+460 ppm/°C) was selected as an additive to form a new composite ceramic with YMAG [45]. Figure 7(a) shows the XRD patterns of the (1−*x*)YMAG–*x*TiO2 (*x* = 0–0.1) ceramic samples sintered at the optimal temperature. As the amount of TiO2 additive (*x*) increases, new diffraction peaks begin to appear in the ceramic samples, and their intensity gradually increases. Comparison with the database reveals that the diffraction peaks match the standard cards of Y3Al5O12 (PDF#01-082-0575), Y2Ti2O7 (PDF#01-071-2065), and TiO2 (PDF#01-078-1510). This indicates that the samples with added TiO2 did not form a solid solution. Furthermore, TiO2 reacted with YMAG to form the Y2Ti2O7 secondary phase.

*
Fig. 7(1−x*)YMAG–*x*TiO2 (*x* = 0–0.1) ceramics sintered at optimum temperature: (a) XRD patterns; (b) microwave dielectric properties; (c–j) SEM images and EDS images of fracture surface of *x* = 0.075 sample.

Figure 7(b) shows the microwave dielectric properties of the (1−*x*)YMAG–*x*TiO2 (*x* = 0–0.1) ceramics. According to the logarithmic mixture rule for permittivity, ln*ε* = *x*1ln*ε*1 + *x*2ln*ε*2 [46], the permittivity of (1−*x*)YMAG–*x*TiO2 ceramics increases from 9.86 to 16.19 as the TiO2 content (*x*) increases, which is consistent with the high permittivity of TiO2. Additionally, the *Q×f* value of the (1−*x*)YMAG–*x*TiO2 ceramics decreases significantly with increasing *x*, dropping from ~89,000 to ~35,000 GHz. This phenomenon may be attributed to the introduction of TiO2, which led to the formation of the Y2Ti2O7 secondary phase. The introduction of additives and the formation of the impurity phase result in a low *Q×f* value (TiO2 ~23,000 GHz, Y2Ti2O7 ~9000 GHz) [47,48]. Combined with the results of the XRD analysis, the TiO2 phase is present in different amounts, and the diffraction peak of Y2Ti2O7 increases with increasing addition amount *x*. An increase in the Y2Ti2O7 impurity phase content corresponds to a decrease in the *Q×f* value, indicating that the introduced TiO2 and the generated Y2Ti2O7 impurity phase are the factors that cause deterioration of sample performance. Although the addition of other phases can partially fill the pores of the sample and limit the growth of the grains, it may lead to an increase in the number of grain boundaries of the sample, resulting in an increase in the internal stress of the lattice distortion, which further leads to an increase in loss [49]. According to the mixture composition rule *τ*f = *x*1*τ*f1 + *x*2*τ*f2 to calculate the ideal case where the constituent phases are only Y3MgAl3GeO12 and TiO2, (1−*x*)YMAG–*x*TiO2 (*x* = 0.075) can obtain a near-zero *τ*f value. In the actual test results, the ceramic sample obtained a near-zero *τ*f value at *x* = 0.08. The reason for this phenomenon is that the actual sample also has a Y2Ti2O7 impurity phase (−30 ppm/°C) [48], which increases the amount of TiO2 added. In summary, the *τ*f value of YMAG ceramics was successfully adjusted to a near-zero level by adding TiO2, and a high *Q×f* value (~43,000 GHz) was maintained in the case of a high-loss impurity phase. Figures 7(c)–7(j) show the SEM images of the fracture surface of the (1−*x*)YMAG–*x*TiO2 (*x* = 0.075) sample and the EDS surface scanning results. The enrichment of Ti can be observed in the surface scanning results, and it is complementary to the distribution of Mg, Al, and Ge. The distribution of Y and O is more uniform, indicating that there may be a second phase in the sample. According to the results of the XRD analysis, Y and O are distributed in a part of the Ti enrichment area, and the corresponding grains are likely to be Y2Ti2O7. In addition, a small part of the Ti enrichment area Y is vacant, and the corresponding grains are likely to be TiO2. However, owing to the small content of TiO2, the observed phenomenon is not very obvious.

In the field of wireless communication, antennas are critical wireless device components. With the rapid development of 5G communication systems, antenna technology has garnered significant attention. Dielectric resonator antennas (DRAs) offer notable advantages, including versatile shapes adaptable to various scenarios, compact sizes, lightweight designs conducive to portable devices, high operational bandwidths for improved data transmission and communication quality, and ease of excitation, which reduces design complexity and energy consumption. These features make DRAs highly competitive and promising for 5G and future wireless communication systems [44,50]. Given the excellent microwave dielectric properties of (1−*x*)YMAG–*x*TiO2 (*x* = 0.075) ceramics, a rectangular dielectric resonator was fabricated using this material. Figures 8(a) and 8(b) show the top and side views of the designed device, respectively, with specific dimensional parameters labeled. Figures 8(c) and 8(d) show the three-dimensional simulated radiation patterns of the DRA at 10.21 GHz for co-polarization and cross-polarization, respectively. The simulation results indicate that the center frequency of the return loss is 10.21 GHz, meeting the requirements for X-band high-frequency applications. The device provides an impedance bandwidth of 329 MHz (10.043–10.372 GHz), with a return loss reaching –40 dB. Additionally, the voltage standing wave ratio (VSWR) is a critical parameter; a higher VSWR indicates stronger reflection and poorer impedance matching. In mobile communication systems, a VSWR of 1 represents an ideal state, but in practice, owing to inherent reflections, the value typically exceeds 1. Generally, a VSWR below 1.5 is needed. The designed DRA achieves VSWR of 1.02 at 10.21 GHz, demonstrating excellent impedance matching and effectively enhancing the transmission power of the antenna, as shown in Fig. 8(e) [51,52]. The simulated gain curve and radiation efficiency at 10.21 GHz demonstrate that the designed antenna achieves a radiation efficiency exceeding 90% within the bandwidth range of 10.043–10.372 GHz. Owing to the low-loss characteristics of the ceramic dielectric, the antenna exhibits a relatively high gain (3.2–4.1 dBi) and reaches its maximum gain within the operational bandwidth, as shown in Fig. 8(f). These results fully confirm the feasibility of (1−*x*)YMAG–*x*TiO2 (*x* = 0.075) microwave dielectric ceramics for X-band dielectric resonator antenna applications.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, YMAG ceramics were synthesized via the solid-state reaction method. XRD analysis confirmed the pure phase nature of the samples, and Rietveld refinement of the XRD results determined the atomic occupancy. The samples achieved a high *Q×f* value (~89,000 GHz) while maintaining a low permittivity (*ε*r = 9.86). Further investigations into the mechanisms influencing the microwave dielectric properties were conducted via Raman spectroscopy, packing fraction analysis, the Clausius–Mossotti equation, P–V–L theory, and bond valence theory. The results revealed that a smaller FWHM and higher packing fractions correspond to lower dielectric loss. The Y–O bond significantly influences the permittivity, whereas the high lattice energy of the Al/Ge–O bond plays a crucial role in achieving a high-quality factor. The temperature-dependent dielectric spectra demonstrated that the permittivity increases with temperature, whereas the loss remains stable within a low range of 0–0.001. Additionally, the permittivity decreases, and the loss diminishes with increasing test frequency. The far-infrared spectroscopy fitting results indicate that the primary polarization mechanism originates from ionic polarization, with limited contributions from electronic polarization. The theoretical *Q×f* value of 94,000 GHz slightly exceeds the experimental value, suggesting high sample preparation precision and minimal extrinsic losses. Terahertz time-domain spectroscopy confirmed the material's low intrinsic dielectric loss and stable dielectric properties at high frequencies. By incorporating TiO2 as a positive *τ*f compensator, the *τ*f value was successfully adjusted from −40 ppm/°C to |*τ*f| 2 addition of 7.5 wt% while maintaining a high *Q×f* value (~43,000 GHz). Furthermore, a rectangular dielectric resonator antenna (DRA) designed with the optimized YMAG–TiO2 composite material exhibited excellent impedance matching (VSWR = 1.02) and high radiation efficiency (> 90%) in the X-band (10.21 GHz). In summary, this ceramic demonstrates significant potential for 5G/6G mobile communication applications, with its exceptional performance in X-band simulations highlighting its immense value for satellite and terrestrial microwave communication systems.
