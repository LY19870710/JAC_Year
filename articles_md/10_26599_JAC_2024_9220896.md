## 1 Introduction

There is a growing demand for ceramic materials with highly elevated hardnesses to meet the harsh application conditions of various industrial sectors. High-entropy carbide (HEC) ceramics, characterized by strong covalent bonds and high valence electron concentrations, have been demonstrated to exhibit exceptional hardness (18–26 GPa) [1]. HEC ceramics are highly promising for use as novel wear-resistant materials for cutting tools and other demanding applications [2−6]. However, achieving dense and single-phase HEC ceramics necessitates high sintering temperatures exceeding 2000 °C [7−9]. Researchers have explored the use of low-melting-point metals as sintering additives to lower the densification temperature for HEC ceramics. For instance, (Hf–Ta–Ti–Nb–V)C and (Ti–V–Nb–Ta–W)C ceramics can be densified at approximately 1400 °C with the addition of 19.2 vol% Co and 12 vol% Ni, respectively [10,11]. Unfortunately, this approach incurs at the cost of reduced hardness [12]. For instance, the reported hardness values for (Hf–Ta–Ti–Nb–V)C-19.2 vol% Co and (Ti–V–Nb–Ta–W)C-12 vol% Ni bulks are only 14.0 and 14.3 GPa, respectively [10,11]. The Vickers hardness of the (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C-10 vol% Co composites decreases by 11.9% (22.07 GPa) compared to that of the (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C bulk materials (25.06 GPa) [12]. Based on Refs. [10−12], the use of low-melting-point metals as sintering aids for low-temperature densification of high-entropy ceramics is achieved at the expense of sacrificing the hardness performance of high-entropy ceramics due to the presence of residual metals within the ceramic matrix. Therefore, achieving low-temperature densification while maintaining the inherent high hardness of HEC ceramics remains a challenge.

Additionally, the solid-solution effect of metals is well known to lower the densification temperature of ceramics [13,14]. Moreover, the solid-solution approach could also avoid the residual metal phase present in the HEC matrix, aiming to maintain a high hardness [14]. Taking advantage of the solid-solution effect of Zr metal, Wang *et* *al*. [13] successfully fabricated ZrC ceramics with an impressive relative density of 98.4% using hot pressing at 1900 °C with 8.94 wt% Zr metal addition, and the density obtained was much greater than the 83% relative density achieved for dopant-free ZrC ceramics sintered even at 2000 °C. Moreover, despite the addition of Zr metal, the measured hardness (17.8 GPa) remains comparable to that of pure ZrC (17.9 GPa) [15]. As a high-melting-point Group VI transition metal, chromium (Cr) exhibits high solid solubility in HEC structures and can potentially be incorporated into HEC ceramics with rock-salt structures [16−18]. Moreover, compared to IVB and VB transition metals, Cr has a smaller atomic size and greater electronegativity, which can potentially contribute to hardness enhancement in transition metal carbides [19−22]. For example, Wang *et* *al*. [19] fabricated dense (Cr,Zr,Nb,Hf,Ta)C ceramics by using spark plasma sintering (SPS) at 2300 °C. This study revealed that the liquid phase of Cr3C2 facilitates the densification process during sintering. Moreover, the solubility of Cr in (Cr,Zr,Nb,Hf,Ta)C was found to be 2.5 at%. Additionally, due to the intensified lattice distortions caused by the incorporation of Cr, the nanohardness of the (Cr,Zr,Nb,Hf,Ta)C ceramics increases from 36.1 to 41.2 GPa, representing a 14% improvement. Additionally, Su *et* *al*. [23] utilized oxide powders and carbon black to prepare (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Cr)C high-entropy powders through carbothermal reduction. Subsequently, dense (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Cr)C ceramics were successfully fabricated via pressureless sintering at temperatures ranging from 1800 to 2000 °C. As the Cr content increased from 3.04 to 20 at%, the hardness of the (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Cr)C ceramics significantly improved due to solid solution strengthening and lattice distortion effects, increasing from 15.5 to 20.6 GPa. Additionally, the introduction of Cr has also been reported to enhance the flexural strength and oxidation resistance of HEC ceramics [18,24,25]. However, in previous studies, Cr carbides or oxides were employed as a means to introduce Cr into HECs. Obtaining dense HEC ceramics still requires high sintering temperatures (> 1800 °C). Consequently, a high sintering temperature leads to significant grain coarsening in ceramics, which subsequently has an adverse effect on their mechanical properties and performance.

To achieve low-temperature densification of (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C ceramics while maintaining high hardness, Cr metal was used as a sintering additive to prepare (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C ceramics by using SPS at 1600 °C in this study. Since the solubility limit of Cr in (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C has not yet been publicly reported in the literature, the amount of Cr metal added was then set to 2.5, 5, and 10 vol%. In this study, the mechanisms of densification and hardness enhancement, phase composition, and microstructure of HEC ceramics with Cr metal addition were investigated and discussed. Moreover, a comprehensive comparative analysis was conducted with Cr-free (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C ceramics.

## 2 Experimental

The raw oxide powders of TiO2, ZrO2, Nb2O5, Ta2O5, and MoO3 (purity > 99.8%, 0.6–2 μm; Shanghai Xiangtian Nano Materials Co., Ltd., China) and graphite (1 μm; Shanghai Yifan Graphite Co., Ltd., China) were batched to prepare (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C powders according to carbothermal reduction. The precursor was homogenized by ball milling for 24 h with Si3N4 balls in ethanol and a ball-to-powder ratio (BPR) of 5 : 1. The ball milling rotation speed was set at 200 r/min. Finally, 5 g of (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C-Cr powders was loaded into a graphite die (diameter: 20 mm) and then sintered by using SPS (HPD-10-FL, FCT Systeme GmbH, Germany) under vacuum (Table 1 lists the sample designations based on Cr content and sintering conditions.

### 3.1 Phase composition and densification behavior

Figure 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of the starting (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C powders derived from the carbothermal reduction reaction, where a predominant phase peak corresponding to the (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C phase with a face-centered cubic lattice is evident [26]. Apart from the HEC phase, a series of weak reflections related to another simple carbide phase are also detected, suggesting a low degree of solid solubility of the synthesized raw high-entropy powders. For Cr-free HEC powders, the HEC0Cr-16 sample consists of two phases after sintering at 1600 °C, and the HEC0Cr-20 sample displays a single-phase structure, indicating that a much higher processing temperature of 2000 °C is required for the formation of the pure (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C phase. In contrast, HEC samples with 2.5 vol% (HEC2.5Cr-16 sample) and 5 vol% Cr addition (HEC5Cr-16 sample), which are both sintered at 1600 °C, exhibit a single phase. These XRD data demonstrate that the solid solubility of high-entropy carbides is possibly enhanced due to the addition of Cr metal. For instance, no other phases are detected in the XRD patterns of these two Cr-containing HEC samples within the resolution limit of the instrument (Fig. 1), which indicates that Cr is completely incorporated into the crystal structure of the HEC phase after sintering at 1600 °C. This can possibly imply that the HEC ceramics, which originally consisted of (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C, transform into (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo,Cr)C due to the addition of Cr metal. With increasing Cr content from 2.5 to 5 vol%, the XRD peaks gradually broadened (Fig. 1(a)), suggesting potential inhomogeneity in the elemental distribution within the HEC ceramic matrix [27,28]. Additionally, a new peak corresponding to the (Cr,Mo)C phase appears in the HEC10Cr-16 sample, along with the high-entropy carbide phase. Due to the similar radii of the Cr and Mo atoms and the limited Cr solubility, the excess Cr forms its own separate (Cr,Mo)C phase at higher Cr contents. Furthermore, the formation of the (Cr,Mo)C phase may also be influenced by the heat of formation, which is a critical factor in determining the stability of carbides. In the high-entropy system investigated in this study, both molybdenum carbide and chromium carbide exhibit similar heats of formation, and their absolute values are the lowest among the six carbides considered. This suggests that in the presence of excess chromium, the formation of the (Cr,Mo)C phase becomes more favorable during the sintering process [20,29].

The introduction of Cr may result in lattice distortion of high-entropy carbides, leading to the concentration of local stress within the crystals and the formation of lattice strain [30]. The Williamson–Hall method was then employed to calculate the lattice strain in the samples sintered at 1600 °C [31], as shown in Fig. 2. The lattice strains of the HEC0Cr-16, HEC2.5Cr-16, HEC5Cr-16, and HEC10Cr-16 samples are 3.74×10−3, 2.36×10−3, 4.45×10−3, and 4.42×10−3, respectively. The HEC2.5Cr-16 sample has a significantly lower lattice strain than the HEC0Cr-16 sample. Generally, high-entropy ceramics exhibit severe lattice distortion, resulting in higher lattice strain and relatively broad XRD peaks. However, the introduction of an appropriate amount of Cr increases the number of carbon vacancies within high-entropy carbide ceramics, leading to lattice relaxation and the release of lattice distortion, thereby reducing lattice strain [28]. Therefore, this phenomenon also explains the narrower XRD peaks observed in the HEC2.5Cr-16 sample than in the HEC0Cr-16 sample. For the HEC5Cr-16 and HEC10Cr-16 samples, the lattice strain significantly increased. This is due to the introduction of excessive Cr, which results in severe lattice distortion and the emergence of new strain. This also leads to microstructural heterogeneity, which, in turn, causes the broadening of the XRD peaks in the samples.

Following Rietveld refinement of the XRD data for the HEC0Cr-16, HEC0Cr-20, and HEC5Cr-16 samples, the lattice parameters are determined to be 4.4336, 4.4558, and 4.4347 Å, respectively, as illustrated in Figs. 1(b)–1(d). The data show that the HEC5Cr-16 sample exhibits a relatively larger lattice parameter than the HEC0Cr-16 sample, suggesting a greater degree of solid solution. The lattice parameter results further confirm the crucial role of Cr in facilitating solid solution formation. The HEC0Cr-20 sample displays the largest lattice parameter among the three samples, which can be attributed to the influence of the elevated sintering temperature on the solid solution process. To investigate the solid solution temperature and behavior of Cr metal, HEC ceramic samples with 10 vol% Cr metal were prepared and sintered at temperatures ranging from 1200 to 1600 °C. The corresponding XRD patterns and refined lattice parameters of the HEC ceramic samples are shown in Figs. 1(e) and 1(f), respectively. The results of the XRD data show that the phase compositions of the samples sintered at 1200–1300 °C consist of a major phase of high-entropy carbide, a weak phase of metal carbide, and a Cr7C3 phase. The presence of the Cr7C3 phase indicates that Cr is not able to be completely dissolved into the lattice of the HEC ceramic matrix below 1300 °C. Additionally, Cr reacts with high-entropy carbides, leading to the formation of the Cr7C3 phase. Furthermore, the lattice parameter data of the HEC samples sintered at 1200–1300 °C show minimal changes, indicating that Cr metal does not undergo a significant solid solution reaction below 1300 °C. After sintering at 1400 °C, the diffraction peaks of the Cr7C3 phase become noticeably weak, and the lattice parameters of the HEC sample exhibit significant growth. This observation indicates the occurrence of a pronounced solid solution process of Cr at ~1400 °C. When the sintering temperature was 1500 °C, the Cr7C3 phase disappeared, and the (Cr,Mo)C phase began to appear. The lattice parameters also further increase. This suggests that the solid solution process of Cr into the HEC ceramic matrix is completed at ~1500 °C. Moreover, the (Cr,Mo)C phase is not detected in the HEC2.5Cr-16 and HEC5Cr-16 samples (Fig. 1(a)), indicating that the (Cr,Mo)C phase is formed due to an excess of Cr metal. Finally, the weak metal carbide phase disappears, and it is dissolved into the high-entropy carbides after sintering at 1600 °C. Moreover, the diffraction peaks of the (Cr,Mo)C phase significantly intensify, indicating a potential decrease in the solubility of Cr in the (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C phase at a higher sintering temperature of 1600 °C.

Table 1 presents the relative density of HEC ceramic samples with different Cr contents and sintering conditions. As anticipated, the relative density of the Cr-free sample after sintering at 1600 °C was only 49.5%, which is much lower than that of the sample sintered at 2000 °C (98.3%). In sharp contrast, the relative densities of the HEC2.5Cr-16, HEC5Cr-16, and HEC10Cr-16 samples were 96.4%, 98.4%, and 99.7%, respectively. The density data reveal a clear trend of increasing relative density with increasing Cr content. Additionally, the results demonstrate that Cr metal introduced as a sintering aid allows for the fabrication of (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C ceramics with relative densities comparable to those processed through high-temperature solid-phase sintering but at a much lower temperature (1600 °C).

Figure 3 compares the sintering curves of the HEC0Cr-20 and HEC5Cr-16 samples. The HEC0Cr-20 sample exhibited classic solid-phase sintering behavior, with significant shrinkage occurring between ~1430 and ~2000 °C (Fig. 3(a)). In comparison, the HEC5Cr-16 sample displays a markedly different shrinkage profile, showing gradual shrinkage initiation at ~900 °C and complete densification at temperatures reaching ~1600 °C (Fig. 3(b)). This further confirms the substantial benefit of the use of Cr metal as a sintering aid for (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C ceramics. The addition of Cr dramatically lowers the initial shrinkage temperature, accelerating densification, and enabling significantly lower processing temperatures.

On the basis of the abovementioned results, it can be concluded that the combination of Cr addition and solid-solution formation effectively reduced the sintering temperature from 2000 to 1600 °C. During the sintering process, Cr dissolves into the HEC ceramic matrix, transforming it into a substoichiometric (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo,Cr)C1−x* phase. It is hypothesized that the presence of substoichiometric (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo,Cr)C1−*x* could improve densification, as demonstrated in this study [32]. In addition, the formation of additional carbon vacancies enhances mass transport through solid-state diffusion, thus facilitating the densification process. Moreover, the melting point of transition metal carbides can be used to assess the bond strength of transition metal carbides [20]. Thus, the M‒C bond strength of Group VI metal carbides is relatively weaker than that of Groups IV and V metal carbides [20]. The incorporation of Cr (Group VI) in the (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C crystal lattice might weaken the covalent bonding of the (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)‒C bonding, thereby reducing the melting point of the high-entropy carbides and enhancing sinterability.

### 3.2 Microstructure and Vickers hardness

Figure 4 displays the metallographically polished surfaces of the sintered HEC ceramic samples. The Cr-free sample (HEC0Cr-16 sample) exhibits a very porous microstructure (Fig. 4(a)), indicating that achieving full densification at a low temperature of 1600 °C is very challenging for pure (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C powders. In contrast, the HEC sample sintered at 2000 °C displays a dense microstructure with few grain boundary pores (as indicated by the white arrow in Fig. 4(b)), which is consistent with Refs. [26,33]. The HEC samples with 2.5 vol% Cr (HEC2.5Cr-16 sample) (Fig. 5(a)) exhibit a much denser microstructure, indicating a notable improvement in sinterability due to the addition of Cr. With increasing Cr metal content, no pores are observed in the HEC5Cr-16 and HEC10Cr-16 samples (Figs. 5(b) and 5(c)), which agrees well with the relative density data (Table 1). These results confirm that the addition of 5 vol% Cr allows for full densification of HEC ceramics at 1600 °C.

Figures 4 and 5 also present the EDS mappings of the sintered HEC ceramic samples. The elements in the HEC0Cr-20 sample are highly homogeneous. For Cr-containing samples sintered at 1600 °C (HEC2.5Cr-16 and HEC5Cr-16 samples), all transition metal elements are uniformly distributed, except for Zr. The segregation of Zr is probably attributed to the relatively low sintering temperature [12,34]. Conversely, Zr-rich regions are absent in HEC0Cr-20 sample due to the much higher sintering temperature of 2000 °C. Furthermore, no Cr aggregation is observed in HEC2.5Cr-16 or HEC5Cr-16 samples, suggesting complete Cr solubility in the HEC matrix. This EDS element mapping confirms Cr incorporation into the HEC ceramic matrix lattice during sintering and supports the role of Cr in expanding the unit cell observed in HEC5Cr-16 sample [19]. However, in the HEC10Cr-16 sample, numerous Cr–Mo-rich areas are observed, corresponding to the (Cr,Mo)C phase, as identified by XRD (Fig. 1). This suggests that 10 vol% Cr exceeds the solid solution limit in the (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C–Cr system, leading to the formation of a separate (Cr,Mo)C phase.

TEM images and the corresponding EDS mappings of the HEC5Cr-16 sample are shown in Fig. 6(a). The EDS mapping results reveal a homogeneous distribution of all the elements, except Zr at the nanoscale, in the HEC5Cr-16 sample. The segregation of Zr has been explained previously. Additionally, no significant Cr enrichment is observed, suggesting complete Cr dissolution within the (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C grains, supporting our earlier discussion. The electron diffraction pattern (G1) matches the face-centered cubic (fcc) structure, which is consistent with the XRD results (Fig. 1). Further TEM-EDS analysis (Fig. 4(b)) on the triple-grain junction of the red-boxed region in Fig. 6(a) reveals slight Cr enrichment at the grain boundaries (white arrow). This could be attributed to the limited solid solubility of Cr in the HEC phase [35,36]. The HRTEM image in Fig. 6(c) further confirms the fcc rock-salt structure of the HC5C-16 sample. Additionally, the lattice spacings of the [111] planes in different high-entropy grains vary slightly, suggesting a subtle difference in the degree of solid solution across these HEC grains.

The average grain sizes of the HEC0Cr-20, HEC2.5Cr-16, HEC5Cr-16, and HEC10Cr-16 samples are 3.03±0.34, 0.17±0.02, 0.17±0.01, and 0.28±0.07 μm, respectively. The microstructure of the solid-sintered bulk materials (HEC0Cr-20 sample) shows noticeable coarsening (3.03±0.34 μm) due to high-temperature sintering at 2000 °C. In contrast, the Cr-containing HEC samples exhibit much finer grain microstructures. Interestingly, increasing the Cr content from 2.5 to 5 vol% does not significantly affect the grain size, while 10 vol% Cr (HEC10Cr-16 sample) results in slightly increased grain growth while achieving even more complete densification. These results confirm the obvious advantage of the Cr addition approach employed in this study over traditional high-temperature solid-phase sintering. Traditional low-temperature liquid-phase sintering often results in significant grain coarsening for HEC ceramics, as confirmed by reports from the references (Table 2). Herein, the HEC5Cr-16 sample prepared at 1600 °C exhibited a remarkably smaller grain size (0.17 μm) than the Co-added (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C ceramics fabricated through SPS at the same temperature (1.51 μm) [33], representing substantial inhibition of grain growth. Luo et* *al*. [12,26] prepared dense (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C-10 vol% Co ceramics by starting HEC powders with an initial particle size of 197 nm and SPS at 1550 °C. It has been reported that the grain size of the (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C phase is ~1.3 μm. The significantly reduced grain growth in our Cr-containing HEC ceramics observed in this study can be attributed to two key factors. First, this may be due to severe lattice distortion in high-entropy carbides. Lattice distortion creates a sluggish diffusion environment, effectively inhibiting grain growth [28]. This effect may even outweigh the potential grain growth promotion from carbon vacancies. Second, this may also be attributed to the slight Cr enrichment at the grain boundaries (Fig. 4(b)). This Cr enrichment acts as a diffusion path during sintering while simultaneously inhibiting grain growth [19,37]. These two combined factors possibly contributed to the ultrafine grain sizes in the Cr-treated HEC ceramics in this study. In addition, Fig. 7(e) shows the EDS mappings of the etched surface of HEC10Cr-16 sample. Similar to Fig. 5(c), the Cr element exhibits a uniform distribution outside the Mo-rich areas.

The Vickers hardness values of the HEC samples are summarized in Table 2. As expected, the pure (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C ceramics (HEC0Cr-20 sample) sintered at 2000 °C display the lowest hardness (23.57±0.31 GPa) due to their relatively coarser grain size. The HEC2.5Cr-16 sample also exhibited a relatively low hardness (21.62±3.01 GPa), mainly due to its relatively low density (96.4%). In contrast, the HEC5Cr-16 sample exhibited the highest hardness (28.16±1.43 GPa) among all of the samples in this study, probably due to the ultrafine-grained microstructure resulting in hardness enhancement. Furthermore, Cr incorporation during sintering plays a dual role in strengthening. First, the smaller atomic size of Cr (166 pm) compared to that of other metals in the HEC matrix (e.g., Ti: 176 pm, Zr: 206 pm, Nb: 198 pm; Ta: 200 pm; Mo: 190 pm) introduces significant lattice distortion, leading to solid-solution strengthening and increased hardness [19,38]. It is generally believed that high-entropy ceramic materials exhibit significant lattice distortions due to differences in their atomic radii and chemical bonding strengths. The greater the lattice distortions are, the stronger the pinning effects on dislocations caused by inner stress, resulting in higher hardness values [39−41]. Previous analysis of the lattice parameters of the HEC5Cr-16 and HEC10Cr-16 samples also confirmed that the introduction of Cr led to an increase in the lattice parameters of the HEC5Cr-16 and HEC10Cr-16 samples, thereby causing more severe lattice distortions and thus significantly enhancing the hardness. Second, the presence of 6-metal carbides generally contributes to a higher hardness than that of 5-metal carbides. The addition of Cr increases random interactions between different elements, increasing the Peierls stress for dislocation slip and further enhancing the hardness [35]. However, exceeding the Cr solubility limit in the HEC10Cr-16 sample leads to the formation of a separate (Cr,Mo)C phase along with larger grains, resulting in a slightly lower hardness (25.69±0.40 GPa) than that of HEC5Cr-16 sample. Despite this slight reduction, the HEC10Cr-16 sample still retains a higher hardness than the HEC0Cr-20 sample. The present study demonstrates that incorporating 5 vol% Cr metal as a sintering aid allows for the fabrication of (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C ceramics with both high hardness (28.16±1.43 GPa) and fine-grained microstructure at a significantly lower temperature (1600 °C) than conventional solid-phase sintering at 2000 °C (23.57±0.31 GPa). This represents a 19.5% increase in hardness while simultaneously reducing processing costs and energy consumption. The proposed Cr addition approach in this study offers an innovative and efficient route to achieve high-performance (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C ceramics.

## 4 Conclusions

A series of (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C ceramics with varying Cr contents ranging from 2.5 to 10 vol% were prepared by spark plasma sintering at 1600 °C. The resulting microstructure and hardness were compared with those of pure (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C ceramics sintered at 2000 °C. The introduction of 5 vol% Cr metal as a sintering aid results in highly densified ceramics with a high relative density (98.4%) and an ultrafine-grained microstructure (0.17 μm). This low-temperature densification is attributed to Cr’s solid-solution effect within the matrix and the increased carbon vacancies generated during the sintering process. The grain size of the HEC ceramics with 5 vol% Cr metal addition is significantly smaller than that of the pure (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C ceramics sintered at 2000 °C (3.03 μm) and those prepared through traditional low-temperature liquid-phase sintering (1.3–1.5 μm). In addition, the introduction of Cr metal significantly increased the hardness, and the sample with 5 vol% Cr exhibited a peak value of 28.16 GPa, which was mainly attributed to fine-grain strengthening and solid-solution strengthening mechanisms. In conclusion, the incorporation of Cr metal as a sintering aid has been demonstrated to be a highly effective strategy for significantly enhancing both the densification and hardness of (Ti,Zr,Nb,Ta,Mo)C ceramics, providing great potential for the development of high-performance high-entropy carbide materials in the near future.
