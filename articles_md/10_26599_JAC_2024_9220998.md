## 1 Introduction

With the rapid development of communication technology, electronic devices and information systems have significantly increased convenience for society by enabling high-speed data transmission, energy harvesting, and wireless power transfer [1−5]. However, the widespread use of electromagnetic technology has introduced serious concerns, including potential threats to the performance of sensitive electronic equipment, adverse effects on human health, risks to information security, and environmental impacts [6−8]. To mitigate these challenges, various measures have been implemented, such as the development of electromagnetic wave absorbing materials, the establishment of regulations and standards, and the monitoring of electromagnetic pollution [9−11]. Among these approaches, electromagnetic wave absorbing materials have gained particular attention because of their capacity to efficiently convert electromagnetic energy into thermal energy, thus providing an effective solution for reducing electromagnetic interference [12,13]. As a result, these materials have become a focal point of research in recent years.

The development of advanced materials with superior microwave absorption properties has become a critical area of research because of the growing demand for effective electromagnetic interference shielding in various industrial and military applications [14,15]. Among the materials studied, ceramics have emerged as a promising class of microwave absorbers because of their unique structural characteristics, high-temperature stability and cost-effective production process [16−20]. Ceramics are particularly known for their dielectric loss properties, making the design of high dielectric-loss microwave materials a key focus in this field. For example, Ding *et al.* [21] fabricated TiC/SiBCN ceramics by utilizing Ti3C2T*x* MXene and a meltable hyperbranched polyborosilazane, achieving exceptional microwave absorption performance with a broad effective absorption bandwidth and a minimum reflection loss value (RLmin) of −45.44 dB at 10.93 GHz. Zhang *et al.* [22] demonstrated that SiC-shaped memory epoxy resin (SSME) composites achieved excellent microwave absorption, with RLmin of −62.02 dB and an effective absorption bandwidth of 7.7 GHz at a 20 wt% SiC content. Wang *et al.* [23] reported ultralight SiC/Si3N4 composite aerogels with outstanding microwave absorption, achieving a reflection loss of −48.6 dB at a thickness of just 2 mm. In addition to these studies, other ceramics, such as SiC [24−26], TiO2 [27,28], Si3N4 [29], polymer-derived SiOC [30,31], SiCuCN [19], and SiBCN [32−34], also exhibit impressive microwave absorbing properties. However, achieving optimal performance via single-component ceramics remains challenging. Consequently, the current research trend involves introducing secondary phases or designing multiphase structures to leverage synergistic effects, thereby increasing the overall microwave absorption performance.

High-entropy ceramics (HECs) present a novel approach to achieve exceptional absorbing properties, attributed to their highly tunable composition and the “cocktail effect” [35]. Since their inception, HECs have attracted considerable attention, driving numerous advanced studies focused on enhancing their absorption performance. Qiao *et al.* [36] synthesized two types of high-entropy MXenes, (Mo0.25Cr0.25Ti0.25V0.25)3C2T*x* and (Mo0.2Cr0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2V0.2)4C3T*x*. (Mo0.25Cr0.25Ti0.25V0.25)3C2T*x* exhibited RLmin of −45.0 dB with an optimal matching thickness of 1.52 mm. Similarly, (Mo0.2Cr0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2V0.2)4C3T*x* achieved RLmin of −52.8 dB with a thickness of 1.58 mm. Zhang *et al.* [37] explored the microwave absorbing properties of the high-entropy transition metal carbide (Zr,Hf,Nb,Ta)C. Owing to the synergistic effects of dielectric loss, the (Zr,Hf,Nb,Ta)C ceramic exhibited outstanding microwave absorbing performance, with RLmin of −41.7 dB at a thickness of 2.11 mm. In our previous study [17], we reported the microwave absorbing properties of single-phase high-entropy ceramic (Hf0.25Zr0.25Nb0.25Ta0.25)C, which crystallizes in a rock‒salt structure. The material demonstrated outstanding microwave attenuation, with RLmin of −64.38 dB at 16 GHz and a thickness of 4.43 mm. While these results highlight the superior microwave absorption capabilities of (Hf0.25Zr0.25Nb0.25Ta0.25)C, challenges remain. Notably, the high synthesis temperature and the unclear mechanisms governing its dielectric loss limit further optimization. Future research should focus on lowering the fabrication temperature and elucidating the underlying loss mechanisms to unlock the full potential of high-entropy ceramics for advanced electromagnetic wave absorption applications.

In this study, novel (Hf0.25Zr0.25Ta0.25Nb0.25)C–SiC biphasic ceramic powders were successfully synthesized via a single-source-precursor route at a low temperature. The microstructures, dielectric properties, and microwave absorption properties of the biphasic ceramic powders were systematically investigated. The role of the SiC phase in modulating the microwave absorption behavior was analyzed in detail, and the underlying absorption mechanisms were comprehensively explored. Furthermore, the adoption of the single-source-precursor route offers a scalable, cost-effective method for large-scale production, enhancing the practical applicability of these advanced ceramics for real-world microwave absorption applications.

### 2.1 Material preparation

The silicon-based precursor containing Hf, Zr, Ta, and Nb was synthesized by reacting methyltrimethoxysilane with four transition metal alkoxides: zirconium n-propoxide, hafnium n-propoxide, tantalum n-propoxide, and niobium n-propanol. Equal molar ratios of the four transition metal alcohol solutions were initially mixed with excess n-butyl alcohol as the solvent. Acetylacetone was then added at a 1 : 1 molar ratio to the total metal content (Hf+Zr+Ta+Nb) to stabilize the metal alkoxides, followed by 2 h of magnetic stirring. Next, phenolic resin was introduced into the solution at a molar ratio of 1.5 : 1 to the total metal content, and the mixture was stirred magnetically for 24 h, producing an orange-colored solution. This solution was subsequently added to separate solutions containing methyltrimethoxysilane, water (10 mL), and metals at molar ratios of 6 : 1, 4 : 1, or 3 : 1, resulting in three formulations labeled HEC–SiC-1, HEC–SiC-2, and HEC–SiC-3, respectively. The reaction mixture was stirred and heated at 80 °C for 8 h to produce a silicon-containing high-entropy ceramic precursor. For comparison, a high-entropy carbide precursor was fabricated by omitting methyltrimethoxysilane, and a siloxocarbon (SiC) precursor was prepared without high-entropy carbide components. All precursor solutions were subsequently dried at 120 °C for 24 h to remove the residual solvent, followed by grinding to yield a faint yellow powder. Finally, the precursor powder was pyrolyzed at 1500 °C for 1 h in an argon atmosphere, resulting in the formation of (Hf0.25Zr0.25Ta0.25Nb0.25)C–SiC biphasic ceramic powders.

### 2.2 Characterizations

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to determine the phase composition of the samples via a D/max-3C instrument equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (*λ* = 0.1541 nm) operating at 40 kV. The microstructure was characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Apreo 22, Thermo Fisher) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; CM300, Philips), coupled with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) for elemental analysis.

The electromagnetic properties of the (Hf0.25Zr0.25Nb0.25Ta0.25)C–SiC biphasic ceramic were evaluated via the transmission/reflection (T/R) coaxial line method with a vector network analyzer (N5230A, Agilent) across the 2–18 GHz frequency range. Prior to testing, the ceramic powders were homogeneously mixed with 40 wt% wax and molded into a cylindrical toroidal shape with an inner diameter of 3.04 mm, an outer diameter of 7.00 mm, and a thickness between 2.5 and 3.0 mm.

### 3.1 Phase compositions and microstructures of biphasic ceramic powders

The XRD patterns of the HEC–SiC biphasic ceramic powders pyrolyzed at 1500 °C are presented in Fig. 1. Both the HEC and β-SiC (PDF#01-1119) phases are clearly identified in the samples. The intensity of the β-SiC peaks decreases with decreasing amounts of methyltrimethoxyilane used in the synthesis. On the basis of the XRD data, the lattice parameters of (Hf0.25Zr0.25Nb0.25Ta0.25)C were calculated to be approximately *a* = *b* = *c* = 4.5537(5) Å, and *α* = *β* = *γ* = 90°, which is consistent with a cubic crystal structure. Owing to the formation of the β-SiC phase, partial volatilization of silicon occurred in the form of Si(g) or SiO(g), resulting in a SiC content lower than the theoretical value [38,39]. The actual SiC content in the samples was quantified via the *K* value method on the basis of the XRD patterns, and the results are summarized in Table 1. The data revealed that substantial silicon volatilization took place, leading to an experimental SiC content significantly lower than the theoretical expectation. Notably, the ratio between the theoretical and experimental SiC contents consistently falls within the range of 2.4–2.6, suggesting that the extent of silicon volatilization at 1500 °C is relatively stable for this precursor. In conclusion, single-phased high-entropy (Hf0.25Zr0.25Nb0.25Ta0.25)C and SiC biphasic ceramic powders were successfully synthesized at 1500 °C via the single-source precursor method.

As depicted in Figs. 3(a), 3(f), and 3(k), the β-SiC nanoparticles (highlighted by red circles) are uniformly distributed within the high-entropy (Hf0.25Zr0.25Ta0.25Nb0.25)C matrix. TEM images revealed that the average grain size of the β-SiC nanoparticles was approximately 3.2 nm. This homogenous distribution of Si, as observed in the EDS mapping (Figs 2(b), 2(i), and 2(n)), can be attributed to the fine distribution of β-SiC nanoparticles among the transition metal elements. The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image of the HEC–SiC-1 sample clearly reveals lattice fringes of approximately 0.228 and 0.246 nm for the HEC and SiC phases, respectively, corresponding well with d*-spacings of the (200) and (111) planes (Figs. 3(b) and 3(c)). For the HEC–SiC-3 sample, the HRTEM images (Figs. 3(l) and 3(m)) indicate lattice fringes at approximately 0.164 and 0.246 nm, corresponding to the (220) and (111) planes of the HEC and β-SiC phases, respectively. Elemental mapping (Figs. 3(d), 3(i), and 3(n)) further confirmed the uniform distribution of Si, Zr, Ta, Nb, and Hf within the HEC–SiC biphasic ceramic particles. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns of the HEC particles are shown in Figs. 3(e), 3(j), and 3(o). The SAED patterns along the zone axis clearly show [02 2¯], [004], and [02 2¯] for the HEC–SiC-1, HEC–SiC-2, and HEC–SiC-3 samples, respectively. ### 3.2 Complex permittivity properties of biphasic ceramic powders

In general, the microwave absorption properties depend on the complex permittivity ( ϵr) and complex permeability ( μr) of the material [43,44]. In this study,  μr is considered to be 1 since the material lacks magnetic components [45]. As illustrated in Fig. 4(a), the real part of the permittivity ( ϵ′) value of HEC–SiC-1 is the highest among the samples, likely because it has the highest content of the β-SiC phase. It has been established that SiC, as a wide bandgap semiconductor, possesses excellent microwave absorbing capabilities. Consequently, the  ϵ′ value decreases with decreasing β-SiC content in the HEC–SiC biphasic ceramic powders. The  ϵ′ value reflects the electric ability of the material to store electrical energy, which explains why the highest  ϵ′ value was observed in the HEC–SiC-1 sample. The imaginary part of the permittivity ( ϵ′′) for all the samples is presented in Fig. 4(b). A similar trend is observed:  ϵ′′ decreases as decreases with increasing β-SiC content. However, sudden increases in  ϵ′′ occur at approximately 10, 12, and 15 GHz for the HEC–SiC-1, HEC–SiC-2, and HEC–SiC-3 samples, respectively. This phenomenon may be linked to dipolar polarization, relaxation processes, and skin depth [46−49]. A corresponding trend is observed in the dielectric loss tangent ( tanδϵ=ϵ′′/ϵ′), with values decreasing as the SiC content decreases and peaking at 10, 12, and 15 GHz for the HEC–SiC-1, HEC–SiC-2, and HEC–SiC-3 samples, respectively. These results indicate that the electromagnetic loss capability follows the order HEC–SiC-1 > HEC–SiC-2 > HEC–SiC-3, with peaks occurring at the aforementioned frequencies.

As mentioned earlier, polarization relaxation occurs when electromagnetic waves interact with a material. To further elucidate this phenomenon, the Cole‒Cole plots for all the samples were analyzed, as shown in Fig. 4(d). According to Debye theory, the relationship between  ϵ′ and  ϵ′′ can be described by Eq. (1) [50−52]:

(1) (ϵ′−ϵs+ϵ∞2)2+ϵ′′2=(ϵs−ϵ∞2)2

where  ϵs represents the static permittivity, and  ϵ∞ is the permittivity at infinite frequency. According to Eq. (1), the  ϵ′– ϵ′′ curves should form semicircles, commonly referred to as Debye semicircles. As depicted in Fig. 4(d), these semicircles (highlighted in orange) are present in all samples, indicating the occurrence of the polarization relaxation process. Notably, two Debye semicircles are observed in the HEC–SiC-1 sample, suggesting the presence of multiple polarization relaxation mechanisms. This multirelaxation phenomenon is likely due to the complex microstructure of the HEC–SiC biphasic ceramic powder, where various interfaces between the HEC matrix and the β-SiC nanoparticles introduce distinct relaxation times. The intricate distribution and encapsulation of SiC nanoparticles within the HEC matrix facilitate diverse polarization mechanisms, thereby enhancing the overall microwave absorption performance.

### 3.3 Microwave-absorption properties of biphasic ceramic powders

To evaluate the microwave absorption performance of the synthesized HEC–SiC biphasic ceramic powders, RL values were calculated via the metal-back plane model. RL values are critical parameters for accessing microwave absorption efficiency and are determined as Eqs. (2) and (3) [53−55]:

(2) RL(dB)=20log|Zin−Z0Zin+Z0|

(3) Zin=Z0μr/ϵrtanh(j(2πfdc)μrϵr)

where * Zin,  Z0,  f,  d, and  c represent the input impedance of the absorber, the impedance of free space, the frequency, the thickness of the absorber, and velocity of light, respectively. Figure 5 shows the two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) plots of the RL values for the HEC–SiC biphasic ceramic powders. As shown in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b), the HEC–SiC-1 sample has RLmin value of −38.97 dB at 17.07 GHz with a thickness of 5.00 mm, along with an effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) of approximately 2.1 GHz, indicating moderate absorption at high frequency. In contrast, reducing the SiC phase content significantly enhanced the microwave absorption performance of the HEC–SiC-2 samples, with an RLmin value of −54.28 dB at 12.39 GHz and a thickness of 3.14 mm, albeit with a narrower EAB of 0.5 GHz (Figs. 5(c) and 5(d)). For the HEC–SiC-3 sample, further reduction of the SiC phase RLmin to −29.93 dB with a thickness of 3.06 mm was accompanied by EAB of 0.15 GHz (Figs. 5(e) and 5(f)). These results suggest that while HEC–SiC-2 achieves excellent absorption efficiency, it does so at the expense of bandwidth.

### 3.4 Mechanism of microwave-absorption properties

Figure 7 illustrates the microwave absorption mechanism of the HEC–SiC composites. The composite’s electromagnetic wave absorption property arises from the synergistic effects of the HEC matrix and SiC particles. These materials jointly contribute to multiple loss mechanisms, including conductive loss, dielectric loss, interfacial polarization, and dipole polarization, resulting in efficient microwave attenuation. Within the HEC matrix, a conductive network forms, which facilitates the movement of free electrons under an alternating electromagnetic field. This movement induces eddy currents, leading to Joule heating and the conversion of electromagnetic energy into heat; this process is commonly referred to as conductive loss [59]. The conductive pathways also enhance the skin effect, limiting the penetration depth of electromagnetic waves and increasing the absorption efficiency in high-frequency regions. The composite further benefits from the presence of numerous defects within the HEC matrix, such as vacancies, dislocations, and grain boundaries, which act as active sites for interfacial polarization. These imperfections generate localized electric fields, promoting the separation of charge carriers across the heterogeneous interfaces between the HEC matrix and SiC particles. The accumulation of space charges at these interfaces creates an electric dipole, which oscillates in response to the dipole polarization and contributes significantly to the dissipation of electromagnetic energy by increasing dielectric loss. The SiC particles play a crucial role in enhancing impedance matching, ensuring that the incoming electromagnetic waves are effectively transmitted into the materials rather than being reflected. The combination of high electrical conductivity in the HEC matrix and the dielectric nature of the SiC particles creates multiple heterogeneous interfaces, each contributing to the generation of interfacial polarization. Furthermore, SiC particles increase the surface area and promote a greater density of interfacial regions, which enhances energy dissipation. In addition, the multiple scattering effects within the composite, where electromagnetic waves are repeatedly scattered by the SiC particles and heterogeneous interfaces, extend the propagation path of the waves within the material, increasing the likelihood of absorption. The overlapping effects of conductive loss, dielectric loss, and scattering lead to a broad absorption bandwidth. In summary, the synergistic effects of the conductive HEC matrix, dielectric SiC particles, optimized impedance matching, interfacial polarization, and dipole polarization result in excellent microwave absorption performance. This combination of mechanisms makes HEC–SiC biphasic ceramics promising candidates for use in advanced electromagnetic wave absorption applications, including stealth technology, electromagnetic shielding, and high-frequency communication systems.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, we successfully synthesized (Hf0.25Zr0.25Ta0.25Nb0.25)C–SiC (HEC–SiC) biphasic ceramic powders via the single-source-precursor method. The HEC matrix contributes to the conductive loss capacity, whereas the *in situ* formation of a nanosized SiC phase improves impedance matching within the composite. Achieving an optimal balance between impedance matching and electromagnetic wave attenuation by tuning the SiC content is critical. The results demonstrate that reducing the SiC content improves wave attenuation but compromises impedance matching. Furthermore, structural defects within the HEC matrix and the presence of nanosized SiC particles facilitate multiple reflections and interfacial polarization, further enhancing the material’s absorption performance. Through the synergistic interaction of the HEC and SiC phases, the HEC–SiC-2 composite (~21.21 wt% SiC) exhibited optimal microwave absorption, with RLmin of −54.28 dB at 12.39 GHz at a thickness of 3.14 mm. These findings highlight that polymer-derived HEC–SiC biphasic ceramics provide a promising platform for designing tunable impedance matching materials for electromagnetic wave absorption applications.
