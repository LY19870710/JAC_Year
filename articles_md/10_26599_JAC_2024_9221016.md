## 1 Introduction

Si3N4 ceramics have excellent wave-transparent performance and mechanical properties, such as low density, low dielectric constant, and excellent strength [1−3]; therefore, they are regarded as potential wave-transparent materials for future hypersonic aircraft in harsh environments [4]. However, the high hardness and low fracture toughness of Si3N4 ceramics make it difficult to mold complex parts [5−7]. Currently, Si3N4 ceramics are mainly prepared by vacuum sintering, pressureless sintering, hot-pressing sintering, etc., and they still have the disadvantages of being difficult to process and time-consuming [8,9], which makes the practical application of Si3N4 ceramics challenging [10,11].

Additive manufacturing (AM), also known as three-dimensional (3D) printing, is a frontier technology for manufacturing three-dimensional objects and complex structures [12−14]. Compared with traditional technologies, additive manufacturing has many advantages, such as high precision, low cost, easy molding of complex shapes, and fast preparation speed [15], and has broad application prospects in molding complex ceramic components.

Stereolithography (SLA) is an accurate 3D printing technology that obtains samples with high surface quality, high precision, and few defects and allows for the integrated molding of large components [16,17]. During the SLA-3D printing process, the ceramic slurry is spread on the build platform and smoothed with a scraper. Then, the area is scanned via an ultraviolet (UV) laser and cured layer by layer according to the data from the 3D-designed model [18,19]. After the polymerization of one layer is accomplished, the build platform is vertically lowered by one layer of thickness, and then the curing of the second layer continues until the end of the printing process [14,20,21]. The ceramic particles are dispersed in the resin and bonded into complex-shaped ceramic bodies after UV light irradiation [22]. The molding method can precisely control the printing of ceramic samples with high shape precision, ensuring that the samples have good mechanical properties. It is a relatively mature and promising technology for preparing ceramic structures [23].

However, dark-colored ceramic particles usually have high absorbance, and the refractive index difference between the particles and photosensitive resin is quite large, making curing and molding with UV light difficult, which poses a great challenge in configuring slurries with high content and low viscosity. In addition, the curing depth of Si3N4 ceramic slurry is too low, which tends to cause loose interlayer bonding during 3D printing [24].

To increase the curing depth, surface oxidation of Si3N4 particles has been used in most current studies. The surface oxidation method mainly oxidizes the surface of Si3N4 to form SiO2 with a lower refractive index, which decreases the refractive index difference from that of the resin, thus increasing the curing depth. Moreover, the oxidized ceramic particles are more closely related to a spherical shape, which reduces the friction between particles and thus decreases the viscosity of the slurry. Chen *et al*. [24] prepared Si3N4−SiO2 composite ceramics via digital light processing 3D printing technology. The curing depth of the slurry significantly increased to 60 μm after the surface oxidation method was used. Liu *et al*. [25] optimized Si3N4 surface modification process on the basis of oxidation pretreatment and surface modification via the silane coupling agent KH550, which resulted in a significant increase in the depth of curing of the slurry. Mixing silicon nitride particles with low-refractive-index oxide particles is another way to achieve an appropriate slurry for 3D printing. Xing *et al*. [26] utilized mixed Si3N4 and Al2O3 slurries to fabricate functionally graded material (FGM) ceramic parts with a curing depth of 1.25 mm. However, surface oxidation and the addition of other oxides introduce secondary phases, which can potentially degrade the mechanical and high-temperature properties of the prepared silicon nitride ceramics.

In addition, a high curing depth can also be achieved by increasing the laser power. Huang *et al*. [27] prepared Si3N4 square honeycombs and simple cubic lattices via SLA technology and reported that a curing depth of ~75–80 μm could be achieved with applied laser energies of ~631.8–1011.0 mJ·cm−2. However, high laser power tends to cause warping of the printed samples because of the large internal stresses generated by resin polymerization. In addition, Si3N4 powders are difficult to mix with resins because of their relatively high surface-free energy and poor compatibility with resins [27]. The high viscosity and internal stress of ceramic slurries reduce their printability and present a great challenge for the stereolithography of Si3N4 ceramics. Obtaining a suitable paste formulation to solve the problem of internal stress during the curing process is a key issue to be solved in the 3D printing of silicon nitride ceramics.

The curing process of the slurry is realized mainly by the polymerization of photosensitive resin to form 3D polymer chains after the double bonds are broken. Plasticizers can lower the glass transition temperature of the photosensitive resin and weaken the long chain force formed by the polymer, thus effectively reducing the internal stresses generated during SLA-3D printing [28,29]. Moreover, as nonreactive diluents, plasticizers can reduce the risk of cracking and delamination of the green body during debonding heat treatment [30,31]. Compared with most plasticizers, tributyl citrate (TBC) is nontoxic and odorless, has low volatility, good compatibility with the resin, and high plasticizing efficiency [32]. It has been reported that TBC can improve slurry stability and extension effects and can also reduce residual stress during the synthesis process [33]. Therefore, it was selected in this work as a plasticizer to reduce warpage in the stereolithography process.

In this work, Si3N4 ceramics were fabricated via stereolithographic 3D printing, and a strategy for obtaining a high solid content oxidation-free Si3N4 ceramic slurry was proposed. The rheological properties and printing performance of the slurry were also investigated. The relative density and line shrinkage in the *X*, *Y*, and *Z* directions were analyzed in detail after debinding thermal treatment and sintering. Furthermore, the mechanical and dielectric properties of the fabricated Si3N4 ceramics were evaluated.

### 2.1 Preparation of Si<sub>3</sub>N<sub>4</sub> slurry

α-Si3N4 powder (SN-E10, UBE Industries, Japan) with a particle size (*d*50) of 0.7 μm was used as the raw material, and the morphologies and particle size distributions of Si3N4 powders are illustrated in Fig. 1. Y2O3 (99.9%, Guangzhou Jianfeng Rare Earth Co., Ltd., China) and Al2O3 (AR grade, Traditional Chinese Medicine, China) were used as sintering aids. Ditrimethylolpropane tetraacrylate (Di-TMPTA, Shanghai Guangyi Chemical Co., Ltd., China) and 1,6-hexanediol diacrylate (HDDA, Covestro Co., Ltd., Germany) were used as the photosensitive resins. Tributyl citrate (TBC, Adamas Reagent Co., Ltd., China) and diphenyl (2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphine oxide (TPO, Shanghai Guangyi Chemical Co., Ltd., China) were used as the plasticizer and photoinitiator, respectively. BYK 9076 (B-BYK Chemie Gmbh, Germany) and BYK 2152 (B-BYK Chemie Gmbh, Germany) were selected as dispersants. The silane coupling agent KH550 (Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was used as a modifier.

The ceramic powders, including 95 wt% Si3N4, 3 wt% Y2O3, and 2 wt% Al2O3 together with KH550 (1.5 wt%), were with alcohol for 12 h by a planetary ball mill and then dried at 80 °C for 4 h. After that, the mixtures were sieved through a 100-mesh sieve. Di-TMPTA and HDDA were mixed at a ratio of 7 : 3 by weight, and 30 wt% TBC was added to the total resin content. A total of 4 wt% TPO photoinitiator, 2 wt% BYK 2152 dispersant, and 3 wt% BYK 9076 dispersant were added to the resin monomer. The ceramic powders were fully mixed with the above organics in a planetary gravity mixer (TM-2000TT, SMIDA Co., Ltd., China) at 1800 r·min−1 for 30 min. Finally, a Si3N4 ceramic slurry with a solid content of 40 vol% was obtained.

### 2.2 Preparation process of Si<sub>3</sub>N<sub>4</sub> ceramics for stereolithographic 3D printing

The green body was printed by an SLA-3D printer (AM150, ZPapid Tech Co., Ltd., China) equipped with UV light at a wavelength of 355 nm. A model with a size of 50 mm × 40 mm × 5 mm was created, and different layer thicknesses were sliced at 25, 30, 35, and 40 μm. The laser power, scanning speed, scanning line gap, and scraper moving speed were set to 650 mW, 3000 mm·s−1, 0.05 mm, and 30 mm·s−1, respectively. Samples with model sizes of 50 mm × 5.2 mm × 4 mm, 40 mm × 5.2 mm × 3 mm, and 21 mm × 11 mm × 3 mm were printed via the optimized slurry and sintered for bending strength, fracture toughness, and dielectric property tests, respectively. The shrinkage measurement was also performed on samples with a model size of 50 mm × 5.2 mm × 4 mm.

The printed Si3N4 green bodies were debounded at 600 °C for 2 h in an Ar atmosphere and then further debinding at 600 °C for 2 h in an air atmosphere. After debinding, the Si3N4 green bodies were sintered at 1750 °C for 2 h in a N2 atmosphere. The preparation process of Si3N4 ceramics by stereolithographic 3D printing is illustrated in Fig. 2.

### 2.3 Characterization

The rheological behavior of the Si3N4 slurry was evaluated on a rheometer (HAKKE MARS 40, Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., USA). Thermogravimetric (TG) analysis of the Si3N4 green body, HDDA, Di-TMPTA, and TBC was conducted in an air atmosphere at a heating rate of 5 °C·min−1 on a thermogravimetric analyzer (TG209F1 Libra R, NETZSCH, Germany). The TG–IR curve of the Si3N4 green body was also obtained via a simultaneous thermal analyzer–infrared spectrometer (STA449F3/Nicolet 6700, NETZSCH/Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Germany/USA). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were obtained via a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Vertex70-Hyperion3000, Bruker, Germany) at room temperature. The surface roughness of diverse cured Si3N4 green bodies was tested via a confocal laser scanning microscope (OLS4100, Olympus Co., Japan). The phase compositions of Si3N4 were characterized by an X-ray diffractometer (Empyrean3X, PANalytical, the Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation. The microstructures of the Si3N4 green bodies and ceramics were observed by the scanning electron microscopes (TM4000, Hitachi, Japan; Zeiss Sigma 300, Zeiss, Germany) with an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. The curing depth was measured via SEM images. The shrinkage was calculated by measuring the dimensions before and after sintering with a Vernier caliper. The relative densities of Si3N4 ceramics were calculated as the mass divided by the volume.

The bending strength and fracture toughness of Si3N4 ceramics were determined via the three-point bending method on a mechanical testing machine (C42.104, Shanghai New Sense Enterprise Development Co., China). The bending strength test process was conducted according to GB/T 6569-2006. Samples with dimensions of 4 mm × 3 mm × 36 mm were used, and the loading speed was 0.5 mm·min−1. The fracture toughness test process was conducted according to ASTM E1820-18. Samples with dimensions of 4 mm × 2 mm × 30 mm and a 0.125 mm incision were used, and the loading speed was 0.05 mm·min−1. Five samples were used for each test. The dielectric constant and dielectric loss tangent of Si3N4 ceramics of different printed thicknesses were obtained via a vector network analyzer (PNA-N5234A, Agilent, USA) via the waveguide method, and samples with dimensions of 15.90 mm × 8.06 mm × (1–2) mm at a frequency of 12–18 GHz were obtained.

### 3.1 Rheological behavior, warpage, and curing depth of Si<sub>3</sub>N<sub>4</sub> slurry

The SLA-3D printing process is unsupported, which requires the slurry to be not only fluid but also supportive, so the viscosity of the slurry cannot be too low. Although a certain viscosity can guarantee self-supportability, a viscosity that is too high significantly deteriorates the printability, causing uneven spreading and even defects. Therefore, it is crucial to study the rheological properties of slurries. The rheological behavior of Si3N4 resin slurries with different contents of TBC at shear rates of 0.01–100 s−1 is shown in Fig. 3(a). Compared with Di-TMPTA, TBC has a lower viscosity and thus can reduce the viscosity of the slurry. The viscosity of Si3N4 ceramic slurries tends to decrease with increasing shear rate, showing a shear thinning phenomenon, and the non-Newtonian fluid behavior increases. The viscosity of the slurries becomes more stable at shear rates of 1–10 s−1. The solid content of the slurry is the main factor affecting viscosity, and the presence of van der Waals forces between ceramic particles leads to particle agglomeration. However, dispersants can effectively hinder the interlayer movement of the slurry because of the steric hindrance effect [34]. An upward shift in the rheology curves when the shear rate is between 10 and 100 s−1 can be observed. This is due to the thixotropic behavior caused by the high solid content of the slurry. Proper thixotropy of the printing process is critical, as it ensures reduced viscosity and enhanced flow under the shear of the scraper, helping to recoat thin layer thicknesses evenly. The viscosity subsequently returns to a high level at the end of the recoating, and the fluidity weakens as the shear force is removed, which helps the recoated layer to easily rest and cure into the target shape with precise dimensions [29]. Figure 3(b) shows that the viscosity of the Si3N4 ceramic slurry tends to stabilize with time and remains in the range of 34.447×106–116.073×106 mPa·s at a shear rate of 0.01 s−1, which ensures that the slurry maintains a constant viscosity under the force of the scraper during the SLA process and satisfies the demand of unsupported 3D printing. The rheological behavior of the Si3N4 resin slurries with different contents of TBC at a shear rate of 0.01 s−1 and temperatures of 20–70 °C is shown in Fig. 3(c), and the viscosity of the pastes tends to decrease with increasing temperature. As the temperature increases, the disentanglement and thermal movement of the molecular chains of the BYK 9076 dispersant and photosensitive resin increase, thus reducing their viscosity. The particles in the slurry move faster, and the time for them to interact with each other decreases, which reduces internal friction or stress, thus lowering the overall viscosity of the slurry.

Figure 4(a) shows the curing depth of the Si3N4 slurry without TBC added at different laser powers. The curing depth increases from 28.88 to 56.20 μm when the laser power is increased from 100 to 700 mW. However, warpage still occurs even at the lowest laser power of 100 mW; therefore, the addition of the plasticizer TBC is necessary to improve the printability of the slurry. Plasticizers can dilute the concentration of photosensitive resin, reduce the density of C=C double bonds, and weaken the contraction caused by covalent forces [35]. To verify the influence of different contents of TBC on the warpage of the Si3N4 green body, sheets with an area of 50 mm × 40 mm were printed, and the warping phenomenon was studied. The printing parameters were as follows: laser power of 650 mW, scanning speed of 3000 mm·s−1, scanning line gap of 0.05 mm, and layer thickness of 40 μm. Warpage was calculated by measuring the warpage height H* and length *L* of the samples. Figures 4(b)–4(h) show the warpage of the Si3N4 green bodies with different contents of TBC and the plasticization mechanism, and the warpage decreases from 12.85 to 0 as the addition of TBC increases from 0 to 35 wt%. The plasticization mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 4(i). Plasticized molecules achieve plasticization by destroying the connections between polymers, increasing the spacing, and reducing the intermolecular interactions. The insertion of the plasticizer molecules into the polymer molecular chains weakens the force between the polymer molecular chains, effectively reducing the internal stress of the green body and thus eliminating warpage. Compared with other plasticizer molecules with larger side chains, TBC has the ability to disrupt polymer‒polymer interactions because its carbonyl oxygen can interact with the carboxyl hydrogen of the copolymer through hydrogen bonding [36].

where C*d is the curing depth; *D*p is the penetration depth of the slurry; *E* is the average laser energy exposed to the surface of the slurry; *E*c is the critical exposure energy; *P* is the UV laser power; *V* is the scanning speed of UV light; *H*s is the filling pitch of the laser on the surface of the slurry. The *D*p value represents how sensitive a slurry is to changes in UV light output. Equations (1) and (2) indicate that the curing depth is positively correlated with the laser power and negatively correlated with the laser scanning speed and filling spacing [22].

The refractive index of Si3N4 is much greater than that of the photosensitive resin. When exposed to UV light, the scattering effect occurs at the interface, causing the laser to be unable to irradiate the slurry at the bottom, thus reducing the curing depth of the slurry. The solid content of Si3N4 ceramic slurry used in this work is as high as 40 vol%, resulting in a more severe scattering effect. Therefore, it is necessary to increase the exposure of the cured layer to UV light to increase the curing depth, which requires increasing the laser power (*P*), reducing the scanning speed (*V*), or decreasing the scanning line gap (*H*s). The relationship between the curing depth and laser power is depicted in Fig. 5(a), which reveals that the curing depth decreases significantly with increasing TBC content, whereas the curing depth tends to increase as the laser power increases from 600 to 750 mW (*V* = 3000 mm·s−1, *H*s = 0.05 mm). Under a laser power of 750 mW, the highest curing depth of the Si3N4 slurry was 51.07 μm with the addition of 25 wt% TBC, whereas the curing depth decreased to 39.48 μm with the addition of 25 wt.% TBC when the laser power was reduced to 600 mW. The scanning speed also plays a crucial role in the SLA-3D printing process, and the faster the laser scanning speed is, the less energy the UV light irradiates the surface. Figure 5(b) shows the relationship between the scanning speed and curing depth. The laser scanning speed has a relatively small effect on the curing depth compared with the laser power. For example, the change in curing depth was less than 2 μm when the scanning speed was varied from ~1500 to 3000 mm·s−1 (*P* = 650 mW, *H*s = 0.05 mm) with the addition of 25 wt% TBC. The curing depth increases from 42.72 to 43.94 μm (25 wt% TBC), 41.83 to 42.87 μm (30 wt% TBC), and 35.55 to 38.4 μm (35 wt% TBC) when the laser fill increases the pitch from 0.02 to 1 mm (*P* = 650 mW, *V* = 3000 mm·s−1), as shown in Fig. 5(c). TBC does not have functional groups that can react with photosensitive resins. The addition of TBC is equivalent to reducing the density of the C=C double bond in the reaction, so the curing depth is reduced [37]. The slurry with 25 wt% TBC still has a 1.16% warpage, making it difficult to avoid the possibility of aggravating the warpage and deformation during subsequent repeated laminations. The curing depth of the slurry with 35 wt% TBC is relatively low, which limits the designed thickness.

On the basis of the above analysis of the curing depth, the photocuring parameters are optimized as follows: TBC content of 30 wt%, laser power of 650 mW, scanning speed and contour scanning speed of 3000 mm·s−1, 2 scanning repeats, and scanning line gap of 0.05 mm. To ensure the accuracy of the overlap between the layers, the designed thickness must be greater than the curing depth (41.83 μm).

### 3.3 Analysis of the debinding process

The polymers need to be removed from the Si3N4 green body before sintering, and the optimal parameters for debinding heat treatment are critical to minimize defects and cracks. The TG–differential thermogravimetry (DTG) curve of the Si3N4 green body in an air atmosphere is shown in Fig. 8(a). The pyrolysis process can be divided into three steps. There is no significant change in the weight of the green body until 150 °C, which is associated with the removal of residual water from the green body. In the temperature range of 150–420 °C, severe weight loss occurs due to the pyrolysis of the photosensitive resin, plasticizer, and dispersant as the temperature increases. When the temperature reaches 550 °C, the mass no longer changes, meaning that the organic phase is completely converted to residual carbon. The TG‒DTG curves of HDDA, TBC, and Di-TMPTA are shown in Figs. 8(b)–8(d). The temperature at which the decomposition of TBC is completed is earlier than the temperature at which Di-TMPTA and HDDA start to lose much of their weight. As a result, the gases generated by the decomposition of Di-TMPTA and HDDA can escape from the channels generated by the decomposition of TBC, thus reducing the risk of cracking. Figure 8(e) shows the TG–IR curve of the green body, which further proves that the –OR and –C=O bonds of TBC are broken before the macromolecular chain –C–C–R begins to decompose.

Since the green body consists of a large amount of resin, which rapidly pyrolyzes in air and instantly generates a large amount of gas, direct debinding in air can lead to cracks on the surface and inside the prepared samples. However, the main reactions that occur during debinding in an Ar atmosphere are the conversion of polymer macromolecules into small molecules and the removal of residual carbon. In an inert atmosphere, polymers only undergo thermal decomposition without oxidation, so the amount of gas generated and the gas generation rate are effectively reduced, thus effectively inhibiting the generation of cracks during debinding [39−41]. Therefore, to completely remove the photopolymer and prevent cracking, the debinding process was carried out as follows: The green body was heated from room temperature to 600 °C at a constant rate of 0.25 °C·min−1 and held for 2 h in an Ar atmosphere. The mixture was allowed to swell at 150 and 420 °C for 30 min while the temperature was ramping to allow the polymer to slowly evaporate from the interior of the body, thus reducing the risk of thermal shock and defects in the ceramic materials. After that, the green body containing residual carbon was heated to 600 °C at a rate of 5 °C·min−1 and kept for another 2 h in air. Finally, Si3N4 green bodies without polymers were obtained.

The changes in chemical bonds during the stereolithographic 3D printing process and debinding heat treatment are shown in Fig. 8(f), where the absorption peak at 2948 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of a single bond (–C–H), and its bending vibration appears at 850–1080 cm−1. The absorption peaks at 1729 and 1186 cm−1 correspond to the stretching vibrations of ester carboxyl (–COOR) and ether (–C–O–C) groups, respectively. These peaks are generated mainly by the photosensitive resin and plasticizer in Si3N4 ceramic slurry, and the curing process involves the polymerization of photosensitive resin to form a three-dimensional polymer chain by breaking the double bonds. Compared with the green body, the corresponding peak of the unsaturated double bonds (–C=C) was detected at 1639 cm−1 in the Si3N4 ceramic slurry, which indicates that the fabrication process of Si3N4 green body is accompanied by the breaking of carbon double bonds after UV light irradiation. This phenomenon suggests that Di-TMPTA and HDDA have polymerized into a three-dimensional network of long polymer chains. After the debinding heat treatment, no peaks corresponding to the organic part were identified. After two different astrospheres are subjected to heat treatment at 600 °C, the absorption peaks of the C–H single bond and the C–C single bond disappear, revealing the removal of the photosensitive resin and plasticizer.

### 3.4 Physical properties of Si<sub>3</sub>N<sub>4 </sub>ceramics

Shrinkage studies can provide accurate dimensional control for the subsequent printing of complex components. Figure 9 shows the physical properties of Si3N4 ceramics prepared by pressureless sintering at 1750 °C in a N2 atmosphere for 2 h, including linear shrinkage in the *X*, *Y*, and *Z* directions and relative density. The results clearly show that the line shrinkage in the *Z* direction is greater than that in the *X* and *Y* directions. Owing to the layer-by-layer molding process during SLA 3D printing, which causes a slight “step effect” in the *Z* direction, the weaker interlayers in the *Z* direction shrink more easily when sintered at high temperatures [42]. The difference in line shrinkage in the *X* and *Y* directions may be due to the difference in the length and width of the printed sample, where the value for length *X* is much larger than that for width *Y*. The relative densities of Si3N4 ceramics with different printed thicknesses are 73.07%, 74.41%, 83.41%, and 83.45%, respectively. During the slurry coating process, under the effect of a fluid driving force, the smaller Si3N4 particles are distributed in the upper part of the printed layer, and the larger particles are distributed in the bottom part of the solidified layer, presenting a periodic gradient distribution structure and forming fuzzy boundaries. A thinner layer means that more layers need to be printed, and an increase in the number of weak interface layers loosens the sintering of the sample, leading to density differences [43]. The wider the thickness is, the easier it is to produce a layer-by-layer texture, which provides growth space for the grains and makes them easier to shrink [42].

### 3.6 Mechanical and dielectric properties of printed Si<sub>3</sub>N<sub>4</sub> ceramics

The bending strength and fracture toughness of Si3N4 ceramics with different printed thicknesses are shown in Figs. 12(a) and 12(b). The bending strength increases from 206.72±30.53 to 338.29±12.08 MPa as the printed thickness increases from 25 to 40 μm. The higher relative densities of the samples with printed thicknesses of 35 and 40 μm endow them with higher bending strengths as well as higher fracture toughness. Additionally, Fig. 12(b) shows that the fracture toughness of the samples in the direction of the build surface is relatively greater than that in the direction of the stacking layer. For example, the fracture toughness values of samples when the load is oriented perpendicular to the exposure surface are 5.61±0.23, 6.03±0.99, 6.36±0.77, and 6.94±0.11 MPa·m1/2, respectively. In contrast, when the load is oriented perpendicular to the stacking surface, the fracture toughness values of the samples are 3.78±0.55, 4.89±0.17, 5.97±0.67, and 5.37±0.99 MPa·m1/2, respectively. The anisotropy of the mechanical properties arises from the bottom-up, layer-by-layer stacking microstructure during the SLA-3D printing process, which can also be seen in Fig. 11. During the loading process perpendicular to the build surface, cracks can propagate along the weak interfaces between the layers, thus possibly achieving a toughening effect. Figure 12(c) compares the bending strength of the 3D-printed Si3N4 ceramics between the present work and previous works reported in Refs. [4,24,44−57]. The Si3N4 ceramic prepared in this work possesses a relatively high bending strength in the range of relative densities of 80%–90%.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, a high solid content of 40 vol% and an oxidation-free Si3N4 ceramic slurry for SLA-3D printing were successfully prepared. The main conclusions are as follows:

1) The viscosity of the slurry is maintained in the range of 34.447×106–116.073×106 mPa·s at 0.01 s−1 with 0–35 wt% addition of TBC, which is suitable for the unsupported 3D printing process. The warpage decreases gradually as the TBC content increases, and the warpage decreases to 0% when the plasticizer content reaches 30 wt% at high laser powers from 600 to 750 mW.

2) The sintered Si3N4 ceramics with a printed thickness of 40 μm exhibit excellent mechanical properties, including fracture toughness values of 6.94±0.11 and 5.37±0.67 MPa·m1/2 in the two directions and a bending strength of 338.29±12.08 MPa. As the printed thickness increases from 25 to 40 μm, the dielectric constant of the sintered Si3N4 ceramics increases from 5.462 to 6.414, and the dielectric loss tangent is maintained in the range of 0.021–0.089.

3) Irregular and complex configurations were successfully printed via the slurry developed in this work, laying the foundation for the future design and fabrication of printed parts with different macroscopic morphologies to meet the requirements of hypersonic aircraft with desirable mechanical and dielectric properties.
