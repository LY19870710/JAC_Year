## 1 Introduction

Piezoelectric ceramics are utilized in a wide range of functional devices, serving as the core components in actuators, sensors, and transducers, as their remarkable ability to convert between mechanical and electrical energy [1−3]. Lead-based piezoelectric ceramics have historically been the most commonly used materials for these applications [4−6]. However, the hazardous nature of lead-based ceramics has prompted the development of lead-free alternatives, even though they offer excellent overall performance [7−9]. Over the years, four main categories of lead-free piezoelectric ceramics have been developed, including materials based on BaTiO3 (BT) [10], (Bi0.5Na0.5)TiO3 (BNT) [11,12], (K0.5Na0.5)NbO3 (KNN) [13,14] and BiFeO3 (BF) [10−17]. These lead-free alternatives aim to maintain the exceptional functionality of lead-based ceramics while eliminating their environmental and health risks.

The suitability of various ceramics for specific applications depends on their unique properties. For instance, BNT-based materials are known for their significant strain response and find applications in piezoelectric actuators [18]. To enhance the strain characteristics of BNT-based ceramics, several approaches are commonly employed, including elemental modification, defect engineering, and texture engineering [19,20]. Element-doped BNT-based ceramics have shown strains ranging from 0.2% to 0.5% at electric fields of 60–100 kV·cm–1 [19]. Lai *et al.* [12] achieved an ultrahigh strain exceeding 1.5% at 0.1 Hz at 100 kV·cm–1 by combining texture engineering and defect engineering. Despite the numerous advantages of BNT ceramics, certain limitations impact their applications. For instance, BNT-based ceramics exhibit minimal strain at low electric fields (10–40 kV·cm–1). However, a substantial strain hysteresis (∆*H*) becomes evident when high electric fields (60–100 kV·cm–1) are applied. ∆*H* is defined as ∆*S*half/∆*S*max, where *∆S*half represents the width of the unipolar strain loop at half of the applied field, and ∆*S*max is the maximum strain. In the [Bi0.5(Na0.8K0.2)0.5](Ti0.0985Nb0.015)O3 system, strains of 0.092% and 0.294% were observed at electric fields of 40 and 60 kV·cm–1, respectively, and correspondingly, ∆*H* values of 37% and 51% were noted [21]. Furthermore, the strain in element-doped BNT-based materials is expected to significantly reduce with rising temperature [22,23]. A high degradation rate of 38% was observed in a 0.83Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3–0.17Bi0.5K0.5TiO3 ceramic over the temperature range from room temperature to 120 ℃ [23].

Compared to BNT-based ceramics, element-modified KNN-based lead-free piezoelectric ceramics, featuring multiphase coexistence, typically exhibit a high strain and reduced ∆*H* when subjected to low electric fields. KNN-based ceramics possess a small coercive field, typically below 20 kV·cm–1, which is approximately one fourth of that in BNT-based ceramics, enabling the generation of substantial strains under low electric fields [24]. Strains ranging from 0.07% to 0.35% were obtained in KNN-based materials under 10–40 kV·cm–1 [19]. Furthermore, a strain of 0.12% at 20 kV·cm–1 has been achieved in the 0.93(Na0.52K0.48)(Nb0.975Sb0.025)O3–0.05BaZrO3–0.02(Bi0.5Na0.5)HfO3–1 wt% MnO2 system [3]. Yu *et al*. [25] also accomplished a giant strain of 0.3% at 40 kV·cm–1.

The ∆*H* is primarily due to domain reorientation, where the kinetics of electric field-induced domain motion lags behind the input voltage [6,25]. A significant ∆*H* results in reduced displacement accuracy but increased energy and heat loss, ultimately leading to decreased performance [26]. In general, BNT-based materials often exhibit a high ∆*H* exceeding 40%, a value much larger than that found in KNN-based ceramics [18,19,27]. Both Liu *et al.* [3] and Yu *et al.* [25] reported that element-doped KNN-based materials could achieve a large piezoelectric coefficient * d33∗ (= S*max/*E*max) exceeding 600 pm·V–1 while maintaining ∆*H* at less than 17%. Due to their temperature-sensitive phase structure, KNN-based piezoelectric ceramics typically have limited strain temperature stability. Attempts have been made to improve strain temperature stability by successive phase transitions and the construction of multi-layer ceramics, but the low * d33∗ and complex preparation processes have hindered industrial-scale production [28,29]. In summary, developing lead-free piezoelectric ceramics with low ∆H*, remarkable temperature stability, and a giant strain remains a long-standing challenging issue. Nevertheless, addressing this challenge is essential for expanding their range of applications and reducing reliance of lead-based counterparts.

It has been demonstrated that defect engineering is an effective method to significantly enhance the strain of KNN-based and BNT-based lead-free piezoelectric ceramics, as well as improving their temperature stability [12,30]. Zhao *et al.* [30] prepared Fe-doped KNN-based ceramics with a superior strain of 0.29% at 30 kV·cm–1, and no electrostrain performance degradation was observed even as the test temperature continuously increased up to 140 ℃. A similar outcome was achieved by Lai *et al.* [21] in a textured [Bi0.5(Na0.8K0.2)0.5](Ti0.0985Nb0.015)O3 system. Building upon the existing research, this work gained an exceptional strain of 0.276% at 20 kV·cm–1 and remarkable temperature stability in KNN-based ceramics. These results were obtained through the combined control of defect concentration, phase composition, and relaxation behavior. A comprehensive structural characterization demonstrated that defect dipoles, R–O–T multiphase coexistence, and increased local heterogeneity all contributed to the enhancement of strain. This study provides an effective strategy for achieving a giant strain in KNN-based lead-free piezoelectric ceramics, further advancing the progress of sustainable materials for actuators.

## 2 Experimental

Ceramics denoted as 0.94Li0.05(Na0.52K0.48)0.95(Nb0.96Sb0.04)O3–0.06Ba(Zr0.9Hf0.1)O3 (abbreviated as LKNNS–6BZH) were prepared by the conventional solid-state method. All raw materials, including K2CO3 (99%), Na2CO3 (99.8%), Li2CO3 (99.9%), Nb2O5 (99.9%), Sb2O3 (99.99%), BaCO3 (99.95%), ZrO2 (99.9%), and HfO2 (99.99%), were weighted according to the nominal composition and then subjected to milling in an ethanol medium for 24 h. The mixed slurries were dried at 80 ℃ and subsequently calcined at 950 ℃ for 6 h. The calcined powder was further milled with ethanol for an additional 24 h. After this process, the calcined powder mixture was pressed into pellets with a diameter of 10 mm, using cold isostatic pressure at 200 MPa for 2 min. The green pellets were sintered in air for 6 h at different selected temperatures, namely *T*sint = 1140, 1150, 1160, 1170, and 1180 ℃.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) with Cu Kα radiation (XRD, D/Max 2500; Rigaku, Tokyo, Japan) was utilized to determine the crystal structure. A Raman spectrophotometer (LabRAM HR, HoRIBA Jobin Yvon, France) equipped with a 532 nm laser was employed for Raman spectroscopy measurements. The thermally etched surfaces were investigated by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, Merlin VP compact Zeiss, Germany). The density of the ceramics was measured by the Archimedes method. The chemical composition was identified using inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Thermo, Waltham, USA). Qualitative testing of oxygen vacancies * VO⋅⋅ was conducted at room temperature using an electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectrometer (SOE-072, Bruker, Switzerland) operating at the X-band frequency of 9.8585 GHz with a field modulation frequency of 100 kHz. The domain morphology of the polished samples was studied using a commercial piezoelectric force microscope (PFM) (MFP-3D, Asylum Research, USA). For the electrical property measurements, the as-sintered samples were polished to 500–600 μm and had silver paste applied to their surfaces before being heated for 30 min at 600 ℃. The temperature dependence of permittivity (ε*r–*T*) was assessed using a precision LCR meter (TH2827C, Changzhou Tonghui Electronic Co., China) with a heating rate of 2 ℃·min–1, at frequencies of 1, 10, 100, and 1000 kHz. The polarization hysteresis loops (*P–E*), bipolar strain (*S–E*), and unipolar strain (*S–E*) were measured using a ferroelectric testing instrument (aixACCT TF Analyzer 1000, Germany) at a frequency of 1 Hz. The *S–E* curves were tested after polarization. Electrical poling was conducted under an electric field of 4 kV·mm–1 at room temperature for 45 min in silicone oil. The quasistatic piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33) was measured using a Berlincourt meter (ZJ-3A, Institute of Acoustics, Chinese Academy of Sciences). The impedance and phase angle spectra at room temperature were determined using an impedance analyzer (HP 4294A, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The planar electromechanical coupling factor (*k*p) and the mechanical quality factor *Q*m were calculated by the resonance–antiresonance method following IEEE standards.

## 3 Results and discussion

The XRD patterns of LKNNS–6BZH ceramics are presented in Fig. 1(a). All ceramics show a typical perovskite structure without any impurity phases. With increasing the sintering temperature, the volatilization of the A-site alkali metal intensifies significantly, leading to a reduced crystal volume and causing the diffraction peak to shift to higher angles. The phase structure is evaluated by the diffraction peak in the range of 44.5°–46°. The peak intensity ratio of (200) and (002) being approximately 1:1 illustrates that LKNNS–6BZH ceramics prepared at different sintering temperatures demonstrate a multiphase coexistence [29]. Figure S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) displays the Rietveld refinement of LKNNS–6BZH ceramics. The crystal structure parameters and the fitted parameters are listed in Table S1 in the ESM. The LKNNS–6BZH ceramics exhibit R–T or R–O–T phase coexistence. Notably, the reasonable reliability factor *R*wp is lower than 15% in all ceramics, confirming the aforementioned phase structure identifications.

The bipolar strain curves of samples at 20 kV·cm–1 are shown in Fig. 1(d), and they exhibit asymmetry, particularly for those sintered at *T*sint = 1170 ℃ and 1180 ℃. The strain induced by a negative electrical field is much greater than that generated by a positive electrical field, a similarity observed in Sr-doped KNN-based ceramics [14]. Notably, the LKNNS–6BZH sample sintered at *T*sint = 1170 ℃ achieves a maximum electrostrain of approximately 0.2% at 20 kV·cm–1, with the * d33∗ value of 976 pm·V–1.

Figure 2(a) shows the bipolar strain of the LKNNS–6BZH ceramic sintered at T*sint = 1170 ℃ under electric field ranging from 20 to 40 kV·cm–1. As the electric field intensity escalates, the strain correspondingly increases, reaching a notable value of 0.32% at 40 kV·cm–1. Figure 2(b) presents a unipolar strain curve, exhibiting strains of 0.1% and 0.18% at 20 and 40 kV·cm–1, respectively. Figure 2(c) illustrates that the ceramics maintain a high * d33∗ value, exceeding 800 pm·V–1, within the electric field range from 20 to 40 kV·cm–1, while the  d33∗ calculated by unipolar strain ranges from 440 to 525 pm·V–1. Figure 2(d) demonstrates that ∆H* remains below ~25% under all electric fields. As the electric field increases, ∆*H* decreases and attains its minimum value of 12.5% at 40 kV·cm–1, which is half of that at 20 kV·cm–1.

The asymmetric *S–E* curves observed in the ceramics can be attributed to the presence of defect dipoles [14,20]. High-temperature sintering conditions led to the formation of A-site vacancies * VA′ and  VO∙∙ defects due to the volatilization of alkali metals, including Li, Na, and K. Simultaneously, Hf4+ and Zr4+ substituted for Nb5+, generating B-site vacancies  BNb′ and  VO∙∙ defects [34]. The defect equations are as Eqs. (1) and (2):

(1) 2AA×+OO×↔A2O↑+2VA′+VO⋅⋅

(2) 2BO2↔2BNb′+4OO×+VO⋅⋅

where A stands for Li, Na, and K; and B for Zr and Hf. Although the raw material contains Sb2O3, the Sb element is mainly in the form of Sb5+, and thus its effect is not considered [3]. The chemical compositions of the samples sintered at T*sint = 1140 ℃ and 1170 ℃ are presented in Table S2 in the ESM, and the contents of * VA′ and  VO∙∙, calculated based on the principle of electric valence balance, are demonstrated in Fig. 3(a). It is evident that a higher sintering temperature leads to a higher concentration of  VA′ and  VO∙∙. Figure 3(b) shows the EPR spectroscopies. It is commonly assumed that the sharp signals at g* = 2.003 are a result of single-electron-trapped * VO∙∙, and the intensity of the signal is related to the concentration of  VO∙∙. A strong resonance signal is detected, confirming the presence of  VO∙∙, which is a key factor for the emergence of defect dipoles. It is essential to note that, due to the constant doping amount and the non-volatility of Zr4+ and Hf4+, their influence on the  VO∙∙ concentration is assumed to remain the unchanged. After poling, two distinct defect dipoles, namely  VA′–VO∙∙ and  BNb′–VO∙∙, are formed in the system (Fig. 3(c)).

Figure 3(d) shows the Raman shift of the A1g (*v*1) mode, which is derived from Fig. S5 in the ESM. The A1g mode denotes the breath-type stretching of the oxygen octahedron, which is naturally influenced by A–O or B–O bonds [35]. The shift of the A1g mode to higher wavenumbers is a significant indicator of intensive lattice distortion that caused by the increased * VA′ and  VO∙∙ [36,37]. The shift in the A1g mode is mainly attributed to lattice collapse, a result of the volatilization of alkali metals (Fig. 1(a)) [33]. Concurrently, the formation of  VA′ may also contribute to this phenomenon (Fig. 3(a)) [38]. Notably, the volatilization of alkali metals can increase the amount of  VO∙∙, thereby weakening the B–O bond strength [39]. However, this impact is overshadowed by the more pronounced effect of lattice collapse, leading the A1g mode to shift towards higher wavenumbers. It is well known that the domains exhibit comparable switching behavior when subjected to an electric field of the same magnitude but with opposite signs. Figures 3(e) and 3(f) display the litho-model phase patterns of the LKNNS–6BZH ceramic sintered at T*sint = 1170 ℃ under ±5 V and ±15 V. The domains can be entirely switched under –5 V, while there is a partial switching of the domains under 5 V. A similar phenomenon occurred at the phase pattern under ±10 V (Fig. S6 in the ESM), but the domains are completely switched under ±15 V. This indicates that an extra electric field with a fixed orientation exists in the system, promoting polarization in the same direction and vice versa. It has been demonstrated that defect dipoles generate an internal bias electric field (*E*i) after poling, which is parallel to the poling direction and leads to similar results [14,20,40]. Therefore, the special phenomena mentioned above indicate the presence of a considerable number of defect dipoles in the system. Furthermore, the increased phase transition temperatures (Fig. 1(b)), the unsaturated *P–E* loops in the samples sintered at *T*sint = 1170 ℃ and 1180 ℃ (Fig. 1(c)), the increased coercive field (*E*C) and enhanced *Q*m (Figs. S2(g) and S2(h) in the ESM) also confirm the existence of defect dipoles. Defect dipoles are considered a major factor in the increased *E*C and *Q*m, while simultaneously obstructing polarization [34]. The contribution of defect dipoles to the strain is depicted in Fig. 4(a). Due to the stable orientation of *E*i, lattice stretching and an increase in strain occur when the direction of the negative electric field aligns with *E*i. Conversely, a positive electric field causes lattice contraction and a decrease in strain [12,14].

To achieve large strain in KNN-based ceramics, the critical factor is the construction multiphase coexistence at room temperature, which is typically realized in R–T or R–O–T multiphase coexistence [20,41]. Due to the reduction of Li content and a decreased K/Na ratio (Table S2 in the ESM), as shown in Fig. 4(b), the *T*R–T or *T*O–T increase with the enhanced sintering temperature [35,42]. As depicted in Fig. 4(c) and derived from Fig. S1 in the ESM, the proportion of the O phase increases with rising the sintering temperature, whereas the proportions of the R and T phases decrease. To further evaluate the influence of the phase structure on strain, the phase contrast and amplitude patterns of domain configuration in the LKNNS–6BZH samples sintered at *T*sint = 1140 ℃ and 1170 ℃ are examined by PFM, as illustrated in Figs. 4(d), 4(e), and Fig. S7 in the ESM. Due to the greater degree of multiphase coexistence, the domains in the ceramic sintered at *T*sint = 1170 ℃ are much smaller compared to those in the sample sintered at *T*sint = 1140 ℃. Specifically, the sample sintered at *T*sint = 1140 ℃ shows an irregular island-like microdomain configuration, whereas the microdomains are considerably decreased, and the isolated island-like nanodomains are significantly increased in the sample sintered at *T*sint = 1170 ℃. The low barrier for nanodomain switching in the ceramics sintered at *T*sint = 1170 ℃ contributes to a higher strain [43]. Considering the symmetry-conforming property of point defects, the reorientation of defect dipoles is more efficient when their orientation aligns with the matrix spontaneous polarization, as demonstrated by Geng *et al.* [14] through DFT calculation. The orientation of the * VA′–VO∙∙ and  BNb′–VO∙∙ defect dipoles matches the spontaneous polarization of the O and T phases, respectively. Reducing the R phase content improves the interaction between the defect dipoles and domains, potentially causing the distinctive asymmetric strain behavior and a giant strain in the samples sintered at T*sint = 1170 ℃ and 1180 ℃ [13,44].

Figures 4(f) and 4(g) show various relaxation indicators for LKNNS–6BZH ceramics, including the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the A1g (*v*1) vibration peak, diffuseness degree (*γ*), frequency dispersion (∆*T*m), and the deviation degree from the Curie–Weiss law (∆*T*), which are derived from Figs. S5 and S8 in the ESM [37,42]. Notably, all of these indices for LKNNS–6BZH exhibit improvement as the sintering temperature is raised. This enhancement in relaxation with higher sintering temperatures results in greater local heterogeneity and a higher concentration of nanodomain. This is consistent with the observation of smaller domains with greater relaxor degrees, as observed in Figs. 4(d) and 4(e) [45]. The effect of local heterogeneity on the material is shown in Fig. 4(h). In the sample sintered at *T*sint = 1140 ℃, a weak level of relaxation limited the ability to switch due to the presence of large macrodomains, consequently resulting in a relatively low strain. However, the sample sintered at *T*sint = 1170 ℃ shows a strong relaxation state, which optimally balanced the interplay between long-range ordering and local heterogeneity. The lowered energy barrier for nanodomain makes them more conducive to switching, thus promoting the switching of large ferroelectric domains, which leads to an impressive piezoelectric response [46]. Under low electric fields, the lattice stretching induced by the electric field is weak but adequate to activate nanodomain switching. This results in an increased extrinsic contribution, leading to a high ∆*H* of 25.32% at 20 kV·cm–1 [25]. As the electric field increases, the contribution of lattice stretching becomes more pronounced, while the effects of nanodomain and macrodomain switching remain relatively consistent. Consequently, ∆*H* decreases, reaching its minimum value of 12.5% at 40 kV·cm–1 (Fig. 2(d)).

The KNN-based ceramics are often characterized by a distinct phase structure, which typically results in limited temperature stability of strain, making them less suitable for practical application. Figures 5(a), 5(d), and Fig. S9 in the ESM illustrate the electrostrain properties of the LKNNS–6BZH ceramic sintered at *T*sint = 1170 ℃ under 20 and 40 kV·cm–1, evaluated from room temperature to 125 ℃. It is clear that the sample sintered at *T*sint = 1170 ℃ demonstrates remarkable temperature stability, regardless of variations in the electric field.

While both bipolar and unipolar strains show a slight fluctuation with increasing the temperature, the performance remains stable without any significant decrease compared to room temperature. When the temperature increases from room temperature to 100 ℃, the bipolar strain increases from 0.194% to 0.276% under 20 kV·cm–1, accompanied by a rise in the * d33∗ value from 976 to 1379 pm·V–1. Meanwhile, the unipolar strain gradually increases from 0.105% to 0.163% with a low ∆H* of 19.42%, and the piezoelectric value is found to increase from 525 to 817 pm·V–1. At 100 ℃, the bipolar and unipolar strains are 0.397% and 0.275% under 40 kV·cm–1, respectively, with corresponding * d33∗ are 992 and 642 pm·V–1 (Fig. S9 in the ESM and Fig. 5(d)). The ∆H* ranges from 9.73% to 13.98% during the temperature range from room temperature to 125 ℃ (Fig. 5(d)).

With increasing the temperature, the dipole moment of defect dipoles enhances, leading to a greater polarizability of these defect dipoles within a specific temperature range, as demonstrated in Fe/Cu-doped KNN ceramics and Nb-doped BNT-based materials [21,30]. The phase structure may transition from R–O–T to O–T–PC or T–PC multiphase coexistence, which is advantageous for increased electrostrain [14]. The nanodomain plays a crucial role by acting as “seeds” that promote macroscopic polarization rotation and enhance electric-field-induced strain [46,64]. Elevating the temperature makes domain switching easier, leading to an augmented contribution from polarization rotation and domain wall motion to the strain response [5].

As the temperature reaches 125 ℃, the decreased strain can be attributed to the dissociation of defect dipoles, the dominance of multiphase coexistence by the PC phase, and significantly increased local heterogeneity [14,30,42]. High temperature can disrupt the interaction between * VO⋅⋅ and  VA′/ BNb′, leading to the breakdown of defect dipoles [40]. Increased local heterogeneity can also contribute to reduce strain [42]. The decreased ∆H* is a result of enhanced dipole moment and local heterogeneity. The increased dipole moment leads to more significant lattice stretching, and nanodomain respond to the external field much faster than macroscopic domains [25,65].

Figure 6(a) illustrates the * d33∗ as a function of temperature for piezoelectric ceramics, distinguishing between N-type and T-type ceramics, representing non-textured and textured ceramics, respectively. It is evident that the  d33∗ of the LKNNS–6BZH ceramics remains higher than the value at room temperature even when the temperature is raised to 125 ℃, which is much superior to other piezoelectric ceramics. Figure 6(b) shows the values of ∆H* for different ceramics, with the LKNNS–6BZH ceramics exhibiting a low ∆*H* close to lead-based piezoelectric ceramics. This difference is especially noticeable when comparing them to BNT-based ceramics, which are known for their high electrostrain. In Fig. 6(c), the graph displays the relationship between bipolar strain and * d33∗ for different ceramics. The LKNNS–6BZH ceramics exhibit significantly higher bipolar strain and  d33∗ values compared to other ceramics when subjected to a low electric field. While some BNT-based ceramics may have a higher strain, the electric field required for them is three to four times greater, making them impractical for commercial use.

## 4 Conclusions

By combining defect engineering, phase engineering, and relaxation engineering, an exceptional achievement of ultrahigh strain with remarkable temperature stability and a low ∆*H* has been realized in the LKNNS–6BZH ceramic at low electric fields. The ceramic maintains a * d33∗ value larger than 800 pm·V–1 under 20–40 kV·cm–1, with an exceptional strain of 0.194% and  d33∗ of 976 pm·V–1 achieved in the sample sintered at T*sint = 1170 ℃ at room temperature under 20 kV·cm–1. The electrostrain remains high even as the temperature increases up to 125 ℃, with an ultrahigh electrostrain of 0.276% and * d33∗ of 1379 pm·V–1 observed at 100 ℃ under 20 kV·cm–1. Remarkably, ∆H* remains below 25.32% under 20–40 kV·cm–1 from room temperature to 125 ℃, with the lowest value of 9.73% achieved at 125 ℃ under 40 kV·cm–1. This superior overall performance can be attributed to increased polarization caused by defect dipoles, optimization of the phase structure, and balancing of local heterogeneity and long-range ordering. This work provides an effective strategy for designing KNN-based materials suitable for a wide temperature range and high-displacement piezoelectric actuator applications.
