## 1 Introduction

The radar stealth capability of high-temperature components in advanced aircraft necessitates the development of electromagnetic (EM) wave-absorbing materials with exceptional thermal stability [1−3]. Currently, EM absorbing materials mainly include magnetic materials (ferrites [4,5], magnetic metals [6,7], etc.) and carbon-based materials (graphene [8], carbon nanotubes [9], etc.). Magnetic materials are prone to magnetic loss and decreased absorption performance at high temperatures. Carbon-based materials suffer from insufficient oxidation resistance in high-temperature aerobic environments, significantly reducing their ability to attenuate EM waves. Ceramic materials are ideal microwave absorbing materials that can be served in extreme environments due to good antioxidation properties and excellent chemical corrosion resistance [10]. Among ceramic materials, high-entropy diborides (HEBs) have garnered significant attention because of their tunable electrical conductivity and excellent chemical stability [11−14]. However, achieving an optimal balance between oxidation resistance and EM wave absorption for HEBs remains a significant challenge.

In high-temperature environments, oxidation resistance plays a critical role in ensuring the long-term stability of materials. Previous studies have shown that the multielement alloying effect in HEBs enabled these materials to exhibit remarkable stability, providing a unique advantage in high-temperature applications. For example, Tallarita *et al.* [15] reported that (Hf0.2Mo0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)B2 demonstrated an exceptionally low oxidation weight gain rate at temperatures as high as 1473 K, significantly outperforming its individual diboride components, such as TiB2, HfB2, and TaB2. Similarly, Guo *et al.* [16] reported that (Zr0.2Hf0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)B2 exhibited superior oxidation resistance up to 1400 °C, surpassing the performance of most constituent metal diborides, including TiB2, CrB2, ZrB2, NbB2, and TaB2. Despite the promising high-temperature stability of HEBs, their EM wave absorption properties are often affected by the formation of oxide layers under high-temperature conditions. Related studies have indicated that as the temperature increased, the EM wave absorbing properties of materials can be significantly altered. For example, Qiao *et al*. [17] systematically investigated the EM wave absorption properties of high-entropy (Mo0.25Cr0.25Ti0.25V0.25)3AlC2 (MCTV) after oxidation at various temperatures, ranging from room temperature to 800 °C. The results revealed that the EM wave absorption behavior of the MCTV-600 and MCTV-800 samples differed significantly from that of the MCTV and MCTV-400 samples, primarily because of phase composition changes. Specifically, as the temperature increased, oxide layers progressively formed on the material surface. The formation of oxide layers significantly affects the EM properties, particularly by altering the conductivity and dielectric constant. These issues were critical parameters to influence the EM wave absorption. Therefore, achieving excellent EM wave absorption and thermal stability in HEBs is highly important.

However, the EM wave attenuation capacity of most absorbers utilizing HEBs is restricted because of their impedance mismatch. Recent studies have demonstrated that component modulation can effectively enhance the EM wave absorption performance of materials. For example, Wang *et al.* [18] synthesized carbonized cellulose paper/HEAs-Mn2.15 with emblematic shell–core heterointerfaces by manipulating the molar ratio of the component elements. Its minimum reflection loss reached −51.35 dB at an ultrathin thickness of 1.03 mm, achieving efficient EM wave absorption. Moreover, Cheng *et al*. [19] significantly improved the microwave absorption performance of polymetallic sulfide (PMS)/acetylene black (ACET) composites by modulating the ACET content. For HEBs, Zhang *et al*. [20] prepared an efficient and broadband EM wave absorbing material, high-entropy (Cr,Zr,Hf,Nb,Ta)B2, with a minimum reflection loss of −56.2 dB at 8.48 GHz by incorporating Cr. Therefore, reasonable component modulation is expected to achieve excellent EM wave absorption performance and thermal stability for HEB.

HEBs show wide application potential due to the large composition adjustable space. At present, the most studied system of HEBs were (Hf0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2Ti0.2Nb0.2)B2 series [21−25]. Therefore, completing the study of HEBs with different components is highly important. However, studies on HEBs containing the VB2 phase are scarce [26,27], which may be related to the fact that the introduction of V increases the difficulty of single-phase solid-solution formation. To mitigate these issues, three novel VB2-containing high-entropy (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2X0.2)B2 (X = Mo, Ti, and Hf) materials were prepared in this work. Among them, Mo, Hf, and Ti were selected as variable elements, mainly on the basis of the following considerations: The introduction of Mo can significantly improve the electrical conductivity of the materials and then increase the dielectric constant of the materials to optimize its absorption performance in a specific frequency range. The introduction of Hf can improve the thermal stability and oxidation resistance, and its good electrical conductivity also plays an important role in enhancing the absorption capacity of EM waves. The introduction of Ti can improve the conductivity and oxidation resistance of the materials, which is expected to synergistically improve the absorption performance and thermal stability.

This work utilized the ultrafast high-temperature sintering (UHS) method to prepare three novel high-entropy (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2X0.2)B2 (X = Mo, Ti, and Hf) materials. By adjusting the components and triggering various EM loss mechanisms, such as defect polarization, dipole polarization, and conduction loss, the EM wave absorption performance of the (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2X0.2)B2 system was improved. The (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2Ti0.2)B2 (HEB-Ti) sample exhibited excellent EM wave absorption performance. Its maximum EAB covered the entire low-frequency range from 2 to 8 GHz, and the RLmin can reach –40.7 dB at 7.68 GHz with a thickness of 2.0 mm. In addition, the materials exhibited excellent thermal stability and can withstand temperatures up to 550 °C in air and temperatures up to 1000 °C in an argon gas environment. Related studies could provide important reference for the preparation and development of EM wave-absorbing materials in high-temperature environments.

### 2.2 Material preparation

Ultrafast high-temperature sintering (UHS) is an innovative sintering method that can efficiently and rapidly prepare high-performance materials. It has significant features such as a uniform temperature distribution, high heating and cooling rates, and high sintering temperatures [28]. This rapid heat treatment can effectively prevent phase separation and excessive grain growth and control the chemical composition and microstructure, thereby improving the material properties [29,30].

In the experiment, three new types of high-entropy (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2X0.2)B2 (X = Mo, Ti, and Hf) were prepared via a UHS apparatus (1030D, Shenzhen Zhongke Jingyan Technology Co., Ltd., China), and the detailed preparation process is shown in Fig. 1. First, these transition metal powders were equimolarly weighed and then well mixed with 20% excess boron powder according to the ratio of the reaction equation [31]. Detailed information on the molar ratios of the raw reactants is listed in Table 2. The transition metal powders and boron powders were then mixed and ground by hand in an agate mortar for 6 h. Afterward, the mixed powders were pressed into a green body of *ϕ*10 mm in diameter with a pressure of 10 MPa for 1 min. The green body was then placed between two pellets of the graphite heater of the UHS apparatus. An alternating current was subsequently applied to both ends of the pellets to heat the samples to 1900 °C within seconds under the protection of argon gas. During this process, borothermal reduction occurred. The temperatures were measured by an infrared radiation thermometer (E1RH, Fluke, USA). After the samples were heated at 1900 °C for 10 min, the current was subsequently decreased, allowing the samples to cool to ambient temperature. The as-obtained samples were collected and then ground into powders for subsequent testing.

### 2.3 Material characterization

The phase composition of the samples was tested via a powder X-ray diffractometer (PXRD; Advance D8, Brucker, USA) using Cu Kα radiation. The surface compositions were analyzed via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB 250XI, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The morphology and elemental distributions were investigated by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Regulus 8230, Hitachi, Japan) with an energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS) system and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200X, Thermo Scientific, USA). Vacancy defect information was obtained via an electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR; EMXplus-6/1, Bruker, USA). The thermal stability of the samples in air and Ar was evaluated via a thermogravimetric analyzer (TG; 209F1, Netzsch, Germany). The EM parameters from 2 to 18 GHz were measured via a vector network analyzer (N5234A, Agilent, USA). Before the measurement, each sample and paraffin wax were mixed at a mass ratio of 7:3 and compacted into a toroidal shape (*ϕ*out = 7.00 mm, *ϕ*in = 3.04 mm). Paraffin was used as the matrix materials to determine the real EM wave absorption capacity because the paraffin framework was lossless for incident EM waves [32].

### 3.1 Phase composition and microstructure

Figure 2 shows the typical XRD patterns of (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2X0.2)B2 (X = Mo, Ti, and Hf) and the variation in the diffraction peaks of the (111), (110), and (101) crystal planes. As shown in Fig. 2(a), all samples exhibited similar diffraction peaks in the range of 10°–90°. These diffraction peaks corresponded to the AlB2-type (*P*6/*mmm* spatial group) phase, which is consistent with the results reported by Liu *et al.* [23]. No diffraction peaks of oxide impurities were observed in any of the samples, indicating that they were single-phase solid solutions. An analysis of the (111), (110), and (101) diffraction peaks depicted in Fig. 2(b) revealed that the peak positions shifted to different degrees. Compared with the peak position of HEB-Mo, there was no significant shift in the peak position of HEB-Ti. In contrast, the peak position of HEB-Hf shifted to the left by approximately 0.9°. The difference in peak position was caused mainly by the different atomic radii of Mo, Ti, and Hf. A larger atomic radius increased the lattice spacing (*d*) [33]. The brag equation showed that when the lattice spacing (*d*) was larger, the angle 2*θ* was smaller [34]. The atomic radii of Mo, Ti, and Hf were 1.40, 1.45, and 1.59, respectively [35]. Therefore, the peak position shift of HEB-Hf was more significant than that of HEB-Ti. Overall, the change in the XRD peak may be related to various defects, such as lattice distortion, dislocations, and vacancies, which are introduced by heteroatom doping and high-temperature synthesis processes.

To further investigate the elemental composition, the as-synthesized samples were analyzed via XPS. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the full XPS spectra of HEB-Mo, HEB-Ti, and HEB-Hf samples revealed the presence of five different transition metal elements. Specifically, the HEB-Mo sample contained V, Nb, Ta, Zr, and Mo. The HEB-Ti sample contained V, Nb, Ta, Zr, and Ti. The HEB-Hf samples contained V, Nb, Ta, Zr, and Hf. These results further confirmed the successful synthesis of the high-entropy (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2X0.2)B2 (X = Mo, Ti, and Hf) system. The high-resolution V 2p, Nb 3d, Ta 4f, Zr 3d, and B 1s spectra were further analyzed to assess the influence of Mo, Ti, and Hf on the surface chemical states of (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2X0.2)B2. As shown in Figs. 3(b)–3(f), the XPS peaks corresponding to V 2p, Nb 3d, Ta 4f, Zr 3d, and B 1s all shifted toward lower binding energies, indicating changes in the chemical state of the surface elements. This shift indicated an increase in the electron density around the atoms, which may influence the lattice constants [36]. According to the XRD results, an increase in the radius of doped atoms can lead to local lattice expansion, altering the electronic environment of the surrounding atoms and potentially inducing lattice distortion. The observed shift of the XPS peaks toward lower binding energies was consistent with the peak shift to lower angles in the XRD patterns. These results further indicated that variations in the high-entropy components induced structural modifications within the material system.

Figures 4(a)–4(c) show the morphology of all the as-synthesized samples. There were irregularly shaped particles and agglomerates in all the samples. With the change in component element content, the grain size clearly increased, accompanied by increased porosity. The formation of intragranular pores may be attributed to rapid grain growth during the sintering process. In this scenario, the migration rate of the grain boundaries was faster than that of the pores, leading to the retention of the pores within the grains [37]. Notably, the presence of appropriate pores in HEB-Ti can improve interface impedance matching, ensuring that more EM waves can enter the materials and be absorbed rather than reflected [38]. The elemental distribution of the samples was characterized via SEM with EDS. As shown in Figs. 4(d)–4(f), the five metal elements in all the samples were homogeneously distributed at the micrometer scale. In addition, no elemental segregation could be observed in the entire scanning area, indicating good element distributions of (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2X0.2)B2 (X = Mo, Ti, and Hf). Combining the XRD and SEM-EDS analyses, it could be concluded that single-phase (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2X0.2)B2 (X = Mo, Ti, and Hf) ceramics with different pore numbers were successfully prepared via component modulation and UHS methods.

The microstructure evolution of the samples was further analyzed via high-resolution TEM (HRTEM), as shown in Figs. 5(a)–5(c). The d*-spacings of the (100) lattice planes for HEB-Mo, HEB-Ti, and HEB-Hf were approximately 0.263, 0.265, and 0.269 nm, respectively. The results indicated that an increase in the atomic radius led to a corresponding increase in the lattice spacing (*d*), which was consistent with the XRD results (Fig. 2(b)). The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns with high symmetry shown in the insets of Figs. 5(a)–5(c) further confirmed the formation of hexagonal structures in all the samples. In addition, defects in the lattice, such as lattice stacking, deformation, disappearance, and point defects, gradually increase with increasing atomic radius. These defects are caused by metal atoms with different radii and masses in high-entropy systems. Figures 5(d)–5(f) show the strong resonance peaks of (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2X0.2)B2 (X = Mo, Ti, and Hf), further indicating the formation of numerous dangling bonds and defects with unpaired electrons in the structure [39,40]. Compared with the HEB-Mo sample, the HEB-Ti and HEB-Hf samples presented stronger peak intensities. The introduction of Ti and Hf clearly caused the electronic transition, creating more dangling bonds and unpaired electrons. These dangling bonds and defects could act as dipole polarization centers to further enhance EM wave attenuation by inducing dipole polarization [41]. Overall, the pore structure and defect density of (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2X0.2)B2 (X = Mo, Ti, and Hf) could be effectively controlled via modulation of the high-entropy component.

### 3.3 EM wave absorption performance

To evaluate the EM wave absorption capability, the reflection loss (RL) values of the samples were calculated via Eqs. (3) and (4) [44]:

(3) RL=20log⁡|Zin−Z0Zin+Z0|

(4) Zin=Z0|μrεr|tanh(j2πfdμrεrc)

where *Z*in, *Z*0, *ε*r, and *μ*r denote the input characteristic impedance, free space impedance, relative complex permittivity, and relative complex permeability, respectively. Moreover, *d*, *c*, *f*, and j represent the absorber thickness, ray speed, frequency, and imaginary number units, respectively. In general, an RL value of −10 dB means that 90% of the incident EM wave radiation is absorbed, and the corresponding bandwidth indicates an EAB [45].

Figure 7 shows the frequency dependence of the 3D RL plots and 2D color mapping of the EAB for HEB-Mo, HEB-Ti, and HEB-Hf. As shown in Figs. 7(a) and 7(b), HEB-Mo exhibited an RLmin of −11.3 dB at 11.28 GHz with a thickness of 2.0 mm, and its maximum EAB was 0.7 GHz. In contrast, HEB-Ti demonstrated superior EM wave absorption performance, achieving an RLmin of −40.7 dB at 7.68 GHz with a thickness of 2.0 mm and a maximum EAB of 2.3 GHz (Figs. 7(c) and 7(d)). For HEB-Hf, the RLmin reached −24.1 dB at 12.64 GHz with a thickness of 1.5 mm, and the corresponding maximum EAB was 2.6 GHz (Figs. 7(e) and 7(f)). HEB-Ti clearly demonstrated the best overall EM wave absorption performance because of its low RL, wide EAB, and thinness. Overall, this study achieved tunable EM wave absorption in high-entropy (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2X0.2)B2 (X = Mo, Ti, and Hf) via modulation of the high-entropy component. According to SEM, TEM, and EPR analyses, component modulation can introduce defects that facilitate the establishment of polarization centers. This may be an important factor in optimizing impedance matching and improving the EM wave attenuation ability.

(6) α=2πfc×μ′′ε′′−μ′ε′+(μ′′ε′′−μ′ε′)2+(μ′ε′′−μ′′ε′)2

A higher attenuation constant means that the material’s ability to convert EM wave energy into other forms of energy is greater [64]. Among the analyzed samples, HEB-Ti exhibited the best impedance matching alongside a moderate attenuation capability. Owing to the synergistic effect of these two factors, HEB-Ti thus exhibited superior EM wave absorption performance.

Therefore, the relative complex permeability (*μ*r = * μ′+ j μ″) and relative complex permittivity (ε*r = * ε′ + j ε″) of the samples were analyzed in the frequency range of 2–18 GHz. As discussed above, HEB-Ti demonstrated superior EM wave absorption performance due to relatively moderate dielectric loss and good EM impedance matching. The EM wave absorption mechanisms of HEB-Ti are shown in Fig. 14. First, the presence of appropriate pores in HEB-Ti improved interface impedance matching, ensuring that more EM waves entered the materials and were absorbed rather than reflected. Moreover, the formation of vacancy defects in HEB-Ti led to the disruption of the original equilibrium states of polarization. Local charge separation and dipole formation under alternating electric fields increase the number of polarization loss sites. These defective sites generate many dipoles, which undergo dipole polarization in the presence of incident EM waves [75]. As a result, the EM wave absorption performance and impedance matching of HEB-Ti could be effectively improved.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, three novel high-entropy materials (V0.2Nb0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2X0.2)B2 (X = Mo, Ti, and Hf) were successfully synthesized within 10 min via a UHS apparatus at 1900 °C via Joule heating. In these material systems, compositional modulation significantly influences the microstructural characteristics of these materials. Notably, HEB-Ti exhibited an appropriate number of microstructural defects, including lattice dislocations, stacking faults, and point defects. These defects trigger multiple dielectric loss mechanisms (defect polarization, dipole polarization, and conduction loss), thereby effectively absorbing EM waves. Moreover, the effect of high-entropy component on the magnetic loss of the system was relatively small, but the effect on the dielectric loss was larger. The HEB-Ti sample exhibited strong absorption properties due to relatively moderate dielectric loss and good EM impedance matching. Moreover, because of its relatively moderate attenuation constant, it could achieve the maximum penetration of the EM wave and the minimum reflection after absorbing wave. As a result, HEB-Ti demonstrated superior EM wave absorption performance. The maximum EAB covered the entire low-frequency range of 2–8 GHz, with a RLmin of −40.7 at 7.68 GHz and a thickness of 1.5 mm. In addition, HEB-Ti exhibited excellent thermal stability and can withstand temperatures up to 550 °C in air and 1000 °C in an argon gas environment. Owing to its excellent thermal stability and outstanding EM absorption performance, HEB-Ti ceramics are promising candidates for EM wave absorption in harsh environments.
