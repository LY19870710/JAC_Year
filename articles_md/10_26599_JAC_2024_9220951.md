## 1 Introduction

Ti3AlC2 is one of the most attractive members of the ternary nanolaminated MAX phase family (M is an early transition metal; A is an A-group element; X indicates C, N, or B) [1−5] because of its desirable combination of properties at both room and high temperatures, such as easy machinability, high electrical and thermal conductivity, good oxidation and corrosion resistance, nonsusceptibility to thermal shock, and crack healing ability [1,5−10]. To promote its engineering applications, several strategies, such as second-phase doping, microstructure modification, and solid solution design, have been adopted to tailor the properties of Ti3AlC2. Among the above strategies, solid solution design has been proven to be an effective approach for tuning material properties. To date, Ti-, Al-, and C-site-substituted solid solutions, such as (Ti1−*x*Cr*x*)3AlC2 (*x* = 0.33) [11], (Ti0.5V0.5)3AlC2 (*x* = 0.5) [12], (Ti1−*x*Zr*x*)3AlC2 (*x* = 0.15–0.82) [13], Ti3(Al1−*x*Si*x*)C2 (*x* = 0–1) [14,15], Ti3(Al1−*x*Sn*x*)C2 (*x* = 0.2) [16,17], Ti2AlC1−*x*N*x* (*x* = 0.25–0.76) [18−20], (Ti1/3V1/6Zr1/6Nb1/6Ta1/6)2AlC1−*x*N*x* [21], and Ti3Al(C0.5N0.5)2 (referred to as Ti3AlCN) [22−24], have been experimentally synthesized and characterized. The effects of solid solutions on mechanical, thermal, electronic, and optical properties have been confirmed. For example, the solid solution strengthening effect was demonstrated in Ti3(Al1−*x*Si*x*)C2 as *x* > 0.15, and the electrical resistivities of Ti3(Al1−*x*Si*x*)C2 increased slightly with increasing *x* value [14]. The calculated lattice thermal conductivities of Ti3(Al1−*x*Si*x*)C2 decreased gradually with increasing Si content [15]. Previous studies [22−24] have shown that, compared with Ti3AlC2, Ti3AlCN has improved mechanical properties and thermal conductivity.

The replacement of C with N to form X-site MAX phase solid solutions is more attractive because the chemical bonding characteristics of N are similar to those of C. Substituting C with N changes the lattice parameters of carbonitride MAX phases but does not alter their crystal structures (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). Correspondingly, changes in the lattice parameters of carbonitride MAX phases inevitably affect their physical properties. Several carbonitride MAX phase powders and bulk materials have been successfully prepared. Table 1 summarizes the processing methods and the resulting products. Most carbonitride MAX phase powders have been commonly used as precursors to prepare two-dimensional carbonitride MXenes through chemical etching methods. However, for dense materials, only 211-type carbonitride Ti2AlC1−*x*N*x* bulk materials with a wide range of *x* values (*x* = 0–1) have been systematically characterized and demonstrated to have tunable physical and electrical properties [18−20]. For example, the values of the lattice parameter of Ti2AlC1−*x*N*x* decreased linearly from 3.06 to 2.99 Å as *x* increased from 0 to 1, and their resistivities at room temperature could be tuned in the range of 40–60 μΩ·cm as *x* varied from 0.25 to 0.9.

For 312-type carbonitride Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solutions, theoretical work has shown that their properties can be tuned by adjusting the N content [33,34]. Manoun *et al*. [34] reported that replacing C with N resulted in a decrease in both the lattice parameters and bulk modulus of Ti3AlCN. However, only bulk Ti3AlCN (*y* = 1 for Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y*) has been synthesized and characterized. Compared with Ti3AlC2, it not only exhibited improved mechanical properties and thermal conductivity but also demonstrated superior catalytic performance [22−24,35,36]. Radovic *et al*. [23] reported that replacing C with N in Ti3AlCN decreased its unit cell volume and thus increased its elastic modulus and phonon thermal conductivity. Ti3AlCN exhibited a greater elastic modulus and phonon conductivity than did Ti3AlC2, i.e., 330 vs. 297 GPa and 34 vs. 18 W/(m·K) at room temperature, respectively. More interestingly, Ti3AlCN acted as an efficient catalyst for MgH2 decomposition and recovery and dramatically enhanced the hydrogen storage properties of MgH2, comparable to many two-dimensional MXene nanomaterials [35]. It was also reported that Ti3AlCN exhibited an attractive catalytic performance for hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) generation, with a H2O2 yield of 835.6 mmol/(g·h), which is higher than the 810.6 mmol/(g·h) for Ti3AlC2.

Based on the above analysis, it is reasonable to believe that changing the N content in Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solutions not only tunes their properties to satisfy engineering requirements but also widens the MAX phase family for fundamental studies and applications. To date, no reports have been on the preparation of dense Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* (with varying N contents) bulks. Our previous work [29] demonstrated that Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solutions with *y* = 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, 1.1, and 1.2 could be achieved by optimizing the processing parameters at temperatures of 1430–1500 °C. However, the synthesis and properties of dense Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solution bulks have not been investigated, and the influence of the N content on the properties of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* is not clear.

Systematic investigations on the properties of dense Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* bulk materials with varying N contents have received little attention, possibly because of the difficulty of synthesizing high-purity Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* bulk materials. Taking Ti3AlCN as an example, its formation region in the Ti–Al–C–N quaternary phase diagram was narrow, accompanied by other intermediate phases such as TiN, TiC, Ti2AlN, and Ti2AlC [37]. The experimental results revealed that high-purity Ti3AlCN was difficult to obtain during pressureless sintering of a Ti/AlN/C mixture at 1500 °C because of the presence of TiN, TiC, and Ti2AlC*x*N*y* impurities [30]. In addition, theoretical work has shown that the stability of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* decreases as more C atoms are replaced by N atoms [33], indicating the difficulty of synthesizing high-purity Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solutions.

In the present study, we aimed to prepare high-purity Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* (*y* = 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0) dense bulks by hot pressing their powder counterparts. A systematic study on the composition and mechanical, thermal, and electrical properties of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* was carried out. The effects of N on the properties of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solutions are discussed.

### 2.1 Material preparation

Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* (*y* = 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0) powders were used to prepare dense bulks in the present study. The preparation of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* powders has been described in detail elsewhere [29]. Briefly, Ti, Al, AlN, and C (graphite) powders were mixed with molar ratios of 3Ti:*y*AlN:(1.1 − *y*)Al:(2 − *y*)C (*y* = 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0) for 10 h and then cold pressed to form compacts (*ϕ*50 mm × 5 mm). The compacts were pressurelessly sintered at 1430 °C for 1 h in an Ar atmosphere to produce Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* (*y* = 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0) samples. Extra Al was used in the mixtures to avoid the loss of Al at the sintering temperature. The prepared samples were pulverized and then sieved with a 200-meshed sieve to obtain Ti3AlC1.7N0.3, Ti3AlC1.5N0.5, Ti3AlC1.2N0.8, and Ti3AlCN powders. The morphologies of the prepared powders with a layered structure are depicted in Fig. S2 in the ESM.

To obtain dense samples at relatively low sintering temperatures and avoid the decomposition of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* powders during hot pressing, a temperature of 1280 °C was selected on the basis of the hot-pressing conditions for preparing dense Ti3AlC2 bulks in our group. Dense Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* (*y* = 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0) bulks were prepared by hot pressing the as-prepared Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* powders in a hot-pressing furnace (ZT-40-21Y, Shanghai Chenhua Furnace Plant, China) at 1280 °C under 32 MPa for 1–3 h in Ar. The heating rate was 15 °C/min. For comparison, dense Ti3AlC2 samples prepared by hot pressing a 3Ti/1.1Al/2C mixture at 1350 °C under 30 MPa for 1 h in Ar were used in the present study.

### 2.2 Property measurements

The dense bulks were machined into bars by a wire electrical discharge machine (STX-202A, Shenyang Kejing Automation Equipment Co., Ltd., China). The bars were ground with sandpapers up to 2000 grit and then cleaned in an ultrasonic bath with ethanol. Bar samples with dimensions of 4 mm × 3 mm × 36 mm were used to measure the flexural strength. The tensile surfaces of the bars for flexural strength measurement were polished to 1 μm with diamond paste. The three-point bending test was performed in a test machine (WDW-100E, Jinan Shijin Co., Ltd., China). The span size was 30 mm, and the crosshead speed was 0.5 mm/min. Three bars were tested to determine the average value of the flexural strength. The Vickers hardness measurement was performed using a hardness tester (TH700, Shanghai Shuangxu Electronics Co., Ltd., China). The hardness indentations were operated at a load of 0.5–2 kg and a dwell time of 15 s. Six measurements in different areas were taken under the same load to obtain an average hardness value.

The thermal expansion of solid solution samples with a diameter of 4 mm and a length of 10 mm was measured under flowing Ar from 25 to 1000 °C via a dilatometer (DIL805L, TA Instruments, USA). The heating rate was 15 °C/min. The coefficient of linear expansion (*α*) was calculated from *α* = Δ*L*/(*L* × Δ*T*), where *L* is the original length of the sample, Δ*L* is the change in length, and Δ*T* is the temperature change.

The electrical conductivity of the samples was measured via the four-point probe method with a tester (Loresta-GX MCP-T700, Nittoseiko, Japan).

### 2.3 Characterization

The content of nitrogen in the Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solution phases was determined via an oxygen‒nitrogen‒hydrogen analyzer (ONH836, American LECO Co., Ltd., USA). The polished surfaces of the samples were etched with a NHO3 + HCl acid solution with a volume ratio of NHO3:HCl:H2O = 1:1:1. After that, the etched samples were rinsed with deionized water and then dried in a vacuum oven (DZF-6050, Shanghai Wujin Aotomatuin Equipment Co., Ltd., China) at 60 °C. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images taken from the etched surfaces of the samples were used to measure the grain size via Nano Measurer software (Version 1.2). To obtain an average grain size, more than 100 grains in different etched areas were measured. The phase composition of the sintered samples was analyzed via X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis via an XRD diffractometer (Rigaku Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. The microstructure of the prepared materials was characterized with a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; JSM-7001F, JEOL, Japan) equipped with an energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS; X-Max, Oxford Instruments, UK) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2 F30, FEI, USA) at an acceleration voltage of 300 kV.

### 3.1 Preparation and characterization of Ti<sub>3</sub>AlC<sub>2−<i>y</i></sub>N<sub><i>y</i></sub>

The hot-pressing conditions and properties of the prepared solid solution samples are listed in Table 2. The theoretical density increases from 4.29 to 4.43 g/cm3 with increasing N content in the solid solutions. Dense solid solution samples with relative densities greater than 98% can be obtained after hot pressing for 1–3 h. In the present study, the plastic deformation of powders at high sintering temperatures contributed to the densification of the solid solution samples. It has been reported that Ti3AlC2 deformed plastically without failure above 1050 °C, suggesting that the brittle to ductile transition temperature for polycrystalline Ti3AlC2 is 1050 °C [38]. Notably, the dwelling time influences the density of a sample. A dense Ti3AlC1.7N0.3 sample with a relative density of 98.6% was obtained by hot pressing for only 1 h, whereas dense Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* samples (*y* = 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0) with relative densities greater than 98% could be obtained after 2–3 h of hot pressing (Table 2). The above results indicate that introducing N induces lattice distortion in solid solution grains. The degree of lattice distortion in the Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solutions increases with increasing N content. Lattice distortion may hinder plastic deformation at 1280 °C, thus correspondingly inhibiting the densification process. This is the reason why Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* samples with high N contents require a longer period for densification. Therefore, 1 h-hot-pressed Ti3AlC1.7N0.3, 2 h-hot-pressed Ti3AlC1.5N0.5, 2.5 h-hot-pressed Ti3AlC1.2N0.8, and 3 h-hot-pressed Ti3AlCN bulks were used for the following study.

XRD is an effective approach to monitor changes in the *a*- and *c*-lattice parameters (*a*- and *c*-LPs) and interplanar spacing of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y*. The replacement of C with N in Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* results in the continuous decrease in the lattice parameters (Fig. 1(b)). For example, *a*-LP decreases from 3.068 to 3.041 Å, and *c*-LP decreases from 18.518 to 18.391 Å as the *y* value increases from 0.3 to 1.0. Lattice parameters have a significant effect on the physical properties of materials. Therefore, the continuous variation in *a*- and *c*-LPs inevitably affects the physical properties of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y*, such as the theoretical density, thermal expansion, and electrical conductivity, which will be discussed in Sections 3.2–3.4.

In addition, the interplanar spacings of the (104) and (105) planes decrease as the *y* value increases from 0.3 to 1.0, corresponding to a (104) peak shifting from 2*θ* of 39.02° for Ti3AlC1.7N0.3 to 39.56° for Ti3AlCN and a (105) peak shifting from 2*θ* of 41.80° for Ti3AlC1.7N0.3 to 42.34° for Ti3AlCN (right column in Fig. 1(a)). Increasing the N concentration induces a corresponding high-angle shift in the Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* peak due to lattice distortion. The same observation for the diffraction peak shift induced by the substitution of C by N has also been observed in Ti2Al(C1−*x*N*x*) solid solutions [19].

The prepared Ti3AlC1.7N0.3, Ti3AlC1.5N0.5, Ti3AlC1.2N0.8, and Ti3AlCN samples have similar microstructures. Their microstructure is mainly composed of plate-like grains with a layered structure (Figs. 2 and 3). The above dense samples have average grain sizes of 6.02±0.11, 6.62±0.32, 7.21±0.04, and 7.96±0.17 μm, respectively (Table 2). These results indicate that prolonging the sintering time does not lead to significant grain growth because lattice distortion inhibits grain growth, especially in high-N solid solutions. Studies on high-entropy materials have demonstrated that the lattice distortion induced by a solid solution can hinder atomic diffusion, thus slowing the grain growth rate and maintaining fine grains [39]. In addition, compared with the higher sintering temperature of 1430 °C used for preparing Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* powders [29], the relatively low temperature of 1280 °C is not effective in promoting grain growth during hot pressing.

Figure 3 shows SEM micrographs of the fractured surfaces of the Ti3AlC1.7N0.3, Ti3AlC1.5N0.5, and Ti3AlCN samples. The fracture surfaces present a plate-like morphology of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* grains (Figs. 3(a)–3(d)). Most Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* grains exhibited a transgranular fracture mode, with layered and cleavage features induced by crack propagation perpendicular and parallel to the basal planes. A high-magnification image shows the nanolaminate structure of the Ti3AlC1.7N0.3 grains (Fig. 3(b)). The fracture surface exhibited a transgranular fracture mode due to the propagation of cracks within the grains. The delamination and buckling features are responsible for the micro area plastic deformation in the prepared Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* samples. Delamination and buckling are typical deformation features detected in MAX phases [2,3].

To characterize the detailed structure of the Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* samples in detail, Ti3AlC1.5N0.5 and Ti3AlCN were selected as representative samples and observed via high-resolution TEM (HRTEM). HRTEM images of Ti3AlC1.5N0.5 and Ti3AlCN show lattice fringes (Figs. 4(a) and 4(c)). The grain boundaries in Ti3AlCN are shown in Fig. 4(c). The grain boundaries are clean, without glass phases, which may contribute to the high-temperature properties and attractive electrical and thermal properties of polycrystalline MAX ceramics. HRTEM images present the ordered arrangement of atoms with periodic spacing between each other (Figs. 4(b) and 4(d)). The measured interplanar spacing is approximately 0.2656 nm, corresponding to the (100) plane of Ti3AlC1.5N0.5 (Fig. 4(b)). However, the (100) interplanar spacing of Ti3AlCN is approximately 0.2640 nm (Fig. 4(d)), indicating that increasing the N content in Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* leads to shrinkage of the (100) interplanar spacing. This result is in good agreement with the XRD results (Fig. 1(a)), which reveal a high-angle shift in the (100) diffraction peak of Ti3AlCN compared with that of Ti3AlC1.5N0.5. The inset in Fig. 4(d) shows fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns with six hexagonal diffraction spots, indicating the typical hexagonal symmetry of Ti3AlCN. Vacancies such as point defects are observed at the bottom of Fig. 4(d) (marked with dashed circles), which influence the physical properties of Ti3AlCN. Figure 4(e) clearly depicts the arrangement of Ti and Al atoms in Ti3AlCN, revealing that one Al atomic layer and three Ti atomic layers are arranged alternatively. An inverse FFT image (Fig. 4(f)) presents many edge dislocations, which may be generated as a result of the replacement of C by N. The existence of vacancies and dislocations affects the properties of the Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solutions.

### 3.2 Mechanical properties of Ti<sub>3</sub>AlC<sub>2−<i>y</i></sub>N<sub><i>y</i></sub>

To study the solid solution strengthening effect in Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y*, the flexural strength and Vickers hardness were investigated. Figure 5 shows how the mechanical properties of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* change due to the *y* value. For comparison, the flexural strength and Vickers hardness values of the 1 h-hot-pressed Ti3AlC2 samples are also shown in Fig. 5.

*
Fig. 5Mechanical properties of Ti3AlC2−y*N*y* samples: (a) flexural strength and (b) Vickers hardness.

Figure 5(a) shows that the three-point flexural strength values of the solid solution bulk samples are relatively higher than the 360 MPa values for the Ti3AlC2 bulk samples. Notably, a significant strengthening effect is observed when *y* is 0.5 in Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y*. The flexural strength significantly increases from 424 to 541 MPa as *y* increases from 0.3 to 0.5. The Ti3AlCN bulk sample has the highest flexural strength among the studied samples, reaching 550 MPa. Figure 5(b) shows that the Vickers hardness linearly increases with increasing N. The measured Vickers hardness value increases from 3.3 to 5.54 GPa as *y* changes from 0 to 1.0 (Fig. 5(b)). The Vickers hardness of 5.54 GPa for *y* = 1 is the highest among the synthetic materials. The indentations on the polished surfaces of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* (*y* = 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0) made under a load of 2 kg are observed via SEM, as shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM. Around the indentations are piled-ups due to permanent plastic deformation under compressive stresses, but no cracks near the indentation corners are found (Fig. S4 in the ESM), suggesting good damage tolerance to consume the extent of microdamage. The above results demonstrated that the flexural strength and Vickers hardness values of the as-prepared Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solutions are tunable in 424–550 MPa and 3.78–5.54 GPa, respectively.

Our previous study demonstrated that MAX ceramics with grain sizes of less than 10 μm possess enhanced strengths [40]. In the present study, the grain sizes of the as-prepared Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* (*y* = 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0) samples are approximately 6–8 μm, which is less than 10 μm. According to the Hall‒Petch relationship, increasing the grain size decreases strength and hardness. However, the flexural strength and hardness values of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* increase with increasing *y* value. The introduction of N in Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* causes lattice distortion, as identified by the XRD results (Fig. 1). Lattice distortion retards the movement of dislocations during the plastic deformation of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* under compressive forces and thus improves its mechanical properties. Therefore, it was concluded that solid solution strengthening is the main mechanism for improving the mechanical properties of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* materials.

### 3.3 CTEs of Ti<sub>3</sub>AlC<sub>2-<i>y</i></sub>N<sub><i>y</i></sub>

The continuous variation in *a*- and *c*-LPs inevitably affects the theoretical density, thermal expansion, and electrical conductivity of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y*, suggesting that the above physical properties are tunable with increasing N concentration (*y*). The tunable theoretical densities of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* are described in Table 2. The influence of various lattice parameters on the coefficients of thermal expansion (CTEs) of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* was also investigated. Figure 6 shows the thermal expansion behavior of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* in the temperature range of 25–900 °C. The plots indicate that the CTEs increase with increasing temperature. The average bulk CTEs of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* (*y* = 0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0) are calculated to be 9.47×10−6, 8.79×10−6, 8.93×10−6, 8.37×10−6, and 7.69×10−6 K−1, respectively, in that temperature range. In the present study, the CTE value of 9.47×10−6 K−1 for Ti3AlC2 is close to the reported value of 9.2×10−6 K−1 for Ti3AlC2 [41]. The CTE of Ti3AlCN is the lowest among the five bulk samples. Overall, the CTEs of the carbonitride Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* MAX phases are lower than those of the Ti3AlC2 phase and tend to decrease with increasing N concentration because of the decreased *a*- and *c*-LPs in the solid solution materials. It is well known that the CTEs of MAX phases are dependent on bonding (including bond lengths and types). Strong bonding or short bond lengths are responsible for the low CTE. The continuous decrease in the *a*- and *c*-LPs of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* (Fig. 1(b)) is caused by the shortening of the Ti–Al, Ti–C, and Ti–N bond lengths. Theoretical work demonstrated that the Ti–Al and Ti–C bond lengths decrease from 0.2903 and 0.2077 nm for Ti3AlC2 to 0.2851 and 0.2068 nm for Ti3AlCN, respectively [33]. As shown in Fig. 6, the CTE of Ti3AlC1.7N0.3 is slightly lower than that of Ti3AlC1.5N0.5, possibly influenced by the TiC*x*N*y* impurity (with a CTE of (7.8–8.6)×10−6 K−1 [42]). According to the XRD results (Fig. 1(a)), although the peak intensity of TiC*x*N*y* in Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* is weak, it is relatively greater in Ti3AlC1.7N0.3 than in the other solid solution samples. Therefore, the presence of TiC*x*N*y* reduces the CTE value of Ti3AlC1.7N0.3. The above results indicate that the CTEs of the Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solutions are tunable in the range of (7.69–8.93)×10−6 K−1 in the temperature range of 25–900 °C.

### 3.4 Electrical conductivity of Ti<sub>3</sub>AlC<sub>2−<i>y</i></sub>N<sub><i>y</i></sub>

Electrical conductivity is another physical property influenced by lattice parameters. The electrical conductivities of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* with different N concentrations were also measured at room temperature. Figure 7 depicts the relationship between the electrical conductivity and N concentration for Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y*. The electrical conductivity linearly decreases from 1.48×106 to 0.95×106 S/m as the *y* value increases from 0 to 1.0. The electrical conductivities of the Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* (*y* = 0.3–1.0) carbonitride MAX phases are tunable in the range of (0.95–1.33)×106 S/m. However, in the present study, the electrical conductivity linearly decreases with increasing N concentration, mainly due to the formation of lattice distortion and defects such as vacancies and dislocations (Fig. 4) induced by the solubility of N in Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y*. Lattice distortion alters the motion path of electrons and scatters the transmission of electrons, thus leading to a decrease in the electrical conductivity. In addition, vacancies and dislocations hinder electron transport, thereby reducing the electrical conductivity of materials. This behavior is similar to that observed in Ti2AlC1−*x*N*x* (0.25 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.9) bulks [18]. The residual resistivity of Ti2AlC1−*x*N*x* increases with increasing N concentration due to the introduction of vacancies. Vacancies play a partial role in electron scattering mechanisms [18]. Scabarozi *et al.* [24] reported that MAX materials containing both N and C have higher residual resistivities than MAX materials containing only N or C. Thus, Ti3AlCN has a higher residual resistivity than Ti3AlC2, i.e., 0.40 vs. 0.353 μΩ·m.

## 4 Conclusions

Dense Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* (*y* = 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0) carbonitride MAX solid solutions were prepared via hot pressing of their powder counterparts at 1280 °C under 32 MPa for 1–3 h in Ar. The measured N concentrations are close to the corresponding theoretical N concentrations, suggesting that the designed solid solution phases have been successfully achieved. The XRD results demonstrated that increasing the N concentration (*y*) results in a linear decrease in both the *a*- and *c*-lattice parameters. The change in the lattice parameters influences the theoretical density, CTE, and electrical conductivity of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y*. The calculated theoretical density increases from 4.29 to 4.43 g/cm3 as the N concentration increases from 0.3 to 1.0. However, the measured CTEs and electrical conductivities of the Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solutions tend to decrease with increasing N concentration, from 8.93×10−6 to 7.69×10−6 K−1 and from 1.33×106 to 0.95×106 S/m, respectively. The decrease in the CTE is ascribed to the continuous decrease in the lattice parameters and the decrease in electrical conductivity is caused by the lattice distortion and defects induced by the solubility of N in Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y*. Mechanical property tests revealed that solid solution strengthening effectively improves the flexural strength and Vickers hardness of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y*. The flexural strength and Vickers hardness improve from 424 to 550 MPa and 3.78 to 5.54 GPa, respectively, as *y* increases from 0.3 to 1.0. Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solutions with tunable properties are attractive for future applications. Future work is planned to study the high-temperature properties of Ti3AlC2−*y*N*y* solid solutions and to promote their high-temperature applications.
