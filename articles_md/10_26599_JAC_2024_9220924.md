## 1 Introduction

As nuclear power, aerospace, and related industries continue to advance, there is growing interest in piezoelectric materials capable of withstanding high temperatures. For instance, in gas-cooled nuclear reactors, piezoelectric sensors should operate at temperatures exceeding 500 °C. In this high-temperature range, bismuth layered-structured ferroelectrics (BLSFs) have emerged as prominent materials for piezoelectric applications. BLSFs exhibit a high Curie temperature (*T*C), exceptional thermal stability, and lead-free characteristics, making them suitable for high-temperature applications and demonstrating their environmental benignity [1,2]. Their general chemical formula is (Bi2O2)2+(A*m*−1B*m*O3*m*+1)2−, where A represents a cation with 12 coordination sites, and B represents a cation with 6 coordination sites. The lattice structure of BLSFs features an alternating configuration of the (Bi2O2)2+ block and the (A*m*−1B*m*O3*m*+1)2− block (a pseudoperovskite block), with *m* indicating the number of B–O octahedral layers positioned between two (Bi2O2)2+ blocks along the *c*-axis [3−6].

CaBi4Ti4O15 (CBT), a member of the BLSF family, has demonstrated promising *T*C of approximately 790 °C, making it a promising candidate for high-temperature applications [7,8]. Compared with other common BLSFs, such as CaBi2Nb2O9 (piezoelectric constant *d*33 = 6 pC/N, *T*C = 943 °C) and Bi4Ti4O12 (*d*33 = 8.0 pC/N, *T*C = 675 °C), CBT (*d*33 = 8.2 pC/N, *T*C = 790 °C) system shows better comprehensive properties at an operating temperature of approximately 500 °C. Although the theoretical operational temperature range (1/2*T*C–2/3*T*C) of the pure CBT ceramics exceeds 500 °C, its practical application is limited by a weak piezoelectric response (*d*33 ≈ 38.2 pC/N), which is attributable to the limited polarization–rotation direction, the stress on the pseudoperovskite block from the (Bi2O2)2+ block, and the uneven potential energy profile from the layered structure and the tilt of the oxygen octahedron [9,10]. For steady application in some high-temperature devices, such as piezoelectric acceleration sensors with a maximum operating temperature of ≥ 500 °C, CBT ceramics are typically required to exhibit a *d*33 value of ≥ 20 pC/N and a resistivity of ≥ 1×106 Ω·cm at 500 °C.

Ion doping has been identified as a pivotal technique for enhancing piezoelectric performance in the large-scale production of BLSFs. Previous studies have predominantly concentrated on the impact of ion doping on the spontaneous polarization (*P*s) and point defects in CBT ceramics [11−14]. Nonetheless, the alteration of the potential energy profile, attributed to the tilting of oxygen octahedra, frequently escapes attention. This uneven potential energy profile establishes a high energy barrier, which hinders the atomic response to external electric fields. Restraining the tilting of oxygen octahedra to flatten the potential energy profile has been shown to significantly enhance the piezoelectric response of perovskite oxides. For instance, a pure BiFeO3 ceramics with an *R*3*c* group always shows lower piezoelectricity; by adding BaTiO3, the oxygen octahedral tilting angle continuously decreases, and the piezoelectricity increases dramatically [15]. Yan *et al*. [16] reported that the piezoelectric constant of BaZr0.2Ti0.8O3–PbTiO3 with *R*3*m*/*P*4*mm* morphotropic phase boundary (MPB) is much greater than that of Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3–PbTiO3 with *R*3*c*/*P*4*mm* MPB, and the tilting/untilting transition of oxygen octahedra at *R*3*c*/*P*4*mm* MPB leads to a high free energy barrier and results in low piezoelectric activity, whereas *R*3*m*/*P*4*mm* MPB with octahedra untilting produces large piezoelectricity due to a low free energy barrier. The above results show that reducing the tilting of oxygen octahedra is effective for flattening the potential energy profile and improving piezoelectricity. In pursuit of the exceptional performance of BLSFs for emerging high-temperature applications, different methods were employed in our previous works to improve the piezoelectric properties of BLSFs, such as enhancing the dielectric breakdown strength, increasing spontaneous polarization and improving the balance between the (Bi2O2)2+ block and the perovskite block. This work innovatively introduces the modification of the potential energy profile through ion substitution in CBT ceramics. The synergistic effect of a flattened potential energy profile and stronger *P*s is explored to significantly improve the piezoelectric response.

Compared to single ion substitution, dual-ion isovalent substitution has proven to be more effective in altering the performance of ceramics without obviously affecting the charge balance. The (Mo1/3Cr2/3)4+ substitution for Ti4+ in Bi3TiNbO9 ceramics increases the *d*33 value from 6 to 20.3 pC/N (*T*C = 901 °C) [17]. The (Li1/2Pr1/2)2+ substitution for Ca2+ in CaBi2Ta2O9 ceramics increases the *d*33 value from 7.2 to 15.1 pC/N (*T*C = 924 °C) [18]. Similarly, the (Zn1/3Nb2/3)4+ substitution for Ti4+ in Bi4Ti3O12 ceramics increases the *d*33 value from 7.3 to 30.5 pC/N (*T*C = 657 °C) [19]. According to the Goldschmidt tolerance factor *t* (*t* = (*r*A + *r*O)/* 2(r*B + *r*O), where *r*A, *r*B, and *r*O are the radii of the ions at the A site, the ions at the B site, and the oxygen ions, respectively), substituting ions with larger radii at the A-site will increase the symmetry of CBT and reduce the tilting of oxygen octahedra. We conceived of a CBT ceramics system with different substitution contents of (Na1/2Bi1/2)2+ (the radii of Ca2+, Na+, and Bi3+ are 1.34, 1.39, and 1.45 Å, respectively) to modify oxygen octahedron tilting, as the positive effect of (Na1/2Bi1/2)2+ on the flattening potential energy profile was proven according to previous studies of the Na0.5Bi4.5Ti4O15 (NBT, *d*33 ≈ 20 pC/N and *T*C ≈ 660 °C) system [20−22]. The large substitution of (Na1/2Bi1/2) 2+ at the A-site can also be thought of as the synthesis of a CBT–NBT solid solution. Furthermore, *P*s of CBT ceramics is designed to be improved by substitution at the B-site. Substituting Nb and Mn ions has been reported to increase *d*33 of BLSFs by enhancing polarization [23,24], which prompts the selection of dual-ions (Nb2/3Mn1/3) as substitutes for Ti at the B-site. By combining the substitution of (Nb2/3Mn1/3) at the B-site and (Na1/2Bi1/2) at the A-site in CBT ceramics, significant enhancements in piezoelectricity are achieved through the flattening potential energy profile and the augmentation of *P*s.

## 2 Experimental

Pure CaBi4Ti4O15 (denoted CBT) and Ca1−*x*(NaBi)*x*/2Bi4Ti3.98Nb0.01Mn0.01 (denoted CBTNM and CBTNM–*x*NB, *x* = 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5) were prepared via the solid-state reaction technique. CaCO3 (99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), Na2CO3 (99.8%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), Bi2O3 (99.999%, Zhongnuo Advanced Material (Beijing) Co., Ltd.), TiO2 (98%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), Nb2O5 (99.99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), and MnCO3 (99.99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) were stoichiometrically mixed and ball-milled for 24 h in tanks filled with alcohol, after which the mixtures were calcined at 850 °C for 4 h. After the second ball-milling process for 24 h, the dried mixtures were formed into desks with polyvinyl alcohol. These desks were then heat treated at 850 °C for 2 h to remove polyvinyl alcohol and subsequently sintered at 1080 °C for 3 h to facilitate grain growth. After electrode plating, the samples were poled in silicone oil at 200 °C with a poling voltage of 100 kV/cm and held for 15 min.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) data were collected by a powder X-ray diffractometer (X’Pert Pro, PANalytical) with Cu radiation (*λ*Kα1/*λ*Kα2 = 1.540598/1.544426), 2*θ* from 20° to 70°, a scan step size of 0.0131303°, a tube current of 35 mA and a working voltage of 40 kV. Raman spectra were collected by a Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR, HORIBA) for analyzing the crystal structure. The grains of the ceramics were observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; S-3400 N, Hitachi). The valence states of the ions were investigated by an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; AXISUItra DLD, Kratos). The domain behaviors were observed by a piezoresponse force microscope (PFM; MFP-3D, Asylum Research), and the atom-scale structure was investigated by a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Spectra 300S, FEI). The piezoelectric constants (*d*33) were obtained by a Berlincourt meter (ZJ-3A, Institute of Acoustics). The cells were subjected to thermal depolarization in a resistance furnace (KSL-1100X, HF-Kejing). The dielectric constant and impedance at different temperatures were measured by an LCR analyzer (HP 4980A, Agilent) with a resistance furnace (KSL-1200X, HF-Kejing). The direct current (DC) resistivity at different temperatures of the samples was obtained by an electrometer (6517B, Keithley Instruments) with a resistance furnace, and the ultraviolet absorption spectra were obtained by an ultraviolet‒visible spectrophotometer (UV-3600, Hitachi).

The first-principles calculations were conducted employing the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP), which relies on density functional theory (DFT). The exchange-correlation functional was processed using the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) parameterized by Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof, which was revised for solids (PBEsol). This method facilitated the attainment of appropriate lattice parameters for solid BLSF crystals. *Γ*-centered *k*-point meshes with a grid spacing of 0.04×2π Å−1 were chosen for Brillouin zone sampling. The wave function was expressed through a plane wave expansion method, with truncation set at a cutoff energy of 500 eV. The self-consistency cycle was iterated until an energy convergence of 10−5 eV was achieved, and the Kohn–Sham orbitals were updated. Macroscopic polarization is defined by the so-called modern theory of polarization using the Berry phase method.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of the pure CBT, CBTNM, and CBTNM–*x*NB ceramics. The most intense peak (119) is consistent with the typical characteristics of BLSFs with *m* = 4 [25,26]. The main diffraction peaks of the ceramics samples agree well with those of PDF#53-1640 (CaBi4Ti4O15), indicating that the target compound was successfully synthesized. The substituted Nb/Mn and Na/Bi are smoothly incorporated into the crystal lattice. Moreover, Raman spectroscopy was utilized to analyze the vibration modes of the crystal lattice. The Raman spectra of the pure CBT, CBTNM, and CBTNM–*x*NB ceramics are displayed in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The main Raman peaks are consistent with previous works on CBT ceramics [27], confirming the synthesis of the target compound and the successful diffusion of substituting ions in the crystal lattice. There are 7 main Raman peaks. When the Raman shift is below 200 cm−1, the peak is related to the vibration of ions at the A-site; when the Raman shift is above 200 cm−1, the peak is related to the vibration of oxygen octahedra [28,29]. The substituting ions have no obvious effect on the Raman peaks except for the peak at approximately 90 cm−1. Figure 1(b) shows that the peak at approximately 90 cm−1 gradually shifts with the introduction of Na/Bi, resulting from the substitution of heavier (Na0.5Bi0.5) ions for lighter Ca ions.

The Rietveld refinement of XRD data was performed to analyze the influence of Mn/Nb on the spontaneous polarization (*P*s) of CBT ceramics, which is related to anion and cation displacement. The Rietveld refinement results for the pure CBT and CBTNM ceramics are displayed in Figs. 1(c) and 1(d). The reliability factors *sig* and *R*wp for these Rietveld refinement results are obtained. The *sig* values are both lower than 2 (1.67 and 1.62 for the pure CBT ceramics and CBTNM ceramics, respectively), and the *R*wp values are both lower than 8% (6.27% and 6.41% for the pure CBT ceramics and CBTNM ceramics, respectively), confirming the reliability of this Rietveld refinement. From the Rietveld refinement results, the lattice parameters (*a*, *b*, *c*) for the pure CBT sample were determined to be 5.4346, 5.4118, and 40.7199 Å, respectively. For the CBTNM sample, the lattice parameters (*a*, *b*, and* c*) are 5.4278, 5.4125, and 40.7311 Å, respectively. Due to the presence of the glide plane along the *b*-axis and the mirror plane perpendicular to the *c*-axis, *P*s of the CBT system is primarily along the *a*-axis. Shimakawa’s model can be used to estimate the total *P*s value (along the *a*-axis) [30]:

(1) Ps=∑imi×Δxi×QieV

where *m*i is the site multiplicity; * Δxi is the atomic displacement along the a*-axis, and *Q*i*e* and *V* are the ionic charge and the volume of the unit cell, respectively. Figures 1(e) and 1(f) show the calculated refined *P*s values for each ion. The total refined *P*s values are 28.7 µC/cm2 for the pure CBT ceramics and 36.6 µC/cm2 for the CBTNM ceramics. These results indicate that the substitution of Nb/Mn at the B-site increases the deviation of the positive and negative ionic centers, thereby enhancing *P*s of the CBT ceramics.

First-principle calculations based on DFT were performed to estimate the effect of Na/Bi substitution on *P*s of the CBT system. By using Berry phase theory of polarization with the Born–von Karman periodic boundary [31−33], the translation of charges cannot be distinguished from one unit cell to the next. To calculate the total polarization for a certain unit cell, the integer multiple of the polarization quantum *e**R***/*Ω* should be considered, where *e* is the electronic charge; ***R*** is the lattice vector in the direction of polarization, and *Ω* is the volume of the periodic unit cell. Through the paraelectric tetragonal structure, a continuing switching path from the positive ferroelectric state to the negative ferroelectric state for the CBT system is built by many interpolations. Figures 1(g) and 1(h) display the calculated switching path for pure CBT and 50% Na/Bi-substituted CBT (CBT–NB). The DFT-calculated *P*s for pure CBT along [100] is 54.7 µC/cm2, and the DFT-calculated *P*s for CBT–NB along [100] is 56.7 µC/cm2. There is little positive effect of Na/Bi substitution on *P*s of CBT. DFT calculations can accurately reflect changes in intrinsic factors. However, due to the low substituting concentration of Nb/Mn at the B-site, it is difficult to analyze the effect of substituting Nb/Mn ions on *P*s through DFT calculations.

The DFT-calculated crystal lattices of pure CBT and CBT–NB are displayed in Fig. 2(a) for analyzing the influence of substituting Na/Bi on the CBT crystal lattice. From the DFT calculations, the lattice parameters (*a*, *b*, and *c*) for pure CBT are determined to be 5.4273, 5.3998, and 40.5342 Å, respectively. For 50% Na/Bi-substituted CBT (CBT–NB), the lattice parameters (*a*, *b*, and *c*) are 5.4385, 5.4076, and 40.5535 Å, respectively. The cell volumes (*V*) are 1187.92 Å3 for pure CBT and 1192.64 Å3 for 50% Na/Bi-substituted CBT. The difference between the DFT-calculated lattice parameters and the refinement lattice parameters is less than 0.5% for pure CBT, which confirms the reliability of the DFT-calculated results. Figure 2(a) compares the DFT-calculated crystal lattice of CBT before and after the substitution of Ca with Na/Bi at the A-site. In contrast to conventional perovskites, the CBT system exhibits significant oxygen octahedron tilting. This is likely related to the high Coulomb repulsive forces of B-site ions from the unit cell with a small radius in the ferroelectric CBT system. The deviation of the Ti–O–Ti bond can be explained by two quantitative factors: the Goldschmidt tolerance factor *t* (*t* = (*r*A + *r*O)/* 2(r*B + *r*O), where *r*A, *r*B, and *r*O are the radii of the ions at the A site, the ions at the B site and the oxygen ions, respectively) and the intersection angle of two oxygen octahedrons. The Goldschmidt tolerance factor *t* of BLSFs is smaller than 1, indicating that the B–O bonds are compressed and the A–O bonds are tensed. The radii of the A-site ions influence the internal pressure. In this work, the substitution of larger ions (Na+/Bi3+) for smaller ions (Ca2+) at the A-site increases *t*. This increase in *t* has a positive effect on enhancing the symmetry of the lattice structure. For the intersection angle, the tilt of the Ti1–O octahedron is defined by the intersection angle ∠O3–O1–O3, while the tilt of the Ti2–O octahedron is defined by the intersection angle ∠O1–O3–O5. From the DFT-calculated crystal lattice in Fig. 2(a), ∠O3–O1–O3 = 157.01° and ∠O1–O3–O5 = 159.02° are obtained for pure CBT. As a result of the different substitution ions in CBT–NB, two distinct sets of intersection angles are observed: ∠O3–O1–O3 = 158.12° and 157.87° and ∠O1–O3–O5 = 159.49° and 159.42°. Obviously, the substitution of Na/Bi increases ∠O3–O–O3 and ∠O1–O3–O5 in the CBT system, decreasing the tilt of the Ti–O octahedron. The decreased Ti–O octahedron tilt can also be confirmed by the XRD-refined crystal lattice of the CBTNM and CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics, as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM.

Figure 2(b) displays the temperature dependence of the permittivity (*ɛ*r) and the *T*C values for the samples. The introduction of Nb/Mn and Na/Bi increases *ɛ*r of the CBT ceramics, indicating an improved atomic displacement response. The peak observed in each plot corresponds to the transformation from the ferroelectric orthogonal phase to the paraelectric tetragonal phase, which indicates the *T*C values for the samples. The increase in *ɛ*r from Nb/Mn substitution is probably related to local lattice distortion. The field-induced strain (*S*) of ferroelectrics can be expressed as Eq. (2):

(2) S=Q×P2∼Qε2E2

where *Q* represents the electrostrictive coefficient; *ɛ* is the permittivity; *P* is the polarization; *E* is the field. The terms related to the high order of *P* are ignored. Therefore, the dielectric constant is the second-derivative response function of the internal energy related to the external electric field as Eq. (3) :

(3) εαβ=−∂2U∂Eα∂Eβ

where *ɛ*αβ is the permittivity, and *U* is the internal energy. Equation (3) can also be expressed as Eq. (4):

(4) εαα=Ω(⟨Pα2⟩−⟨Pα⟩2)ε0kBT

where *ɛ*αα is the permittivity; * ⟨⟩ represents the ensemble average; k*B is the Boltzmann constant; *P*α is the polarization; *T* is the temperature. This result indicates that a high dielectric constant demands high polarization flexibility, implying a flat energy profile for polarization rotation or stretching. This is why an ultrahigh dielectric constant can be obtained near *T*C, where the ferroelectric and paraelectric phases coexist with nearly equal energy. This coexistence leads to very strong fluctuations in polarization. Therefore, with increasing Na/Bi substitution, the change in the potential energy profile, due to the inhibited tilt of the oxygen octahedron, gradually increases *ɛ*r of the CBT ceramics. Figure 2(c) shows a schematic of the Landau energy for pure CBT and CBT–NB, and the flatting energy profile in CBT–NB influences the polarization process.

It can be observed that minor Nb/Mn substitution at the B-site has a negligible effect on *T*C of the pure CBT ceramics. However, substantial Na/Bi substitution at the A-site progressively decreases *T*C of CBTNM ceramics as the substitution level increases, probably due to the inhibited oxygen octahedron tilt. It is well established that there is a tilting–untilting transition of the octahedron at the temperature where ferroelectric materials transition to the paraelectric phase. The inhibited oxygen octahedron tilt results in lower energy requirements for the ferro-paraelectric phase transition. *T*C of the CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics (752 °C) is still higher than 750 °C, suggesting that its operating temperature can reach 500 °C.

Figures S3(a)–S3(e) and S4(a)–S4(e) in the ESM display the microstructures of the surface and cross-sections of the pure CBT, CBTNM, and CBTNM–*x*NB ceramics, respectively, and the pie-like grains result from the difference between the growth rate along the *a*–*b* plane and the *c* axis of the BLSFs. Figure S3(f) in the ESM shows the average diameter and thickness of the grains in the samples. After the substitution of Mn/Nb at the B-site, the average diameter increases to 1.44 μm, and the average thickness increases to 0.67 μm. This increase in grain size is probably attributed to the introduction of MnCO3, which can be used as a flux in piezoelectric ceramics. Following the substitution of Na/Bi for Ca at the A-site, the average diameter and thickness of the grains decrease. This change is due to the larger radius of Na/Bi compared to that of Ca, which makes the diffusion of Na/Bi ions in the crystal lattice more difficult, thereby inhibiting grain growth during the heating process. Vertical piezoresponse force microscopy (VPFM) in out-of-plane mode was employed to explore the domain behaviors of the samples under an electric field. Figures 3(a)–3(c) show the amplitude images of VPFM for the pure CBT, CBTNM and CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics with a scanning area of 4 μm × 4 μm. Submicron domains with irregular shapes and directions can be observed in the pure CBT sample, and the regions with relatively strong responses are disconnected. Compared with that of the pure CBT ceramics, VPFM of the CBTNM ceramics shows more regions with a strong response, likely due to the improved *P*s and increased grain size. Notably, some regions exhibit distinct long rectangular-shaped domains, as shown in Fig. 3(c). In addition, the VPFM image of the CBTNM–0.3NB sample is almost filled with long rectangular domains. Considering the grain size, the domains with the same direction are probably in one grain. The alternating arrangement of long yellow and blue rectangular regions indicates the alternating arrangement of domains with different directions. Due to the shallow and flat potential energy for polarization rotation after the substitution of Na/Bi, the piezoelectric response of the CBTNM–0.3NB sample becomes stronger than those of the pure CBT and CBTNM ceramics. In addition, a high density of domain walls can be observed, and the internal electric field and strain from long-range ferroelectric chains can more easily come over the potential energy for polarization rotation, resulting in a large number of domain walls. In addition, a large area with a strong piezoelectric response can be observed near the grain boundary, which is probably related to the broken long-range ferroelectric chain. The existence of grain boundaries disturbs the regular arrangement of the crystal lattice, resulting in local structural heterogeneity and lowering the energy barrier for the piezoelectric response near the grain boundaries.

To analyze the local domain switching of the samples, switching spectroscopy piezoresponse force microscopy (SS-PFM) was utilized. Figures 3(d)–3(f) show the local coercive biases (*V*c) of the 4 μm × 4 μm regions with an applied voltage of 15 V. The substitution of Nb/Mn at the B site and Na/Bi at the A site decreases the average *V*c of the CBT ceramics. The influence of Nb/Mn probably comes from the increased size of the grains. According to reported experimental and DFT-calculation results [34−36], the grain boundaries provide pinning sites for domain walls. Larger grains result in a lower concentration of grain boundaries and inhibit the pinning effect on the domain wall. Additionally, the influence of Na/Bi probably comes from the flattening profile of the potential energy. Figures 3(g)–3(i) display the representative amplitudes of these local regions. The amplitude of the CBT ceramics increases after the introduction of Nb/Mn and Na/Bi, from behind 1.5 nm for the pure CBT to approximately 2.0 nm for the CBTNM ceramics and then to approximately 2.5 nm for the CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics, confirming the stronger piezoelectric response.

The detailed structure of the domains can be directly observed through TEM. Figures 4(a)–4(c) show the bright-field TEM images of the CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics at different magnifications. The long rectangular domains with submicron widths observed in Fig. 4(a) align with those depicted in the PFM images. It is worthwhile mentioning the aggregation of many nanodomains within the CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics is shown in Fig. 4(b), and adjacent nanodomains with similar sizes and directions are located in some circular areas. Nanodomains are known to undergo faster switching than microdomains, and these nanodomains probably originate from broken long-range ferroelectric chains and local structural heterogeneity. The aggregation of these nano domains results in a large area with a strong piezoelectric response near the grain boundaries, as shown in Fig. 3(c). Figure 4(c) displays the layered structure of the samples, and an alternating arrangement of (Bi2O2)2+ blocks and pseudo-perovskite blocks can be observed. Figure 4(d) displays the corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern of the CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics with zone = [110]. The atomic displacement mapping of the CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics along the [110] direction can be analyzed through HRTEM. To investigate the atomic displacement of B-site ions from the center of the nearby A-site ions, fitting of two-dimensional Gaussian peaks was carried out to obtain the positions of ions at the A site and B site. In Fig. 4(e), the arrows positioned above the B-site ion represent the polar displacement vectors. The colors and lengths of these arrows indicate the intensity of the atomic displacement for the B-site ions. The atomic displacement of the central B-site ion columns is primarily oriented within the *a–b* plane. The atomic displacements along the *c*-axis of two nearby B-site ion columns tend to neutralize each other, and the main atomic displacements are similar to those of the central B-site ion columns.

Figure 5(a) shows the d*33 values and thermal annealing behaviors of *d*33 for the samples. The introduction of Nb/Mn at the B-site and Na/Bi at the A-site increased *d*33 from 8.2 pC/N for the pure CBT ceramics to 25 pC/N for the CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics. The increase in the piezoelectric strength is primarily due to the increase in *P*s and the flattening of the energy profile. Figure 5(b) displays the depolarization of *d*33 with increasing temperature, where *T*R is the temperature at room temperature. The change in the *d*33 value is approximately 1.2% for the CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics from 25 to 500 °C, suggesting outstanding temperature stability. The CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics can be used at 500 °C. The deterioration of *d*33 below *T*C is attributed to the pronounced thermal fluctuations and the gradual transformation from ferroelectric to paraelectric structures. The pronounced thermal fluctuation induces the depolarization of domains, and the ferro-paraelectric structure transformation contributes to the deterioration of *P*s. The temperature dependence of tan*δ* of the samples can be seen in Fig. S5 in the ESM. The substitution of Na/Bi and Nb/Mn ions decreases tan*δ* of CBT ceramics, and the tan*δ* values of the samples are all lower than 0.2 below 550 °C. The planar electromechanical coupling factor (*k*p) and mechanical quality factor (*Q*m) of the samples are shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM. The introduction of substituting ions has little effect on *k*p, and *Q*m slightly increases from 390 for the pure CBT ceramics to 411 for the CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics. Figure 5(c) displays the resonance response for the CBTNM–3NB ceramics in the temperature range of 50–500 °C. The minimum of the impedance resonance corresponds to the resonance frequency (*f*r), and the maximum of the impedance resonance corresponds to the anti-resonance frequency (*f*a). Both *f*r and *f*a slightly shift to lower frequencies with increasing temperature. Based on *f*r and *f*a of the samples, the temperature dependence of *k*p for the pure CBT, CBTNM, and CBTNM-0.3NB ceramics is calculated and shown in Fig. 5(d). The great temperature stability of *k*p from 50 to 500 °C can be observed.

The resistivity of piezoelectric materials significantly influences their applicability, making it an important aspect to discuss. Figure 6(a) illustrates the temperature dependence of the direct current resistivity (*ρ*dc) for samples annealed at 450–700 °C. The substitution of Nb/Mn increases the *ρ*dc of the CBT ceramics from approximately 2×106 Ω·cm to approximately 2×107 Ω·cm at 500 °C, and the substitution of Na/Bi slightly decreases *ρ*dc to approximately 1×107 Ω·cm at 500 °C. The conduction process in CBT ceramics involves a combination of intrinsic conduction and ionic conduction at high temperatures. To analyze the change in resistivity, the Arrhenius equation (Eq. (5)) is used to calculate the activation energy of the conduction process (*E*cond) [37]:

*

(5) ρdc=ρ0exp(EcondkBT)

where *ρ*0 is the exponential factor and *T* is the measuring temperature. Figure 6(b) displays the relationship of ln*ρ*dc and 1000/*T*. After linear fitting, the calculated *E*cond values of the samples are shown in Fig. 6(c). As is known that the volatilization of bismuth at high temperature results in some oxygen vacancies in CBT ceramics, as Eq. (6):

(6) 2BiBi*+3OO*→2V‴Bi+3VO⋅⋅+Bi2O3↑

The concentration and distribution of oxygen vacancies have an obvious effect on the conduction process. The substitution of Nb/Mn at the B site increases *E*cond for CBT ceramics from 0.94 to 1.53 eV, and 0.94 eV is near the activation energy of doubly ionized oxygen vacancies, which has been observed to be approximately 1 eV in previous works [38]; therefore, the conduction process of the pure CBT ceramics is dominated by the migration of doubly ionized oxygen vacancies at high temperature, where the main current carrier is the oxygen ion [39]. However, the *E*cond values of the CBTNM and CBTNM–*x*NB ceramics are approximately half of the energy gap of CBT ceramics (*E*g, approximately 3 eV) [40−42], indicating a shift in the main charge carriers from oxygen ions to intrinsic carriers.

To investigate the variation in the oxygen vacancy concentration among the samples, high-resolution XPS was performed for Bi and O ions in the pure CBT, CBTNM, and CBTNM–0.3NB samples, as shown in Fig. 6(d). The XPS spectra for Bi ions can be fitted as two Lorentz fitting peaks. The peak at approximately 157.7 eV is originated from Bi 4f7/2, and the peak at approximately 163.0 eV is originated from Bi 4f5/2. The spin–orbit splitting energy of Bi in the core level spectra is 5.3 eV [43]. Both peaks are associated with Bi3+, and no peaks indicative of Bi ions in other valence states are present. This suggests that the average valence states of substituted Na/Bi ions are equivalent to those of Ca ions. In addition, the Lorentz fitting peak at approximately 528.9 eV is attributed to O 1s, and the Lorentz fitting peak at approximately 529.8 eV is related to the intrinsic O2−, indicating the presence of an oxygen vacancy [44,45]. The proportions of the Lorentz fitting peak at approximately 529.8 eV to the total area are 40.08%, 39.43%, and 41.10% for the pure CBT, CBTNM, and CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics, respectively. It can be concluded that the dual-ion substitutions of Nb/Mn for Ti and of Na/Bi for Ca have no significant influence on the concentration of oxygen vacancies. Therefore, the main reason for the increase in *ρ*dc is not related to changes in the concentration of oxygen vacancies.

Figure 6(e) shows the complex impedance for the samples annealed at 550 °C. According to previous reports on the impedance of BLSFs, the Cole‒Cole semicircles in the low-, middle-, and high-frequency regions correspond to the ceramic–electrode interface, grain boundary, and grain bulk, respectively [10,13]. From the single Cole‒Cole semicircle observed for each sample in Fig. 6(e), it can be concluded that the resistivity is mainly attributed to the grain interior [46,47]. Therefore, the effect of grain boundaries on the resistivity is minor and can be ignored in this study. To investigate the change in the conduction process, Fig. S7 in the ESM shows the ultraviolet absorption spectra of the samples, and Fig. 6(f) shows the relationship of (*ahv*)2 and *hv*; then, *E*g can be obtained from Eq. (7) [48]:

(7) (ahν)1m=B(hν−Eg)

where *a* is the absorption coefficient; *h* is the Planck constant; *v* is the frequency for the incident photon; *m* is 1/2 for the direct band gap and 2 for the indirect band gap; *B* is a constant. According to previous studies [49−51], the direct band gap formula is appropriately employed here. The calculated *E*g values of the samples are displayed in Fig. 6(f). The substitution of Nb/Mn and Na/Bi ions does not increase the *E*g of the CBT ceramics, so the change in *E*cond is mainly related to the extrinsic conduction process. No extra charge carriers could be induced from the substitution of dual ions, so the increased *ρ*dc and *E*cond in the Nb/Mn-substituted CBT samples are probably attributed to the restricted mobility of carriers. As shown in Fig. 6(g), * MnTi′′ will attract oxygen vacancies and restrict the oxygen vacancy transition induced by the extra electric field, and the larger radius of Nb/Mn also has a negative effect on the carrier migration. Then, the substitution of Na/Bi increases the concentration of oxygen vacancies. Although the substituted ions restrict the movement of carriers, the higher concentration of oxygen vacancies mitigates the change in ρ*dc.

## 4 Conclusions

High-temperature ferroelectric CBT ceramics with the substitution of Nb/Mn and Na/Bi have been prepared in this work. Rietveld refinements of the XRD patterns show that the introduction of Nb/Mn effectively increases the *P*s of CBT ceramics, which is attributed to the larger deviation of the positive and negative ionic centers. From the DFT-calculated results, it can be found that the Na/Bi substitution at the A site inhibits the tilt of the oxygen octahedron, leading to a flat potential energy profile, and substituting ions with a larger radius decrease the tolerance factor *t*. The PFM images of the samples suggest that the piezoelectric response is obviously improved after substitution, and dense long rectangular domains and high-density domain walls can be found in the CBTNM–0.3NB ceramics with a flat potential energy profile. TEM images show the nano-domain created by the local structural heterogeneity near the grain boundaries. In addition, the restricted mobility of oxygen vacancies due to point defects increases the *ρ*dc of CBT ceramics, and the conduction mechanism of the samples is discussed in detail. The flat potential energy profile and stronger spontaneous polarization effectively improve the *d*33 of CBT ceramics to 25 pC/N when *T*C = 752 °C and *ρ*dc ≈ 1×107 Ω·cm at 500 °C, implying that these materials are good candidates for high-temperature ferroelectric devices.
