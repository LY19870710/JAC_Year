## 1 Introduction

With the growth of high-power electronic devices and sophisticated pulse power systems, dielectric capacitors are increasingly vital in many applications owing to their high current density, substantial power density, and fast charge‒discharge speed [1−3]. Miniaturization and integration are key development trends, which require dielectric capacitors with high energy storage density and efficiency [4,5]. According to the energy storage calculation formula [6], a higher applied electric field and polarization strength contribute to a greater energy storage density, and a smaller remanent polarization (*P*r) facilitates the improved energy storage efficiency (*η*) [7].

For four typical ceramic dielectrics [8−15], antiferroelectric ceramics undergo an antiferroelectric‒ferroelectric (AFE‒FE) phase transition in response to the applied electric field, and the enormous maximum polarization (*P*max) contributes to the realization of a remarkably elevated recoverable energy storage density (*W*rec) [16]. In the antiferroelectric (Pb0.91La0.06)(Zr0.96Ti0.04)O3 (PLZT) system, a *W*rec of 7.65 J/cm3 was obtained with large *P*max (~42 μC/cm2) through Mn doping [17]. Luo *et al*. [18] achieved high *W*rec of 8.95 J/cm3 and *P*max of ~45 μC/cm2 by varying the Sr content in the PSNZS system. However, improving *ƞ* (˂ 85%) in antiferroelectric ceramics is challenging because of the AFE‒FE phase transition hysteresis, which significantly impacts their practical applications. Moreover, most antiferroelectric materials are Pb-based, and Pb volatilization occurs during preparation and utilization, harming the environment and the human body [19]. Recently, lead-free relaxor ferroelectric (RFE) ceramics with pinched *P‒E* loops have presented antiferroelectric properties [20], making them a research topic as alternative materials.

Among them, Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3 (BNT) usually has high *P*max because of the strong hybridization between the 2p orbitals of O2− ions and the 6s orbitals of Bi3+ ions [21,22]. Nevertheless, the unignorable *P*r severely limits its ability to enhance energy storage performance. Recently, component design and phase-structure engineering have typically been regarded as effective strategies to destroy long-range ordered ferroelectric phases. Wang *et al*. [23] proposed a phase-structure engineering strategy by adding BiScO3 to BNT-based materials, and *P*max increased from 6.27 μC/cm2 at 40 kV/cm to 46.2 μC/cm2 at 80 kV/cm, attaining a large *W*rec of 5.41 J/cm3. Che *et al*. [24] proposed a strategy to modulate antiferroelectric-like properties via phase structure and defect engineering in BNT-based materials, achieving large *P*max (~50 μC/cm2) and ultrahigh *W*rec of 6.6 J/cm3. In addition, decreasing the domain size and increasing the antiferroelectric-like RFE characterize are used to reduce *P*r, improving *ƞ*. Ning *et al*. [25] constructed dynamic nanoscale domains (polar nanoregions (PNRs)) via the high-entropy strategy, whereas *W*rec of 7.3 J/cm3 and high *ƞ* of 94.3% were obtained in high-entropy ceramic (Ba,Na,Bi)0.205(Sr,Ca)0.1925Ti0.92Nb0.08O3 [25]. Long *et al*. [26] incorporated Sr0.7Nd0.2TiO3 (SNT) into BNT to improve the relaxor behavior and achieved ultrahigh *W*rec values of ~8.08 J/cm3 and *ƞ* of ~92.1%. Zhang *et al*. [7] enhanced the antiferroelectric-like feature in RFE BNTs via defect engineering and achieved the superior *ƞ* of 94.5% because of the pinched *P‒E* loops.

Currently, most studies focus on doping multiple elements to induce relaxation behavior and enhance compositional heterogeneity at the atomic level [27,28]. However, this method is usually accompanied by a decrease in *P*max. A higher applied electric field is needed to obtain large *W*rec. In previous work, doping BNT-based with a single rare earth element, Gd, was shown to modify the phase structure and induce relaxation behavior while reducing the oxygen vacancy concentration and improving the reliability of the materials [29,30]. In this study, we propose a simple and universal strategy through single-element modification under a moderate electric field to improve energy storage performance, which is more advantageous for large-scale production and practical application. (Bi0.5−*x*Pr*x*Na0.5)TiO3 components (abbreviated as BPNT-100*x*) are designed for dielectric energy storage (Fig. 1).

Like Bi3+ and Pb2+, the ion of Pr3+ has the same electronic outer configuration (6s2) [31], which sustains the contribution of A-site polarization to achieve large W*rec under a moderate electric field [32]. In addition, the different ionic radii of Pr3+ and Bi3+ lead to local structural heterogeneity, inducing PNRs and thus enhancing relaxor behavior [33−35]. Combined with phase-structure engineering, Pr3+ doping also induces the *P*4*bm* phase to achieve antiferroelectric-like properties, realizing higher *ƞ*. Meanwhile, the expansion of the bandgap contributes to obtaining higher electric field (*E*b), and specifically, defect carrier capture during sintering improves the reliability of dielectric materials, which facilitates the realization of ultrahigh *W*rec. Finally, superior *W*rec of 11.01 J/cm3 and *η* of 86.7% were obtained for the BPNT-18 lead-free relaxor antiferroelectric-like ceramics. This work provides an effective strategy involving the use of single elements to promote the next generation of dielectric materials for energy storage applications.

## 2 Experiment and characterizations

(Bi0.5−*x*Pr*x*Na0.5)TiO3 (abbreviated as BPNT-100*x*, *x* = 0.12, 0.14, 0.16, 0.18, and 0.20) ceramics were prepared via the traditional solid-state reaction method. The raw materials used were Bi2O3 (> 99.999%), Pr6O11 (> 99.9%), NaHCO3 (> 99.5%), TiO2 (> 99.8%), and MnCO3 (> 99.985%). The mass of the raw materials was calculated and then ball milled for 4 h using ZrO2 balls in ethanol. The homogeneous powder was synthesized at 850 °C, and then ball milled for 6 h. The powder was molded by adding a binder (PVA), pressed into discs, and held at 750 °C for 2 h. The sintering temperature of BPNT-100*x* was 1150 °C, which was maintained for 4 h. The test sample featured a complete electrode on one side and an electrode diameter of 100 μm on the other. To reduce the volatilization of Bi and Na, the ceramic tablets were buried in powder with the same composition. The sintered ceramic tablets were thinned to 80–100 μm, and a layer of gold electrode was placed on the top and bottom surfaces to test their performance.

The crystal structure of the ceramics was characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/MAX-2550 V, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5406 Å). The morphology was measured via a TM3000 Tabletop field emission scanning electron microscope microscope (FE-SEM; S-4800, Hitachi, Japan). The polarization hysteresis (*P*–*E*) loops were tested with a ferroelectric measuring system (TF Analyzer 2000, aixACCT Co., Germany). Raman spectra were measured by a Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba, Japan). Energy gap (*E*g) was measured via a UV–visible diffuse reflectance spectroscope (UV-3600, Shimadzu, Japan). The underdamped and overdamped charge/discharge experiments were realized with a commercial charge/discharge platform (CFD-001, Shanghai Jiguo Instruments Co., Ltd., China), which was connected to capacitors, resistors, and inductors in a complete circuit (RLC).

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 2(a) shows the unipolar *P‒E* loops of the BPNT-100*x* ceramics under different electric fields. All the components have slim and narrow *P‒E* loops, contributing to better energy storage properties. BPNT-100*x* exhibits significant pinched behavior on *P‒E* loops. Observing the *I*‒*E* curves shows that the current first increases and then decreases as the electric field increases, which usually implies antiferroelectric-like properties [36]. With the introduction of Pr, this behavior is gradually disrupted, and the bunching phenomenon diminishes, indicating an increase in relaxation properties [37,38]. Moreover, the energy storage performance of all the groups is calculated and compared, as shown in Fig. S1 and Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The *W*rec and *η* at various electric fields of BPNT-18 are calculated and shown in Figs. 2(b) and 2(c). The optimum component of the BPNT-18 ceramics at 552 kV/cm resulted in the maximum *W*rec of 11.01 J/cm3 with relatively high *P*max (52.8 μC/cm2) and *η* of 86.7%. Compared with other reported lead-free ceramics, it has superior energy storage performance, as shown in Fig. 2(d). Since single-element doping is more advantageous in large-scale production and practical applications, we efficiently realize superior energy storage performance by introducing single rare-earth-ion Pr3+. Compared with other lead-free perovskite systems with complex chemical elements, the BPNT-18 ceramics exhibit outstanding performance (Fig. 2(e)). Thus, this component has a high potential for engineering applications since it reduces the complexity of system ions while obtaining excellent energy storage characteristics.

The microstructures are subsequently analyzed to further explore the origins of the excellent energy storage properties. The grain size is small and uniformly distributed, as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. Figure 3(a) shows EDS mapping images of the BPNT-18 ceramic. The constituent elements Bi, Pr, Na, Ti, and O are uniformly distributed in the grains without any remarkable elemental enrichment. The XRD patterns of the BPNT-100*x* ceramic powder at room temperature are shown in Figs. 3(b) and 3(c). A significant second phase can hardly be observed when the logarithms of the measured intensity values are taken, indicating that Pr3+ ions are doped into the lattice. The ionic radius of Pr3+ (1.29 Å) is smaller than that of Bi3+ (1.39 Å), causing the diffraction peaks to shift to a greater angle. The diffraction peaks at 2*θ* ≈ 46.7° for the BPNT-12 and BPNT-14 fractions are relatively sharp, suggesting the predominant rhombohedral symmetric phase (R, space group *R*3*c*) in the ceramics [39]. In contrast, the (200) peak tends to split into two peaks in the BPNT-18 and BPNT-20 ceramics, (002) and (200), indicating that the tetragonal symmetric phase (T, space group *P*4*bm*) becomes dominant [24,40]. Moreover, the XRD results reveal that the content of the R-phase in the crystal structure gradually decreases and that of the T-phase gradually increases with the addition of Pr3+, as shown in Fig. 3(d) and Fig. S3 in the ESM. The R-phase of the BPNT-20 fraction is reduced to 18.34%, and the T-phase content increases to 81.66%, which indicates the enhanced antiferroelectric properties of the material.

Raman spectroscopy was implemented to analyze the evolution of the local structures of BPNT-100*x* (Fig. 3(e)). It can be segmented into four regions. These regions include A-site vibrations (−1), Ti–O band vibrations (200–400 cm−1), TiO6 octahedron vibrations (400–700 cm−1), and overlapping A1 and E bands (> 700 cm−1). All the spectra were fitted, and the relevant information of the full width at half maximum (FWHM) was added to the ESM [41,42]. The FWHM of the peak 2 in region I gradually increase, exhibiting broadening of the peak 2 and implying that the disorder at the A-site increases, as illustrated in Fig. S4 in the ESM [41]. The shift of the peak 3 toward a lower wavenumber indicates softening of the B–O band [43]. Notably, the transition from a triple-split peak to a double-split peak in Region III signifies a gradual reduction in the ferroelectric phase [20]. To further explore the existence of the antiferroelectric phase, dark field images of the BPNT-18 ceramic were obtained via transmission electron microscopy (TEM), as shown in Fig. 3(f). The BPNT-18 ceramic has a small domain size (~12 nm), which facilitates the relaxor properties of the material. Simultaneously, we observe ordered barred ferroelectric domains, which are associated with the ferroelectric *R*3c phase and contribute to the overall significant polarization [44−46]. This phenomenon has also been reported in other studies [43,44,47]. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) images display both the [111]*C* and [001]*C* directions, and the 1/2{oe} superlattice spots can be observed in Figs. 3(g) and 3(h). The occurrence of short-range ordered nanoscale domains in BPNT-18 ceramics clearly indicates the emergence of random fields [43,48]. In the *R*3*c* symmetry, 1/2{ooo} are present but 1/2 {ooe} superlattice spots are missing, whereas in the *P*4*bm* symmetry, the opposite situation exists, where 1/2{ooe} are found but 1/2{ooo} superlattice spots are absent [47,49,50]. Accordingly, the emergence of the 1/2{ooe} superlattice spots confirms the existence of the *P*4*bm* phase, further suggesting that the BPNT-18 component possesses antiferroelectric properties at room temperature (RT).

The variations in the relative dielectric constant (*ε*r) and dielectric loss (tan*δ*) with temperature for the BPNT-100*x* ceramics at different frequencies are plotted in Figs. 4(a)–4(e). *ε*r gradually decreases with the introduction of Pr3+ and tends to flatten in the temperature range of −75–125 °C, meeting the temperature characteristics of X8R capacitors. It exhibits outstanding potential for ceramic capacitor applications (Fig. 4(f)). Figure S5 in the ESM shows that *γ* increases from 1.678 to 1.767 with increasing Pr content. This indicates the gradual increase in the relaxor behavior of the material. Moreover, piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) is used to show the dynamic response of the domains at the external voltage to further explore the relaxor behavior. Figures 4(h) and 4(i) show the out-of-plane phase images of the BPNT-12 and BPNT-18 ceramics with an applied voltage of 20 V. Components form long-range ordered FE phases and distinct boundaries under a voltage of ±20 V. The domain boundaries gradually disappear 3 min after the voltage withdrawal for the BPNT-12 component. However, the domain boundaries of the BPNT-18 ceramic obviously vanished at 1 min, indicating its high PNR content, high domain activity, and enhanced relaxor behavior [6]. Overall, the smaller domains favor smaller *P*r and improve energy storage efficiency.

First-principles calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) are applied to theoretically verify the ion bonding ability, and the supercells used for calculation are shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM. The density of states (DOS) of the Bi and O atoms in BNT and BPNT are shown in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b). The overlap of the DOS peaks increases from −3 to 0 eV, suggesting a stronger interaction between the Bi and O bands with increasing Pr3+ [51,52], which sustains the contribution of A-site polarization to BPNT-18, thus achieving large *P*max [32]. Enhancing the breakdown of electric fields and the reliability of dielectric materials are essential for improving energy storage performance. Theoretically, *E*g of Pr6O11 (3.9 eV) is greater than that of Bi2O3 (2.8 eV), and the substitution of Pr for Bi could increase *E*g of the material. UV–vis spectroscopy of the BPNT-100*x* ceramics was performed to determine the effect of Pr substitution on the band gap (*E*g), as shown in Figs. 5(c) and 5(d). *E*g can be derived from the Tauc plots in Fig. 5(d)**,** and *E*g of the BPNT-100*x* ceramic is subsequently calculated, as shown in Fig. 5(e). *E*g increases from 1.69 to 1.84 eV as Pr is substituted, facilitating higher breakdown field strengths and *W*rec. Moreover, the grain size (*G*) decreases with increasing Pr concentration, as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM, which is beneficial for *E*b according to the equation *E*b ∝ *G*−a (*a* represents a constant) [53]. The Weibull distribution of the BPNT-100*x* ceramics was calculated, as illustrated in Fig. S7 in the ESM [54]. The fitted *E*b values gradually increase as the Pr content increases, consistent with the above results. The direct current (DC) leakage current was used to demonstrate the reliability, as shown in Fig. 5(f). The leakage current is significantly restrained with the addition of Pr. The charge change of Pr4+ into Pr3+ during sintering could reduce the number of free carriers, which decreases the number of defects, limits the leakage current, and improves the reliability of the materials [55]. XPS was used to analyze the oxygen vacancies in the BPNT-12 and BPNT-18 components, as shown in Fig. S8 in the ESM. The decrease in OV/OL calculated from the integral area indicates a gradual reduction in the oxygen vacancy content [56]. Therefore, the higher *E*g and lower carriers contribute to the superior breakdown strength of the BPNT-18 ceramics.

Evaluating the stability of ceramic capacitors in practical applications is particularly important. The temperature stability of BPNT-18 was estimated over the temperature range of −60–120 °C, as shown in Fig. 6(a). *W*rec varies within 6.2%, whereas *η* remains above 80%, indicating excellent temperature stability (Fig. 6(b)). *In situ* temperature-dependent XRD was performed further to investigate the variation in the phase structure with temperature, as shown in Fig. 6(c). The BPNT-18 ceramics maintain minimal phase structure changes at temperatures ranging from −60 to 120 °C, revealing excellent structural stability at different temperatures. In addition, the fatigue characteristics of pulsed power devices are also essential for dielectric ceramics. After 106 cycles under an applied electric field of 340 kV/cm, the *P‒E* loops of the ceramics are still quite slim, with no significant changes in *P*max and *P*r, and the ∆*W*rec is less than 0.53%, whereas *η* remains above 81%, as shown in Figs. 6(d) and 6(e). Moreover, ∆*W*rec is less than 1.5% under the frequency variation from 10 to 300 Hz in Fig. 6(f), demonstrating a very strong low-frequency stabilization characteristic.

The feasibility of using ceramic capacitors as commercial products need to be evaluated via pulsed charge‒discharge measurements. Figure 7(a) shows the behavior of the underdamped charge/discharge of the BPNT-18 ceramic. The current density (*C*D) and power density (*P*D) of the ceramics are calculated according to the following equations: * CD=Imax/S and  PD=EImax/2S, respectively [57−59], where S* is the effective electrode area, *I*max is the peak current during discharge, and *E* is the applied electric field. With an increase in the applied electric field from 100 to 300 kV/cm, *I*max, *C*D, and *P*D gradually increased from 4.4 A, 596 A/cm2, and 31 MW/cm3 to 15.6 A, 2096 A/cm2, and 312 MW/cm3, respectively, as illustrated in Fig. 7(b). The overdamped charge/discharge results at electric fields from 100 to 300 kV/cm and a 300 Ω load are shown in Fig. 7(c). BPNT-18 ceramics have an ultrafast discharge rate (*t*0.9 ≈ 37 ns@300 kV/cm), which is among the higher levels reported for other ceramic materials (Fig. 7(d) and Fig. S9 in the ESM). The discharging energy density (*W*d) of the ceramics varied from 0.13 to 2.69 J/cm3 from 100 to 300 kV/cm. The above results demonstrate that BPNT-18 ceramics exhibit good charging and discharging properties and have great potential for pulsed power applications.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, we propose a simple and universal strategy for achieving superior energy storage performance in BNT ceramics through the single rare-earth ion Pr3+. The relaxor behavior is induced while maintaining a large *P*max and negligible *P*r, breaking the long-range ordered ferroelectricity with excellent energy storage properties. The BPNT-18 ceramic component achieved an optimum value, with *W*rec of 11.01 J/cm3 and *ƞ* of 86.7%. The larger *E*g and higher reliability contribute to a higher breakdown field (552 kV/cm). The more active PNRs and the antiferroelectric phase reduce *P*r to improve *ƞ*. The BPNT-18 component has excellent thermal stability from −60 to 120 °C, which is attributed to structural stability combined with superior fatigue characteristics and low frequency stability. In addition, BPNT-18 ceramics also have a high power density (312 MW/cm3) and an ultrafast discharge rate (*t*0.9 ≈ 37 ns@300 kV/cm). The above research indicates that BPNT-18 has a simple elemental composition and achieves outstanding performance in perovskite structures, which is expected to be applied in lead-free advanced pulse power systems.
