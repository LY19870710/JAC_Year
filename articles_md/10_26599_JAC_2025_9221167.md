## 1 Introduction

Piezoelectric ceramics enable the mutual conversion of electrical and mechanical energy, equipping devices with energy conversion capabilities and supporting their extensive use in actuators, transducers, and energy harvesters [1−4]. Piezoelectric energy harvesters (PEHs) offer a safe and environmentally friendly power source by efficiently transforming mechanical vibrations into electricity. They provide an alternative to traditional chemical batteries and facilitate the self-powering of microelectronic devices, including biomedical implants, aerospace equipment, and Internet of Things (IoT) nodes [5,6]. A significant challenge to the widespread adoption of IoT technology lies in the self-powering power demands of critical components, such as sensors and wireless transceivers.

The output power of the PEH is critically influenced by the piezoelectric figure of merit (FOM), defined as * FOM=d33×g33, and  g33=d33/(ϵ0×εr), where ε*r represents the relative dielectric constant, *g*33 denotes the piezoelectric voltage coefficient, *ε*0 is the vacuum dielectric constant, and *d*33 is the piezoelectric charge coefficient [7]. Consequently, the development of high-power PEHs requires not only a high *d*33 but also a low dielectric constant. Pb(Zr,Ti)O3-based (PZT-based) ceramics have dominated the piezoelectric ceramic market. However, lead toxicity poses significant risks to both human health and the environment [8]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop high-performance lead-free alternatives. Among lead-free piezoelectrics, (K,Na)NbO3-based (KNN-based) ceramics have garnered significant attention because of their excellent overall performance [9]. For example, textured KNN-based ceramics produced by Saito *et al*. [10] via the reaction template grain growth (RTGG) technique exhibited a high *d*33 of 416 pC·N−1, sparking extensive research efforts. However, the *d*33 values of approximately 400 pC·N−1 remain inferior to those of commercial PZT-based ceramics, underscoring the need for performance optimization strategies. Simultaneously, the direct proportionality between *d*33 and *ε*r presents challenges for improving *g*33 [7].

In KNN-based lead-free piezoelectric ceramics, multiphase coexistence can effectively reduce the free energy barrier, thereby enhancing piezoelectric performance. For example, Zhang *et al*. [11] achieved orthorhombic (O) and tetragonal (T) phase coexistence at room temperature by constructing an O–T phase boundary, resulting in *d*33 ≈ 510 pC·N−1. Similarly, Shi *et al*. [12] introduced the rhombohedral (R) phase atop the O/T phase, forming a novel R–O–T triphasic boundary (NPB) that delivers exceptional performance (*d*33 ≈ 508 pC·N−1, Curie temperature (*T*C) ≈ 268 °C). Moreover, texturing techniques can improve *d*33 while maintaining *T*C. For example, Lin *et al*. [13] employed template grain growth (TGG) technology to induce highly oriented grains, increasing *d*33 from 185 to 435 pC·N−1 in KNN ceramics while maintaining *T*C of ~360 °C. Notably, local structural heterogeneity has garnered significant attention for its role in enhancing the piezoelectric performance of ceramics [14]. Gao *et al*. [14] demonstrated that dopants could induce the formation of high-density nanostructures. These structures significantly improve piezoelectric performance by modulating the interfacial energy and flattening the energy barrier. In addition, PEH demands a high electromechanical coupling coefficient (*k*p) and a large *g*33. It has been reported that acceptor doping (such as Fe, Cu, and Mn) can induce a weak pinning effect that moderately suppresses *ε*r while acting as a sintering aid to increase densification and improve *d*33. This approach promises the synergistic optimization of high *d*33 and *g*33 values [15,16].

Here, on the basis of the considerations above, as shown in Fig. 1, we designed (1−*x*)(K0.5Na0.5)(Nb0.938Sb0.02Ta0.04Fe0.002)O3–*x*(Bi0.5Na0.5)HfO3 (*x* = 0.02, 0.03, 0.04, and 0.05) ceramics through multiscale reconfiguration design. Crystal orientation was achieved via RTGG technology, while multiphase coexistence of R–O–T phases and local structural heterogeneity were achieved, enabling synergistic optimization of piezoelectric properties. Additionally, Fe acceptor doping was introduced to maintain a low *ε*r, resulting in outstanding comprehensive performance for KNN-based lead-free piezoelectric ceramics. Therefore, cross-scale structural collaborative optimization is achieved by integrating atomic-scale doping regulation, nanoscale construction of local structural heterogeneity, and microscale control over texture as well as mesoscopic multiphase coexistence. As a result, the textured KNN-based ceramics demonstrate outstanding piezoelectric properties (*d*33 ≈ 551 pC·N−1, *T*C ≈ 321 °C, *k*p ≈ 77%, *g*33 ≈ 54.2 mV·m·N−1). The PEH exhibited ultrahigh instantaneous output power (*P*out ≈ 4.85 mW) and instantaneous output power density (*P*D ≈ 70.2 μW·mm−3). These exceptional piezoelectric properties suggest that ceramics obtained in this work can effectively replace lead-based piezoelectric ceramics in energy harvesting applications, highlighting their potential as viable alternatives to PZT piezoelectric ceramics.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

The quality of the template seed crystal directly determines the texture of piezoelectric ceramics. In this study, textured ceramics were prepared using a NaNbO3 template (NN template), which has a high degree of compatibility with the KNN-based ceramic lattice. The NN template prepared by the two-step molten salt method has a large length‒to‒diameter ratio, good dispersion, and no agglomeration (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)).

Random and textured ceramics of (1−*x*)(K0.5Na0.5)(Nb0.938Sb0.02Ta0.04Fe0.002)O3–*x*Bi0.5Na0.5HfO3 (*x* = 0.02, 0.03, 0.04, and 0.05) were prepared via conventional solid-state reaction and RTGG methods. The raw materials used were as follows: Nb2O5 (99.98%, Aladdin, China), Na2CO3 (99.8%, Aladdin, China), Bi2O3 (99.98%, Alfa Aesar, USA), Fe2O3 (99.99%, Aladdin, China), Ta2O5 (99.99%, Aladdin, China), Sb2O3 (99.99%, Aladdin, China), K2CO3 (99.99%, Aladdin, China), and HfO2 (99.99%, Aladdin, China). For each batch, 20 g of material was weighed according to the stoichiometric formula. The powders were mixed with a 2/3 volume of anhydrous ethanol and ball milled for 24 h with 55 g of ZrO2 ceramic balls. The resulting slurry was poured into a crystallizing dish, dried at 100 °C, and then presintered at 850 °C for 5 h. After presintering, the powder underwent secondary ball milling for 12 h followed by another drying step. The dried powder was granulated with 8 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) as a binder. The discs (12 mm diameter and 1 mm thickness) were pressed at 300 MPa and subjected to debinding at 600 °C for 10 h. Finally, sintering was performed via a two-step method: heating to 1160–1200 °C, rapidly cooling to 1060–1100 °C, and holding at this temperature for 8–10 h.

Textured ceramics were prepared via the RTGG method. The powder was mixed with 4 wt% NN templates and various organic solvents to prepare a tape-casting slurry. The well-mixed slurry was cast onto a carrier film and dried. The dried tapes were laminated into 12 mm × 12 mm square sheets via hot pressing, followed by binder removal. The two-step sintering method was then applied: heating to 1170–1210 °C, rapid cooling to 1070–1110 °C, and holding for 8–10 h to obtain textured ceramics.

### 2.2 Fabrication of piezoelectric energy harvesters

The ceramics were fabricated into piezoelectric circular diaphragm (PCD) energy harvesters for experimental study. First, ceramics (~22 mm diameter) were prepared via the RTGG method described above. The sintered ceramics were then thinned to ~0.22 mm thickness. Silver electrodes (~21 mm diameter) were applied to both sides and annealed at 560 °C for 30 min. The samples were fully poled under an alternating current (AC) electric field of 25 kV·cm−1. The proof mass (14 g), a poled ceramic disc, a studdle and wires were bonded onto a brass substrate and cured to form the PCD energy harvester. The harvester was mounted on a mechanical shaker for testing. The vibration frequency and amplitude (fixed at 4 V) were controlled via a signal generator (33220A, Agilent, USA). The vibration shaker was operated at an acceleration of 2*g* (*g* = 9.8 m·s−2), as measured by an accelerometer. Real-time voltages across a load resistor were recorded via a digital oscilloscope.

### 2.3 <i>Ex situ</i> structural characterization

The surface and cross-sectional microstructures of the ceramics were examined by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; TM4000Plus, Hitachi, Japan).

The room-temperature phase structure was analyzed via an X-ray diffractmetor (XRD; D2 PHASER, Bruker, Germany). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atomic-resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) samples were prepared by mechanical thinning, ultrasonic cutting, and argon-ion milling to increase the electron transparency (30–50 nm thickness). Vertical piezoelectric response force microscopy (V-PFM) images were acquired via a piezoelectric response force microscope (PFM; Dimension Icon, Bruker, USA). Texture analysis was performed via electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) pole figures and inverse pole figures. A transmission electron microscope (200 kV, HF500, Hitachi, Japan) was used to obtain dark-field images for regional morphological analysis. Atomic-scale imaging was conducted on the Cs-corrected version of this microscope.

### 2.4 <i>In situ</i> structural characterizations

*In situ* temperature XRD: XRD patterns at different temperatures were acquired on an X-ray diffractometer (PANalytical Empyrean, the Netherlands) equipped with a heating stage.

*In situ* electric field XRD: Phase structures under applied electric fields (0–35 kV·cm−1, sample thickness ≈ 0.3 mm) were examined via custom-built *in situ* electric field XRD sample holders. Semitransparent gold electrodes deposited via direct current (DC) sputtering were employed for *in situ* XRD tests under variable electric fields.

### 2.5 Electrical property characterization

The ceramic samples were mechanically polished to a 0.5 mm thickness, and silver electrodes were applied to both sides. After the electrodes were fired at 560 °C for 30 min, the ferroelectric, dielectric, and electric field-induced strain properties were characterized. Characterization of polarization*‒*electric field (*P‒E*) loops and strain*‒*electric (*S‒E*) field loops was performed at a frequency of 10 Hz via a ferroelectric test system (Precision Premier II, Radiant Technologies, USA). The samples were subsequently poled under a DC electric field of 25 kV·cm−1 at room temperature. The piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33) was then measured at room temperature via a quasistatic *d*33 meter (ZJ-6A, Institute of Acoustics, China). A heating stage integrated with an LCR meter (E4980A, Agilent, USA) and source meter (2410, Keithley, USA) was employed to measure the temperature-dependent dielectric constants (*ε*r–*T*) from 25 to 400 °C. The *k*p was evaluated with an impedance analyzer (HP4294A, Agilent, USA).

## 3 Results and discussion

In Figs. S1 and S2 in the ESM, SEM characterization reveals distinct microstructural evolution with well-developed grain architectures in both random ceramics (marked as R-*x*BNH) and textured ceramics (marked as T-*x*BNH). The grain size in both types of ceramics progressively decreases with increasing BNH doping content, which is correlated with increased configurational system entropy [17]. Notably, T-*x*BNH, prepared through the RTGG process, exhibits significant template-induced anisotropy, forming a regular brick-like morphology. Furthermore, its overall grain size, ranging from 20 to 30 μm, remains larger than that of R-*x*BNH ceramics because template nucleation sites facilitate grain growth [18]. To analyze the crystalline phase, comparative XRD patterns of the R-*x*BNH and T-*x*BNH ceramics were obtained and are presented in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b). All the ceramics display a single perovskite-type structure with no detectable secondary phases, indicating that BNH is fully dissolved into the KNN perovskite lattice. The (002) to (200) peak ratios for the R-*x*BNH and T-*x*BNH ceramics range between 1 : 2 and 2 : 1, indicating the coexistence of T and O phases in KNN-*x*BNH [19]. With increasing BNH concentration, both the R-*x*BNH and T-*x*BNH ceramics show analogous evolution in their 45° diffraction profiles: a systematic intensification of the (200) reflection and attenuation of the (002) peak, suggesting a gradual increase in the T phase and a corresponding reduction in the O phase. Further Rietveld refinement of the XRD patterns of the T-*x*BNH ceramics (Fig. 2(c)) indicated that the KNN-2BNH ceramics contained O–T phases, whereas the introduction of BNH contributed to the emergence of an R phase. As the BNH content increases, the proportion of the R phase incrementally increases from 0% to 19.6%, with all error factors (*R*p) being less than 5.

The quantitative phase analysis in Figs. S3–S5 in the ESM highlights that R-*x*BNH and T-*x*BNH ceramics with identical compositions exhibit comparable phase fraction distributions, confirming that the texturing process does not significantly alter the phase structure but rather induces grain orientation. Comparative XRD analysis further revealed a pronounced increase in the C diffraction intensity for T-*x*BNH compared with R-*x*BNH, indicative of superior crystallographic texture development [8]. The Lotgering factor *f*(00*l*), which is used to quantify the degree of texturing in KNN-based piezoelectric ceramics, equals 1 for completely oriented ceramics and 0 for randomly oriented ceramics [20]. All T-*x*BNH samples demonstrated significantly high *f*(00*l*) orientation parameters exceeding 90%, with the T-3BNH variant showing exceptional texturing characterized by a maximum orientation factor of 97.2%. To validate the degree of texture observed in the XRD analysis, EBSD was employed (Figs. 2(d) and 2(e)). The analysis of the inverse pole figure (Figs. 2(d1)–2(d3)) and inverse pole/pole figure sets (Figs. 2(e1) and 2(e2)) reveals that the grain orientation of polycrystalline ceramics T-3BNH is nonrandom, exhibiting a consistent regularity. Nearly all the grains have their C direction aligned parallel to the *Z*-axis (perpendicular to the casting direction) rather than the *X* or *Y* axes. These findings confirm that T-3BNH possesses a strong C texture [21]. The observed multiphase coexistence and enhanced crystallographic anisotropy are anticipated to significantly enhance the performance of KNN-based piezoelectric ceramics [20,22,23].

To further validate the existence of the R–O–T phase boundary, selected area electron diffraction (SAED) and *ε*r–*T* curves were analyzed. Figure 2(f) presents the SAED results for T-3BNH. Notably, in Fig. 2(f1) region, the SAED image obtained using the [110] zone axis displays a distinct 1/2 (*ooo*) superstructural reflection, indicating the R phase [23]. Moreover, in Fig. 2(f2) region, the SAED image obtained using the [100] zone axis exhibited a distinct 1/2 (*ooe*) superstructural reflection, indicating the O phase [24]. Figures 2(f3) and 2(f4) show SAED results captured in the same region along the [100] and [110] zone axes. No superstructural reflection spots corresponding to the O and R phases were observed, confirming the presence of the T phase. These SAED observations conclusively demonstrate that the phase structure of T-3BNH features the coexistence of R, O, and T phases. The *ε*r–*T* curves for T-*x*BNH are shown in Fig. 2(g) and Fig. S6 in the ESM. With increasing BNH doping, both the *T*C and the phase transition temperatures (*T*R–O and *T*O–T) progressively shift toward room temperature, ultimately forming the NPB. The *ε*r–*T* curves for R-*x*BNH exhibit a similar trend (Fig. S7 in the ESM), confirming that engineering does not significantly alter the phase structure of the ceramics, which aligns with the XRD results. In the low-temperature region, multifrequency *ε*r–*T* curves for T-*x*BNH are displayed in Fig. S6 in the ESM. These curves indicate that as the BNH content increases, the dielectric anomaly peaks of *T*R–O and *T*O–T broaden, demonstrating that a higher BNH content introduces more relaxation elements within the ceramics (including smaller domains and more localized random fields) [25]. Additionally, the phase structure of the T-3BNH ceramics was observed via *in situ* temperature-variable XRD (Figs. 2(h) and 2(i)). As the temperature changes, a distinct abrupt transition zone is observed, corresponding to *T*O–T and *T*C, which is consistent with the findings from the *ε*r–*T* curves. The transformation from O/T two-phase coexistence to R–O–T three-phase coexistence reduces the polarization-flipping barrier in ceramics, thereby promoting polarization flipping. This, in turn, enhances the piezoelectric and ferroelectric properties of the ceramics [26].

In addition to the anisotropy of the crystal and phase structures mentioned above, the domain structure plays a crucial role in influencing the polarization and piezoelectric properties [27−29]. To investigate the domain structure, PFM, TEM, and high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) were utilized [30−32]. Figures 3(a) and S8 and S9 in the ESM reveal the domain structures in R-3BNH and T-3BNH, respectively. Both samples exhibit diverse domain structures among different grains, including various angles (109°, 120°, and 180°) and varying sizes, such as submicron stripe domains with widths of hundreds of nanometers, nanodomains (1–100 nm) and nanoscale heterostructures. Additionally, the domain size increases significantly following texture engineering, which correlates with changes in the grain size [33].

The intrinsic piezoelectric properties of ceramics are theoretically positively correlated with domain size. While large domains exhibit high intrinsic piezoelectric properties, their substantial flipping energy barrier prevents effective flipping under an electric field. According to typical domain theory  D∝EDW, where D* represents the domain size, and *E*DW denotes the domain wall energy, smaller domain sizes can reduce the domain wall energy [34]. Excessive diffusion and miniaturization of domain structures can lead to the formation of a metastable system dominated by nanodomains. Despite the fast polarization response offered by such metastable structures, their thermodynamic instability induces spontaneous rebiasing, ultimately causing significant deterioration in macroscopic piezoelectric properties [35]. Consequently, while nanodomains flip easily, their intrinsic piezoelectric properties remain low. To overcome these challenges, constructing a multidomain polar structure with local structural heterogeneity—embedding moderately nanoscale polar heterogeneity within a long-range ordered matrix—reduces the flipping barrier of the overall domain, thereby providing excellent ferroelectric and piezoelectric properties. Figures 3(b) and S10 in the ESM present PFM images of the T-*x*BNH and R-*x*BNH ceramics, illustrating the refinement and dispersion of domain sizes as the BNH content increases. Three samples, T-2BNH, T-3BNH, and T-5BNH, were subjected to 10 V tip biases. The results revealed that the domain of the T-2BNH sample was not fully switched under the electric field because of the larger overall domain size. In contrast, the domain structure dominated by dispersed small domains struggles to retain stability upon removal of the electric field. In the T-3BNH sample, the cross-scale configuration of nanodomains and large-sized domains generates a multidomain polar structure system with local structural heterogeneity. Within this system, nanoscale polar heterogeneous domains act as sources of easily switching polarization, whereas stable large-size domains maintain the long-range ordered polarization state, thereby stabilizing the macroscopic piezoelectric performance. As a result, optimal flipping and stability under an electric field are achieved.

A featured atomically resolved HAADF-STEM image captured along the [100] zone axis illustrates the polarization vector determined via 2D Gaussian peak fitting, derived from the shift of the B-site cation relative to its four neighboring A-site cations (*δ*). Figure 3(c1) highlights inhomogeneity in the *δ* vector, with polarization vectors aligning along both the pseudocubic axis and diagonals, corresponding to the T and R/O symmetries [36]. However, distinguishing R and O phases from polarization vector projections along the [100] zone axis is infeasible, as their characteristics project identically on the (100) plane. To confirm the multiphase coexistence of R–O–T in these ceramics, the projection of the polarization vector on the [110] plane was analyzed, as depicted in Fig. 3(c2). Figures 3(c3) and 3(c4) provide magnified HAADF-STEM images along with a schematic projection of the cell along the same axis. The local symmetry of ferroelectric materials is governed by their spontaneous polarization, arising from the relative displacement between positive and negative ion centers, thereby forming electric dipoles. The symmetry of the three phases (T, O, and R) in a perovskite piezoelectric structure is distinguishable by the spontaneous polarization (*P*S) directions: [001], [110], and [111]. Analysis of the displacement vector distributions along the [110] and [100] zone axes reveals large-scale, long-range, ordered polarization regions associated with the R/O/T phases in the local atomic structure, further confirming the coexistence of R, O, and T phases in T-3BNH. The formation of the R/O/T multiphase coexistence induces local polymorphic distortion, which typically manifests as structural heterogeneity [19]. Consequently, short-range ordered polarization vector distributions related to R/O/T phases can be observed in T-3BNH ceramics, reinforcing the conclusion that T-3BNH ceramics exhibit local structural heterogeneity due to this multiphase coexistence. Magnified views further reveal that polarization is prone to flipping between regions with differing orientations (Fig. 3(d)). The low Gibbs free energy characteristic of this multiphase structure notably reduces the polarization anisotropy, promoting an efficient polarization response under an external electric field.

Figures 4(a) and S11 in the ESM present the *P‒E* loops and *S‒E* loops for the R-*x*BNH and T-*x*BNH ceramics at 10 Hz. The results reveal that the remanent polarization (*P*r) of textured ceramics is considerably greater than that of random ceramics. This finding indicates that the texturing treatment facilitates a more orderly alignment of electric dipoles following polarization under an external electric field, thereby increasing the polarization efficiency and improving the ferroelectric and piezoelectric performance [21]. For the T-*x*BNH ceramics, *P*r initially increases and then decreases as the BNH content increases, reaching optimal ferroelectricity at *x* = 0.03. This trend is attributed to the appropriate domain structure configuration within the ceramics. The piezoelectric properties of ceramics are closely tied to electric field-induced phase transitions. To explore the phase transformation process in T-3BNH ceramics under an applied electric field, *in situ* electric field-dependent XRD analysis was performed. Figure S12 in the ESM presents the *in situ* electric field-dependent XRD patterns for the T-3BNH ceramics. Under an external electric field, the intensity of the (002) peak progressively increases, whereas the (200) peak gradually decreases, particularly near the coercive field. This signifies an electric field-induced sequential phase transition in the local structure, transitioning from the T phase to the O phase and subsequently to the R phase [25]. Following the removal of the voltage, the distorted diffraction peak in the T-3BNH ceramics exhibited minimal rebound, which was consistent with its highly saturated polarization state. Given the relationship * d33∝P3×ϵr(where P*3 represents the polarization along the polar axis, approximately corresponding to *P*r), the piezoelectric properties of textured ceramics are markedly superior to those of random ceramics (Fig. S13 in the ESM).

In practical electromechanical applications, ideal piezoelectric ceramics must exhibit both high *d*33 and elevated *T*C. However, in perovskite ferroelectrics, a constrained relationship typically exists between *d*33 and *T*C [7,11,13,19,36−41]. Figure 4(b) shows the *d*33 and *T*C values for the T-*x*BNH ceramics. With increasing BNH content, *d*33 initially increases before it decreases, whereas *T*C decreases consistently. T-3BNH ceramics demonstrate optimal piezoelectric properties, achieving *d*33 ≈ 551 pC·N−1 and *T*C ≈ 321 °C. This exceptional performance can be attributed to three primary factors: the optimized phase coexistence ratio of the ceramics R/O/T, suitable domain structure configurations, and enhanced crystallographic anisotropy induced by texturing. Figure 4(c) highlights the correlation between *d*33 and *T*C across various piezoelectric ceramics, showing that the T-3BNH ceramics developed in this study achieve substantially improved *d*33 values while maintaining a high *T*C, enabling a broader range of practical applications.

Additionally, *g*33 serves as a critical performance parameter. In recent years, research has focused predominantly on *d*33 improvement, with relatively less attention given to enhancing *g*33. Nevertheless, the *g*33 coefficient is crucial for piezoelectric devices, particularly in energy harvesting applications. In PEH, higher *k*p values are directly linked to greater energy conversion efficiency of piezoelectric ceramics under resonant conditions, thereby improving the energy harvesting performance. Figure 4(d) and Fig. S14 in the ESM reveal the phase angle and frequency dependence across different components, with all ceramic samples exhibiting phase angles nearing 90°, indicating adequate polarization. Figure 4(e) presents the *k*p and *g*33 values for the T-*x*BNH ceramics, among which T-3BNH records the highest *k*p (77%) and *g*33 (54.2 mV·m·N−1) values. As *x* increases, *k*p first increases but then decreases. This phenomenon is related to changes in the domain structure [13,19]. The elevated *g*33 of T-3BNH is attributed not only to its increased *d*33 values but also to the acceptor doping in this study, which reduces the dielectric constant to a certain extent [17,19,25,38,42−50]. Figure 4(f) compares the *g*33 and *k*p properties achieved in this research with those of other lead-free piezoelectric ceramics, demonstrating the highly competitive performance of this work. These results underscore the significant potential of T-3BNH ceramics for lead-free energy harvesting applications.

In the development of high-performance lead-free piezoelectric ceramics, device design and performance evaluation are critical. To verify the feasibility of the synthesized high-performance T-3BNH ceramics for electromechanical energy conversion, a PEH was fabricated as a model system for corresponding research. A schematic diagram of the energy-harvesting unit is presented in Fig. 5(a). As illustrated in Fig. S15 in the ESM, a sintered, large-sized T-3BNH round ceramic sheet (22 mm in diameter and 0.22 mm in thickness) was integrated into a PCD PEH. The energy-harvesting measurements were performed via the electrical circuit depicted in Fig. S16 in the ESM. Figure 5(a) highlights the FOM of T-*x*BNH ceramics, showing that T-3BNH achieves an optimal FOM value of approximately 29.98×10−12 m2·N−1. This performance is comparable to that of commercial lead-based piezoelectric materials (PZT-4: 6.47×10−12 m2·N−1) [51].

Figures 5(b) and 5(c) illustrate the output voltage (*V*out) and *P*out of the PEH based on T-3BNH ceramics as functions of the vibration frequency and load resistance. The results indicate that the T-3BNH ceramic PEH achieves maximum instantaneous *V*out and *P*out values of approximately 15.7 V and 4.85 mW, respectively, under a matched load resistance and optimal vibration frequency, yielding a *P*D of up to ~70.2 μW·mm−3. Figure 5(d) compares the *P*out and *P*D of various PEHs fabricated with lead-free and lead-based piezoelectric ceramics [13,25,36,43−47,52−65]. Notably, the *P*out and *P*D of the T-3BNH ceramic PEH surpass those of most PEHs made with lead-free piezoelectric ceramics and are comparable to those of PEHs made with commercial PZT-5H lead-based piezoelectric ceramics. Figure 5(e) illustrates the illumination of a PEH composed of T-3BNH ceramics under a matched load resistance and optimal vibration frequency. At the optimal vibration frequency of 320 Hz, the T-3BNH ceramic PEH is capable of effortlessly lighting hundreds of bulbs. This ultrahigh *P*out and *P*D were exhibited by the T-3BNH ceramic PEH, underscoring its remarkable potential for applications in electromechanical energy conversion.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, this study achieved exceptional piezoelectric energy harvesting performance in KNN-based ceramics through multiscale structural engineering. By simultaneously regulating the texture, phase coexistence (R–O–T), and domain structure, we induced local structural heterogeneity and enhanced crystallographic anisotropy. This synergistic approach yielded outstanding piezoelectric properties (*d*33 ≈ 551 pC·N−1, *k*p ≈ 77%). Simultaneously, Fe acceptor doping was employed to maintain a low dielectric constant, and a fine balance between the piezoelectric coefficient and the dielectric constant was achieved, resulting in a high *g*33 ≈ 54.2 mV·m·N−1). Consequently, the optimized T-3BNH ceramics demonstrated ultrahigh device performance (*P*out ≈ 4.85 mW, *P*D ≈ 70.2 μW·mm−3). This research sheds light on the structure‒property relationships in KNN-based piezoelectric ceramics and offers valuable insights for the design and fabrication of high-performance lead-free piezoelectric materials and devices tailored for energy harvesting applications.
