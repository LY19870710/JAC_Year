## 1 Introduction

With the development of hypersonic aircraft for increased speed and longer flight times, thermal protection systems must meet strict environmental requirements while remaining lightweight [1,2]. Carbon/carbon (C/C) composites are the most promising candidates for lightweight aerospace materials because of their good thermal shock resistance, low thermal expansion, and excellent high-temperature mechanical properties [3]. However, the poor oxidation resistance of C/C composites under high temperatures in air severely limits their aerospace applications [4]. Thermal protective coatings with ablation resistance can effectively address this issue by utilizing ultrahigh temperature ceramic (UHTC), which possess high melting points and good chemical stability [5].

ZrC is widely considered to be the main component of anti-ablation coating materials [6]. However, many investigators have found that the ZrO2 produced by the oxidation of ZrC coatings tends to exhibit a porous and loose structure, accompanied by an increase in volume and the generation of many cracks [7]. The common strategy for improving the ablation resistance of ZrC coatings is to introduce second phases to promote the sintering of porous ZrO2 to form a dense oxide layer. As one of the most classical systems, ZrC–SiC coatings have been widely researched due to the self-repairing capacity of the *in* *situ* glassy SiO2 phase for porous ZrO2 films. The preferentially generated SiO2 can hinder oxygen diffusion and hinder the active oxidation of the inner SiC. However, their service temperature is restricted by the low melting point of SiO2 (*T*m = 1650 °C) [8,9]. The ablation resistance of the ZrC–SiC coating can be further improved by the addition of TaC since the generated Ta2O5 (*T*m = 1800 °C [10]) and Zr6Ta2O17 (*T*m = 1900 °C [11]) have higher melting points, as demonstrated by our previous study of ZrC–SiC/TaC coatings [12]. However, the high service temperature causes heat accumulation and a large thermal stress gradient on the surface of the coatings, which will result in large local defects and accelerate the failure of the coating.

To decrease the ablation heat accumulation of the coatings, the surface temperature can be reduced by incorporating nanomaterials with high thermal conductivity, such as graphene [13], boron nitride (BN) [14], and carbon nanotube (CNT) [15], which possess high thermal conductivity values of over 1000 [16], 300 [17], and 100 W/(m·K) [18], respectively. Moreover, improving the heat dissipation efficiency can decrease the evaporation [19]. However, the application of nanomaterials in coatings is limited by extra internal defects due to the agglomeration effect, preventing the improvement of ablation properties [20]. A CNT-modified ZrC–SiC coating fabricated by Shi *et* *al.* [21] showed inferior ablation resistance owing to more grain boundaries and increased open porosity. Therefore, establishing an anti-ablation and dense coating containing carbon nanomaterials by mechanical mixing is far from trivial. A core–shell structure is advantageous because it can endow the outer carbon shell with good dispersion and a thermal conduction network [22]. However, achieving a uniformly dispersed core–shell structure within a dense coating remains challenging due to structural retention difficulty that is limited by complex preparation processes [23]. Inspired by the *in* *situ* carbon shells in polymer-derived ceramic (PDC) [24], a ceramic@carbon core–shell structure can be constructed in a dense ZrC-based coating, which is expected to further improve the stability of the oxides and prolong the anti-ablation life.

In this work, ZrC coatings modified by polymer-derived SiC/TaC with a carbon shell were prepared. The ablation resistance of the coating was tested under an oxyacetylene flame, aiming to determine the role of the carbon shell uniformly distributed in the anti-ablation coating and establish the relationship between the sintering effect of low-melting-point oxides and heat dissipation by carbon nanomaterials. More broadly, this work is expected to shed new light on designing dense UHTC coatings containing carbon nanomaterials with excellent heat dissipation to improve the ablation resistance of C/C composites.

### 2.1 Materials

2.5D needling carbon fibers (approximately 0.45 g/cm3; Yixing Tianniao High-Tech Co., Ltd., China) were used to prepare the C/C composites. Powder of Si (99.0% purity, ~48 μm; Tianjin Fengyue Chemical Co., Ltd., China), C (99.0% purity, ~48 μm; Shanghai Buwei Applied Material Technology Co., Ltd., China), and Al2O3 (99.0% purity, ~75 μm; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) was employed to fabricate the SiC transition layer. Polycarbosilane (PCS; National University of Defense Technology, China) with a molecular weight of 1200–2000, white tantalum chloride powder (TaCl5; 99.9% purity, 1–3 μm; Macklin, China), and liquid divinylbenzene (DVB; 80% isomer mixture; Macklin, China) were selected as the starting materials to synthesize the single-source precursors (SSPs). Xylene liquid (Fuyu Fine Chemical Co., Ltd., China) was used as the reaction solvent. Commercial ZrC (99.0% purity, ~48 μm; ENO High-tech Material Co., Ltd., China) was chosen to prepare the sprayed coatings.

### 2.2 Synthesis of polymer-derived SiC/TaC@C nanocomposites

First, 7.0 g of PCS and 3.0 g of TaCl5 powder was added to a 250 mL Schlenk flask (Synthware, China), and liquid xylene was added to dissolve the powder. Then, 1.0 g of liquid DVB was introduced into the mixture. A highly oxygen-free reaction environment was obtained by vacuuming and venting Ar gas for 3 cycles, and the reaction was performed at 80 °C. After the xylene was removed under vacuum, the SSPs labeled P–Ta–D were obtained. For comparison, SSPs labeled P–Ta without DVB were synthesized. Finally, the two kinds of SSPs were put in an Al2O3 crucible, cross-linked at 300 °C for 2 h, pyrolyzed at 1000 °C for 2 h at a heating rate of 5 °C/min in a quartz tube furnace (OTF-1100X, Hefei Kejing Materials Technology Co., Ltd., China), and then cooled to room temperature (RT) under an Ar atmosphere. The as-pyrolyzed powder was transferred to a graphite furnace (ZGS 350, Sante Furnace Industry Technology Co., Ltd., China), annealed to 1800 °C at 3 °C/min and held for 2 h under an Ar atmosphere. After annealing, the powder was furnace-cooled to RT to obtain PDC nanocomposites. P–Ta- and P–Ta–D-derived nanocomposites were labeled SiC/TaC and SiC/TaC@C, respectively.

### 2.3 Coating preparation

Before preparing the sprayed coating, a SiC-coated C/C substrate was used. First, C/C composites with a final density of ~1.70 g/cm3 were made from 2.5D needling carbon fiber preforms and densified by thermal gradient chemical vapor infiltration. The dense C/C composites were cut into *φ*30 × 5 mm3. Then, a SiC transition layer was fabricated on the pretreated C/C composites by pack cementation [25] to decrease the coefficient of thermal expansion mismatch between the C/C and the sprayed coatings. The pack cementation was held using Si, C, and Al2O3 as the original powder and heated at 1900–2100 °C for 2 h.

Two kinds of ZrC–SiC/TaC and ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coatings were fabricated by supersonic atmospheric plasma spraying (SAPS). Then, 70 wt% ZrC and 30 wt% PDCs were agglomerated by ball-milling and spray granulation, and granulated powder with ~75 μm particles was selected for spraying. ZrC–SiC/TaC and ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coatings were prepared on SiC-coated C/C composites using a SAPS system (HEPJ-II type, The Academy of Armored Forces Engineering, China) with appropriate thermal spraying parameters (Table 1).

### 2.5 Characterizations

The chemical bonding states of the SSPs were characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscope (FT-IR; Vertex 70, Bruker, Germany) via the KBr method. The pyrolysis process of the SSPs was evaluated by thermogravimetric differential scanning calorimeter (TG-DSC; STA 449F3 Jupiter, Netzsch Group, Germany) at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under flowing Ar. The microstructure of the free carbon (Cfree) in the PDCs and sprayed coatings was investigated by Raman spectrometer (Raman, Alpha300R, WITec GmbH, Germany) with a 532 nm excitation wavelength. The Si and Ta contents were measured by inductively coupled plasma‒optical emission spectrometer (ICP‒OES; Agilent 725, Agilent, USA). The carbon content was calculated as 100% minus the sum percentage of the remaining elements. Phase analysis of the PDCs and coated specimens was conducted by the X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X’Pert Pro, Almelo, the Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation. The fraction and lattice parameters of phases in the coatings after ablation were obtained by XRD Rietveld refinement using GSAS II software (Version 5652). Scanning electron microscope (SEM; Carl Zeiss EVO 10, Zeiss, Japan) was employed to observe the morphology of the coated specimens before and after ablation. The grain size of the ZrO2 particles in the ablated coatings was static and calculated by Image Pro Plus (Version 6.0). The microstructures of the PDCs and powder before and after coating were investigated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Tecnai F30 G20, FEI, USA). The element content and distribution were evaluated by coupled energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS; Oxford, INCA, UK).

### 3.1 Molecular structure and pyrolysis of SSPs

Figure 1 shows the FT-IR spectra and TG-DSC curves of the as-prepared SSPs. According to Ref. [12], the FT-IR spectrum of P–Ta in Fig. 1(a) retains the main typical absorption peaks of PCS, such as those of C–H, Si–CH3, Si–H, C = C, Si–CH2–Si, and Si–C bonds [28]. The formation of Ta–C and Ta–O bonds indicates that TaCl5 accessed the PCS chain by removing HCl [29,30]. For the synthesized P–Ta–D polymers, aromatic C–H bonds and phenyl C=C, C–C, and C–H bonds belonging to DVB verify the successful introduction of DVB into the SSP network [31,32]. To evaluate the effect of DVB in the SSPs on the pyrolysis behavior, TG curves obtained by synchronous DSC and DTG analysis were obtained, as shown in Figs. 1(b)–1(d). There are three distinct stages of weight loss in the synthesized SSPs. In stage I, below 220 °C, weight loss is primarily caused by the escape of volatile organic matter from small molecules. The maximum weight loss rates reached 113 and 142 °C for the P–Ta and P–Ta–D polymers, respectively, corresponding to the first peak of the DTG curves in Fig. 1(d). In stage II, between 220 and 760 °C, the synthesized polymers undergo organic‒inorganic conversion, and the molecular structure is rearranged to break Si‒H and C‒H bonds and form a Si‒C network [33]. The volatilization of organic molecules during pyrolysis accompanied by heat release (Fig. 1(c)) results in significant weight loss. Similarly, the temperatures at the peaks of the DTG curves of P‒Ta‒D are greater than those of P‒Ta (Fig. 1(d)), which suggests that the addition of DVB increases the crosslinking density and delays organic‒inorganic conversion [32]. In stage III, above 760 °C, the curves reveal a slight weight loss (Fig. 1(b)), which corresponds to the amorphous-crystalline transition [34]. Overall, the addition of DVB changes the molecular structure, resulting in a 4.3% decrease in the final weight loss.

### 3.2 Phase and microstructure of the PDCs

Figure 2 displays the XRD patterns and Raman spectroscopy of the PDCs obtained at 1800 °C. The diffraction peaks of TaC (JCPDS#74-1223) and 3C–SiC (JCPDS#29-1129) appear in the XRD pattern of the SiC/TaC nanocomposites (Fig. 2(a)). The diffraction peak at 26.2° (002) is attributed to C (JCPDS#75-1621), which indicates the presence of C in the SiC/TaC nanocomposites. The diffraction peaks of C, TaC, and 3C–SiC are also detected in the XRD pattern of the SiC/TaC@C nanocomposites. The half-peak width becomes narrower than that of SiC/TaC nanocomposites, indicating an increase in the crystallinity of PDCs due to the addition of DVB as a cross-linking agent [35]. To investigate the microstructure of *C*free in PDCs, Raman spectroscopy was carried out, and the results are plotted in Fig. 2(b). The spectrum exhibits the major bands corresponding to SiC and TaC (790 cm−1 for transverse optical (TO) and 960 cm−1 for longitudinal optical (LO)) [36]. The D band centered at 1360 cm−1 (disordered-induced carbon) and the G band centered at 1580 cm−1 (graphitic-like mode) are additional carbon features in PDCs, and the 1620 cm−1 shoulder characteristic is associated with an imperfect graphite crystal [37]. The *I*D/*I*G value indicates the disordering of carbon [38], and the higher *I*D/*I*G value of SiC/TaC@C than that of SiC/TaC reveals that an increased content of amorphous carbon also exists in SiC/TaC@C. The relative intensity of TO and LO decreases, illustrating the improved Cfree content in SiC/TaC@C with the addition of DVB to the feed [39]. Moreover, an increase in the Cfree content can be demonstrated in Table 3. With the introduction of DVB, the Cfree content of the obtained PDCs significantly increases from 1.92 to 30.86 wt%. The TaC content in SiC/TaC@C slightly increases due to the increased cross-linking effect of DVB.

To obtain detailed information about the phases in the PDCs, the SiC/TaC and SiC/TaC@C nanocomposites were also subjected to TEM. Figure 3 shows the TEM images of SiC/TaC and SiC/TaC@C annealed at 1800 °C, which reveals that nanocrystalline TaC grains are distributed in the SiC matrix (Figs. 3(a), 3(c), and 3(f)). Moreover, few Cfree grains can be observed at the border of the TaC grains from the SiC/TaC nanocomposites, as shown in Fig. 3(b). The unique core–shell microstructure of TaC encapsulated by C is observed in the SiC/TaC@C nanocomposites (Fig. 3(d)). The carbon shell, as marked in Fig. 3(d), is 3–5 nm thick and shows the structure of graphene. Graphene displays exceptionally high thermal conductivity, which is conducive to heat conduction [40], thus reducing the surface temperature. An abundance of separate graphitic carbon can also be observed in the SiC/TaC@C nanocomposites, demonstrating the presence of large amounts of Cfree. The Cfree in PDCs originates from the hydrocarbons of DVB incorporated into the preceramic precursors [24]. In the pyrolysis stage, hydrogen atoms in DVB are eliminated to form short carbon chains. On the one hand, the carbon chains can act as nuclei for further growth of carbon clusters, leaving behind an excess of Cfree. On the other hand, the carbon chains react with Si or Ta chains to form SiC and TaC ceramic. When the carbon dissolution in the ceramic nanoparticles reaches saturation, the precipitation of Cfree around the nanoparticles starts in the form of graphite and forms a graphene shell. Crystalline SiC and TaC can be detected by the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern of the SiC/TaC@C nanocomposites in Fig. 3(e). The HAADF pattern in Fig. 3(f) shows that the distribution of SiC is continuous as a matrix with scattered TaC nanoparticles, similar to our previous study concerning SiC/TiC nanocomposites [41].

### 3.3 Phase and microstructure of the coatings

The XRD patterns of the as-sprayed ZrC–SiC/TaC and ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coatings are plotted in Fig. 4(a). The diffraction peaks of ZrC (JCPDS#35-0784), TaC (JCPDS#74-1223), and SiC (JCPDS#29-1129) are detected, where t-ZrO2 (JCPDS#88-1007) and m-ZrO2 (JCPDS#78-1807) are identified, caused by the reaction of ceramic and oxygen at high temperature (approximately 10,000 K) during spraying. Raman spectra of the sprayed coatings illustrate the improved Cfree content in ZrC–SiC/TaC@C based on the lower relative intensities of TO and LO in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating (Fig. 4(b)). The high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) image of the powder from the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating in Fig. 4(c) shows the unique core–shell microstructure of TaC encapsulated by graphene layers, which confirms that the graphene shells in the PDCs still exist after spraying. The HAADF pattern shows that the sprayed powder are composed of Zr, Si, Ta, C, and O, where the structure of the TaC nanoparticles embedded in the SiC matrix is also partially preserved after spraying, as shown in the dashed circle.

### 3.4 Ablation performance of the coatings

Figure 6 shows photographic images of the ZrC–SiC/TaC and ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coatings after ablation. The exposed surfaces of the ZrC–SiC/TaC and ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coatings after ablation for 30 s, as shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b), are covered with white oxides in the central region. The surface morphology of the coatings consequently changes after ablation for 90 s × 2. The area of the ablation center region in the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating is approximately 35.9 mm2, which is more significant than that after ablation for 30 s (14.0 mm2), and the oxides become darker, as shown in Fig. 6(c). The oxides in the ablation center region of the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C specimens do not differ significantly from those in the surrounding area, with only a few white traces of oxides appearing in Fig. 6(d). Compared with the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating, the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating possesses better ablation resistance due to the presence of the graphene shell.

The mass and linear ablation rates of the ZrC–SiC/TaC and ZrC–SiC/TaC@C specimens after ablation are displayed in Fig. 7. After ablation for 30 s and 90 s × 2, the R*m of the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating is −0.980 and −1.665 mg/s, respectively, which is only 0.126 and 0.144 mg/s for the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating (Fig. 7(a)), indicating that the mass in the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating increases but that in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating decreases. This phenomenon is induced by the oxidation of the plentiful graphene shell in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating. After ablation for 30 s, both coatings possess a negative *R*l (Fig. 7(b)), which implies that the oxidation of coatings accompanied by volume expansion is dominant in the early stage of ablation [42]. With the ablation time extended to 90 s × 2, the *R*l of both coatings becomes positive because of the sintering effect caused by the prolonged exposure to flame [43]. Compared with that of the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating, the *R*l of the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating is always lower, only 0.009 μm/s after ablation for 90 s × 2, a decrease of 93.7%, indicating superior ablation resistance. Compared with the literature data given in Fig. 7(c), the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating provides an advanced anti-ablation performance with a low *R*l, effectively protecting the C/C composites. The surface center temperatures of the specimens are different, as presented in Fig. 7(d). The surface temperature of ZrC–SiC/TaC@C rapidly climbs to 1940 °C within 30 s, which is lower than that of ZrC–SiC/TaC (2106 °C). The specimens suffer a notable thermal shock during the initial 30 s and form a service-derived Zr–Ta–O film on the sprayed coatings and then at a steady temperature during the subsequent ablation process. The surface temperatures of the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C and ZrC–SiC/TaC coatings reach 2032 and 2224 °C, respectively, corresponding to a temperature difference of nearly 200 °C. PDCs contain the high thermal conductivity phases SiC and C, while SiC/TaC@C contains more Cfree. Consequently, the heat dissipation effect of the coating during ablation is primarily attributed to Cfree. In the early stage of ablation, the existence of Cfree in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating allows heat transfer along the thermal conduction network. The initial temperature difference between the two coatings influences the microstructure of the generated oxide layer and subsequently affects the surface temperature of the coatings during the subsequent ablation process.

### 3.5 Compositions and microstructure evolution upon ablation

Figure 8 shows the XRD patterns of the coated specimens after ablation. The oxidation products of both coatings are detected as m-ZrO2 (JCPDS#78-1807) and Zr6Ta2O17 (JCPDS#42-0060) phases. No SiO2 is detected due to the low melting point for the evaporation and amorphous state of SiO2 in the coatings. To determine the phase contents of the oxide scale on the coatings after ablation, Rietveld refinement of the XRD results was conducted. The content of Zr6Ta2O17 in the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating after ablation for 30 s is 5.5 wt%, while that in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating (Figs. 8(a) and 8(b)) is 3.6 wt% ascribed to the oxidation of the graphene shell. In addition, the diffraction peak position shifts to a higher angle than that of m-ZrO2 (JCPDS#78-1807), considering the minor Ta solid solution. According to the Rietveld refinement results, the lattice constants of m-ZrO2 in the ZrC–SiC/TaC and ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coatings, *α*, are calculated to be 5.134 and 5.147 Å, respectively, which means that the content of the Ta solid solution in the m-ZrO2 from the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating is higher [44]. A higher content of molten Zr6Ta2O17 and Ta solid solution in the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating can promote sintering to a significantly greater extent than in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating in the early stage of ablation. After ablation for 90 s × 2, the calculated lattice constant (*α*) of m-ZrO2 in the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating (5.136 Å) is also smaller than that in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating (5.159 Å), implying that the content of the Ta solid solution in m-ZrO2 from the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating is higher and the sintering degree is more severe. However, the content of Zr6Ta2O17 in the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating is only 1.7 wt%, while that in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating is 5.1 wt% greater (Figs. 8(c) and 8(d)). With increasing ablation time, the Zr6Ta2O17 in the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating gradually decomposes and volatilizes. The generation of Zr6Ta2O17 in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating is delayed due to the oxidation of the graphene shell, and the heat dissipation efficiency reduces the volatilization of low-melting-point Zr6Ta2O17, thus leaving a high residual amount of Zr6Ta2O17.

In the early stage of ablation, the high thermal conductivity of the graphene shell allowed heat transfer along the thermal conduction network in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating, and the oxidation of the graphene shell could take heat away, thus lowering the surface temperature (Fig. 7(d)). Conversely, after ablation for 30 s, the oxidation of the carbide phases in the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating and higher surface temperature left a greater content of molten Zr6Ta2O17, which promoted sintering of the ZrO2 and increased the grain size (Fig. 12(c)). With increasing ablation time, the ZrO2 films in the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating progressively densified under the effect of sintering, and the oxidation of the graphene shell in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating left a porous surface with tiny pores (Figs. 9(e) and 9(f)). The higher convective heat transfer coefficient of the porous surface on the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating kept the surface temperature low and the sintering degree slow. At higher surface temperatures for the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating, Zr6Ta2O17 decomposed and volatilized, which made the dense Ta-doped ZrO2 coating produce larger cracks. The heat dissipation efficiency of the graphene shell reduced the volatilization of Zr6Ta2O17 in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating, forming a dense coating with uniformly distributed needle-like Ta-containing oxides in the grain boundaries of Ta-doped ZrO2 after ablation for 90 s × 2 (Fig. 10(f)). The grain size of the Ta-doped ZrO2 particles was only ~1.5 μm for the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating, as shown in Fig. 10(f), which is smaller than that for the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating (Fig. 10(c)), proving that the graphene shell in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating delays the sintering of ZrO2 particles and prolongs the anti-ablation life.

## 4 Conclusions

Graphene shells with high thermal conductivity were introduced into polymer-derived SiC/TaC ceramics by adding DVB in polymers. The corresponding ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating on SiC-coated carbon/carbon composites was fabricated by SAPS to enhance the ablation resistance. The graphene shell in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating had a robust heat dissipation effect for lowering the surface temperature to approximately 200 °C under an oxyacetylene torch with a heat flux of 2.38 mW/m2 for 90 s × 2. The linear ablation rate of the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating decreased to 0.009 μm/s, 93.7% lower than that of the ZrC–SiC/TaC coating. The microstructural evolution revealed that the high thermal conductivity of the graphene shell allowed heat transfer throughout the thermal conduction network, and the oxidation of the graphene shell removed heat, thus lowering the surface temperature and delaying the sintering of the ZrO2 particles in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating. The formation of Ta-doped ZrO2 particles with smaller grain sizes in the ZrC–SiC/TaC@C coating resulting from the delayed sintering of the graphene shell promoted the formation of a denser coating, further prolonging the anti-ablation life.
