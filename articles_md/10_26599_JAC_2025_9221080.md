## 1 Introduction

Alumina nanofibers, a remarkable class of nanomaterials, have garnered significant attention in recent years because of their unique characteristics in terms of their large specific surface area, excellent thermal and chemical stability, and excellent mechanical properties [1−4]. These materials have broad applications in high-temperature insulation, catalysis, filtration and separation, composite reinforcement, and biomedical carriers of vaccines [5−8]. Over the past few decades, the synthesis of Al2O3 nanostructures, particularly nanofibers and whiskers, has garnered significant research attention. A variety of fabrication techniques have been developed for this purpose, including electrospinning [9,10], sol–gel [11], hydrothermal synthesis [12], template-based methods [13], vapor deposition [14], etc. In a representative study, Valcárcel *et al*. [15] implemented vapor‒liquid‒solid (VLS) deposition to fabricate ultralong Al2O3 whiskers with exceptional aspect ratios exceeding 10³. Yu *et al*. [16] explored an electrospinning approach using aluminum 2,4-pentanedionate/acetone precursor solutions. Post-spinning thermal treatment under a flowing argon atmosphere enabled the formation of microcrystalline α-Al2O3 nanofibers (200–400 nm diameter) within a moderate temperature range of 800–1300 °C. Shen *et al*. [17] employed hydrothermal synthesis to precipitate Al3+ from tetraalkylated ammonium hydroxide, which was then subjected to solidified steam treatment to prepare boehmite nanofibers. After calcination, γ-Al2O3 with an aspect ratio of 150 was produced. Although these methods can be used to fabricate one-dimensional Al2O3 fibers with stable morphologies and structures, certain challenges and spaces remain for improvement in future development and practical applications, as summarized in Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The advancement of Al2O3 nanofibers is subject to limited output, complex processes, stringent preparation conditions, and costly expenses, which fail to meet the application requirements of current high-tech industrial and technological development. Therefore, exploring a new synthesis method to prepare Al2O3 nanofibers with precisely controllable morphology and size, large aspect ratio, low cost, and scalable industrial yield is highly important in state-of-the-art industrial systems.

Dealloying, a corrosion-driven process that selectively removes one or more components from an alloy, enables the precise fabrication of nanostructured fibers. For example, Lei *et al.* [18] and Yushin *et al*. [19], for the first time, attributed this phenomenon to a bimetallic Al‒Li alloy that can be transformed into alkoxide nanofibers once immersed in alcohol. Subsequently, metal oxide nanofibers (MgO, CuO) and pure metallic Zn nanofibers were synthesized [20−23]. Nevertheless, in the case of Al2O3 nanofibers, the evolution process of the crystal structure of alumina nanofibers obtained by calcination at different temperatures has not been explored; in particular, research on monocrystalline α-Al2O3 nanofibers tends to be rare.

In contrast, we have investigated a simple dealloying-based approach to prepare alumina nanofibers without the use of expensive catalysts or synthesis agents. For the synthesis of alumina nanofibers, a binary Al‒Li alloy is used as the starting material. Through specific chemical processes, the highly active lithium in the alloy is preferentially removed. Moreover, on the basis of the mechanism of boundary strain energy minimization, the directional growth of Al alkoxide nanofibers from the grain boundaries to the interior of the crystal is promoted. After the dealloying treatment, the sample was directly annealed in an air atmosphere to obtain alumina nanofibers.

In this work, we present several novel contributions beyond the scope of existing studies. Specifically, we investigated the structural evolution of the alloy system toward the formation of one-dimensional Al alkoxide nanofibers. Using the molecular dynamics module of Gromacs software, we quantitatively analyzed the ligand structures of the solvents and ions involved in the reaction, providing mechanistic insights into the preferential interaction between dry ethanol and Li+. Furthermore, we examined the reactivity of Al–Li alloys with different compositions and characterized both the Al alkoxide and alumina nanofibers in terms of their physical structure and morphology. Most importantly, we have, for the first time, systematically investigated the crystal structure and morphological evolution of alumina nanofibers synthesized via this dealloying route under different calcination temperatures. These findings significantly advance the understanding of nanofiber formation mechanisms and offer a cost-effective, scalable route for alumina nanofiber fabrication.

### 2.1 Fabrication of binary Al‒Li alloys

First, aluminum pellets (9.5 mm, purity 99.99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) and lithium flakes (16 mm × 0.6 mm, purity 99.95%, Benoit Battery Material Co., Ltd., China) are selected and melted to fabricate an Al‒Li binary alloy. The composition of the alloy is 5–15 wt% Li, and the remaining Al is present. The raw material was placed in graphite molds coated with boron nitride and melted in a melting furnace (GS-1A, GuangShu, China), and the process was carried out in a glove box (Lab2000, Etelux, China) under an argon atmosphere. The melting temperature was set at 720 °C, and the heating rate was 25 °C/min. After approximately 5 min of smelting, the molten alloy was immediately poured out and flattened on a graphite plate. Binary Al‒Li alloys with varying lithium contents of 5, 10, and 15 wt% were obtained and designated as Al‒5Li, Al‒10Li, and Al‒15Li, respectively.

The prepared Al‒Li binary alloy was placed in an inorganic alcohol solvent (such as methanol, ethanol, or isopropanol; Shanghai Huyantong Technology Co., Ltd., China) for dealloying. The dealloying process is carried out in an inert gas (argon) environment at a temperature of 20–60 °C for 48–170 h to obtain aluminate nanofibers. The alloy reacts with ethanol to produce soluble Li-alkoxide (LiEtO) and a white precipitate of Al-alkoxide (Al(EtO)3). Ethanol was selected as the preferred solvent, and the dealloying process was conducted at 60 °C. Under these conditions, the alumina nanofibers presented the highest aspect ratio, reaching 600.

### 2.2 Fabrication of Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub> nanofibers

The obtained Al-alkoxide nanofibers were centrifuged, washed, dispersed, and dried to obtain alumina nanofiber precursors. The obtained precursor was calcined in a muffle furnace (SRJX-4-13A, Daoxu Yanguang Instrument Equipment Factory, China) under an air atmosphere with a heating rate of 1 °C/min from room temperature to 600–1200 °C and held at the target temperature for 30 min. ### 2.3 Characterization and analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were plotted on the basis of the results of the X-ray diffraction experiment (Smartlab9, Rigaku Corporation, Japan) with a range of 2*θ* angles from 5° to 90° and a glancing rate of 8.5 (°)/min. To observe the microstructure of the Al(EtO)3 nanofibers, the Al2O3 nanofibers were diluted with dry ethanol, dripped onto silicon wafers to dry, and then sputtered with Pt. The microstructures and compositions of the samples were then observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-7001F, JEOL, Japan) and an energy dispersive spectroscope (EDS; JSM-7001F, JEOL, Japan). The Al2O3 nanofibers were diluted with dry ethanol, subjected to ultrasonication for a period of 10 min using an ultrasonic machine (F-009SD, China), and then filtered through a copper mesh. The resulting material was observed via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2 F30, FEI, USA; JEM-F200, JEOL, Japan) to analyze the microstructural and crystal structure of the nanofibers. The distribution of functional groups in the Al(EtO)3 nanofibers was investigated via a Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscope (Nicolet 6700, Thermo Fisher, USA) with a scanning wavelength range of 500–3500 cm−1 and a resolution of ≤ 0.09 cm−1. A simultaneous thermal analyzer (STA, 449 F3, NETZSCH, Germany) was employed to conduct thermogravimetric-differential scanning calorimetric testing of the Al(EtO)3 nanofibers. The changes in sample mass with respect to temperature and phase transition temperature were analyzed according to the thermogravimetric analysis-differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC) curves. The heating rate of the nanofiber sample under air was 10 °C/min, and the measurement temperature range was 0–1200 °C. The molecular dynamics module of the Gromacs software was used to simulate the molecular dynamics of the electrolytes. Cubic cells 3.73 nm in size were used (including 15 Al3+, 10 Li+, 600 C2H5OH, and 5 H2O). For each system, initial energy minimization was performed via the steepest descent approach. The anneal contains ten cycles to find the best density, and the last frame was chosen as the initial dynamic structure. The thermostat temperature was maintained at 298 K, and the coupling time constant was set to 0.1 ps. A semi-isotropic Parrinello–Brahmanbaria was used to maintain the pressure at l bar. The particle mesh Ewald method was used to address electrostatic interactions. The total simulation time is 10 ns to obtain an equal product.

### 3.2 Microstructure and phase analysis of the Al(EtO)<sub>3</sub> nanofibers

The systematic dealloying process of Al‒Li in different alcoholic solvents was inspected, and the corresponding reaction products were characterized. As shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM, after immersion of Al–Li in methanol and isopropanol, no nanofibers were identified instead of particles or rods. Amazingly, ethanol gives rise to whole nanofibers with high aspect ratios. The various morphologies after dealloying in different solvents are likely related to the polarity of the solvent (methanol > ethanol > isopropanol) and the acidity (methanol (pKa ≈ 15.5) > ethanol (pKa ≈ 15.97)) > isopropanol (pKa ≈ 17.1)) [24,25]. When the alloy reacted in methanol, both the reaction rate and delithiation were excessively rapid, which hindered the formation of nanofibers. This was primarily because the overly fast reaction rate caused a discontinuity in the traction force, disrupting the equilibrium state of strain energy minimization at the grain boundaries. In isopropanol, the reactions of the alloys were slow, and the obtained nanorods had small aspect ratios. This could be ascribed to the occurrence of radial growth, and it was found that the polarity and acidity of the solvent significantly affected the aspect ratio of the nanorods.

Since the nanofibers did not emerge in either methanol or isopropanol, the subsequent experiments were carried out in ethanol. Compared with other solvents, ethanol and metals tend to spontaneously polymerize into metal-ethanol. The volume, electronegativity, and structure of the alcohol strongly influence the coordination number and intermolecular packing of the metal. These factors determine the polymerization mode and degree, which in turn determine the morphology of the material [26,27].

The organic composition of the Al(EtO)3 nanofibers obtained from Al–15Li was characterized via FTIR, as shown in Fig. 3(a). The weak peaks appearing at 2970 and 2841 cm−1 correspond to the C‒H stretching mode of the ethanol portion, the corresponding peak at 1391 cm−1 is caused by C‒H bending of the ethanol portion, and the characteristics of the Al‒O‒C group and the Al‒O bonding are observed up to 1200 cm−1, respectively. Specifically, the bands observed at 1051, 1098, and 1172 cm−1 are due to the characteristic—Al–O–C stretching vibrations in the Al alkoxide. The stretching vibrational peak of the Al‒O bond in the pentahedral is observed at 889 cm−1, whereas the Al‒O bond stretching vibration peaks in the octahedral disposition are observed at 699, 641, and 504 cm−1 [28].

To understand the chemical composition of the alkoxide nanofibers, XRD analysis of the Al(EtO)3 nanofibers was conducted. As shown in Fig. 3(b), the presence of strong diffraction peaks indicates that Al(EtO)3 possesses a certain degree of crystallinity, which may be attributed to the formation of microcrystalline regions during heating. In the range of 30°–80°, the diffraction pattern has a relatively flat background with no prominent peaks, suggesting that the sample contains many amorphous components. The Al(EtO)3 nanofibers consisted of chemical groups, and no other impurities were found. As shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM, we performed EDS tests on the Al(EtO)3 nanofibers, and the results revealed the presence of Al, O, and C components.

The relationships between the Li content in the Al‒Li alloy and the reaction time, as well as its influence on the morphology of the nanofibers, were explored. Figure 4 shows the morphology of the Al(EtO)3 nanofibers after dealloying in ethanol with Al‒15Li (Figs. 4(a) and 4(d)), Al‒10Li (Figs. 4(b) and 4(e)), and Al‒5Li (Figs. 4(c) and 4(f)). The Al‒5Li alloy exhibited complete reactivity within approximately seven days, the Al‒10Li alloy within approximately five days, and the Al‒15Li alloy within a mere two days. After centrifugation and washing, the sample was heated for 72 h. The image shows that the fibers are morphologically complete, well dispersed, and without any impurities. Alloys with a high lithium content have a faster reaction. The more abundant the Li atoms are, the more free space is available for the rearrangement of the Al-ethoxy atoms, the greater the local regulation of the volume increment, and the lesser the internal stresses. Therefore, the higher the lithium content is, the faster the reaction rate. Accordingly, in alloys with low lithium contents, the reactions of the Al(EtO)3 nanofibers are slow, the reactivities are relatively low, and the reaction times are longer.

### 3.3 Microstructure and phase analysis of Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>nanofibers

To study the transformation process of Al(EtO)3 nanofibers into Al2O3, TG and DSC analyses were performed on the Al(EtO)3 nanofibers obtained from Al–15Li, as shown in Fig. 5(a). Significant weight loss occurs at 0–400 °C (approximately 65%), which is related to the decomposition of the organic –C–H groups. The mass tends to stabilize above 500 °C. Weight loss in the range below 150 °C can be attributed to the loss of free water on the surface of the Al(EtO)3 nanofibers and the stratification of chemisorbed water. The endothermic peak at approximately 359.7 °C may be related to the thermal decomposition of the alcohol oxides. At higher temperatures, two distinct endothermic peaks were found at 843 and 1099 °C, indicating that the phase transformation occurred at these temperatures. The transition from the γ-Al2O3 monoclinic phase (*d* = 3.56 g/cm3) to the α-Al2O3 hexagonal phase (*d* = 3.98 g/cm3) is accompanied by a volume decrease of approximately 10%, which leads to a significant increase in alumina density [29]. The transformation of the different crystalline forms of alumina occurs through nucleation and grain growth mechanisms and is influenced by parameters such as grain size, chemical composition, and heating rate [30]. Aluminum oxide exists in different transition states and substable states in different temperature ranges, such as α-Al2O3, β-Al2O3, γ-Al2O3, θ-Al2O3, κ-Al2O3, δ-Al2O3, χ-Al2O3, and η-Al2O3 [31,32].

To investigate this process, XRD patterns at different temperatures were recorded. Figure 5(b) shows the XRD patterns of γ-Al2O3 from Al–15Li prepared at 600, 700, 800, 900, and 1000 °C. The deviation in phase transformation was attributed primarily to the higher heating rate (10 °C/min) used in the DSC process. The combination of XRD data also further validates this view. Among various aluminum oxides, α-Al2O3 crystals exhibit the best physical and chemical stability. The degree of crystallization of α-Al2O3 is relatively high. In the crystal structure of α-Al2O3, three O²− form the coplanar surface of two adjacent octahedra, and numerous octahedra are combined through the coplanar surface to form a complete crystal. Therefore, α-Al2O3 crystals are more stable than other crystal forms of Al2O3, making them ideal raw materials for the preparation of ceramics, heat-resistant materials, and other new functional materials [35]. Compared with α-Al2O3 materials synthesized via other approaches, the α-Al2O3 material prepared via this method possesses not only a monocrystalline structure but also a fiber diameter of approximately 100 nm.

The overall morphology and continuity of the calcined fibers and Al(EtO)3 nanofibers essentially remained unchanged and homogeneous. This might be attributed to the phase transitions causing atomic rearrangements during high-temperature calcination and the absence of –OH groups, resulting in annexation between the nanocrystals.

### 4.1 Analysis of the transformation of alloys to Al(EtO)<sub>3</sub> nanofibers.

Bimetallic Al‒Li alloys are obtained by melting active metal Li and inactive metal Al. Subsequent immersion of the alloys in an appropriate quantity of dry ethanol results in the formation of Al(EtO)3, LiEtO, and H2. The reaction equation is as Reaction (1) [18]:

(1) AlLi＋4EtOH→Al(EtO)3＋LiEtO＋2H2↑

Figure 8(a) shows the morphology of the pristine grains of the Al‒15Li alloy. Figures 8(b)–8(d) show the morphology of the grains immersed in ethanol for 5 min, 30 min, and 2 h, respectively. The dry ethanol molecules first react preferentially with Li+ to produce LiEtO along with the release of hydrogen. The dissolution of Li promotes the reaction of inactive metallic Al. Because the formation of oxides on the surface of pure Al inhibits the reaction, it cannot react with alcohols unless a catalyst is used to prevent passivation of the Al [38,39]. Thus, by dissolving Li in Al–Li alloys, continuous exposure to a fresh Al surface results in sufficient reactivity of the remaining unsaturated bonds of Al. This provides the conditions for the ensuing reaction with alcohols, thus inducing the formation of aluminum alkoxide compounds. Atomic rearrangements due to continued dissolution and volume changes in Li also significantly prevented the passivation of Al. The overall nanofiber growth process of Al–Li alloys in ethanol can be illustrated by a schematic diagram, as shown in Fig. 8(g). Ethanol first reacts immediately with the Li-rich regions, leading to the formation of Al-rich regions in the grooves of the planar abrasive grain surface. Ethanol rapidly occupies the voids left by the dissolution of the Li-rich grain boundaries and reacts with the Al to become a stable nucleus that is converted into an Al alkoxide. As the reaction proceeds, the nanoclusters continue to extend from the grain boundaries toward the center until the alloy grains are completely transformed into well-aligned Al alkoxide nanoclusters, which are then reheated and dispersed in preheated fresh ethanol to further split into single nanofibers. From the perspective of kinetics, the outward diffusion of H2 and Li+ generated by the reaction and the inward diffusion of alcohols are also favorable for the formation of one-dimensional fiber morphology. From the perspective of thermodynamics, the growth process is thermodynamically driven, and the direction of growth is always chosen to minimize free energy. In summary, aligned organic metal‒alcohol nanofibers were formed on the basis of the mechanism of boundary strain energy minimization growing inward from the boundary. The coordination polymerization of Al atoms is linked together by Al–O–Al bonds, so the Al alkoxide nanofibers have better ductility to withstand the stresses induced by the phase transition, thus avoiding fragmentation. Notably, metal‒alcohol compounds are sensitive to water, so strict measures are taken to prevent hydrolysis during the reaction. Figure 8(e) shows that removing the LiEtO at the end of the reaction gave nanoclusters of Al(EtO)3. At the beginning of the reaction, Li reacts with ethanol to form Li-alkoxides, which are flower-like. We found via EDS that they do not contain Al, confirming that they are indeed Li-alkoxides (Fig. S5 in the ESM). The use of fresh dry ethanol washed away the highly soluble LiEtO, leaving only the Al(EtO)3 nanoclusters of the desired substance. The Al(EtO)3 nanoclusters were heated at 60 °C for 72 h to obtain well-dispersed nanofibers. This is due to the reaction of aluminum atoms with ethanol molecules to form nanoclusters of Al(EtO)3 consisting of interconnected long polymer chains on the basis of the mechanism of boundary strain energy minimization. The formation of such polymer chains is then dependent on the ability of Al and oxygen donor atoms to form stable bonds. It was shown that Al reacts with ethanol molecules to form Al mononuclear complexes with EtOH groups, which are then hydrogen-bonded to aluminum monomers. Several H-bonds exist between the polymer chains, making the polymer chains firmly connected to each other. Nanoclusters can be dispersed to nanofibers by increasing the temperature because the heating reaction breaks the Al‒O bonds at the octahedral vertices, releasing ethanol molecules, which desaturates the AlO6 to lower-coordination-number aluminum atoms. This process changes the coordination number of the Al atoms from VI to V and IV. The presence of Al(V) is essential for maintaining bridges between individual chains, thus preserving the integrity of the one-dimensional fiber structure. Figure 8(f) shows the fiber morphology after 72 h of dispersion. The dispersion time during heating markedly influences the morphology of the Al alkoxide nanofibers. As illustrated in Figs. 8(e) and 8(f), unheated nanofibers predominantly exhibit a clustered structure, whereas those subjected to 72 h of heating are well dispersed. Nevertheless, prolonged heating (168 h) results in a noticeable tendency toward reaggregation (Fig. S6 in the ESM) [40].

### 4.2 Simulation calculations

To further investigate the reaction process of ethanol solvents and ions during dealloying. The ligand structures of the solvents and ions used during the reaction were quantitatively investigated via the molecular dynamics module of Gromacs software. Radical distribution function (RDF, *g*(*r*)) trajectories imply the two-dimensional probability distribution of a particle at the distance *r* from a reference particle and describe the solvation structure. According to the RDF results (Fig. 9(a)), a significantly higher intensity of the Al3+-EtOH peak than the Li+-EtOH peak can be observed in the solvation structure, suggesting an inner solvation sheath dominated by Al3+-EtOH. However, the Li+ obtained by RDF binds to ethanol at 1.85 Å to form the first solvated shell layer, and the Al3+ binds farther away in comparison, occurring at 2.43 Å. This is further evidence that the dry ethanol molecule reacts first with Li+ and later with Al3+. In the solvation structure, the Li+-solvated sheath has a higher probability of a priority response than free solvents/anions do [41]. In addition to the RDFs, several integral calculations were performed to determine the number density of atoms around the reference atom at the distance *r*′ (Fig. 9(b)). ### 4.3 Analyses of phase transitions

Through heating in air, these metal‒organic nanofibers can be oxidized into one-dimensional metal oxide nanofibers. Most organometallic compounds have strong bonds between the metal and the organic ligand [43,44]. These are also the covalent bonds required for the conversion of organic alcohol oxides from substitution, oxidation, or hydrolysis reactions to ceramic oxides. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the TG-DSC spectra clearly reveal the disappearance of organic carbon-containing or hydrogen-containing groups with increasing temperatures in the range of 0–400 °C. The apparent broad peak at 843 °C is due to the phase transition of the substance from the amorphous state to γ-Al2O3. The XRD patterns also confirmed this phenomenon, as shown in Figs. 5(b) and 5(c), where the γ-Al2O3 crystalline phase started to appear at 800 °C. γ-Al2O3 belongs to one of the many transition phases of aluminum oxides (α-Al2O3, β-Al2O3, γ-Al2O3, θ-Al2O3, κ-Al2O3, δ-Al2O3, χ-Al2O3, and η-Al2O3), a cubic crystal system, and an octahedral structure. Octahedra form interconnected tetrahedra and spinel structures with a density of 3.42–3.47 g/cm3. O2− forms a cubic close packing, whereas Al3+ fills the voids surrounded by O2−, which has a relatively loose structure and a large specific surface area [45,46]. In these transition phases, the most significant difference in the crystal structure is the change in the position of the oxygen ions in the same arrangement as the aluminum ions. The transition between phases is usually continuous, with no change in the oxygen ion stacking pattern or local aluminum ion transfer. The phase transition is the displacement type, the chemical bond is not broken, and the original crystal morphology is not destroyed. As shown in Fig. 5(b), only the crystallinity increased with increasing temperature, and the morphology observed did not change. α-Al2O3 adopts a trigonal crystal system in which O2− ions form an HCP structure. Al3+ ions occupy two-thirds of the octahedral interstices, positioning themselves as far apart as possible to minimize electrostatic repulsion and conform to the Pauli exclusion principle, thereby contributing to the excellent structural stability of α-Al2O3 [47]. The transformation of γ-Al2O3 to α-Al2O3 at temperatures above 1000 °C is a phase transition from a face-centered cubic (FCC) arrangement of O2− ions to an HCP structure. During this process, O2− ions are rearranged, and chemical bonds are broken and reformed. The phase transition occurring from γ-Al2O3 to α-Al2O3 belongs to the crystalline lattice reconfiguration type of phase transition, which requires more energy than the displacement type of phase transition and can only be carried out at more than 1000 °C [48,49]. The transformation of Al2O3 from polycrystalline to monocrystalline may be the result of a reconstructive phase transition during the heating process, in which the atoms are rearranged and the direction of crystal growth is changed [50].

## 5 Conclusions

In this research endeavor, an economically viable and highly efficient dealloying-based approach is proposed for the preparation of monocrystalline α-Al2O3 and polycrystalline γ-Al2O3 nanofibers, obviating the necessity for any catalyst. The principal conclusions are delineated as follows:

1) The solvent has a crucial effect on the reaction products, with substantial variations arising from differences in solvent properties such as polarity and acidity. Methanol gives rise to particulate reaction products, ethanol yields nanofibers, and isopropanol results in nanorods. Consequently, the selection of ethanol as the reaction agent provides the essential foundation for the preparation of Al2O3 nanofibers.

2) In alloys with a low lithium content, a deceleration in the reaction rate is observed, accompanied by relatively low reactivity and an increase in the reaction time. The reaction times for the Al–5Li, Al–10Li, and Al–15Li alloys are 7, 5, and 2 d, respectively. After 72 h of heating, well-dispersed Al(EtO)3 nanofibers are obtained, maintaining an intact fiber morphology, with a diameter in the range of approximately 50–80 nm and a length of 20–30 μm.

3) The transformation of the material’s crystal structure occurs during the course of the calcination process. At 800 °C, the nanofibers begin crystallizing, transitioning from an amorphous state to polycrystalline γ-Al2O3. Above 1000 °C, γ-Al2O3 undergoes a structural transition from the FCC O2− arrangement to the HCP O2− arrangement of α-Al2O3, accompanied by O2− rearrangement, chemical bond cleavage, and reorganization. At 1200 °C, there is a complete transformation from polycrystalline γ-Al2O3 to stable monocrystalline α-Al2O3.

4) On the basis of the molecular dynamics module of the Gromacs software, a quantitative investigation of the ligand structures of solvents and ions during the reaction is conducted. In the solvated structure, the Li+ solvated sheath layer has a higher preferential response probability than the free solvent/anion. The Li+ obtained by RDF binds to ethanol at a distance of 1.85 Å to form the first solvated shell layer, whereas the Al3+ binds at a relatively greater distance of 2.43 Å.
