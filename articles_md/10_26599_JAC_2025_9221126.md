## 1 Introduction

Textured ferroelectric perovskite ceramics, which exhibit single-crystal-like anisotropic properties, have attracted significant attention because of their excellent comprehensive ferroelectric performance, such as remnant electric polarization and piezoelectricity [1−5]. Among various processing techniques, templated grain growth (TGG) has emerged as a promising method to achieve high degrees of crystallographic orientation in textured ceramics [6−14]. However, the morphology of the templated grains makes the ceramics difficult to densify as randomly oriented ceramics, which is the fundamental challenge for obtaining high-quality textured ceramics.

Conventionally, liquid-phase sintering (LPS) is widely employed in TGG to facilitate template alignment and grain growth. By introducing oxides with low melting points, highly textured ceramics, such as Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3–PbTiO3 and Sr0.53Ba0.47Nb2O6, have been easily fabricated [15−20]. For textured BaTiO3-based ceramics, LPS has been used to promote densification by adding excessive TiO2 to form a Ti-rich liquid phase (Ba6Ti17O40) at 1300–1350 °C or sintering aids such as CuO [21−23]. However, several intrinsic drawbacks arise for LPS. Template instability in reactive liquid environments hinders the heteroepitaxial growth of oriented grains, limiting texture quality [24]. Residual liquid phases or secondary nonferroelectric compounds often remain at grain boundaries, degrading piezoelectric and dielectric properties and increasing hysteretic losses [15,16]. Furthermore, excessive grain coarsening induced by LPS may also compromise ferroelectric and mechanical reliability [21,25−27]. Some textured BaTiO3-based ceramics prepared through LPS exhibit large grain sizes exceeding 40 μm, with a piezoelectric constants (*d*33) not exceeding 350 pC·N**−**1 [28−30]. These limitations emphasize the need for alternative densification mechanisms.

In this work, we propose a liquid-phase-free sintering approach for obtaining highly [001]c-textured BaTiO3 ceramics and explore its sintering mechanism. By adjusting the size and concentration of the templates via computational phase-field modeling and experimental validation, we find that the symmetry-constrain strains usually relieved via LPS can instead be overcome through orientation fluctuations during nucleation and growth. The sintering method based on this liquid-phase-free mechanism avoids secondary phases while maintaining fine-grained structures (c-textured BaTiO3 ceramics exhibit a maximum piezoelectric constant (*d*33) of 490±11 pC·N**−**1, exceeding that of their single-crystal counterparts, confirming the validity of liquid-phase-free processing for high-performance ferroelectric ceramics. This advancement not only addresses the challenges of LPS but also rediscovers the role of template engineering in microstructural control, opening another avenue for scalable fabrication of defect-minimized textured ceramics.

## 2 Materials and methods

The tetragonal [001]c-textured BaTiO3 ceramics were fabricated via the TGG method with 5 vol% BaTiO3 templates. Highly pure BaCO3 (≥ 99.8%), TiO2 (≥ 99.0%), Bi2O3 (≥ 99.0%), NaCl (≥ 99.5%), KCl (≥ 99.5%), and BaCl2·2H2O (≥ 99.5%) powders procured from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. were used as raw materials. Tetragonal [001]c-textured BaTiO3 templates were synthesized via a three-step topochemical reaction in NaCl–KCl and KCl–BaCl2·2H2O molten salts. The average size of the prepared templates is approximately 5 μm × 5 μm × 1 μm, and their morphology has been presented in previous work [31]. BaCO3 and TiO2 powders were weighed according to the stoichiometric composition and mixed with ethanol and ZrO2 balls for 8 h via a planetary ball mill. The mixture was dried and calcined at 1200 °C for 4 h to synthesize BaTiO3 powders. After ball milling and drying, the resulting powders underwent a second calcination and ball milling process to homogenize the phase distribution, improve the elemental uniformity, and reduce the particle size. BaTiO3 powders, solvents, binders, dispersants, plasticizers and templates were mixed into slurries. The slurry was tape cast, dried and cut into plastic green tapes (10 mm × 10 mm). The green body was obtained by laminating green tapes at 60 MPa, followed by cold isostatic pressing at 200 MPa and binder burnout at 600 °C. BaTiO3 ceramics with different degrees of texture were sintered at temperatures ranging from 900 to 1550 °C. The randomly oriented BaTiO3 ceramics were prepared via a traditional solid-state reaction method.

A scanning electron microscope (SEM; GeminiSEM 560, ZEISS, Germany) equipped with an EBSD detector (Symmetry S3, Oxford, UK) was used to obtain microstructure images and orientation information. The grain size was statistically analyzed via ImageJ software (US National Institutes of Health, USA) [32]. The changes in the dimensions of the green bodies during heating were analyzed via a dilatometer (DIL 402, NETZSCH, Germany) with a heating rate of 5 °C·min**−**1 in ambient atmosphere. The mass and heat changes during the sintering process of the green bodies were measured by a simultaneous thermal analyzer (STA 449, NETZSCH, Germany). The phase structure and degree of texture were examined by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X’Pert Pro, PANalytical, the Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA) and a scan step of 0.013°. Ferroelectric hysteresis loops were measured via a ferroelectric tester (Precision Premier II, Radiant Technologies Inc., USA). A Berlincourt piezoelectric meter (ZJ-6A, Institute of Acoustics, China) was used to determine the piezoelectric constant (*d*33). The samples were poled via an AC/DC withstand voltage tester (ET2671A, Entai Electronic Instruments Plant, China) before the piezoelectric test.

The phase-field modeling approach employed in this study simulates grain growth phenomena during the sintering processes of both randomly oriented and textured ceramics, building upon the established framework developed by Fan and Chen [33]. The microstructure evolution is governed through the Allen–Cahn equation formalism, where the temporal development of the grain orientation order parameters (*η**i*) follows the relationship ∂*η**i*/∂*t* = −*L**i*(δ*F*/δ*η**i*) (*i* = 1, 2, …, *N*) with *t* representing the time, *L**i* denoting the mobility coefficient, *F* denoting the total free energy, and *N* representing the number of orientations, which is set to 20. The numerical implementation uses a finite difference discretization scheme applied to a two-dimensional computational domain comprising 1000 × 1000 grid points. Initial microstructural configurations were generated through Voronoi tessellation for random polycrystalline structures, yielding an average grain area of 244 grid points. For textured ceramic simulations, rectangular template grains with controlled aspect ratios (length/width) were strategically embedded to emulate anisotropic grain growth along preferred crystallographic orientations during sintering. The spatial and temporal discretization parameters were optimized to balance the computational efficiency with the microstructural resolution requirements.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1 shows the processes of grain growth simulated by phase-field modeling. Fixing the initial area ratio of template grains as 14.4% in matrix grains, a series of template grains with different sizes and numbers are constructed to investigate the change in the area ratio. Compared with random grain growth, as shown in Fig. 1(a), the grain growth induced by the templates is hindered by the rotation of the grains (Figs. 1(b) and 1(c)). Changing the amount and aspect ratio of the template facilitates the rotation of the grains, as shown in Figs. 1(d) and 1(e). When the initial number of templates is 1, the area ratio is 40.4% at step 5000. When the initial number of templates increases to 8, the area ratio reaches a maximum of 85.0%. When the initial number of templates is increased to 16, the area ratio decreases to 77.5%. Therefore, eight template grains are appropriate for obtaining the ceramic with the highest degree of texture; that is, the thickness of the templates and the spacing between the templates should be ~3 and ~8 times greater than the particle size of the matrix grains, respectively. As grain growth proceeded, both the length and width of the template increased, whereas the aspect ratio decreased from 5.33 to 2.60 (Fig. 1(e)), demonstrating anisotropic grain growth along different orientations of the template. Since we have considered the rotation of the grains in our phase-field modeling, the orientation fluctuation of the grains around the template can be clearly captured in the intermediate sintering process. In addition, abnormal grain growth was also observed, revealing that low-temperature sintering was needed.

Figure 2 shows the sintering process of the textured BaTiO3 ceramics. There are three main stages for densification, as shown in Figs. 2(a)–2(i): orientation fluctuation of the template grains, template-induced grain growth, and anisotropic grain growth. The typical microstructures, inverse pole figure maps, and corresponding pole figures of the textured ceramics of each stage were captured on the perpendicular surface, as shown in Fig. 2(k). In the first stage (Figs. 2(a), 2(d), and 2(g)), irregular fluctuations (marked in Fig. 2(a)) appear at the lateral grain boundaries of the BaTiO3 template grains, with the inverse pole figure map indicating a low degree of texture at this stage. Thermodynamically, the critical driving force for sintering is inversely proportional to temperature [34,35]. The lower temperature conditions during the initial sintering stage enable only coarse grains (i.e., template grains in textured ceramics) to grow, whereas the growth of fine grains (i.e., matrix grains) is suppressed. Earlier studies have demonstrated that template grains grow through lateral boundary migration in systems containing a liquid phase [36]. In the second stage (Figs. 2(b), 2(e), and 2(h)), as the sintering temperature further increased, characteristic pyramidal protrusions evolved from fluctuations along the template grain boundaries, accompanied by intergranular contact formation between adjacent templates. The template-induced grain growth led to an increase in the degree of texture.

During the two stages, the matrix grains undergo crystallographic orientation modification through contact with the smooth [001]c-oriented surfaces of the template grains, generating an isolated fluctuation step on the (001) surfaces. This process is remarkably similar to the island growth phenomena observed in thin film deposition on substrates. Notably, the high-temperature cubic phase of BaTiO3 has significant differences in surface energy among its (001), (110), and (111) crystallographic planes (γ*(001) = 1.19 J·m−2, *γ*(011) = 1.44 J·m−2, and *γ*(111) = 8.42 J·m−2, respectively) [37−40]. The energy differences between these planes are large enough to allow matrix grains to adjust their orientation on (001) template grain surfaces and reduce the exposure of non (001) planes, which is thermodynamically favorable. Since the orientations of the grains in the matrix powder are random, the growth of these grains is controlled by thermal fluctuations. In other words, the orientation differences of these planes are balanced by thermal fluctuations. Thus, we can attribute the sintering character in the first stage to the orientation fluctuation. These orientation fluctuations of the morphologies agree well with those of the calculations in the previous phase-field modeling.

The third stage is anisotropic grain growth, as shown in Figs. 2(c), 2(f), and 2(i). During this stage, surface fluctuation of the template grains stops, and planar grain boundary (marked in Fig. 2(c)) migration starts. Statistical analysis of the grain boundary orientations through the inverse pole figure map (Fig. 2(f)) reveals the predominant crystallographic planes of the final textured ceramics. In Fig. 1(j), the angular distribution between the grain boundaries and the sample coordinate X-axis exhibits a normal distribution centered at 37.5°, strongly indicating that the {112} family of crystallographic planes is the energetically preferred orientation.

Figure 3 shows the dynamic evolution of the dimensional, volumetric, and thermal parameters during green body sintering of textured BaTiO3 ceramics. The length variation (Δ*L*/*L*0) curves and their first derivatives (d(Δ*L*/*L*0)/d*T*) for textured ceramics along both parallel and perpendicular orientations (as defined in Fig. 2(k), abbreviated as "Para." and "Prep." respectively in legend) are presented in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b), respectively, with comparative data from randomly oriented BaTiO3 ceramics. Δ*L* and *L*0 represent the length variation and the initial length of the green body during sintering, respectively. Prior to the onset of sintering shrinkage at 750 °C, all three green bodies exhibit nearly identical expansion behavior. However, during the most intensive shrinkage phase (1150–1300 °C), the textured ceramics display significant anisotropic linear contraction, reaching 18.66% in the parallel orientation, which is higher than the 14.75% in the perpendicular orientation. Within this temperature range, the shrinkage rate along the parallel orientation is also greater than that along the perpendicular orientation. By comparison, randomly oriented ceramics exhibit intermediate values in both shrinkage magnitude and kinetics.

*
Fig. 3(a) Length variation of textured and randomly oriented BaTiO3 ceramics during the sintering process and (b) related first derivative curves; (c) volume change and (d) relative density calculated from the length variation; (e) heat flow and mass loss during the sintering process; (f) activation energy calculated from the length variation.

This temperature window covers the three main densification stages illustrated in Fig. 2, demonstrating that densification is synergistically coupled with template grain growth. In the first stage, matrix grain rotation facilitates pore expulsion toward the ends of the template grains. During the second stage, template grains prefer to grow along surfaces with the (001) crystallographic plane, whereas the third stage involves oblique migration of the grain boundaries. Both the grain rotation and orientation fluctuation mechanisms reduce the aspect ratios of the template grains, constituting the microscopic origin of anisotropic shrinkage.

Figure 3(c) presents the dynamic volume changes of textured and randomly oriented BaTiO3 ceramics during the sintering process, which were calculated from the length variation according to Eqs. (1) an (2):

(1) ΔVV0=1−(1−(ΔLL0)random)3

(2) ΔVV0=1−(1−(ΔLL0)textured,para.)(1−(ΔLL0)textured,prep.)2

where ΔV*/*V*0 denotes the volume change, and the footnote of Δ*L*/*L*0 represents the sample type (i.e., the randomly oriented sample or the textured sample) and direction of shrinkage (i.e., parallel or perpendicular direction for the textured sample). Randomly oriented ceramics were considered isotropic systems, whereas anisotropy was accounted for in textured ceramics. The influence of linear thermal expansion is compensated for prior to calculation. The relative density change during the sintering process can be calculated from the dynamic volume change and the relative density of the as-prepared BaTiO3 ceramics, as shown in Fig. 3(d). Although textured ceramics exhibit greater final linear shrinkage along the parallel orientation than randomly oriented ceramics do, their anisotropic shrinkage results in marginally lower overall volumetric changes. At a sintering temperature of 1400 °C, the relative densities of the textured and randomly oriented ceramics reached 95.6% and 97.0%, respectively. These values align closely with the measurements via Archimedes' principle and confirm that, compared with their randomly oriented counterparts, textured ceramics exhibit hindered densification [17,36].

Another characteristic of hindered densification in textured ceramics is their high energy requirement. As evidenced by the heat flow versus temperature curves in Fig. 3(e), although both textured and randomly oriented ceramics undergo endothermic processes during sintering, the peak heat flow of the former is nearly 3 times that of the latter. The heat flow peak at 1150 °C for the textured ceramics corresponds to the formation of fluctuation steps, indicating that this stage requires overcoming the highest energy barrier. Using the Dorn method [41], the sintering activation energy (*Q*) of a sample with an initial length (*L*0) can be calculated according to Eq. (3):

(3) Q≃RT1T2T1−T2ln(d(ΔL1/L0)dt1/d(ΔL2/L0)dt2)

where *R* is the universal gas constant; *T*1 and *T*2 are the temperatures at the time *t*1 and *t*2, respectively; and Δ*L*1 and Δ*L*2 represent the contracted lengths of the sample at *t*1 and *t*2. The *Q* values of the textured and randomly oriented ceramics calculated via Eq. (1) are shown in Fig. 3(f). Since the sintering activation energy is orientation independent, the *Q* values of the textured ceramics calculated from the parallel and perpendicular shrinkage curves are nearly identical at 404 kJ·mol−1. This value exceeds the value of 383 kJ·mol−1 obtained for randomly oriented ceramics. The thermodynamic analysis confirmed that the formation of fluctuation steps serves as the rate-controlling mechanism during sintering. Despite the requirement of high activation energy, this step facilitates subsequent densification and texture processes.

To investigate the thermal dynamic process of grain growth, the dependence of the grain size on the sintering temperature and holding time is displayed in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b). Although the grains grow anisotropically in parallel and perpendicular orientations during the sintering process, the equiaxed grains are finally obtained when a high temperature of 1450 °C and long holding time of 16 h are reached (Figs. 4(c) and 4(d), respectively). The formation of equiaxed grains is attributed to the high degree of cubic symmetry of BaTiO3 above 130 °C, leading to uniform grain growth. This characteristic is beneficial for the densification of textured BaTiO3 ceramics and the stability of their properties.

The orientation fluctuation mechanism can be further revealed by the changes in crystallographic plane orientation at different sintering temperatures, as shown in Fig. 5, where the holding time was maintained constant at 1 minute. Figures 5(a) and 5(c) present the XRD patterns of textured BaTiO3 ceramics and the degree of texture as a function of sintering temperature, respectively. The degree of texture increased with increasing sintering temperature. The relative intensities of the diffraction peaks for the (001), (110), and (111) crystallographic planes significantly change as the sintering temperature increases, which corresponds to the first stage of sintering with orientation fluctuations. Furthermore, the texture process of the sample was almost complete above 1300 °C, and densification also reached completion at this temperature, indicating the synergistic progression of the two processes. The Lotgering factor F*(001) of the ceramic sintered at 1350 °C reached 95.7%. By extending the holding time to 8 hours, the Lotgering factor could be further improved to 98.5%. As shown in Fig. 5(c), the piezoelectric constant (*d*33) tends to be consistent with the degree of texture across sintering temperatures. Textured BaTiO3 ceramics sintered at 1350 °C achieve a substantial *d*33 enhancement to ~350 pC·N−1, demonstrating well-developed texture formation.

Figures 6(a) and 6(b) show that the piezoelectric constant (*d*33) depends on the poling field and aging time. In well-sintered textured BaTiO3 ceramics (1350 °C, 8 h), *d*33 increases with the poling field until it reaches saturation. Textured ceramics poled under an 8 kV·cm−1 electric field exhibit an abnormally high *d*33 value of 490±11 pC·N−1, which is 111% higher than that of the randomly oriented ceramics. The aging time has a small influence on *d*33. When the aging time is longer than 76 h, the value of *d*33 decreases slightly by 4% and then remains constant, suggesting the elimination of residual stress and the completion of stabilization of the ferroelectric domain and domain walls [42,43]. As a whole, the absolute value of *d*33 is much larger than that of random ceramics, which is also superior to that of single crystals [44].

*
Fig. 6Variations in the piezoelectric constants (d*33) of textured and randomly oriented BaTiO3 ceramics with (a) poling field and (b) aging time; ferroelectric hysteresis loops of (c) textured and (d) randomly oriented BaTiO3 ceramics; (e) temperature-dependent dielectric spectra and (f) *in situ* temperature-dependent piezoelectric constants of textured and randomly oriented BaTiO3 ceramics.

Note that the corresponding ferroelectricity of polarization has no such promotion, as shown in Figs. 6(c) and 6(d), where the remnant polarization values under electric fields of 15 and 10 kV·cm−1 are 8.5 and 10.0 μC·cm−2 for textured and randomly oriented BaTiO3 ceramics, respectively. The abnormal comprehensive ferroelectric properties may be attributed to the following mechanisms: (i) Enhanced domain wall mobility of nanodomain structures, enabling large strain responses without high intrinsic polarization. (ii) Structural instability near twin boundaries, as we reported in our previous study, promotes lattice distortion and efficient electromechanical coupling despite moderate spontaneous polarization [31]. As a result, both mechanisms may decouple the polarization magnitude from the piezoelectric performance via optimized microstructural and texture engineering.

The thermal stability of the textured and randomly oriented BaTiO3 ceramics was characterized by temperature-dependent dielectric spectra and *in situ* temperature-dependent piezoelectric constant *d*33 measurements, as shown in Figs. 6(e) and 6(f). The two ceramics exhibit Curie temperatures (*T*C) of 126 and 131 °C, indicating that the liquid-phase-free sintering method does not compromise *T*C of the textured BaTiO3 ceramic. Fig. 6(f) shows the relative change in the temperature-dependent *d*33 of both textured and randomly oriented ceramics compared with their room temperature values. *d*33 dramatically decreases when approaching *T*C. The inset of Fig. 6(f) highlights variations in *d*33 prior to reaching *T*C, where the maximum variations for textured and randomly oriented BaTiO3 ceramics are observed at 12% vs. 24%, respectively, demonstrating the excellent thermal stability of textured ceramics.

## 4 Conclusions

A template-aided sintering method for preparing [001]c-textured BaTiO3 ceramics with comprehensive abnormal ferroelectric properties was proposed, and the liquid-phase-free mechanism was elucidated through phase field simulations and microstructure characterization. The thermal dynamics of the evolution of the grain size and morphology depend on the sintering temperature, and the holding time confirms that the sintering driving force is orientation fluctuations. The resulting highly textured BaTiO3 ceramics exhibit an abnormally high piezoelectric constant (*d*33 = 490±11 pC·N−1), which is much larger than that of random ceramics and superior to that of BaTiO3 single crystals.
