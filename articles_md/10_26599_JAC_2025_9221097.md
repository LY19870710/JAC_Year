## 1 Introduction

Piezoelectric ceramics now have diverse applications, including sensors, actuators, energy harvesting devices and piezoelectric motors [1−3]. Among them, lead zirconate titanate (PZT, PbZr1−*x*Ti*x*O3) ceramics stand out as the dominant piezoelectric ceramic materials due to their high piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33), high dielectric permittivity (*ε*), and good thermal stability [4,5]. The composition of PZT can be tailored to achieve the desired piezoelectric properties required for different applications. Within the phase diagram of PZT ceramics, there is a distinctive region known as the morphotropic phase boundary (MPB, *x* = 0.48), where the traditional view is the coexistence of tetragonal phase and rhombohedral phase [6−8]. However, recent studies have introduced a new model suggesting the coexistence of tetragonal and monoclinic phases [9,10]. Within the MPB, the variety of polarization directions enables the ceramics to respond more effectively to mechanical stress or electric fields, thus enhancing their piezoelectric properties [11−13]. Many researchers choose the MPB PZT as the base material and apply various approaches, including aliovalent and/or isovalent doping at Pb sites of the PZT perovskite, to further improve its properties for specific applications [14−17].

In PZT ceramics, the piezoelectric properties, such as the piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33) and electromechanical coupling coefficient (*k*p), can be improved through doping with high-valence cations [18,19]. This doping process produces “soft” PZT ceramics, which exhibit notably enhanced properties compared with those of pure PZT ceramics [1,20]. Lanthanum (La) is one of the most attractive “donor” dopants under investigation in the PZT system. Although the introduction of La3+ dopants can improve the electrical properties of PZT ceramics, the detailed microscopic mechanism underlying the enhancement of piezoelectricity in La-doped PZT (PLZT) ceramics is still under debate [21]. Kour *et al*. [22] prepared Pb1−*x*La*x*Zr0.52Ti0.48O3 ceramics with high piezoelectric coefficients (*d*33) (~300 pC/N), but the remnant polarization (*P*r) of the ceramics was consistently below 10 μm/cm2, and the dielectric losses were quite high. More detailed dielectric studies on PLZT ceramics have shown that the relative dielectric permittivity can be increased by La doping to approximately 1100; however, no discussion on *d*33 was provided [18,23,24]. Although some studies have been conducted on La-doped PZT ceramics, the impact of the La dopants on the electrical properties has not been thoroughly researched or comprehensively discussed, and the proposed mechanisms for improving the dielectric and piezoelectric constants remain inconsistent. Moreover, only a few of the above studies focused on the planar electromechanical coupling factor (*k*p), mechanical quality factor (*Q*m), and temperature stability of the piezoelectric properties of PLZT ceramics.

Here, we focus on PZT ceramics with compositions near the MPB region and introduce small amounts (0.25%–1.5%) of La3+ ions at A-sites of the perovskite through traditional solid-state synthesis. A detailed analysis of the crystal structure of the La-doped PZT ceramics was carried out. Compared with undoped PZT ceramics, a significant improvement in piezoelectric properties, particularly the piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33), is observed, and thorough discussions are conducted regarding the effects of La substitution on the phase composition, thermal stability, and electric properties.

### 2.1 Ceramics fabrication

The ceramic samples were synthesized using the solid-state reaction method. Initially, PbO (purity * ≥ 99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), La2O3 (purity  ≥ 99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), ZrO2 (purity  ≥ 99%, Sigma-Aldrich), and TiO2 (purity  ≥ 99.8%, Sigma-Aldrich) powders were dried at 200 °C for 24 h. Then, they were mixed according to the stoichiometric formula Pb1−3/2x*La*x*(Zr0.52Ti0.48)O3 (*x* = 0, 0.25%, 0.5%, 1.0%, and 1.25%; abbreviated as PZT, 0.25PLZT, 0.5PLZT, 1.0PLZT, and 1.25PLZT; here, “*x*” denotes the molar ratio of La per formula unit. The values are expressed as percentages (e.g., 0.25% and 0.5% correspond to molar percentages of La) and were ball-milled (180 r/min) with zirconia balls in ethanol for 5 h. After air-drying, the powders were calcined at 800 °C for 2 h. Then, the second ball milling was carried out on the calcined powder, with 5 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) binder added during the final 10 min of milling. The slurry was dried overnight to ensure complete removal of moisture. Then, the calcined powders were pressed under 200 MPa to obtain pellets with a diameter of 13 mm and thickness of approximately 1 mm. To remove the PVA binder, the pellets were heated at 750 °C for 2 h. Next, the pellets were sintered at 1150 °C for 2 h at a heating rate of 3 °C/min. To minimize lead loss during sintering, the pellets were covered with sacrificial PZT/PLZT (calcined) powder and sealed in an alumina crucible.

### 2.2 Characterizations

To perform phase analysis, the pellets were pulverized into fine powders for X-ray diffraction (XRD; PANalytical X’Pert Pro diffractometer, PANalytical, UK) with Cu Kα radiation and a 2*θ* range from 5° to 120°. The XRD patterns were fitted by the Rietveld refinement method via GSAS software [25]. Raman spectroscopy was performed using a LabRam microprobe system (ISA/Jobin-Yvon/Horiba, France) with a 633 nm Ar ion laser. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed with a monochromated Al Kα X-ray source and a photoelectron spectrometer (XPS system; Thermo Scientific Nexsa, USA). High-resolution XPS spectra were acquired with a pass energy of 20 eV and analyzed using Thermo Avantage software v5.9931 (1995 Microfocus Ltd., UK).

For electrical measurements, pellets were polished, painted on major surfaces with silver paint (Sun Chemical S.A Ltd., Saudi Arabia, C2050926P2) and then annealed at 700 °C to obtain conductive electrodes. The relative dielectric permittivity (* ϵr) and loss tangent ( tanδ) were measured across frequencies (100 Hz–10 MHz) using a precision impedance analyzer (Agilent 4294A, Agilent, Japan). The temperature-dependent dielectric permittivity and loss were measured at four different frequencies (1 kHz, 10 kHz, 100 kHz, and 1 MHz) via an LCR meter (Model 4284A, Agilent, Japan) with a personal computer (PC)-controlled furnace. Ferroelectric hysteresis loops were obtained with a triangular alternating current (AC) voltage at 1 Hz using a ferroelectric hysteresis measurement tester (National Physical Laboratory (NPL), made with a pair of NXB2-AL nanosensors, HP33120A function generator, and LabView software controlled, Teddington, UK) [26]. The samples were poled in a silicon oil bath at 130 °C under a direct current (DC) field (15–30 kV/cm) for 10 min. For the poled samples, the absolute values of the complex impedance (Z*) and phase angle (*θ*) were measured from 500 kHz to 1 MHz via an impedance analyzer. The resonance impedance peaks were used to determine the planar electromechanical coupling factor (*k*p) and the mechanical quality factor (*Q*m). The piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33) was measured with a quasi-static *d*33 meter (ZJ-3B, Institute of Acoustics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China). For temperature-dependent *d*33 measurements, a commercial furnace (PKD3-T300B, PolyK Technologies, USA) was attached to the *d*33 meter.

## 3 Results and discussion

PLZT ceramics at room temperature with La concentrations ranging from 0 to 1.25% adopt a perovskite (ABO3-type) structure (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). The doped La3+ ions completely enter the A-sites of the perovskite, and no traces of a secondary phase are observed by XRD. Rietveld analysis of the XRD data revealed that the doped PLZT ceramics were composed of a tetragonal (T) phase (space group (SG): *P*4*mm*) and a monoclinic (M) phase (SG: *C*1*m*1).

The decreased monoclinic distortion (Fig. 1(b)) implies that La doping also decreases * Ps in the monoclinic phase of the prepared PLZT ceramics. Thus, one can propose that the progressive substitution of Pb2+ ions with La3+ ions in PZT causes a systematic reduction in the lattice distortion in both the tetragonal and monoclinic phases of PLZT ceramics, thereby leading to a gradual suppression of  Ps and, consequently, to a reduction in the Curie temperature (T*C) of the ferroelectric phase transition [29].

Figure 1(c) presents the XRD patterns of the poled and unpoled PLZT ceramics in the selected 2*θ* range from 28° to 50°. Two prominent diffraction peaks are observed at approximately 44° and 46°, corresponding to the (002) and (200) planes of the tetragonal phase, respectively. Notably, the intensities of these peaks significantly differ between the poled and unpoled samples. The changes in the peak intensities manifest the poling field-induced reorientation of the ferroelectric 90° domains [30−32]. The diffraction peak at approximately 31° corresponds to the (110) plane of the tetragonal phase and exhibits distinct changes in intensity for the poled and unpoled samples [31]. The monoclinic phase is typically characterized by a distortion of the crystal lattice that results in a polarization along the direction that is oblique to the principal axes of the tetragonal or rhombohedral phases. This oblique direction can be detected through diffraction peaks such as the pseudo-cubic (110)c peak. Here, (110)c represents the (110) plane indexed using a pseudo-cubic perovskite structure, which is commonly used for comparison across different distorted phases, such as tetragonal or monoclinic phases. It can be used for evaluating the unit cell distortion caused by polarization [33]. Hence, the decrease in the (110)c peak intensity after poling suggests a field-induced phase transition from the tetragonal phase to the monoclinic phase, which is associated with polarization rotation [34].

Figures 2(a) and 2(b) display the relative dielectric permittivity and loss tangent as a function of frequency (100 Hz–100 kHz) for the unpoled and poled PZT, 0.25PLZT, 0.5PLZT, 1.0PLZT, and 1.25PLZT ceramics at room temperature. The dielectric permittivity slightly decreases with increasing frequency due to the different polarization mechanisms: (i) atomic polarization, (ii) ionic polarization, (iii) orientation polarization, and (iv) space charge polarization [35]. If the field frequency increases, certain polarizations with long relaxation time, such as orientation polarization and space charge polarization, are unable to follow the field changes, resulting in decreased dielectric permittivity [36,37]. After poling under a DC field, the permittivity of all the samples under consideration significantly increased, which can be attributed to the field-induced phase transition similar to that observed in layered perovskite-like Aurivillius oxides [38]. The electric field causes the transformation from a weekly polar phase to a strong polar phase, resulting in increased permittivity.

Additionally, the relative dielectric permittivity slightly increases upon doping (for La concentrations up to 0.5%), as shown in Fig. 2(c). La doping introduces charged defects such as  VPb⋅⋅ and  LaPb⋅. These charged defects induce lattice stress, leading to the growth of additional domain walls to alleviate the strain energy [39,40]. As the density of domain walls increases, the dielectric permittivity correspondingly increases. However, as discussed before, too much dopant will cause the distortion to gradually decrease (Fig. 1), which results in fewer 90° domain walls to reduce the strain energy. The formation of ferroelectric 90° domain walls is governed by the strain energy and depoling field [41]. The strain energy is directly related to ferroelectric distortion, which is reduced by La doping. Owing to the reduced strain energy, the density of 90° domain walls decreases through minimization of the overall free energy. Therefore, upon doping, the density of 90° domain walls decreases as a consequence of the reduced lattice distortion, and the dielectric permittivity tends to decrease. After poling, the dielectric permittivity of all compositions increases due to the field-induced transition and because of the additional contribution of the decreased density of the 180° domain walls [38]. The dielectric loss of the ceramics shows the same trend as the dielectric permittivity during doping and poling. Figure S2 in the ESM displays the frequency-dependent dielectric permittivity and loss tangent across a broad frequency range (from 100 Hz to 100 kHz). In the poled samples, a series of permittivity peaks are observed at high frequencies, indicating a piezoelectric behavior [37]. The first resonance peaks emerge along both the planar and thickness directions of the radial plate.

Figure 3(a) illustrates the variation in dielectric permittivity and dielectric loss with temperature for unpoled and poled PLZT ceramics from 200 to 430 °C at 1 kHz. For all the samples, the temperature-dependent permittivity exhibits one obvious peak at the Curie point (T*C), at which point the material transforms from a ferroelectric phase to a paraelectric phase. Figure S3 in the ESM shows the temperature dependence of the dielectric permittivity and loss of the PLZT ceramics in the temperature range from 25 to 460 °C at four different frequencies (1 kHz, 10 kHz, 100 kHz, and 1 MHz). Above *T*C, the permittivity of the PZT sample at 1 kHz increases with temperature (Fig. 3(a) and Figs. S3(a) and S3(b) in the ESM), which can be attributed to the thermally induced electrical conductivity mediated by oxygen vacancies [42−44] In the La-doped ceramics, owing to the reduced concentration of oxygen vacancies, the temperature dependence of the dielectric permittivity follows the classical Curie–Weiss law, with a gradual decrease in the permittivity upon heating in the high-temperature (paraelectric) region (Fig. S3 in the ESM). In conventional ferroelectrics, long-range ordered ferroelectric domains exist below the Curie temperature (*T*C). Upon heating through *T*C, the ferroelectric ordering at long distances is broken, and thermally active nanoclusters are only polar objects observed in the paraelectric phase (above *T*C) [36,45,46]. The measured dielectric permittivity (Fig. 3(a) and Fig. S3 in the ESM), therefore, is affected not only by ferroelectric domains but also by oxygen vacancies (* VO⋅⋅). In the ferroelectric phase,  VO⋅⋅ are typically distributed along the ferroelectric domain walls [41,47] and act as pinning centers for domain wall movement, thereby suppressing the dielectric response of the ceramics at temperatures below T*C. Above *T*C, however, the pinning effect disappears, which is reflected by the increased permittivity of the undoped PZT sample in the paraelectric region (Fig. 3(a)). Hence, one can infer that, in PZT, the temperature dependence of the permittivity above *T*C is dominated by * VO⋅⋅, and the contribution of dynamic polar nanoclusters to the permittivity is minor. As a consequence, the dielectric behavior of the undoped PZT ceramic above T*C does not follow the classical Curie–Weiss law. On the other hand, in the PLZT ceramics, the concentration of * VO⋅⋅ is dramatically decreased by doping, leading to the classical Curie–Weiss behavior in the paraelectric phase, where the dominant contribution to the permittivity is from polar nanoclusters. Typically, oxygen vacancies tend to increase the measured T*C [48]. Therefore, as shown in Fig. 3(b), the observed decrease in *T*C with increasing dopant concentration suggests that the gradual reduction in the number of oxygen vacancies weakens the effect. Additionally, as previously discussed, the spontaneous polarization (*P*s) for both the tetragonal and monoclinic phases decreases with increasing dopant concentration, resulting in a corresponding decrease in *T*C. After poling, *T*C decreases across all the compositions (Fig. 3(b)). When an electric field is applied, the domains align in the same direction, forming larger domains and reducing the density of domain walls. Domain walls are regions where charged defects such as oxygen vacancies can become trapped or accumulate. Domain walls are interfaces between two differently oriented polar domains, which have positive and negative charge ends. * VO⋅⋅ has a positive charge, and thus, it prefers to distribute along the negative side of domain walls [41,47,49]. Therefore, the density of domain walls determines the overall concentration of oxygen vacancies. The lower the domain wall density is, the smaller the accumulated charge concentration [47]. Therefore, a lower density of domain walls implies a lower concentration of accumulated charges, further shifting the T*C value to a lower temperature.

As shown in Fig. 3(a), the dielectric loss shows two broad peaks below the transition temperature (*T*C). The lower-temperature dielectric loss peak can be attributed to the dielectric relaxation of defect dipoles associated with the formation of oxygen vacancies and adjacent negative charges. These defect dipoles differ from ferroelectric dipoles in their relaxation behavior and are typically activated at lower temperatures because of their localized and thermally assisted relaxation processes [50,51]. Upon La doping, it progressively diminishes due to the reduction in the number of oxygen vacancies. The peak just below *T*C is attributed to domain wall movement [52−54] and can be observed in both unpoled and poled PLZT ceramics. For the poled samples, as shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM, several additional peaks emerge at approximately 80 °C because of the resonant behavior caused by the piezoelectric effect.

Figures 4(a)–4(e) display the polarization–electric field loops (*P–E* loops) and current–electric field loops (*I–E* loops) of the PLZT ceramics under AC electric fields of 4.5 and 5 kV/mm. The remnant polarization (*P*r) as a function of the La concentration is shown in Fig. 4(f). A comparison of the *P–E* loops recorded at 4.5 and 5 kV/mm reveals that the undoped PZT ceramic has not yet reached saturation at 5 kV/mm, while the PLZT ceramics exhibit well-saturated *P–E* loops. As shown in Fig. 4(f), upon doping, the remnant polarization sharply increases when 0.5% La is added and remains at this level (*P*r ≈ 33–37 μC/cm2) for subsequent PLZT compositions. This enhancement is linked to the reduction in the oxygen vacancy concentration due to La doping, which minimizes the pinning of domain walls and facilitates better polarization alignment.

Under a high electric field, classical displacive-type ferroelectrics typically show two strong current peaks on the *I–E* curve, reflecting field-induced domain switching. As shown in Fig. 4(a), there are four current peaks in the *I–E* loops of the undoped PZT ceramic, which can be attributed to the effect of oxygen vacancies [26,55]. The doped PLZT ceramics exhibit only two sharp current peaks, which are observed in the first and third quadrants of the *I–E* loops. This behavior is primarily due to the reduced oxygen vacancy concentration resulting from La doping, which suppresses extrinsic contributions and highlights the dominant effect of domain switching [37,56,57]. Although the ferroelectric *P*–*E* loops are saturated and well developed, the current peaks in the *I*–*E* loops (Figs. 4(b)–4(e)) clearly depend on the current maxima of the applied electric fields. A higher peak maximum emerges at a higher field. The electrical current response is influenced by the change rate of the electric field, especially when it is measured using a triangular waveform. It can be expressed as Eq. (2) [26]:

(2) I=ϵAdEdt

where * A is the electrode area;  ϵ is the dielectric permittivity; dE/*d*t* is the change rate of the electric field. In this study, the current measurement was carried out at the same frequency, which means that at higher fields, higher rates (d*E/*d*t*) are achieved. As a consequence, with increasing applied electric field, a more pronounced current peak is observed in the *I*–*E* loops.

To reveal the microscopic mechanism by which La doping reduced the dielectric loss (Fig. 3) and conductivity (Fig. 4), the defect chemistry approach introduced by Li *et al*. [58] was adopted here for the PLZT ceramics as Reactions (3)–(5):

(3) La2O3→Pb(Zr,Ti)O32LaPb⋅+3OO×+VPb′′

(4) La2O3+VO⋅⋅→Pb(Zr,Ti)O32LaPb⋅+3OO×

(5) La2O3+2h⋅→Pb(Zr,Ti)O32LaPb⋅+2OO×+12O2

Reaction (3) shows that Pb vacancies are created in the PLZT ceramics when La ions replace Pb ions. According to Reaction (4), La doping can reduce the number of oxygen vacancies (* VO⋅⋅), which is consistent with the decrease in dielectric loss (Fig. 3(a)). PZT ceramics are known to contain positively charged carriers (holes), which largely influence electrical conductivity [59−61]. Equation (5) clearly shows that introducing La ions into PZT can reduce the concentration of holes, thereby decreasing the electrical conductivity (Fig. 4).

The oxygen O 1s XPS spectra of the PLZT ceramics are presented in Fig. 5. For the PZT ceramics (Fig. 5(a)), the O 1s spectrum is formed by three distinct peaks: O[1], O[2], and O[3]. The O[1] peak is attributed to lattice oxygen and reflects the well-ordered oxygen environment within the crystalline lattice. The O[2] peak is associated with oxygen vacancies, which are relevant to structural defects and play a crucial role in the piezoelectric properties. The O[3] peak corresponds to oxygen adsorbed on the surface [62]. In contrast, the O 1s XPS spectra of the La-doped PZT ceramics (Figs. 5(b)–5(e)) indicate that the oxygen vacancy concentration is very low and that no distinct O[2] peak is observed, which is consistent with previously reported results for similar perovskite-based systems [63−65]. The two peaks identified in the PLZT XPS spectra correspond to lattice oxygen and surface-adsorbed oxygen. Therefore, the XPS data support the above discussion that La doping improves the dielectric and ferroelectric properties of PZT ceramics by reducing the number of oxygen vacancies.

The relationships among the piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33), electrostriction coefficient (*Q*m), dielectric permittivity (* ϵ33T), and remnant polarization ( Pr) can be expressed as Eq. (6) [66]:

(6) d33=2Qmϵ33TPr

While the values of dielectric permittivity of the doped samples are approximately 1500, the PZT ceramic has a lower permittivity of approximately 1000 (Fig. 2). Similarly, the remnant polarization P*r of the La-doped PZT samples is nearly four times greater than that of the pure PZT samples (Fig. 4). Hence, the calculated value of *d*33 of the PLZT ceramics must be much larger than that of PZT ceramics, as validated by piezoelectric measurements. According to Eq. (6), the 0.5PLZT ceramics show the highest piezoelectric coefficient among all the compositions under consideration because of the optimal combination of high dielectric permittivity and large remanent polarization. As mentioned above, doping with La3+ ions at A sites (in pure PZT occupied by Pb2+ ions) introduces lattice defects (* LaPb⋅,  VPb′′), which can facilitate the formation of domain walls and thus increase the domain wall density, thereby improving the dielectric and piezoelectric properties of La-doped PZT ceramics. Figure 6(b) compares the values of the piezoelectric coefficient (d*33) previously reported for the PZT-based ceramics [67−74] and the *d*33 value achieved in this work. The developed 0.5PLZT ceramics clearly show the highest *d*33 value, confirming the effectiveness of our doping strategy.

The *d*33 value of PZT gradually decreases upon heating due to the adverse effects of oxygen vacancies [43]. In poled electroceramics, ferroelectric domains are expected to revert back to their original state and ultimately disappear with increasing temperature up to their Curie temperature because of the enhanced domain wall motion [75]. Unlike those of the PZT ceramics, the *d*33–*T* curves for the doped samples follow a similar trend, increasing steadily with temperature and reaching a peak at approximately 300 °C, followed by a sharp decrease at approximately 400 °C (just above *T*C). According to Eq. (2) and the results presented in Fig. S3 in the ESM, the abrupt increase in *d*33 in the PLZT ceramics at temperatures close to 400 °C can be explained by the observed rapid growth in the dielectric permittivity near the ferroelectric phase transition. Above 400 °C, thermal depoling occurs above point *T*C, which results in the *d*33 value being nearly zero within this temperature range [76]. Importantly, the addition of a relatively large amount of La ions (≥ 0.5%) substantially improves the piezoelectric stability of the ceramics within a temperature range of 25–300 °C. For example, the *d*33 of the 0.5PLZT sample changes by only 8.53% when the surrounding temperature varies between room temperature and 300 °C (for higher La concentrations, the change in *d*33 is less than 25%). The improved thermal stability of the piezoelectric properties of the PLZT ceramics can be attributed to La-substitution-enhanced domain switching. Owing to the easier domain switching in the doped ceramics, *P*r decreases faster with temperature than with the remnant polarization of pure PZT. From Eq. (2), the increased * ϵ33T accompanied by the simultaneous decrease in P*r contributes to the high thermal stability of the piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33). The improved dielectric, ferroelectric and piezoelectric properties indicate the great potential of PLZT ceramics for high-temperature sensor applications.

The mechanical quality factor (Q*m) quantifies the efficiency of a resonant mechanical system in terms of energy dissipation [82−84]. The introduction of La ions at A sites improves the domain wall movement by reducing the number of oxygen vacancies, which act as pinning centers for domain walls. The reduced pinning effect enables the ferroelectric domain walls to move more freely under an applied field, which can significantly influence the mechanical behavior of the system. The higher mobility of domain walls can lead to, for instance, greater internal friction and energy loss during oscillation, ultimately reducing *Q*m.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, we investigated the effects of La doping on the crystal structure and electrical properties of PZT ceramics with compositions near the MPB (Zr/Ti = 52/48). At room temperature, the La-doped ceramics adopt a perovskite structure with the coexistence of tetragonal (*P*4*mm*) and monoclinic (*C*1*m*1) phases. As the La concentration increases, the Curie point (*T*C), lattice distortion, and concentration of oxygen vacancies decrease. After poling, the relative dielectric permittivity of the La-doped PZT (PLZT) ceramics increased, which was attributed to the electric field-induced domain switching and phase transition. Owing to the reduced number of oxygen vacancies, the remanent polarization of the doped PLZT ceramics is greater than that of the undoped PZT ceramics. The largely increased piezoelectric coefficient *d*33 (> 370 pC/N) in the doped PLZT ceramics suggests that La doping effectively enhances the conversion between mechanical energy and electrical energy. In addition, the introduction of La ions at the A-sites of the PZT perovskite is demonstrated to improve the thermal stability of the piezoelectric properties. These findings indicate that the partial substitution of Pb ions by La ions at A sites is an effective way to develop advanced PZT-derived ceramics with improved piezoelectric performance.
