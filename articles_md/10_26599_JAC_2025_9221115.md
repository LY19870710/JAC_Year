## 1 Introduction

Reversible solid oxide cell (rSOC) is an up-and-coming new energy conversion technology [1,2], offering two operational modes. In solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) mode, it enables efficient, low-emission power generation [3], while in solid oxide electrolysis cell (SOEC) mode, it can achieve efficient hydrogen production via water electrolysis [4,5] or enable carbon-neutral processes through CO2 electrolysis [6]. However, commercially available SOCs face several challenges, including a short lifespan at high operating temperatures (above 700 °C) [7], low mechanical strength, and poor thermal shock resistance [8]. In recent years, metal-supported reversible solid oxide cells (MS-rSOCs) [9,10] and reversible proton ceramic cells (rPCCs) [11,12] have been identified as promising candidates for overcoming these challenges and advancing the commercialization of rSOC technology owing to their unique advantages.

MS-rSOCs utilize metals such as nickel or stainless steel as the support structure [13,14]. Compared with conventional anode- or electrolyte-supported cells, MS-rSOCs offer greater mechanical strength [15] and improved thermal cycling stability in solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) mode [16,17]. Furthermore, the high thermal conductivity of the metal support facilitates rapid startup in SOFC mode [18−20]. However, MS-rSOC typically employs oxygen-ion conducting electrolytes [7,21], which require high operating temperatures. Additionally, under operating conditions, water vapor on the metal support side may lead to metal oxidation [22]. rPCCs, on the other hand, use proton-conducting electrolytes, which have lower activation energies for proton conduction [23,24]. When combined with mixed ionic-electronic conductor (MIEC) [25,26] electrodes, rPCCs can operate at intermediate to low temperatures (below 700 °C). This lower operating temperature not only reduces the operating costs but also enhances stability. Furthermore, in rPCC operation, water vapor is generated on the air electrode side, avoiding the detrimental effects of water vapor on the fuel electrode side that occur in oxygen-ion conducting cells (e.g., fuel dilution in FC mode [27] or the inability to produce dry hydrogen in EC mode [9,28]). Current research on rPCCs focused mainly on developing electrode materials, with many promising new electrode materials pushing the performance limits of rPCCs [29−31]. However, since rPCCs predominantly use electrodes or electrolytes as support structures [11], they suffer from lower mechanical strength and poor thermal cycling stability in FC mode [15,32], which hinders their widespread application.

Both MS-rSOCs and rPCCs have their distinct advantages and limitations, which are potentially complementary. This synergy led to the concept of metal-supported reversible proton ceramic cells (MS-rPCCs), which combine high mechanical strength with efficient low-temperature operation. MS-rPCCs enhance the thermal cycling stability of the FC mode and enable rapid startup [19,33]. Additionally, it avoids the presence of water vapor on the metal support side in the SOEC mode, thereby improving electrolysis stability and facilitating the direct production of dry hydrogen [34].

There are significant challenges in the research and development of MS-rPCCs, primarily focused on selecting the support material and the preparation process. Among the commonly used metal supports, stainless steel offers greater strength and a lower coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) than nickel-based metals, making it better suited for matching with other components in the rPCC material system, such as the electrolyte and fuel electrode [35]. However, proton-conducting ceramic electrolytes require long-term sintering in an oxidizing atmosphere at temperatures above 1350 °C to achieve densification, which can lead to the oxidation of stainless steel [36,37]. Additionally, during the sintering process, chromium oxide (Cr2O3) and iron oxide (Fe2O3) present in stainless steel may react with the proton-conducting electrolyte [38,39], damaging its structure and reducing its proton conductivity, which significantly impacts its overall performance. In contrast to stainless steel, nickel exhibits superior catalytic activity. Adding a certain amount of Fe can reduce the CTE, but this comes at the cost of sacrificing some catalytic capability, thereby reducing the cell’s performance. Nickel-based supports can be prepared using co-sintering oxides followed by reduction [40,41], which avoids the issue of densification under a protective atmosphere. However, the CTE of nickel-based metals is relatively high, leading to a significant mismatch with the other components of the proton ceramic fuel cell. As a result, during temperature fluctuations, the difference in deformation between the components weakens the interface bonding strength [42], leading to a reduction in the cell’s performance or failure.

There are few reports on metal-supported proton ceramic fuel cells, and even fewer studies have addressed the reversible operating mode of such cells. Zheng *et al*. [43] used PLD to fabricate a BaZr0.5Ce0.4Y0.1O3−*δ* electrolyte on a Fe–Cr alloy (ITM). This approach achieved an electrolysis current density (*I*) of −0.84 A·cm−2 at 600 °C and a 1.3 V thermoneutral voltage (*V*). Their research also revealed the impact of the diffusion of Cr and Mn in the ITM on the stability of the cell. Kim *et al*. [44] used PLD to fabricate dense BaZr0.44Ce0.36Y0.2O3−*δ* and La1-*x*Sr*x*ScO3 (LSSc, *x* = 0.1–0.25) thin films on a Ni0.9Fe0.1 alloy. However, the high cost of the PLD process presents significant challenges for the commercialization and large-scale production of rSOCs. To reduce the CTE, Vafaeenezhad *et al*. [45] added 10 wt% yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) to the Ni-based support. The peak power density (*P*) of the cell at 700 °C was only 0.33 W·cm−2. The introduction of YSZ reduced the three-phase reaction interface for H2 adsorption and dissociation, leading to a decline in performance [46]. Ciucci *et al*. [47] employed a Ni0.33Fe0.67 alloy as the support, which has CTE similar to that of the electrolyte. The peak output power of this cell at 700 °C was 0.59 W·cm−2, and the electrolysis current density at 1.3 V was −0.428 A·cm−2, both lower than those of conventional ceramic-supported cells [47,48]. In summary, the diffusion of interface elements and the mismatch in thermal expansion between the metal support and the cell are key issues limiting the performance and stability of current MS-rPCCs. Selecting suitable metal materials for support and addressing the thermal expansion mismatch are crucial breakthroughs for advancing MS-rPCC development. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct research on the cell preparation process and structural design to avoid element diffusion at the metal support and cell interface while mitigating the interface bond weakening caused by thermal expansion mismatch. This will contribute to the development of new high-performance and high-stability MS-rPCC technology.

This study proposes the use of pure nickel metal as a support material to fabricate MS-rPCCs, avoiding the detrimental effects of elements such as Fe and Cr on the proton-conducting electrolyte. This approach leverages the performance advantages of MS-rPCCs, enabling high-efficiency and high-strength rSOC to operate at low temperatures. Therefore, the issue that needs to be addressed is the thermal expansion mismatch between nickel metal and other cell components. This work proposes an interface engineering approach that realizes state-of-the-art MS-rPCCs: introducing a transition layer with a CTE between the nickel metal support and the fuel electrode. This approach helps alleviate the reduction in the interface bonding strength caused by thermal expansion mismatch during temperature changes, thereby enhancing the structural stability and interface strength of the cell. A relatively simple drop–coating co-sintering process is employed to achieve stable fabrication and operation of the MS-rPCCs.

### 2.1 Powder preparation

BaZr0.1Ce0.7Y0.2O3−*δ* (BZCY) was synthesized via a self-propagating combustion method. In accordance with a metal ion to citric acid (CA) molar ratio of 1 : 1.5, Ba(NO3)2 (Aladdin, China), Zr(NO3)25H2O (Aladdin, China), Ce(NO3)3·6H2O (Aladdin, China), and Y(NO3)3·6H2O (Aladdin, China) were sequentially dissolved in 300 mL of deionized water. Citric acid (CA) was then added, and the mixture was thoroughly stirred to ensure complete dissolution. An ammonia solution (NH3·H2O) was subsequently introduced to adjust the pH of the solution to 8–9. The solution was thoroughly stirred until the volume was reduced to 100 mL, then transferred to an evaporation dish and heated until complete combustion yielded white ash, thereby obtaining the BZCY precursor. The resulting precursor was subsequently calcined at 1100 °C for 3 h to produce the final BZCY powder. The synthesis methods for La0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3−*δ* (LSCF) are similar and are not described here.

### 2.2 Preparation of NiO support

The NiO support was prepared using a tape-casting process. The detailed preparation method has been previously reported in our study [49]. Anhydrous ethanol (Aladdin, China) and xylene (Sinopharm, China) were mixed in a specific volume ratio to serve as the solvent. Polyvinyl butyral (PVB98) was used as the binder, and fish oil was employed as the dispersant. NiO (Inco, Japan), the binder, dispersant, and other components were added to the solvent and thoroughly ball-milled to obtain a slurry suitable for tape casting. The slurry was then cast onto a polyester film and dried to obtain NiO tape. The tape was subsequently punched into circular discs with a diameter of 14 mm and a thickness of 0.9 mm using a die, resulting in NiO support.

### 2.3 Preparation of cells

The half-cell was prepared using a drop-coating and co-sintering method. The suspension preparation used for drop coating was as follows: NiO, BZCY, and PVB98 (binder) were dissolved in anhydrous ethanol, and grinding beads were added for ball milling. After thorough mixing, a uniform suspension was obtained. Suspensions for the transition layer, fuel electrode, and electrolyte were prepared according to specific mass ratios. These suspensions were sequentially drop-coated onto degreased NiO discs, starting with the transition layer, followed by the fuel electrode and the electrolyte. The coated discs were then sintered at 1350 °C for 6 h to form the half-cell. For the air electrode slurry, BZCY and LSCF were mixed at a 4 : 6 mass ratio and ground thoroughly with a binder to achieve a homogeneous mixture. This air electrode slurry was then applied onto the half-cell, which was subsequently sintered by microwave at 1100 °C for 10 min. The final structure of the single cell was NiO//80 wt% NiO–20 wt% BZCY//60 wt% NiO–40 wt% BZCY//BZCY//60 wt% LSCF–40 wt% BZCY. The final size of the single cell is a disk with a diameter of 11 mm and a thickness of 0.8 mm, with the effective reaction area of the air electrode being 0.196 cm2.

### 2.4 Thermal expansion coefficient measurement

Taking the 60 wt% NiO–40 wt% BZCY sample as an example, NiO and BZCY were first dispersed in anhydrous ethanol at a 6 : 4 mass ratio. To this solution, PVB98, accounting for 2 wt% of the total powder mass, was added as a binder. The resulting solution was thoroughly ground and heated under an infrared lamp until the anhydrous ethanol had completely evaporated, resulting in a mixed powder. The powder was then pressed into rectangular sample strips under a pressure of 10 MPa, with holding time of 1 min. The resulting strips were sintered at 1450 °C for 6 h to obtain dense 60 wt% NiO–40 wt% BZCY sample strips. Using the same procedure, 80 wt% NiO–20 wt% BZCY and NiO sample strips can also be fabricated. The CTE of the three sample strips were measured using a NETZSCH DIL-402C dilatometer.

### 2.5 Electrochemical performance testing

Prior to the electrochemical tests, a reducing gas was introduced to the support side of the cell, and after 2 h of reduction, NiO was reduced to metallic Ni. The electrochemical tests were then conducted. During the tests, dry H2 was supplied to the support side, with air supplied to the air electrode side in the SOFC mode and a 3% H2O–air mixture supplied in the SOEC mode. The *I*–*V*–*P* curves and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results (ranging from 106 to 10−1 Hz) of the single cell were measured at temperatures ranging from 550 to 650 °C. The stability tests for the cell were performed at 600 °C, with discharge current densities of 0.2 A·cm−2 for the SOFC mode and ±0.2 A·cm−2 for the reversible fuel cell/electrolysis cycling test every 2 h.

The thermal cycling test conditions were as follows: First, the cell was discharged at a constant current of 0.2 A·cm−2 for 20 h at 600 °C, referred to as T0. Then, the temperature was decreased to 300 °C at a rate of 5 °C·min−1, followed by a brief hold (approximately 20 min). The temperature was subsequently increased to 600 °C at the same rate, and another 20 h of constant current discharge was conducted, maintaining the same discharge current as that in T0, referred to as T1. This process simulated repeated temperature cycling during SOFC operation. T1 was repeated, and the changes in the open-circuit voltage (OCV) and the voltage during constant current discharge were recorded for each subsequent cycle, with each cycle labeled T2, T3, T4, and T5 (Fig. 7(a)).

### 2.6 Morphological observation and elemental analysis characterizations of cells

The cross-sectional samples of the cells were prepared as mounted samples using epoxy resin, followed by grinding and polishing for morphological observation and elemental analysis. A conductive platinum (Pt) layer was sputter-coated onto the mounted and cross-sectional cell samples to enhance electrical conductivity via a Model 682 precision etching coating system (Gatan, USA). Sputtering was performed at a current of 200 μA for 5 min. In this study, the sample morphology was observed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; GeminiSEM 300, Carl Zeiss, Germany) and an environmental scanning electron microscope (Quanta 200, FEI, USA). The elemental content and distribution on the sample surface were analyzed using an EDAX energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy probe.

### 3.1 Structural design

As shown in Fig. 1(a), we introduce a transition layer with a CTE between the support and the fuel electrode to alleviate the differences in volume changes between the cell components during temperature variations, reduce lateral stresses at the interface, and improve the overall stability. The most commonly used electrolyte material in current rPCC systems, BaZr0.1Ce0.7Y0.2O3−*δ* (BZCY), was selected for this work. Inspired by the mechanical composite approach in electrode design and drawing an analogy to traditional rPCC fuel designs [11,50,51], we chose 80 wt% NiO–20 wt% BZCY as the transition layer. Figure 1(c) illustrates the thermal expansion vs. temperature curves for several materials. According to the test results, within the 500–1000 °C range, the average CTE for 60 wt% NiO–40 wt% BZCY, 80 wt% NiO–20 wt% BZCY, and NiO are 12.1×10−6, 13.4×10−6, and 14.6×10−6 K−1, respectively, while the average CTE of BZCY is 10.2×10−6 K−1 [52]. Therefore, we incorporated 80 wt% NiO–20 wt% BZCY as the transition layer between the support and the fuel electrode and used La0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3−*δ* (LSCF)–BZCY as the air electrode material. During testing, a reducing gas was introduced on the support side for reduction, converting NiO to metallic Ni, thereby resulting in a pure Ni metal-supported proton ceramic cell (Ni|TL|MS-rPCC). To distinguish it, we refer to the single cell without the transition layer as Ni|MS-rPCC.

Figure 2(a) compares the stress analysis at the interface of the two designs, with and without the addition of a transition layer. Owing to the differences in CTE of the layers in the cell, varying degrees of volumetric expansion and contraction during temperature changes generate lateral stresses at the interface, thereby reducing the bond strength at the interface. The Ni|MS-rPCC single cell exhibited structural peeling, while Ni|TL|rPCC maintained its structural integrity. As shown in Figs. 2(e) and 2(f), after testing, the microstructure of the Ni|MS-rPCC single cell shows structural fractures, whereas the Ni|TL|MS-rPCC does not exhibit similar cracks. This further demonstrates the significant enhancement in the structural stability of the cell due to the introduction of the transition layer.

### 3.2 Cross-sectional analysis of the cell

SEM micrographs of the cell prepared with the introduction of the transition layer design are shown in Fig. 3 and Figs. S1–S3 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). After sintering, a dense electrolyte with a thickness of approximately 8 μm was formed, and the interfaces between the electrolyte, fuel electrode, transition layer, and support were tightly bonded. The NiO support exhibits a porous structure. Previous studies have reported that Ni from the fuel electrode may diffuse into the BZCY-based electrolyte during the co-sintering process [53−55]. Considering the use of pure Ni as the support, the Ni content on the fuel electrode side is much higher than that in traditional anode-supported cells. Therefore, conducting an elemental analysis of the co-sintered cell is essential to investigate whether significant Ni diffusion has occurred. The specific areas of the fuel electrode and transition layer were selected for average elemental content analysis. As shown in Fig. 3(b), the results indicate that the Ni contents in the fuel electrode and transition layer of the half-cell are approximately 49.97 and 61.08 wt%, respectively. These values are in close agreement with the theoretical values, as the designed NiO contents in the fuel electrode and transition layer are 60 and 80 wt%. The calculated Ni contents in the half-cell fuel electrode and transition layer are 47.23 and 62.9 wt%. The error is within 5%. Therefore, the Ni content in the fuel electrode and transition layer of the half-cell is within the error range. Additionally, X-ray diffraction (XRD) and EDX analyses of the electrolyte in the half-cell (Fig. S8 in the ESM) reveal that the co-sintered electrolyte exhibits a pure BZCY phase. Combined with the cell’s OCV performance, which reaches 1.07 V at 600 °C, this observation strongly suggests the absence of significant nickel migration. In contrast, reported cells exhibiting minor nickel diffusion typically achieve OCV of only 1.05 V under identical conditions [55]. The higher OCV observed in our study indicates superior electrolyte densification and reduced electronic leakage. This suggests that the cell maintains good structural integrity after high-temperature sintering, and no significant element diffusion has occurred.

Figures 3(c) and 3(e) and Figs. S2 and S3 in the ESM show cross-sectional SEM micrographs of the cell that underwent only reduction and the cell subjected to 200 h of stability testing, respectively. Both cells maintain structural integrity, with tight bonding between the components. The reduced cell exhibits a well-formed porous structure, while the support structure of the cell tested over a long period shows a slight increase in porosity. Considering previous studies that suggest the diffusion and migration of metallic elements from the support to the fuel electrode/electrolyte and other components after long-term testing [43,53], we used EDX to analyze the elemental content before and after 200 h of testing. The results are shown in Figs. 3(d) and 3(f). In the cell that underwent only reduction, the Ni contents in the fuel electrode and transition layer were 61.51 and 76.18 wt%, respectively. After 200 h of testing, the Ni content in the fuel electrode and transition layer decreased to 61.40 and 68.10 wt%, respectively. The observed deviation from the expected Ni content suggests that there may have been some changes in the elemental distribution. To eliminate potential errors in the EDX analysis caused by surface irregularities and to further confirm whether any changes in elemental composition occurred, the three types of samples mentioned above were prepared as polished cross-sections. ### 3.3 Electrochemical performance test

The electrochemical performance testing setups for Ni|TL|MS-rPCC cell and Ni|MS-rPCC cell are shown in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b). The electrochemical performance of the Ni|TL|MS-rPCC cell in FC mode is summarized in Table 1. The peak power densities of the Ni|TL|MS-rPCC cell in FC mode at 650, 600, and 550 °C were 0.8, 0.42, and 0.24 W·cm−2, respectively, and those of the Ni|MS-rPCC cells were 0.41, 0.27, and 0.13 W·cm−2, respectively. The cell with the added transition layer shows a performance improvement in the FC mode at 650 °C, reaching twice the original performance. Figure S7 in the ESM shows the OCV variation of the Ni|MS-rPCC cell during the cooling process in the test. The OCV behavior does not follow the Nernst equation prediction. When the temperature decreased from 600 to 550 °C, OCV decreased from 1.065 to 1.038 V, whereas theoretical calculations suggest that OCV should increase with decreasing temperature. This deviation may be due to the mismatch in thermal expansion during cooling, leading to a reduction in the interface strength and resulting in cracking. This is demonstrated in Figs. 2(e) and 2(f). The DRT analysis of the impedance spectrum of the Ni|TL|MS-rPCC cell is shown in Fig. 5(d). The results indicate that as the temperature decreases, the peaks in the 1–10 Hz range significantly increase, with the most notable increase occurring during the transition from 600 to 550 °C. This suggests that the reaction process corresponding to this part is the dominant rate-determining step. Moreover, on the basis of a previous study [56], when LSCF-BZCY is used as the air electrode, reactions in this range are more sensitive to oxygen partial pressure, implying a greater influence of the air electrode. Therefore, it is speculated that the lower performance at lower temperatures is due to the reduced activity of the electrode at these temperatures [56]. The cell underwent constant current discharge for 200 h without significant degradation (0.02 mV·h−1).

### 3.4 Thermal cycle stability and cold start test

One of the key advantages of metal-supported cells is their thermal cycling stability and rapid cold startup capability [18,19,33]. Ni|TL|MS-rPCC was subjected to SOFC thermal cycling stability tests and cold start tests, and the results are shown in Fig. 7.

As shown in Fig. 7(b), the cell exhibited a degradation rate of approximately 1% before and after each cycle within the temperature range of 300–600 °C. After approximately 120 h and 5 cycles, the total degradation rate was approximately 2.69%, with an average degradation rate per cycle of approximately 0.538%. This occurred because, during the continuous discharge process after each cycle, the performance improved. The exact cause of this phenomenon remains unclear and requires further investigation in subsequent studies. As shown in Fig. 7(d), after 5 thermal cycles, the peak power density of the cell decreased from 0.41 to 0.395 W·cm−2, with a degradation rate of approximately 3.66% and an average degradation rate per cycle of approximately 0.732%. As shown in Fig. 7(c) and Fig. S9 in the ESM, the cell maintained good structural integrity after the thermal cycling tests. Considering the negative impact of repeated temperature cycles on cell sealing and current collection, it can be concluded that the cell demonstrates good thermal cycling stability.

Before and after the cold start, the cell’s OCV decreased from 1.06 to 1.04 V, and the peak power density decreased from 0.4 to 0.38 W·cm−2 (Fig. 7(e)). Figure 7(f) and Fig. S10 in the ESM demonstrate that the cell maintained its structural integrity after the cold start test. Similarly, considering the adverse effects of rapid heating on cell sealing and current collection, the Ni|TL|MS-rPCC configuration has the potential to achieve rapid cold startup capabilities. However, further validation and testing of faster cold start performance are needed in subsequent studies due to limitations in testing furnace conditions.

## 4 Conclusions

This study designed an interface engineering method involving a pure Ni metal-supported proton ceramic cell by introducing 80 wt% NiO–20 wt% BZCY as a transition layer with a CTE intermediate between those of the fuel electrode and the Ni support. This transition layer alleviates the dimensional mismatch between different cell components during temperature changes, enhancing the interface bonding strength and enabling the efficient and stable operation of MS-rPCC. Comparative analysis reveals that the cell with the transition layer has enhanced structural stability and better performance than its transition-layer-free counterpart. The cell, which was fabricated with this structural design, demonstrated a peak power density of 0.8 W·cm−2 at 650 °C in the FC mode, with no significant degradation observed during the 200 h constant current discharge test. In EC mode, under conditions of 650 °C and 3% H2O, the electrolysis current density at 1.3 V thermoneutral voltage was 1.25 A·cm−2, with no noticeable degradation during a 100 h reversible fuel cell/electrolysis cycling test. In FC mode, the cell exhibited stable discharge performance with a low degradation rate after 5 thermal cycles. Additionally, the cold start test indicated that the cell designed with this structure has the potential for rapid cold start capabilities in FC mode operation. In conclusion, the interface engineering design with the introduction of a transition layer provides an effective approach for achieving efficient and stable operation of MS-rPCCs.
