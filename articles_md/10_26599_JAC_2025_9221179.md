## 1 Introduction

With the rapid development of 5G and 6G communication technologies, microwave dielectric ceramics (MWDCs), as core materials in high-frequency communication, present new development opportunities while simultaneously imposing more stringent performance requirements [1−4]. Specifically, a low permittivity (*ε*r) reduces the signal delay and enhances the transmission efficiency. Consequently, low-*ε*r materials have garnered significant attention in high-frequency communication systems. The high-quality factor (*Q×f*) and near-zero temperature coefficient of the resonant frequency (*τ*f) are considered critical parameters for achieving excellent frequency selectivity and enhancing device operational stability [5,6]. Moreover, low-temperature co-fired ceramic (LTCC) technology is essential for achieving higher integration density and more reliable packaging [7,8]. Among these, molybdates have garnered significant attention because of their nontoxic composition and favorable overall performance, making them a research hotspot. For example, in the Na2O–MoO3 system [9], compounds such as Na2MoO4, Na2Mo2O7, and Na6Mo11O36 can be sintered at relatively low temperatures (510–660 °C) and exhibit outstanding properties (*ε*r = 4.1–12.9, *Q×f* = 35,000–62,000 GHz, *τ*f = −61–(−76) ppm/°C). Similarly, in the La2O3–MoO3 system [10,11], La2Mo2O9 (*ε*r = 20.6, *Q×f* = 7,160 GHz, *τ*f = −38 ppm/°C, sintering temperature of 920 °C) and La2Mo3O12 (*ε*r = 10.1, *Q×f* = 60,000 GHz, *τ*f = −80 ppm/°C, sintering temperature of 930 °C) demonstrate excellent performance and can be sintered at relatively low temperatures.

In recent years, scheelite-structured AMoO4 (A = Ca, Sr, Ba, etc.) ceramics have garnered significant attention because of their superior dielectric properties. Research has demonstrated that the *ε*r, *Q×f*, and *τ*f of these ceramics are closely correlated with cation size and crystal structure [12]. Notably, scheelite-structured molybdates exhibit structural adaptability akin to that of perovskite-structured ABO3 compounds. In scheelite-type AMoO4 ceramics, both A and Mo sites can accommodate substitution with various ions while maintaining structural stability [13]. The substitutional flexibility offers broad potential for exploring the relationship between the crystal structure and dielectric properties. For example, Zhou *et al*. [14] reported a novel Bi3FeMo2O12 ceramic synthesized through partial substitution of Mo by Fe, which exhibited excellent dielectric properties (*ε*r = 27.2, *Q×f* = 14,500 GHz, *τ*f = −80 ppm/°C) when sintered at 845 °C. Xi *et al*. [13] pioneered the investigation of the dielectric properties of NaLaMo2O8 ceramics, in which the A site was occupied by Na and La (*ε*r = 11, *Q×f* = 25,050 GHz, *τ*f = −59 ppm/°C). A series of NaLnMo2O8 (Ln = Ce, Nd, Y, Er, Bi) ceramics with the same scheelite structure was subsequently reported to possess excellent dielectric properties and relatively low sintering temperatures [15−18]. Notably, NaBiMo2O8 ceramics exhibited a positive *τ*f value (+43 ppm/°C), and Li *et al*. [19] successfully adjusted the *τ*f to nearly zero (+0.28 ppm/°C) by compositing with NaLaMo2O8 ceramics while preserving the original structure. Additionally, Pang *et al*. [20] reported a series of (Li0.5Ln0.5)MoO4 (Ln = Nd, Er, Gd, Y, Yb, Sm, Ce) ceramics, all of which presented significantly positive *τ*f values (53–228 ppm/°C). Xi *et al*. [21] further studied [(Li0.5Ln0.5)1−*x*Ca*x*]MoO4 (Ln = Sm, Nd) ceramics, successfully reducing the sintering temperature to 925 °C by replacing Ca2+ with (Li0.5Ln0.5)2+ via the solid-state method, without the formation of secondary phases. By exploiting the contrasting *τ*f characteristics, the composition with *x* = 0.8 achieved a *τ*f value close to zero (+4.3 ppm/°C), along with *ε*r = 11.0 and *Q×f* = 18,695 GHz.

Notably, the scheelite-type AMoO4 ceramics maintain structural stability despite simultaneous substitution of the A-site by three different cations. Recent studies have shown that NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = La, Nd, Sm) ceramics exhibit excellent dielectric properties (*ε*r = 9.55–10.59, *Q×f* = 38,951–58,412 GHz, *τ*f = −44.92–(−48.43) ppm/°C) at sintering temperatures below 950 °C and are compatible with Ag, highlighting their suitability for LTCC technology [22,23]. However, the relationship between the structural characteristics and dielectric performance of scheelite-type Na2O–SrO–Ln2O3–MoO3 ceramics remains inadequately understood. Interestingly, the incorporation of five lanthanide elements (Ce3+ (1.143 Å), Pr3+ (1.126 Å), Eu3+ (1.066 Å), Y3+ (1.019 Å), and Yb3+ (0.985 Å), all with a coordination number of 8) does not disrupt the scheelite structure. Instead, these substitutions induce subtle structural variations, offering valuable insight into the influence of Ln–O bonding on the dielectric behavior of NaSrLnMo3O12 ceramics.

In this study, a series of novel NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics were synthesized, and their crystal structures, sintering characteristics, and dielectric properties were systematically investigated. A comprehensive analysis was performed via a range of advanced techniques, including first-principles calculations, complex chemical bond theory (Phillips–Van Vechten–Levine (P–V–L)), ultraviolet–visible absorption spectroscopy (UV–Vis), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), Raman spectroscopy, and far-infrared reflection spectroscopy (FTIR). These methods provide in-depth insights into the intrinsic relationship between the structural features and functional properties of materials. Furthermore, using NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramics as a representative material, microstrip patch antennas and dielectric resonator antennas (DRAs) were designed and fabricated. This work offers critical experimental evidence and technical insights, supporting their potential integration into practical microwave device applications.

## 2 Experimental

High-purity (> 99%, Macklin, China) Na2CO3, SrCO3, CeO2, Pr6O11, Eu2O3, Y2O3, Yb2O3, and MoO3 were used as starting materials to fabricate the NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) series of ceramics via the traditional solid-state reaction method. Owing to the hygroscopic nature of lanthanide oxides and Na2CO3, the raw materials were dried in a muffle furnace at elevated temperature for at least 2 h before weighing to ensure accuracy. The required powders were weighed stoichiometrically and placed in a ball mill jar with zirconia balls. Using anhydrous ethanol as the dispersion medium, the powders were ball-milled for 12 h to ensure homogeneous mixing and particle size reduction. The resulting slurry was dried in an oven and calcined at 600 °C for 2 h to initiate phase formation. The calcined powders were then subjected to a second ball milling and drying step, followed by blending with 9 wt% paraffin and sieving through a 60-mesh screen to obtain uniform granules. These granules were pressed into cylindrical green bodies (10 mm diameter × 5 mm height) via a dry pressing method. The green bodies were heated in a muffle furnace at a rate of 1 °C/min to 500 °C and held for 4 h to completely volatilize the paraffin binder. Final sintering was performed at temperatures ranging from 750 to 975 °C for 4 h to yield dense ceramic samples.

The phase compositions of the NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics were analyzed via a powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Rigaku D/Max-2400) over a 2*θ* range of 10°–80°, with a step size of 0.02° and a scanning speed of 2 (°)/min. Detailed crystal structure information was obtained through Rietveld refinement via FullProf software. A high-resolution transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEOL, JEM2010-HR) was employed to obtain high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns. The ceramic microstructures were examined by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; FEI, Quanta FEG 250), and the grain sizes were measured with Nano Measurer software. Apparent densities (*ρ*apparent) were determined at 25 °C via the Archimedes method with deionized water, and the reported values represent the arithmetic means of three independent measurements [24]. Raman and FTIR spectra were obtained with a Raman spectrometer (HORIBA, LabRAM HR Evolution) and an FTIR spectrometer (Bruker, IFS 66v/s), respectively. UV–Vis spectra were measured with a spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, Lambda750) using powder samples placed in an integrating sphere. Terahertz time-domain spectra (THz-TDS) were acquired via a THz-TDS system (Zomega Terahertz Cor., Z3). Low-frequency dielectric spectra and complex impedance spectra were measured via an impedance analyzer (Agilent 4294A), with gold electrodes formed by coating gold paste onto both sides of the ceramic discs and sintering at 600 °C. The oxygen ion migration pathways in the NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramics were analyzed via SoftBV software [25]. The microwave dielectric properties were evaluated with a vector network analyzer (AVNA; Agilent E5071). The *ε*r was determined via the Hakki–Coleman method; *Q* was measured via the closed-cavity method; *τ*f was determined from resonant frequencies measured at 85 and 25 °C (*f*85, *f*25) via Eq. (1).

First-principles calculations for NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics were based on density functional theory (DFT) and implemented in the Cambridge Serial Total Energy Package (CASTEP) code [26−28]. The meta-generalized gradient approximation (meta-GGA) with the RSCAN functional was adopted for the exchange–correlation interaction [29,30]. All the systems employed OTFG norm-conserving pseudopotentials, and the plane-wave basis set cutoff energy was fixed at 800 eV [31]. Brillouin zone integration was performed via the Monkhorst–Pack scheme with a 4 × 4 × 5 *k*-point grid, corresponding to a *k*-point spacing of approximately 0.05 Å−1 [32]. Given that the A-site lattice is occupied by a combination of Na+, Sr2+, and Ln3+ ions with a total occupancy of 1, the virtual crystal approximation (VCA) was used to model the mixed-ion configuration [33]. To ensure computational accuracy, the convergence criterion for the ground-state wave function was set at 2×10−6 eV. A density mixing algorithm was employed to accelerate electronic structure convergence.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(a) shows the diametric shrinkage ratio curves of the NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics as a function of temperature. As the sintering temperature increased, all the ceramics displayed nonlinear shrinkage behavior, which initially increased but then decreased. This trend is attributed to the migration of powder particles with high specific surface areas and elevated surface energies within the green body. The migration process reduces surface energy, promotes grain boundary motion, and facilitates pore elimination, ultimately leading to densification and the formation of mechanically robust ceramic bodies. At excessively high temperatures, however, abnormal grain growth and secondary crystallization can trap pores inside the matrix, thereby reducing the shrinkage rate [34]. Figure 1(b) reveals that the apparent density follows a similar trend to that of the shrinkage rate. In addition, an increase in the ionic radius of Ln corresponds to a decrease in the apparent density. This phenomenon can be attributed to a decrease in the relative atomic mass of Ln atoms. Importantly, all ceramics attain relative densities above 90% at optimal sintering temperatures below 950 °C, indicating excellent densification and promising compatibility with LTCC applications. As shown in Fig. 1(c), the densification temperature decreases with increasing Ln ionic radius. This behavior is governed by the balance between covalent and ionic bonding, which affects the melting point of the composite oxide. Specifically, as interatomic distances increase, both electrostatic attraction and electron sharing are weakened, resulting in lower melting and densification temperatures [20].

Figure 2(a) presents the XRD patterns of the NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics prepared at their respective optimal sintering temperatures. All ceramics exhibit the characteristic structure of the tetragonal scheelite-type I*41/*a* (88) space group, with diffraction peak positions that closely match those of the standard card for Na1.33Sr1.33La1.33Mo4O16 (JCPDS#96-154-5796). No additional diffraction peaks were observed, confirming that these ceramics are single-phase [22]. Moreover, as the ionic radius of Ln increases, the diffraction peaks gradually shift to lower angles, further supporting the successful formation of the intended phase. To assess the compatibility of the NaSrLnMo3O12 ceramics with the Ag electrodes, all the samples were mixed with 20 wt% silver powder and sintered for 4 h at their respective optimal temperatures. The XRD results are displayed in Fig. 2(b). Except for the NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramics, which react with Ag to form a secondary phase, the XRD patterns of the other four ceramics exhibit only the characteristic peaks of the matrix and Ag phases. These findings indicate that these compounds exhibit good compatibility with Ag and are appropriate for LTCC applications using Ag electrodes.

To further investigate the influence of different Ln on the crystal structure of the scheelite ceramics, Rietveld refinement was conducted on the XRD data of the five ceramics, as shown in Fig. 3(a). The excellent agreement between the experimental and fitted curves confirms the reliability of the refinement. Additionally, Table 1 summarizes the relevant parameters of the NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics obtained after refinement. The R*p and *R*wp values for all ceramics are less than 15%, further confirming the accuracy of the structural refinements [35]. Figure 3(b) shows the variation in the cell parameters of the NaSrLnMo3O12 ceramics as a function of the Ln ionic radius. It is evident that as the A-site ion radius increases, both *a* (= *b*), *c*, and *V* increase progressively, whereas the *a*/*c* ratio slightly decreases. This indicates that an increase in the A-site ion radius has a more pronounced effect on increasing the *c*-axis length. In conjunction with existing research, it can be concluded that an enlarged A-site ion radius effectively expands the unit cell volume of the scheelite structure ABO4 [36−38].

To further investigate the phase composition of the NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics, HRTEM images and SAED patterns were examined, as shown in Figs. 4(a)–4(e). In the SAED patterns of all the samples, bright rings corresponding to the (112) crystal plane are clearly visible, indicating that all the samples are polycrystalline. Moreover, the lattice fringe spacings observed in the HRTEM images closely match the theoretical values obtained from Rietveld refinement. These results further confirm the formation of a tetragonal scheelite structure with space group *I*41/*a* (88). Figure 4(f) shows the crystal structure features of the NaSrLnMo3O12 ceramics. The structure is consistent with that of traditional scheelite-type CaMoO4 ceramics, which exhibit body-centered tetragonal symmetry (space group *I*41/*a* (88)) and are approximately pseudocubic in nature. The detailed Wyckoff positions and atomic occupancy data for each atom are summarized in Table 2. In the structure, all the A2+ cations are equivalent, and the Mo6+ cations and oxygen atoms are similarly equivalent. Specifically, Na+, Sr2+, and Ln3+ cations jointly occupy the 4b Wyckoff position and are coordinated with eight oxygen atoms to form (Na/Sr/Ln)O8 polyhedra, whereas Mo6+ cations occupy the 4a Wyckoff position and are coordinated with four oxygen atoms to form MoO4 tetrahedra. The (Na/Sr/Ln)O8 polyhedra are interconnected by sharing edges, whereas the MoO4 tetrahedra are not directly connected but are instead linked to the (Na/Sr/Ln)O8 polyhedra via shared oxygen atoms, thereby forming a three-dimensional (3D) network structure.

The Williamson–Hall method was employed to investigate the influence of various Ln-series elements on the grain size distribution of NaSrLnMo3O12 ceramics [39]. On the basis of the XRD data collected at the optimal sintering temperature and via Eq. (2), the lattice strain (*ɛ*) and crystallite size (*D*) were calculated:

(2) βTcosθ=kλD+4ϵsinθ

where *β*T represents the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the XRD peak; *λ* denotes the incident X-ray wavelength (1.5406 Å); *k* is the shape factor (0.9). As shown in Fig. 5(b), the variation in *D* and *ɛ* as a function of the Ln ionic radius indicates that, except for the NaSrYMo3O12 ceramics, which are under compressive stress, the remaining four ceramics are subjected to tensile stress. Notably, tensile stress promotes ceramic grain growth, whereas compressive stress inhibits grain growth. Furthermore, the *D* value initially decreases and then increases as the Ln ion radius changes. A larger crystallite size facilitates the nucleation and growth of ceramic grains, leading to increased grain size. On the basis of the stress analysis, the NaSrYbMo3O12 and NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramics, owing to their larger crystallite sizes and tensile stress, are more conducive to achieving larger grain sizes. However, excessive tensile stress may result in abnormal grain growth, which may lead to a more uniform grain distribution in the NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramics. In contrast, despite their relatively large crystallite size, NaSrYMo3O12 ceramics undergo compressive stress during grain growth, thereby hindering the formation of larger grains.

Figure 6 presents the microstructures and grain size distributions of the NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics at their respective optimal sintering temperatures. All the samples exhibited highly dense microstructures with negligible porosity, indicating excellent densification of the ceramic materials. Furthermore, statistical analysis of the grain sizes revealed that as the Ln ion radius increased, the grain size initially decreased but then increased. Among these ceramics, NaSrYbMo3O12 and NaSrCeMo3O12 have relatively large grain sizes. However, the grain distribution in the NaSrYbMo3O12 ceramics is heterogeneous, characterized by the coexistence of large and small grains, whereas the NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramics exhibit a more uniform grain distribution and well-defined grain boundary characteristics. The grain distribution pattern correlates well with the results predicted by the Williamson–Hall method. Generally, ceramics with uniform and larger grains tend to exhibit superior material performance; consequently, NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramics exhibit the most favorable dielectric properties.

The crystal structure of the NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics was optimized via the XRD-refined structure as the initial model. The band structures and electronic density of states were calculated for the five ceramics, and the results are presented in Fig. 7. The band gap width of all the ceramics is approximately 3.5 eV, indicating their semiconducting nature. Additionally, the Fermi level (E*F) represents the highest energy level occupied by electrons when they fill the energy bands in ascending order at absolute zero [40]. In the NaSrYMo3O12 ceramics, the EF lies closer to the valence band, indicating p-type semiconducting behavior, whereas in the other four ceramics, the EF is closer to the conduction band, indicating n-type semiconducting behavior. Figure 7(f) illustrates the relationship between the band gap width and the Ln ion radius. As the Ln ion radius increases, the band gap width tends to increase. However, owing to the distinct conduction characteristics of the NaSrYMo3O12 ceramics, their band gap deviates from the trend. Notably, a greater band gap width implies a greater energy barrier for electron transitions, which contributes to a reduction in electrical conduction losses. Consequently, as the ion radius increases, the electrical conduction loss gradually decreases, making the NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramics the most favorable in terms of dielectric performance. The partial and total density of states (DOS) diagrams (Fig. 7) indicate that the valence band mainly originates from the p orbitals of O atoms, whereas the bottom of the conduction band is dominated by the d orbitals of Mo atoms. A distinct resonance peak is clearly visible within the Mo–O interaction range, indicating a strong covalent bond between these atoms [41].

Owing to the limited measurement frequency range, no features indicative of an electrode response were observed in this regime. Furthermore, the impedance spectra were fitted via the equivalent circuit model shown in the inset, and the fitting results strongly agreed with the experimental data, confirming the validity of the analysis. Above 500 °C, semicircular arcs associated with the electrode response become apparent. Specifically, in NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Pr, Eu) ceramics, these arcs increase markedly above 600 °C without subsequently decreasing, indicating substantial ionic conduction in the temperature range, which is closely associated with Na+ migration. In contrast, NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Y, Yb) ceramics exhibit more moderate behavior in their electrode response arcs above 600 °C, suggesting that electronic conduction mechanisms dominate these compositions. Notably, the electrical conductivity of all the ceramics shows a pronounced increase near 500 °C, accompanied by a corresponding change in *E*a. According to the literature, scheelite-structured ceramic materials may undergo phase transitions at elevated temperatures, resulting in substantial changes in electrical conductivity [46,47]. Additionally, Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Materials (ESM) presents the differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and thermogravimetric (TG) curves of the five ceramics, which clearly show an exothermic peak near 500 °C, accompanied by a noticeable weight change, further confirming the occurrence of phase transitions. The possible presence of Ce4+ ions in the NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramics may account for their relatively higher conductivity below 500 °C than the other compositions. Moreover, at temperatures below 500 °C, the *E*a values associated with conductivity are significantly lower than half of the *E*g values, indicating that the electrical conductivity is nonintrinsic [48]. This behavior likely involves extrinsic charge carriers such as electrons or ions, which contribute to increased conductivity loss and reduced dielectric performance. According to the crystal-chemical criteria reported in the literature, a framework structure with isolated metal–oxygen tetrahedra (MoO4) is conducive to ion transport [49]. However, the conventional solid-state synthesis process may cause Na volatilization, leading to the formation of oxygen vacancies to maintain charge neutrality. In the structure, Na+, Sr2+, and Ln3+ ions are randomly distributed at the A sites, and the disorder inhibits Na+ mobility, leading to low conductivity at low temperatures and thereby minimizing conductivity loss [22]. Consequently, at lower temperatures, electrical conductivity is likely governed by electron and ion transport associated with oxygen vacancies. Structural changes induced by phase transitions can alter the conduction behavior. The electrode response characteristics observed in the complex impedance spectra can serve as preliminary indicators for identifying the dominant conduction mechanism following the phase transition.

To further investigate the conduction mechanism of oxygen ions in the specific structures of the five ceramics, this study focused on the NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramic and investigated the migration pathway of O2− ions via the bond-valence method. The calculations were performed with a spatial resolution set to 0.1 Å [45,50]. As shown in Fig. 11, in the NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramics, when the isosurface energy is set to 0.124 eV, the potential migration pathways of O2− ions are clearly visualized. These results indicate that O2− migration is facilitated by the MoO4 tetrahedra, whereas the presence of Na/Sr/CeO8 polyhedra hinders O2− mobility. Notably, in the scheelite-type structure, although the MoO4 tetrahedra are not directly connected, their short intertetrahedral distances enable efficient oxygen ion migration. Therefore, at low temperatures, oxygen vacancies within the scheelite framework may play a critical role in enhancing both electronic and ionic transport, thereby increasing conductivity and resulting in the deviation of *E*g from 2*E*a.

To gain a deeper understanding of the relaxation mechanism in NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics, the relationships between the complex impedance (Z*′, *Z*″) and electric modulus (*M*′, *M*″) were systematically investigated across various temperatures and frequencies. As shown in Fig. 12(a), at 300 °C, the *Z*′ of the NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramic displays two distinct impedance platforms as the frequency increases. On the basis of the capacitance values, the low-frequency platform is attributed to the grain boundary response, whereas the high-frequency platform is associated primarily with the bulk response. As the temperature increases, the grain boundary resistance decreases, leading to the gradual disappearance of the grain boundary-related platform. Moreover, the magnitude of all the impedance platforms decreases with increasing temperature because of the reduced resistivity. At higher frequencies, these platforms tend to overlap as a result of space charge release [15]. Figure 12(b) shows that *Z*″ exhibits distinct Debye peaks across different temperatures, which are primarily associated with the grain response. In contrast, the peaks corresponding to the grain boundary response appear as minor protrusions and progressively vanish with increasing temperature, which is consistent with the *Z*′ analysis and indicates a marked decrease in the grain boundary resistance. These findings suggest that in NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramics, the impedance behavior is predominantly governed by grains, with relatively low grain boundary resistance. Furthermore, the frequency corresponding to the Debye peak maximum shifts toward higher frequencies as the temperature increases, indicating the thermally activated nature of dielectric relaxation. Figure 12(c) presents the frequency-dependent behavior of *M*′, which shows an increasing trend with frequency due to an insufficient restoring force under the induced electric field to suppress charge carrier migration. Figure 12(d) displays the frequency dependence of *M*″, revealing only relaxation peaks associated with the grain response, which suggests that the grain regions possess relatively low capacitances. The peak heights remain largely stable across temperatures, indicating that the grain capacitance is temperature independent. As with *Z*″, the *M*″ relaxation peak shifts toward higher frequencies with increasing temperature, further confirming thermally activated dielectric behavior. The relationship between *Z*″ and *M*″ for NaSrCeMo3O12 at 400 °C is shown in Fig. 12(e). Generally, the coincidence of the *Z*″ and *M*″ peak frequencies indicates long-range carrier transport, whereas significant offsets suggest hindered migration [44,51]. At low temperatures, the close alignment of peak frequencies in NaSrCeMo3O12 indicates relatively high conductivity. In contrast, Figs. S2–S4 in the ESM show substantial offsets between the *Z*″ and *M*″ peak frequencies for the other three ceramics, NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Pr, Eu, Y), indicating more restricted conduction and, consequently, reduced conductivity at low temperatures, as reflected in Fig. 10(f).

Raman spectroscopy provides insights into lattice vibrations, allowing correlations to be drawn between crystal structure and dielectric properties [52]. However, no studies have reported the Raman spectra of NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics to date. This work presents the first detailed Raman analysis of these five ceramics, providing a foundation for future research. Notably, except for those of NaSrEuMo3O12, the Raman spectra of the other four ceramics are largely consistent with those reported for NaSrLaMo3O12. The intrinsic luminescence of Eu3+ under 532 nm excitation may obscure specific Raman features in NaSrEuMo3O12, resulting in spectral discrepancies compared with those of the other samples [53]. All the ceramics crystallize in a scheelite-type structure with space group *I*41/*a* (No. 88) and point group *C*4h (4/*m*). The irreducible representations of the vibrational modes are summarized in Table S1 in the ESM. The corresponding Wyckoff positions for each vibration mode are summarized in Table S2 in the ESM. According to group theory, the Brillouin zone center contains 45 optical modes and 3 acoustic modes, although the experimentally observed Raman peaks are fewer than those of the predicted modes (3Ag+7Bg+71Eg+72Eg), which is likely due to peak overlap and limitations in spectral resolution, as shown in Figs. 13(a)–13(e). The Raman modes associated with (MoO4)2− units can be broadly classified into internal and external vibrations. The high-wavenumber region corresponds to symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations, whereas the mid-wavenumber region is associated with bending vibrations. In contrast, the low-wavenumber region (below ~250 cm−1) involves external modes related to translations and rotations of the (MoO4)2− group and motions of Na+, Sr2+, and Ln3+ ions. These modes are difficult to assign precisely because of complex coupling. Notably, lower FWHM values indicate reduced damping of bond vibrations and correspond to lower intrinsic dielectric loss [54]. As shown in Fig. 13(f), the FWHM values tend to decrease with increasing ionic radius, implying a concurrent reduction in intrinsic dielectric loss.

Figure 14 shows the low-frequency dielectric properties of the NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics measured at their respective optimal sintering temperatures. As shown, the permittivity of all ceramics decreases with increasing frequency and gradually stabilizes in the high-frequency region. The frequency-dependent behavior is closely related to the polarization mechanisms active within the material. In the low- and medium-frequency ranges, dominant contributions arise from space charge and dipolar polarization [55]. These mechanisms require longer relaxation times and the accumulation of mobile charges, which enhance both permittivity and dielectric loss. However, as the frequency increases further, these polarization mechanisms fail to keep pace with the rapidly oscillating electric field, leading to a reduction in their contribution and resulting in a stabilized permittivity. Specifically, space charge polarization is dominant in the low-frequency region and is responsible for the sharp increase in permittivity, whereas dipolar polarization becomes more prominent in the medium-frequency range. Owing to its shorter relaxation time, dipolar polarization contributes less significantly to increases in permittivity or dielectric loss. Consequently, both permittivity and dielectric loss tend to rapidly decrease at low frequencies, followed by a gradual reduction with further increases in frequency. Notably, NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Pr, Eu, Yb) ceramics display distinct dielectric loss peaks in specific frequency regions. These peaks are attributed to polaron relaxation, a process involving the hopping of localized electrons or holes between ion sites [56].

However, the packing fraction decreases as the Ln ionic radius increases, suggesting that it is not the main contributor to the *Q×f* trend observed in these ceramics. Lattice distortion and phonon scattering, both of which adversely affect *Q×f*, are influenced by disorder in the ionic radius and electronegativity. These disordered parameters were calculated via Eqs. (S25) and (S26) in the ESM [61], and the results are shown in Fig. 18(c). As the Ln ionic radius increases, both types of disorder decrease, leading to a reduction in dielectric loss and a corresponding increase in *Q×f*. Thus, the ion radius and electronegativity disorder are also important internal factors influencing *Q×f*. Bond valence theory offers a useful approach for evaluating local structural stability by calculating the total valence of chemical bonds surrounding each atom. A higher bond valence indicates reduced anharmonicity in lattice vibrations, which positively impacts *Q×f*. Figure 18(d) presents the calculated bond valence values for Na, Sr, Ln, and Mo. As the Ln3+ radius increases, the bond valence values of Na and Sr decrease, whereas those of Ln and Mo increase. Therefore, the increase in *Q×f* is attributed primarily to the increasing bond valence at the Ln and Mo sites, whereas the Na and Sr positions contribute less favorably to high *Q×f* values.

Figure 19(a) shows the variation in *τ*f for the NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics as a function of the sintering temperature. As observed, *τ*f irregularly fluctuates with increasing temperature but remains within a relatively stable range without significant deviation. To further analyze the influence of the Ln ionic radius, the *τ*f values at the optimal sintering temperatures were plotted against the Ln3+ radius, as shown in Fig. 19(b). A clear downward trend is observed, indicating a gradual deterioration in the thermal stability of the materials with increasing Ln3+ radius. As shown in Fig. 16(c), the decrease in *τ*f is governed primarily by the characteristics of the Na/Sr/Ln–O bonds. Although the *E* of the Na/Sr/Ln–O bond increases with increasing Ln3+ ions, the corresponding increase in *α*L dominates, leading to a decrease in thermal stability. Since the lattice vibration frequencies of ceramics fall within the far-infrared region, their intrinsic dielectric response mechanisms can be effectively analyzed via FTIR spectroscopy [62]. Figure 20(a) displays the FTIR spectra of the NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics sintered at the optimal temperatures. Owing to their shared scheelite-type structure, all ceramics exhibit similar peak profiles. The data were fitted via Reffit software, and the *ε*r and loss tangent (tan*δ*) were calculated via Eqs. (S27)–(S29) in the ESM. The fitting results are summarized in Tables S3–S7 in the ESM. The number of infrared-active modes identified by fitting is slightly lower than the number predicted by group theoretical analysis (6*A*u+61*E*u+62*E*u), which is likely due to mode overlap and degeneracy. As shown in Fig. 20(a), the fitted curves align well with the experimental spectra, confirming the reliability of the fitting results. Figures 20(b) and 20(c) show the real (*ε*′) and imaginary (*ε*″) parts of the complex permittivity. The *ε*r values obtained via TE011 mode measurements are consistent with the *ε*′ values derived from the fitted data. Similarly, the calculated dielectric loss values are of the same order of magnitude as those measured in the TE01*δ* mode, indicating that the polarization response in the microwave frequency range corresponds closely to infrared lattice vibrations. A further analysis of the contributions of various infrared modes is presented in Fig. 20(d). The optical-frequency permittivity (*ε*∞) contributes less than 35% to *ε*′, indicating that ion displacement polarization is the dominant mechanism. Notably, vibrational modes below 400 cm−1 contribute approximately 60% to *ε*′ and over 95% to dielectric loss, highlighting that low- and medium-wavenumber lattice vibrations are the primary contributors to both dielectric polarization and loss. These findings suggest that phonon absorption is the main origin of the dielectric behavior in the microwave region.

With the rapid advancement of mobile communication technology, the demand for high-quality signal transmission continues to rise. As a crucial electronic component, patch antennas have gained widespread adoption because of their excellent performance. Within the limited space of mobile phones, patch antennas can support multiple frequency bands, satisfying the requirements of diverse communication standards such as global system for mobile communications (GSM), code division multiple access (CDMA), long term evolution (LTE), and 5G. For satellite communication terminal devices, microstrip patch antennas are an ideal solution because of their low profile and ease of installation. Additionally, in Internet of Things (IoT) sensor nodes, microstrip patch antennas provide reliable wireless data transmission [59,64]. On the basis of these considerations, in this study, a high-performance NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramic operating at 2.45 GHz was selected to design and fabricate a patch antenna suitable for the S-band. The frequency band is license-free and widely used in industrial, scientific, and medical (ISM) applications, including Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, wireless home appliances, microwave ovens, and IoT devices, highlighting its broad application potential. Figure 23(a) shows the structure of the designed and fabricated antenna. To evaluate the antenna’s performance, its return loss (S11) was measured. The S11 value indicates the proportion of the signal reflected at the transmission line or port. A value below −10 dB signifies that the antenna meets practical requirements [24]. As shown in Fig. 23(b), the measured center frequency is 2.48 GHz, with a bandwidth of 38.19 MHz, which closely aligns with the simulation results. Minor discrepancies are due mainly to dimensional deviations during fabrication. Figure 23(c) presents the voltage standing wave ratio (VSWR) response as a function of frequency. The VSWR exhibits a nonmonotonic trend, initially decreasing and then increasing, with a minimum value of 1.02 at the optimized center frequency of 2.45 GHz. A VSWR value near 1 corresponds to minimal S11 and excellent impedance matching, indicating efficient signal transmission with minimal reflection losses. Figure 23(d) displays the three-dimensional gain pattern of the patch antenna. The maximum gain is oriented perpendicular to the radiation patch, forming a symmetrical, unidirectional radiation pattern with a peak gain of 5.57 dBi. Figures 23(e) and 23(f) show the electric and magnetic field distributions on the patch surface. The electric field intensity varies along the length direction, peaking at the edges and remaining nearly constant along the width direction. The field is weakest at the feed point and is directed toward it. Similarly, the magnetic field intensity decreases with distance from the feed point along the length direction, with the field direction pointing outward at the feed point. These distributions align well with the antenna’s structural design. Figures 23(g) and 23(h) show the plane gain patterns of the electric and magnetic fields, confirming a symmetrical, unidirectional maximum gain orientation and confirming the antenna’s excellent radiation performance.

In contrast, DRAs have emerged as promising solutions for millimeter-wave applications, offering broad impedance bandwidths, minimal conductive losses, and excellent suitability for high-frequency operation [65,66]. Additionally, the DRA maintains low cross-polarization levels, indicating a high signal-to-noise ratio and strong anti-interference capability, further supporting its potential for X-band communication applications.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, a series of novel NaSrLnMo3O12 (Ln = Ce, Pr, Eu, Y, Yb) ceramics with a tetragonal scheelite structure was successfully synthesized via the conventional solid-state method. The sintering behavior, crystal structure, electrical properties, chemical bonding, and dielectric performance of these materials were systematically investigated. XRD and Rietveld refinement confirmed the formation of a tetragonal scheelite structure within the sintering temperature range of 775–925 °C. With increasing Ln ionic radius, the XRD peaks shifted to lower angles, which was attributed to lattice expansion at the Ln site. Density measurements and SEM analysis revealed that the optimal sintering temperature decreased as the Ln ionic radius increased. Raman spectroscopy provided insights into the lattice vibration, showing that the FWHM values decreased with increasing Ln ionic radii, indicating reduced intrinsic dielectric loss. First-principles calculations revealed enhanced covalency in the Mo–O bonds. Complex impedance spectroscopy was used to study electrical behavior, including relaxation mechanisms and potential O2− migration paths. The NaSrLnMo3O12 ceramics exhibited excellent dielectric performance at relatively low sintering temperatures, with *ε*r = 9.8–10.34, *Q×f* = 30,487–69,445 GHz, and *τ*f = −20.55−44.24 ppm/°C. On the basis of P–V–L theory, the increase in *ε*r was attributed primarily to enhanced polarizability and Na/Sr/Ln–O bond ionicity. The improvement in *Q×f* resulted from the increased Mo–O lattice energy, increased bond valence at the Mo and Ln positions, and reduced ionic radius and electronegativity disorder. The decrease in *τ*f was related to the increasing *α*L values of the Na/Sr/Ln–O bonds. FTIR spectroscopy indicated that lattice vibrations below 400 cm−1 predominantly contributed to dielectric polarization and loss. The dielectric behavior in the terahertz band closely matched that in the microwave band, demonstrating the suitability of NaSrLnMo3O12 ceramics for high-frequency applications. To validate their practical use, S-band microstrip patch antennas and X-band DRAs were designed and fabricated using NaSrCeMo3O12 ceramics, confirming their strong application potential. In conclusion, NaSrLnMo3O12 ceramics with scheelite-type structures are promising candidates for next-generation microwave and high-frequency communication technologies.
