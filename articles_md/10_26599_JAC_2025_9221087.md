## 1 Introduction

Triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) are a family of devices for low-frequency energy harvesting [1−4] that can effectively collect mechanical energy from the environment and convert it into electrical energy [5−8]. TENGs exhibit excellent performance in the areas of energy harvesting and self-power sensing and are considered next-generation devices for solving energy problems [9−11]. The structure of a typical TENG is illustrated in Fig. 1. As shown in the figure, a TENG is constructed with two electrodes, which cyclically contact, separate or slide with each other. Each electrode consists of a triboelectric layer and a conductive layer. The triboelectric layer has different abilities to gain/lose electrons. During the cycle of contact–separation of the two electrodes, the triboelectric layers gain/lose electrons from the other triboelectric layer by contact charging. Moreover, the conductive layer collects/provides electrons from the triboelectric layers by electrostatic induction to generate an electric current and outputs energy or sensing signals [12−15].

In some articles on TENGs, the conductive layer itself was considered an electrode [16−18]. However, in most articles, the electrode includes a conductive layer and a triboelectric layer [19−21]. An electrode in TENGs needs to have the ability to generate electric signals, in addition to just conducting the electrons. Thus, it is more reasonable that an electrode includes a conductive layer and a triboelectric layer. In this review, an electrode consisting of a conductive layer and a triboelectric layer is adopted.

The performance and applications of TENGs are controlled by the properties of conductive layers (conductivity, flexibility, etc.) and those of triboelectric layers (dielectric properties, surface charge density, effective frictional contact area, etc. [22]). Generally, metal foils are selected as conductive layers because of their high electrical conductivity, and polymer films are selected as triboelectric layers because of their high surface charge density and excellent dielectric properties. Compared with the conductive layer, the triboelectric layer is more important for realizing high-performance TENGs because it directly affects the charge generation and transfer efficiency. Therefore, the selection of suitable triboelectric materials is important to improve the performance of TENGs.

According to the working principle of TENGs, two polymer film materials can usually be selected to construct the electron exchange system of a TENG. One film is a positive triboelectric layer that loses electrons, and the other is a negative triboelectric layer that gains electrons. Therefore, to improve the output performance of TENGs, nylon 66 [23−25] and ethyl cellulose [26−28] are selected as positive layers, while polytetrafluorethylene (PTFE) [5,29], polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) [30,31], and polyvinyl chloride (PVC) [32,33] are used as negative layers.

However, the performance of pure polymer films cannot meet the requirements for high-performance TENGs. Various functional fillers have been introduced into the polymer matrix to make composite films with excellent performance. In addition to ceramic particles [34−37], two-dimensional (2D) materials, such as MoS2 [38−40], graphene [41,42], and hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) [43,44], are widely used as the fillers. 2D materials have large specific surfaces and abundant surface functional groups, which promote the formation of effective interfaces between the polymer matrix and fillers and, in turn, extensively enhance the performance of the film for TENGs [45].

MXenes are a family of novel 2D materials [46,47] that are obtained by selectively removing “A-element” layers from MAX phases by using an etchant (such as HF [48], NH4HF2 [49,50], NaOH [51], fluorine salts [52], and hydrobromic acid (HBr) [53]). MAX phases are a family of ternary layered carbides/nitrides with the chemical formula of Mn*+1AX*n* (*n* = 1, 2, and 3), where M is an early transition metal, A is a group A element, and X is either carbon and/or nitrogen [54−60]. After etching, the multi-layered MXene has an accordion-like morphology. Subsequently, fewer layers of MXene can be obtained by ultrasonic stripping via intercalation with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Ti3C2 MXene was the first MXene and has been extensively researched [48]. Generally, MXenes have a high specific surface area, are highly electrically conductive, and are highly hydrophilic. The surface of MXenes is always negatively charged, and MXenes have high electronegativity.

Recently, there are many reports on the application of MXenes to construct TENGs [61−64]. Interestingly, MXenes are not just used as fillers of composite films for triboelectric layers [65]. The pure films or composite films of MXenes are extensively applied in all parts of TENGs, including conductive layer [66−69], triboelectric layer [70,71], and even a charge trapping layer between the previous two layers [72−82]. Owing to their unique structure and properties, the introduction of MXenes in all the layers can remarkably enhance the performance of TENGs.

There are many studies (including research and review articles) on TENGs based on MXenes; however, most studies focus on the construction, performance and practical applications of TENGs based on MXene composite films [83−87]. In addition, studies based on polymers [88], waste [89], MOF [90,91], oxides [92], and graphene materials [93] have been widely reported. However, the applications of MXenes in different layers of TENGs have not been classified in a clear way. The roles of MXenes in pure/composite films have not been clarified. The reasons and advantages of MXenes in three layers (conductive layers, triboelectric layers and charge-trapping layers) have not been well summarized and thoroughly discussed. Therefore, a notable gap in the existing literature remains: a comprehensive review and classification of MXene applications from a materials science perspective, along with a detailed elucidation of the rationale and advantages of utilizing MXenes in TENGs.

The first work to construct TENGs by MXene was reported in 2017 by Dong *et al*. [70]. Thereafter, the applications of MXenes in TENGs have been extensively investigated. As summarized in Fig. 1, the application can be classified into 4 types. The percentages of the corresponding published articles of each type are given in brackets after i, ii, iii and iv in Fig. 1. Type i: MXene films were used as conductive layers because of their excellent conductivity. In some cases, second phases were added to improve the properties of the pure MXene films, especially mechanical properties. Compared with traditional metal foils, the MXene films are flexible. TENGs with excellent flexibility can be made with MXene films as the conductive layer. Type ii: MXene films were used as the triboelectric layer because of their high electronegativity. Unlike general polymer films used as triboelectric layers, MXene films are conductive and can also be used as conductive films. Thus, in this review, if an MXene film has contact–separation cycles with other films in the TENG, the film is considered a triboelectric layer. Otherwise, the film is considered a conductive layer, which only collects and conducts electrons from the triboelectric layer. Type iii: MXene nanosheets were used as fillers of polymer composite films as triboelectric layers. The MXene nanosheets clearly enhanced the triboelectric performance of the polymer films. Type iv: In some TENGs, a new layer was introduced between the triboelectric layer and conductive layer to trap the charges exchanged between the two layers. MXene films were used as the charge-trap layer.

In the next parts of this review, the four applications are reviewed and discussed. The reasons why MXenes are applied in different layers of TENGs and the advantages of MXenes in various applications are summarized and analyzed. Three points need to be clarified before the following review: (1) the type of MXenes applied in TENGs, (2) the working mode of TENGs, and (3) the applications of TENGs.

(1) In terms of the type of MXenes, almost all MXenes applied in TENGs are Ti3C2 MXenes, despite various types of MXenes (Ti3C2, Nb2C, V2C, Mo2C, etc.). Thus, this article focuses on Ti3C2 MXenes. In the following sections of this article, if not specified, MXene refers to Ti3C2 MXene.

(2) For the working mode of TENGs, in general, there are four working modes: vertical contact–separation mode, lateral sliding mode, single-electrode mode, and free-standing mode [94]. The TENGs based on MXenes generally work in 2 modes: vertical contact–separate mode and single electrode mode. In vertical contact–separate mode, the electrode vertically contacts and separates from another electrode. In single electrode mode, the electrode contacts and separates from the skin of the tester. In 2024, our group reported a novel mode: deformation mode. In deformation mode, two triboelectric layers are glued together to obtain stable signals during deformation [95].

(3) For TENGs, there are two main applications for TENGs based on MXenes. One is energy harvesting; the other is self-powered sensing. The TENGs for energy harvesting work mainly in vertical contact–separation mode. This kind of TENG requires excellent triboelectric performance; however, the flexibility is not required. The TENGs for self-powered sensors work mainly in single-electrode mode. This kind of TENG requires excellent flexibility and stability, while the triboelectric performance is not a prior requirement.

## 2 MXene films applied in conductive layer

Conductive layers are used to collect the charges generated from triboelectric layers and output as signals/energy. Thus, there are two prior requirements for the conductive layer. One is the ability to trap charges from the triboelectric layer. The other is high conductivity to output signals/energy. Conductivity, being readily measurable and comparable, is a primary consideration for the selection of a conductive layer. Owing to their high conductivity, metal foils (Cu, Al, etc.) are often selected as conductive layers. However, the metal foils have poor flexibility and no stretchability. Some TENGs, especially TENGs applied as flexible sensors, require excellent flexibility and/or stretchability. Thus, new films with high conductivity and flexibility are being developed to replace metal foils. MXene films can be prepared from the water dispersion of MXenes via various methods. The MXene films are highly conductive (15,100 S·cm−1) and flexible [96]. Moreover, MXene nanosheets have excellent electron-attracting capabilities [97,98]. Therefore, MXene films have been researched as the conductive layer of TENGs.

As illustrated in Fig. 2(a), He *et al*. [66] developed a flexible and self-healing sandwich structural film composed of two layers of nitrile butadiene rubber (NBR) with one layer of MXene in the middle. The NBR layers were triboelectric layers, which generated electrical signals during the contact–separate cycles with another triboelectric layer. The MXene film collected and output the electrical signals. Both the NBR film and MXene film were flexible. After being cut by a surgical blade, the NBR/MXene/NBR composite films rapidly self-healed within 5 s. A TENG based on the NBR/MXene/NBR thin film can be used as a sensor to detect basketball players’ dribbling posture (Fig. 2(b)).

In 2020, Cao et al*. [67] proposed a TENG electrode by injecting a mixture of MXene/cellulose nanofibers (CNFs) into a silicon rubber capsule. The silicon rubber capsule works as a triboelectric layer. As shown in Fig. 2(c), the MXene/CNFs packed with silicon rubber constitute the conductive layer of the electrode, which is highly flexible, stretchable and shape-adaptive. In a mixture of MXenes/CNFs, CNFs act as dispersants and interlocking agents to promote hydrogen bonding between 2D MXene nanosheets, which facilitates accelerated charge transfer. MXene nanosheets provide a charge storage layer to increase the electron-trapping capability for increased electrical output. The TENG works in single-electrode mode and can produce an open-circuit voltage of 300 V, a short-circuit current of 5.5 µA, short-circuit transferred charge of 120 nC, and maximum power density of 504.9 mW·m−2. Owing to the stretchability and shape adaptability of the electrodes, the TENG can be attached to human skin as a sensor for real-time monitoring of various human motions (Fig. 2(d)).

To endow TENGs with better tensile properties and long-established stability, our group reported a novel TENG with Ti3C2 MXene supported by cotton fabric as a conductive layer and silicone rubber as a triboelectric layer in 2023 [68]. The conductive layer is obtained by immersing the cotton fabric in a mixed suspension of Ti3C2 MXene and CNF. The optical images of the cotton fabric before and after being dipped in the MXene/CNF mixture are shown in Fig. 2(e). In the conductive layer, CNFs promote the interconnection of MXene nanosheets and form a conductive network with MXenes for electron transfer, and the fabric substrate provides strength and flexibility to the conductive layer. The TENG could output an open-circuit voltage of 400 V, a short-circuit current of 680 nA, and a maximum power density of 40 mW·m−2 in single-electrode mode during the press–release cycles of a human palm. The energy harvested by the TENG can light up light-emitting diodes (LEDs) and charge capacitors. The TENG exhibited excellent performance as a self-powered flexible sensor to measure the mass of touched objects with good repeatability and linear relationship (Fig. 2(f)). Owing to the cotton fabric ending not being fixed during the encapsulation process, the TENG can be given excellent stretchability and stability. Owing to its excellent flexibility and sensing properties, the TENG was used as a wearable sensor to detect the physiological activities of the human body.

In 2024, He *et al*. [99] developed a flexible chitin nanocrystal (ChNC)/MXene composite film by vacuum-assisted filtration (VAF). As shown in Fig. 2(g), chitin nanocrystals were used to promote interconnections between MXene nanosheets through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, and van der Waals forces, enabling the composite film to have a high electrical conductivity (656.3 S·m−1). Positively charged ChNCs could shield the negative charges on MXene nanosheets, thus enhancing the adsorption of positive charges on the composite films. Therefore, the chitin MXene composite film can effectively improve the charge storage and transfer capability as a conductive layer of the TENG during electrostatic induction. The chitin nanocrystal/MXene composite film was encapsulated by PDMS, which could produce a power density of 99.5 mW·m−2 in the single-electrode operation mode. The TENG was utilized for low-frequency energy harvesting and as a flexible self-powered sensor to monitor human joint motion and tactile sensing.

MXenes are susceptible to oxidation if exposed to aqueous solutions or aerobic environments, which limits their reliability in long-term applications [100]. To improve the oxidation resistance of MXenes, Hao *et al*. [101] reported a new composite fiber, which consists of *in-situ* copolymerization dopamine-modified MXene (P-MXene) and wet-spinning MXene/TPU (MP) fibers, as shown in Fig. 3(a). By introducing *in-situ* copolymerized dopamine on the surface of MXenes, the antioxidant effect of MXenes in moist air can be improved, and the electrical conductivity of MP fibers can be significantly improved. The surface-functionalized P-MXene-integrated MXene/TPU (MMP) wet-spun fiber was found to exhibit high conductivity (4.32 S·cm−1), a wide deformation range (~675% strain), and good mechanical strength (~3.76 MPa). As shown in Fig. 3(b), the woven fiber-based TENG using MMP fibers as the conductive layer and commercial PTFE yarns as the triboelectric layer can generate a peak open-circuit voltage of 21.07 V, a short-circuit current of 0.92 μA, and an output power of 0.16 mW·m−2 at 3 Hz. Owing to the excellent flexibility of MMP fibers, TENGs can be employed as wearable self-powered sensors to monitor a wide range of human physiological signals by the real-time electrical outputs.

Hydrogels are emerging flexible polymers benefiting from their numerous hydroxy side groups and hydrogen bonds, excellent stretchability, excellent biocompatibility, and self-healing [102−104]. However, hydrogels have obvious disadvantages, such as low conductivity and slow response to external stimuli, which greatly restricts their practical application as a TENG conductive layer. The MXene nanosheets were integrated into hydrogels, which were able to enhance the conductivity of the hydrogels while improving their mechanical strength and biocompatibility [105−110].

In 2021, Luo et al*. [111] introduced MXene nanosheets into polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) hydrogels via a chemical crosslinking method as stretchable conductive layers of TENGs. As shown in Fig. 3(c), silicone rubber was used as the triboelectric layer of TENG, and the MXene/PVA hydrogel was encapsulated to form a sealed capsule structure. Cross-linking between MXene and PVA through hydrogen bonding endows the hydrogel with excellent self-healing and stretching properties. The MXene/PVA hydrogel-based TENG can be stretched up to 200% and demonstrates a monotonically increasing relationship between the stretchable length and the short-circuit voltage. In addition, MXenes can form microchannels inside the hydrogel to promote the transport of positive ions in the hydrogel and generate additional triboelectric output. Even in single-electrode mode, the MXene/PVA hydrogel-based TENG exhibited an open-circuit voltage of 230 V, a short-circuit current of 270 nA, a charge transfer of 38 nC, and a power density of 0.33 W·m−2. Benefiting from the excellent stretchability and ultrahigh sensitivity to mechanical stimuli of the MXene/PVA hydrogel, TENGs can be used in wearable motion monitoring and high-precision writing recognition sensors.

MXenes are prone to aggregating in hydrogels due to the abundance of polar groups, leading to reduced conductivity and mechanical properties of the hydrogels. In particular, the initiator used to activate the hydrogel polymerization reaction generates free radicals that induce MXene aggregation [114,115]. In this case, the MXene was oxidized to prepare oxidized MXene/polyacrylamide (PAM) composite hydrogels by Zhang *et al*. [112]. As shown in Fig. 3(d), the active Ti atoms from Ti3C2T*x* reacted with the hydroxyl group and were oxidized to produce TiO2, which acted as a spacer that prevented the agglomeration of MXene and improved the dispersion of MXene in the hydrogel. As a result, as shown in Fig. 3(e), the conductivity of the hydrogel was improved. The TENG is constructed with polyacrylamide/oxidized MXene hydrogel as the conductive layer and silicone rubber as the triboelectric layer. It is capable of generating a maximum open circuit voltage of up to 243 V and a peak power density of 2.38 W·m−2, demonstrating its great potential for self-powered sensing and energy harvesting.

Notably, as shown in Fig. 3(f), Chen *et al*. [113] prepared a polyacrylamide hydrogel based on 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl radical (TEMPO)-oxidized cellulose nanofibers (TOCNF)/tannic acid (TA)@MXene. The hydrogen bonding formed between the catechol groups of TA and the functional groups on the surface of MXene, as well as the three-dimensional network structure constructed with TOCNF, effectively prevented the aggregation of MXene in the hydrogel and significantly increased the stretchability of the hydrogel (up to 800%) (Fig. 3(g)). The TENG of this hydrogel in single-electrode mode achieved an open-circuit voltage of 106 V, a short-circuit current of 2 μA, and a transferred charge of 31 nC. Ultimately, the TENG was used as a self-powered sensor for real-time monitoring of human motion.

The MXene films used as conductive layers of TENGs are summarized in Table 1. Because MXene films are highly conductive, they can be applied as conductive layers. Compared with conventional metal foils as conductive layers, the first advantage of MXene films is their excellent flexibility/stretchability. All the MXene films are flexible. Some composite films of MXenes with polymers exhibit excellent stretchability (1800%), as shown in Table 1. As a conductive layer, the flexible MXene films can be used to construct flexible TENGs, which is essential for achieving self-powered flexible sensing. The second advantage is the excellent charge trapping ability of MXenes. The surface of MXenes is always negatively charged. It is easy for MXene films to obtain electrons from the triboelectric layer. In Section 5, MXene films are even used as charge trapping layers between the conductive layer and triboelectric layer. The third advantage of MXene films is the diverse processability of MXenes. As shown in Table 1, pure MXene films and composite films can be made by different methods, such as spray-coating, immersion, and solution-blending. The fourth advantage is that MXene nanosheets easily form strong bonds with various polymer-based triboelectric layers due to the high degree of surface termination. Strong bonds are beneficial for electron transfer between the conductive layer and triboelectric layer.

To utilize MXenes more effectively as a conductive layer for TENGs, the following strategies can be adopted. First, the high conductivity of MXenes is utilized. By combining MXenes with hydrogels, their high conductivity is utilized to increase the conductivity of the hydrogel. In addition, the abundant functional groups (e.g., –OH and –H) on the surface of MXene nanosheets can provide additional hydrogen-bonding binding sites to promote cross-linking of the hydrogel, thus enhancing its self-healing and tensile properties. Second, the diverse processing properties of MXenes were utilized. The appropriate preparation method is selected according to the application requirements (e.g., spraying for large-area devices and dipping for porous substrates). This technique is combined with patterning techniques (e.g., masking and printing) to achieve customized design of the conductive layer and optimize device performance. The third is antioxidant encapsulation. A hydrophobic polymer layer (e.g., polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), silicone rubber, and PDMS) covers the surface of the MXene film to prevent oxidation-induced performance degradation.

## 3 MXene films applied in triboelectric layer

A triboelectric layer gains/losses electrons from another triboelectric layer during the contact–separation cycles. Thus, a triboelectric layer needs a very strong or weak ability to adsorb electrons. This property is generally measured by electronegativity. MXenes always have very high electronegativity. This is because the surface of MXene is always terminated by OH/F groups. OH termination easily results in the loss of H+ during processing/dispersion in water. The loss of H+ results in the surface readily gaining electrons to achieve a negatively charged surface. Moreover, the other termination, F, possesses the strongest electron-withdrawing ability among all the elements [116]. Therefore, surface MXenes are ideal acceptors of electrons during triboelectric cycles. Owing to their excellent ability to gain electrons, MXene films can be used as negative triboelectric layers. A special advantage of MXene films as triboelectric layers is that MXenes are highly conductive. Thus, the charges generated by the contact–separation cycles are easily conducted. Compared with insulating triboelectric layers, this fast conduction eliminates the requirement for a conductive layer. The disadvantage of MXene films is that compared with general polymer films, pure MXene films have poor mechanical properties. Therefore, many articles have reported that MXene nanosheets can be used as fillers to improve the properties of polymer films, as reviewed in Section 4.

In 2017, Dong *et al*. [70] first reported MXene as a triboelectric material for TENGs. The aqueous suspension of Ti3C2T*x* was sprayed on a piece of glass as a triboelectric layer for TENG. Figure 4(a) illustrates the structure of TENG with MXene as the triboelectric layer. As shown in Figs. 4(b) and 4(c), two types of TENGs were constructed. For the first type of TENG, the two electrodes were MXene/glass: polyethylene terephthalate (PET)–indium tin oxide (ITO). For the second type of TENG, the electrode used was PTFE:PET–ITO. The two types of TENGs exhibited similar open-circuit voltages. This implies that both Ti3C2T*x* MXene and PTFE are placed equally below PET/ITO on the triboelectric series. Ti3C2T*x* MXene is on par with PTFE in the triboelectric series.

Based on this result, some researchers reported MXene films on flexible substrates or without substrates for flexible TENGs. Yi et al*. [71] prepared an electrode by three-dimensional (3D) printing MXene ink on a styrene–ethylene–butylene–styrene (SEBS) substrate. SEBS is only a substrate that provides mechanical strength. The MXene film on the substrate works as a triboelectric layer as well as a conductive layer due to its high electronegativity and high conductivity. Figures 4(d) and 4(e) demonstrate the working mechanism of TENG in vertical contact separation mode and single electrode mode, respectively. This TENG can produce an open-circuit voltage of 400 V and a power density of 816.6 mW·m−2. The MXene-based capacitive pressure sensor exhibited a sensitivity of ~6.03 kPa−1, a low detection limit of ~9 Pa, and fast response time of ~80 ms.

The performance of the TENG based on pure MXene film as a triboelectric layer can be improved by changing the structure of the film. As shown in Fig. 4(f), a film with a crumpled structure was obtained by brushed-coating MXene inks on a pre-stretched latex substrate and subsequently releasing the substrate [117]. The crumpled film had improved stretchability and surface roughness. Benefiting from the rough surface and crumpled structure, the output power density of the TENG delivered a maximum power density of 2.89 µW·cm−2, which is 36 times greater than that of the flat MXene film. In terms of strain sensing, the crumpled MXene film also exhibited excellent sensitivity and cycling stability, which can be used for self-powered pressure/strain sensors to monitor finger movement with a wireless real-time gesture monitoring system.

MXene films can also be assembled on a flexible leather substrate by vacuum filtration [118]. Then, the Au–laser induced graphene (LIG) electrodes were obtained on the backside of the leather by laser induced and HuAuCl4 solution dip-coating on the LIG surface. Figure 4(g) schematically illustrates the preparation process of leather-based MXenes as well as the backside of LIG. The MXene film forms a rough structure on the leather surface, which greatly improves the interfacial strength of MXene/substrate. A rough MXene film is used as the negative triboelectric layer of single-electrode TENG, and an open-circuit voltage of 199.56 V, an output current of 9.04 μA, a transferred charge of 32.7 nC, and a power density of 0.469 mW·cm−2 can be obtained by contact with a polyurethane (PU) film. Subsequently, as shown in Fig. 4(h), Au–LIG arrays were used as the conductive layer of TENG, which was prepared as a 3 × 3 cross-interactive sensor array and applied in various human–machine interface (HMI) applications.

The introduction of graphene oxide (GO) also decreases the self-stacking of MXene nanosheets. As shown in Fig. 5(a), Yang *et al*. The interaction between GO and MXene effectively hinders the agglomeration of the two nanoflakes and improves the conductivity, electrochemical activity, and mechanical strength. A series of MXene/GO TENGs with different GO sizes were prepared. The MXene/GO composite film with 400 nm GO (MGM) shows the highest conductivity and presents optimal performance. TENGs can generate electrical signals without physical contact, which is named non-contact TENG. In 2023, Wang et al*. [120] prepared a non-contact TENG based on cellulose nanofiber (CNF)/MXene porous aerogels by the freeze-drying-assisted 3D printing method. Owing to the excellent electron-trapping performance of MXenes, CNF/MXene aerogels can preserve more induced charges in a deep trap for a long time, effectively enhancing the electrical output of TENGs. For CNF/MXene-TENG with 5 wt% MXene loading, the output voltage and charge density of the TENG reach a maximum of 25 V and 900 mC·cm−3, respectively. Non-contact TENG is used as a 3D sensor for multi-site and multi-dimension motion trajectory monitoring.

The surface termination of MXenes can affect the performance of MXene films. Generally, F surface termination, which possesses the strongest electron-withdrawing ability, enhances the electronegativity of MXenes. Thus, MXenes with more F surface terminations can have better triboelectric performance. As shown in Fig. 5(c), the MXene powders with more F terminations were made by fluorinated functionalization [121]. The XPS spectra of pristine MXene and F-MXene are shown in Fig. 5(d). XPS spectroscopy showed that the modified MXene nanosheets contained more fluorine terminal groups. As shown in Fig. 5(e), benefiting from the MXene with the scale morphology and rich fluorine groups, the output voltage of the TENG was increased to 30 V.

Chen *et al*. [122] prepared MXene/polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) composite films by vacuum filtration. The composite films exhibited excellent conductivity and high flexibility and demonstrated superior charge trapping ability, which enabled them to act as both triboelectric layers and conductive layers, thus further simplifying the structure of the TENGs (Fig. 5(f)). The TENG achieves its best performance when the mass fraction of PMMA is 4%, generating a maximum open-circuit voltage of 37.8 V, a short-circuit current of 1.8 μA, and a transferred charge of 14.1 nC. This excellent performance is attributed mainly to the tile nanostructure formed by the PMMA microspheres and MXene nanosheets in the composite films, which effectively enhances the charge trapping and storing ability and thus significantly improves the electrical output performance of TENG (Fig. 5(g)).

Zhou *et al*. [123] prepared MXene/TEMPO oxidized cellulose nanofiber (TOCNF) composite films with a hierarchical laminar structure by a simple vacuum filtration method. As shown in Fig. 5(h), the composite membrane was used as both the negative triboelectric layer and the conductive layer of TENG and was assembled with the positive triboelectric layer of nylon to form the TENG. When the MXene content is 50%, an open-circuit voltage of 210 V, a short-circuit current of 0.84 μA, and a charge transfer of 8.6 nC can be generated in the TENG. This excellent performance is attributed to the presence of carboxylate and fluorine-containing groups in the composite films, which increase the electron gain capability. Finally, this TENG was used as a self-powered sensor for monitoring human movement and writing.

The applications of MXene films as triboelectric layers of TENGs are summarized in Table 2. There are three advantages for this application. First, MXene films have high electronegativity. Owing to the loss of H+ from OH termination and the strong electron-withdrawing ability of F termination, MXene nanosheets easily gain electrons. The surface of MXenes is always negatively charged and highly electronegative. The films made from MXenes have high performance as negative triboelectric layers. Second, MXene films are highly conductive, unlike general polymers used as triboelectric layers. Thus, MXene films have a high ability to collect and conduct the electrons generated by triboelectric cycles. This decreases the requirement of the conductive layer. In those cases, the MXene films are both triboelectric layers and conductive layers [70,71,117−119,123]. Some substrates are highly flexible (SEBS, latex, leather, etc.); thus, flexible TENGs can be constructed from those kinds of MXene films on flexible non-conductive substrates [71,117,118,122].

To utilize MXenes more effectively as a triboelectric layer for TENGs, the following strategies can be adopted. First, the high electronegativity of MXenes is fully utilized. Through chemical modification to modulate the OH/F end-group ratio (e.g., the F end-group to enhance the electron affinity), the charge trapping ability of MXene films can be optimized to enhance the electric output performance of friction. ## 4 MXene as filler of polymer film applied in triboelectric layer

Pure MXene films can be used as triboelectric layers with high performance, as reviewed in Section 3. However, compared with general polymer films, these films always have poor mechanical properties. Therefore, another important application is the use of MXenes as fillers to enhance the properties of polymer films. The reasons for the use of MXenes as fillers of the triboelectric layer are as follows: 1) As two-dimensional materials, few-layer MXene nanosheets have very high specific surface areas and are usually horizontally oriented in the polymer matrix, which enables them to form a wide range of contact interfaces with the polymer matrix. 2) There are rich surface functional groups on the surface of MXene nanosheets that can form strong bonds with the polymer matrix, resulting in strong interfacial interactions, which in turn lead to the formation of a micro-capacitive structure at the interface and a significant improvement in the dielectric constant of the polymer. The dielectric constant strongly affects the surface charge density of the film, which positively affects the electrical output performance of the TENG. 3) MXene nanosheets have high electronegativity, which can enhance the triboelectric properties of the matrix. Therefore, there are a lot of reports on polymer films with MXene fillers as triboelectric layers of TENGs.

PVDF films are often used as negative triboelectric layers because of their large dipole moments, strong flexibility, formability, and electronegativity [124]. However, PVDF has the shortcomings of low dielectric properties and a low surface charge density. In 2021, Bhatta *et al*. [125] blended Ti3C2T*x* nanosheets as a conductive filler into the PVDF matrix, which significantly improved the dielectric properties and surface charge density. Owing to the introduction of conductive MXenes, the formation of microscopic dipoles and micro capacitive networks can be promoted, leading to an increase in the dielectric constant (Fig. 6(a)). When the loading of MXene was 10 wt%, the dielectric constant and surface charge density of the PVDF nanofibers were improved by 270% and 80%, respectively. The TENGs based on PVDF/MXene composite nanofibers and nylon 6/6 nanofibers were fabricated and achieved a power density of 11.213 W·m−2, which is 1.58 times higher than that of pure PVDF nanofiber TENGs.

In 2022, Cao *et al*. [45] prepared negatively modified N-functionalized Ti3C2 (N-Ti3C2)/PVDF-TrFE and positively modified NH2-functionalized Ti3C2 (NH2-Ti3C2)/nylon 11 composite films via spin-coating methods. In addition, the dielectric constants of both functionalized composite films were significantly increased. Consequently, the TENGs fabricated using the friction pair of PVDF-TrFE/N-Ti3C2 and nylon 11/NH2-Ti3C2 demonstrated an open-circuit voltage of 250 V, a short-circuit current density of 280 µA·cm−2, a charge density of 210 µC·m−2, and an output power density of 13 mW·cm−2.

PDMS is another polymer that is used as a triboelectric layer of TENGs. MXene nanosheets have been reported to enhance the performance of PDMS as a conductive filler [127−133]. Wang *et al*. [127] prepared MXene/PDMS nanocomposites with 3D interconnection by unidirectional freeze-drying and vacuum-assisted impregnation methods. The 3D network structure formed by the interconnection of MXene nanosheets in PDMS conferred both high thermal/electrical conductivities to the composites. The structure of TENG is shown in Fig. 6(e). The introduction of conductive MXene into the PDMS formed a microcapacitor structure, which increased the dielectric constant and surface charge density of the composite film, and the output of the TENG was improved (Fig. 6(f)).

By spin-coating MXene/PDMS on a Cu-Ni/textile, He *et al*. [128] fabricated a flexible single-electrode TENG for human motion sensors. The preparation process and application of flexible single-electrode TENGs based on MXene/PDMS films are presented in Fig. 6(g). The doping of MXenes increases the surface charge density by increasing the capacitance of TENG and decreasing the internal resistance of PDMS, thus improving the output performance of TENG. A maximum open-circuit voltage of 225 V, a short-circuit current density of 30 μA·cm−2, and a power density of 10 mW·cm−2 can be achieved at a load resistance of 100 MΩ when the content of MXene reaches 3 wt%. Finally, the TENG was attached to a human finger and leg to detect various human motions as a human motion sensor (Fig. 6(h)).

Silicone rubber is widely used as a triboelectric layer in TENGs with excellent hydrophobicity, flexibility, and stretchability [61,134,135]. In 2023, Zhang *et al*. [136] developed a MXene/silicone-based TENG and an alternating current electroluminescence (AC-EL) device based on a shared MXene electrode, as shown in Fig. 7(a). MXene as a conductive filler for silicone enhances the dielectric constant of the MXene/silicone triboelectric material, and the output performance of the TENG is enhanced. The TENG fabricated from the MXene silicone demonstrated an output voltage of 290 V, a short-circuit current of 24 µA, and a power density of 0.9 W·m−2, which can directly light up the flexible patterned AC-EL device. MXenes are used as shared electrodes for TENGs and AC-ELs to achieve device integration without any electrical interconnections, including copper wires. Based on this property, the authors explore a novel ACEL-patterned emission-based information encryption strategy. By dropping a conductive electrolyte solution on the surface of the AC-EL and hand tapping the TENG, the encrypted information can be precisely revealed via self-powered ACEL emission for real-time visualized information interaction.

Compared with other fillers (MoS2, hexagonal boron nitride, graphene, ferroelectric barium titanate, mica, etc.), the MXene filler has five advantages. First, unlike graphene, whose inert surface makes it difficult to blend directly with polymers, MXenes blend easily with polymers without premodification [141]. Second, the films with the MXene filler can maintain excellent mechanical properties. This is because strong bonding can form at the interface between MXene and polymer matrix, which is due to the abundance of termination groups (OH/O/F) on the surface of MXene nanosheets. Third, the dielectric constants of polymer films can be significantly increased. The hydrogen bonding formed between MXene and the polymer generates more dipoles and interfacial polarization, and the micro-capacitor structure can further increase the dielectric constant of the polymer [142], thus increasing the surface charge density of the polymer and improving the output performance of TENGs. Fifth, the composite film can be relatively conducive to collecting triboelectric electrons because of the high conductivity of MXenes. The last advantage is very important because it decreases the requirement of the conductive layer.

To utilize MXenes more effectively as fillers for polymers, the following strategies can be adopted: First is modulation of functional group on surface. Through chemical modification to modulate the ratio or type of –OH/–O/–F end groups of MXenes, their chemical bonding (hydrogen bonding/covalent bonding) with the polymer matrix (e.g., PVDF, PDMS, and TPU) can be enhanced to improve the tensile strength and dielectric properties of composite films. The second is integrated design of conductive–triboelectric layers. By regulating the MXene content (near the percolation limit), the composite film combines both triboelectric and conductive properties, which reduces the need for a separate conductive layer and simplifies the device structure. In addition, the design of films with a gradient of MXene concentrations (high content in the top layer to enhance the friction electrical properties and low content in the bottom layer to maintain the insulating properties) enables efficient charge collection and conduction. The third is multifunctional composite preparation of flexible stretchable devices. MXene fillers are compounded with elastomers (e.g., silicone rubber, PDMS, and TPU) to enhance their dielectric properties to prepare TENGs without conductive layers, which are suitable for wearable flexible sensing devices.

## 5 MXenes or MXenes/polymers applied in charge trapping layers

In most TENGs, an electrode consists of two layers: a triboelectric layer and a conductive layer. However, in some TENGs, there is a third layer: a charge trapping layer. This layer is located between the triboelectric layer and the conductive layer, which helps the conductive layer trap charges from the triboelectric layer. MXenes demonstrate excellent charge trapping capabilities because of their remarkable electronegativity, electron affinity, and abundance of surface functional groups [143,144]. Therefore, MXene films can be used as a charge trapping layer to enhance the performance of TENGs.

Anwer *et al*. [72] deposited Ti3C2T*x* MXene sheets (TMSs) on an Al foil by spray coating. Then, the MXene film was enclosed and shielded by a PET film with a thickness of 50 µm. As shown in Fig. 9(a), Al-MXene-PET was used as an electrode to construct a TENG. The MXene film was used as a charge trapping layer to accelerate charge trapping. Compared with that of the TENG without the MXene film, the output performance of the TENG was obviously improved. The optimized TENG showed an open-circuit voltage of 390 V, a short-circuit current of 96 μA, and a power density of 6.66 W·m−2. As shown in Figs. 9(b) and 9(c), the designed TENG can charge a capacitor, power LEDs, and an electronic calculator through a rectifier bridge and can be used as a self-powered sensor for observing human physiological movements.

The charge decay experiments in Fig. 9(f) indicate that the MXene/TiO2 hybrid layers play an important role as intermediate layers in facilitating charge trapping and blocking diffusion.

Rahman *et al*. [74] reported a MXene/silicone rubber composite film-based double-layered TENG with a 3D-printed template. The structure of the TENG is shown in Fig. 9(g). In the TENG, a piece of conductive fabric was used as the conductive layer (labeled as electrode in Fig. 9(g)), and a piece of nano porous cobalt oxide (NPCO)/silicone rubber film was used as the triboelectric layer (labeled as charge-generating layer in Fig. 9(g)). A piece of MXene/silicone rubber composite film was used as the charge trapping layer. In the charge trapping layer, the MXene was incorporated into silicone rubber to enhance the charge trapping ability and electronegativity of the film. This MXene/silicon film, as a charge trapping layer, can prevent triboelectric charges from combining with the induced opposite charges to improve the performance of the TENG (Fig. 9(h)). As a result, the TENG exhibits excellent output performance, including a current density of 560 mA·m−2 and a power density of 10.4 W·m−2.

In 2024, Yan *et al*. [76] prepared MXene/polyimide (PI) yarn, which was used as a charge trapping layer between the triboelectric layers and the conductive layer in a TENG, and the structure of the TENG is shown in Fig. 9(i). The introduction of MXene as an interlayer can effectively capture the triboelectric charge and prevent it from canceling and recombining with the induced charge, thus significantly enhancing the output performance of TENG. When the mass fraction of MXene is 1 wt%, TENG achieves the best performance, with an output voltage of 153 V, a current of 13.13 μA, and a power density of 0.84 W·m−2. The TENG based on the MXene/PI yarn also exhibits excellent flame retardant properties, enabling it to be used as a smart wearable system in stable applications in high-temperature environments.

Venkatesan *et al*. [77] prepared TiO2-MXene-incorporated polystyrene (PS) nanofiber films (PTMx) by electrostatic spinning technique and applied them as a charge trapping layer in a single electrode mode TENG (S-TENG) (Fig. 9(j)). Moreover, PVDF mixed with PU nanofiber films (PPU/PU) were used as triboelectric layers, and Ag nanoparticle precursor (AgNP)-coated styrene butadiene (SBS) was used as a conductive layer to enhance the mechanical and tensile properties of the TENG. As shown in Fig. 9(k), the PTMx increases the charge storage capacity and effectively prevents the charge drift by the electrode. By optimizing the thickness of the PTMx interlayer, the S-TENG achieves excellent performance, with a maximum open-circuit voltage of 280 V, a short-circuit current of 20 μA, a transfer charge of 120 nC, and a power density of 25.2 μW·cm−2. Finally, its applications in biomechanical sensing and wound healing are explored.

Compared with other charge-trapping layer materials, such as gold films [145] and PDMS [146], there are four advantages for MXenes in application. First, MXenes have a high ability to trap electrons due to their high electronegativity. Second, MXenes have high conductivity and can transfer electrons to the conductive layer. Third, MXene nanosheets are easily processed to make pure films or composite films. Fourth, MXene films have a strong adhesive ability to metal films and polymer films.

## 6 Summary and perspectives

In summary, MXenes have been applied in all parts of an electrode of TENGs: conductive layer, triboelectric layer, and charge trapping layer. For the application in triboelectric layers, MXenes can be divided into two forms: MXene films or the fillers of polymers film. Therefore, the applications of MXenes in TENGs can be classified into four types. MXenes exhibit excellent performance in all four types of applications. As shown in Fig. 10, as conductive layers, the advantages of MXene pure films are summarized as follows: excellent flexibility/stretchability, high charge trapping ability, diverse processability, and rich surface termination. Owing to these advantages, pure films or composite films of MXenes can be made. The films exhibit excellent flexible/stretchable, highly conductive, highly charge trapping, and can be used as conductive layer in TENG for self-powered sensing. However, the conductive layer for TENGs has significant disadvantages. MXene films are easily oxidized in air/water, and their long-term stability as flexible sensors is poor.

As triboelectric layers, the advantages of MXene films are summarized as follows: high-electronegativity, high-conductivity, and no-conductive substrates are acceptable. As triboelectric layers, MXene films exhibit excellent performance and do not require conductive layers.

As fillers of polymer films as triboelectric layers, the advantages of MXene nanosheets are summarized as follows: strong bonding with the polymer matrix, a high dielectric constant, strong electronegativity, and high conductivity. The composite films have good mechanical properties (such as strength, flexibility, and tensile properties) because of the polymer matrix, and the MXene filler endows the composite films with certain electrical conductivity and enhanced electron-gain capability. Furthermore, the polymer matrix protects the MXene fillers from oxidation. The oxidation of MXenes in air/water is the main shortcoming that limits their application. Owing to their advantages, polymer films with MXenes as fillers exhibit excellent mechanical properties and triboelectric performance for energy harvesting. However, there are several disadvantages. First, the preparation process of composite films is relatively complicated. MXene composites with polymers such as PVDF, PVDF–TrFE, TPU, and PVA usually need to be prepared by the electrostatic spinning method. The preparation process for the spinning solution is relatively difficult, which increases the difficulty and cost of composite film preparation. Second, there is the problem of leakage current. MXenes tend to form a conductive network in the polymer matrix, which can lead to a significant increase in the leakage current between the composite film and the conductive layer, which in turn reduces the output performance of TENG.

As charge trapping layers, the advantages of MXene films are summarized as follows: excellent charge ability due to high electronegativity, high charge transfer ability due to high conductivity, easy processing into films, and strong adhesion to metal and polymer films. Owing to their advantages, MXene films exhibit excellent performance in trapping charges from the triboelectric layer and transferring them to the conductive layer.

It is unusual for a material to exhibit various advantages in different layers of an electrode of TENGs. Most materials can only be applied in a specific layer of TENGs. Therefore, MXene films offer a unique advantage that enables the application of TENGs, which would be difficult to achieve with TENGs based on other materials. (1) Versatile integration. MXene films have a strong electron-gain ability and excellent conductivity. Thus, MXene films can be used as either triboelectric layers or conductive layers. This kind of multifunctional integration is not common in other materials. For example, metal foils cannot be used as triboelectric layers because of their poor electron gain/loss ability. In contrast, polymer films can be used as triboelectric layers because of their strong electron gain/loss ability but cannot be used as conductive layers because of their weak conductivity. (2) Simpler structure. If conductive MXene films are used as triboelectric layers, they can be used as conductive layers. Conductive layers and charge trapping layers are not needed. Thus, a TENG with simple structure can be constructed [70,71,117−119]. (3) Excellent flexibility and stretchability. The metal foils used as conductive layers always have poor flexibility and no stretchability, which limits the flexibility of TENGs. MXene films or MXene hydrogel composite films, as the conductive layer of TENGs, can endow TENGs with increased flexibility/stretchability. This characteristic is especially important for wearable and flexible electronic devices. Additionally, by removing the non-stretchable metal conductive layer of TENGs, it is possible to endow TENGs excellent flexibility/stretchability [95].

According to Tables 1−4, MXenes can be applied in two forms in TENGs. One is the MXene film, and the other is the filler of polymer films. Generally, MXene films have better conductivity and electron-gain ability. Thus, pure MXene films exhibit excellent performance as conductive layers [66] or triboelectric layers [70,71,117,118]. In addition, the preparation process of pure MXene films is relatively simple and can be realized by spraying or vacuum-assisted filtration. However, pure MXene films always have poor mechanical properties and are easily oxidized in air. Therefore, some materials, such as CNF and ChNCs, added to modify the performance of MXene films can effectively improve their mechanical strength and stability. The most popular method is to use MXenes as fillers to make polymer composite films. The composite films have good mechanical properties (strength, flexibility, stretchability, etc.) because of the polymer matrix. Additionally, the composite films have some conductivity and strong electron-gain ability because of the MXene fillers. Moreover, the polymer matrix protects the MXene fillers from oxidation. The oxidation of MXenes in air/water is the main shortcoming that limits their application. Thus, the polymer composite films with MXenes as fillers are a very promising area for the application of MXenes in TENGs.

For MXene/polymer composite films, three properties are desired for application in TENGs: electron-gain ability, conductivity, and mechanical properties. A composite film with strong electron-gain ability, good conductivity, and excellent mechanical properties is an ideal triboelectric layer of TENGs. To make the desired MXene/polymer films, the following directions are suggested for future research:

1) Selection of MXene type. There are many types of MXenes, such as Ti3C2 MXenes, V2C MXenes, Nb2C MXenes, and Mo2C MXenes. However, most previous studies focused on Ti3C2 MXenes. Whether other types of MXenes may have better performance in specific scenarios needs to be further investigated. For example, Mo2C MXenes have better thermal stability, making them potentially valuable for use in TENGs operating in high-temperature environments. Future research could consider using Mo2C MXene composite films as triboelectric layer materials for TENGs to improve the performance and stability of devices under high-temperature conditions. In addition, first-principles calculations can be used to investigate the effects of etching conditions and surface functional groups on the triboelectric properties of different MXenes, which can help prepare high-performance MXene composite films. These findings provide theoretical support for the in-depth study of the mechanism of the influence of etching conditions and surface functional groups on the output performance of TENGs.

2) Selection of polymer matrix. Many polymer matrixes, such as PDMS, PVDF, TPU, and PVA, have been researched. If an MXene-based TENG is used for energy harvesting, the dielectric properties of the polymer need to be considered. For example, selecting a PVDF or P(VDF–TrFE) composite with MXenes can significantly improve the dielectric properties of the polymer to increase the electrical output of TENG. If an MXene-based TENG is used for self-powered sensing, the flexibility and stretchability of the polymer matrix need to be considered. For example, hydrogels are selected to be compounded with MXenes to increase the conductivity and stretchability of hydrogels for flexible sensing. If long-term stability, high mechanical properties, and high tensile properties of TENGs are required, the mechanical stability and wear resistance of the polymer need to be considered. For example, TPU compounded with MXenes can be used to increase the long-term stability and tensile properties of TENGs. However, many polymers have not been attempted. There is no theory for selecting a polymer matrix for MXenes. Therefore, more polymer matrixes need to be explored and compared. Based on those works, a theory needs to be developed for selection of a polymer matrix.

3) Design of TENG work mode and structure for polymer/MXene composite films. The traditional structures of TENGs are based on polymer films without conductivity as triboelectric layers. Metal foils are required as conductive layers to trap and conduct the chargers generated by triboelectric layers, which limits the flexibility/stretchability of TENGs. However, polymer/MXene composite films can be conductive and have strong electron-gain ability. Thus, a conductive layer is not necessary. The new work mode and structure of TENGs with conductive polymer/MXene films as the conductive layer need to be explored.

## 7 Conclusions

In this article, we analyze the role of two-dimensional MXene materials in TENGs from a materials perspective, revealing their considerable potential for enhancing the performance of TENGs. MXene films as conductive layers with excellent flexibility offer a new path to develop flexible TENGs. MXene films as triboelectric layers with excellent conductivity reduce the need for conventional conductive layers, increasing the flexibility of TENG design and expanding its application areas. As polymer fillers with rich surface termination and high electronegativity, MXene films significantly improve the triboelectric properties of polymers, providing new solutions for the design of high-performance TENGs. MXene films, as charge-trapping layers with excellent electron-trapping and electron-conducting capabilities, significantly improve the energy conversion efficiency of TENGs. The multifunctionality of MXenes enables them to play a role in different parts of TENGs, thus promoting the development of high-performance and multifunctional TENGs. This review provides a deeper understanding of the application of MXenes in TENGs and provides literature support for future research and innovative design of TENGs.
