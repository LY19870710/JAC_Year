## 1 Introduction

Low-temperature densification technology for ceramic materials has attracted much attention because of its significant advantages in terms of energy conservation, environmental friendliness, and nanostructure regulation [1−3]. Although traditional ceramic sintering processes are effective, they typically require high temperatures (> 1000 °C) to achieve densification [4]. This process consumes substantial energy and may induce abnormal grain growth, component volatilization, or decomposition, thereby restricting its application in precision electronic devices and functional materials [5]. To address these challenges, the cold sintering process (CSP) has established a groundbreaking framework for ceramic consolidation at markedly reduced temperatures (6−8]. To date, CSP has been successfully applied to the preparation of various ceramic materials, including oxides, nitrides, and carbides, demonstrating its broad application prospects in material preparation [9−12].

The transient liquid phase (TLP) exhibits dual functionality in CSPs. Through surface wetting of particles, TLP simultaneously reduces the interfacial energy to promote particle rearrangement while functioning as a mass transfer pathway that accelerates dissolution‒precipitation reaction kinetics [13,14]. For water-soluble ceramic materials, such as NaNO2, Li2MoO4, and KH2PO4, deionized water can be directly used as the TLP to achieve low-temperature densification [7,15,16]. However, the solubility of most water-insoluble materials in water is limited, and deionized water cannot be used as a TLP. Therefore, acidic/alkaline solutions or customized mixed solvents must be selected specifically [17−20]. For example, to obtain dense barium titanate (BaTiO3) ceramics, a water-based mixed solution of Ba(OH)2 and TiO2 is used as the TLP to avoid inconsistent BaTiO3 dissolution during the CSP process [7]. Notably, the choice of solvent must demonstrate chemical compatibility with the material surface and control low-temperature volatility to sustain adequate liquid phase duration [21,22]. However, this empirical trial-and-error approach to TLP selection has greatly limited the universality and practical application of CSP, making this promising technology inaccessible to many ceramics, especially those with limited solubility in common solvents.

Among many water-insoluble ceramic materials, Li2TiO3 is particularly prominent and has key application value in the fields of solid electrolytes, microwave dielectric devices, and solid tritium breeders [23−26]. However, this functional ceramic faces significant preparation challenges: traditional high-temperature sintering techniques can easily lead to grain coarsening and lithium volatilization, which greatly decreases its ionic conductivity and structural stability [27,28]. Implementing cold sintering processes to achieve densification at reduced temperatures presents a promising solution that could simultaneously suppress grain growth (preserving advantageous nanostructures) and prevent thermal-induced compositional instability, enhancing functional performance. However, the exceptionally low aqueous solubility of Li2TiO3 (29]. More importantly, due to the lack of suitable organic solvents or acidic TLP media, CSP has not yet been applied to Li2TiO3 ceramics. This technical bottleneck highlights the necessity of rethinking the transient liquid phase selection strategy, especially exploring whether it is possible to break through traditional methods and develop a simple, efficient, and universal transient liquid phase system for water-insoluble ceramics.

In this context, exploring water as a transient liquid phase for the cold sintering of Li2TiO3 has significant scientific and engineering implications. If the solubility limitations in aqueous media can be overcome, it would substantially simplify the processing steps, reduce both the raw materials and postprocessing costs, and align with the growing trend of green manufacturing. This study aims to overcome the solubility limitation of Li2TiO3 in CSP by leveraging nanoscale effects. The cold sintering behavior of Li2TiO3 in water as a transient liquid phase was systematically investigated through optimization of the size and morphology of the raw materials, revealing the underlying densification mechanism. These findings provide a new paradigm for the low-energy fabrication of high-performance nanoceramics and have the potential to extend low-temperature processing technologies to a broader range of material systems.

### 2.1 Synthesis of Li<sub>2</sub>TiO<sub>3</sub> powder

The β-Li2TiO3 precursor powders were synthesized via a modified solid-state synthesis route adapted from reference [30], as shown in Fig. 1(a). Briefly, stoichiometric amounts of LiOH·H2O (98%, Shanghai McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) and H2TiO3 (98%, Shanghai McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) were dissolved in 300 mL of deionized water at a molar ratio of 2 : 1. The aqueous suspension was subjected to wet ball milling at 180 r/min for 1 h using agate balls to ensure homogeneity. The mixture was subsequently dried at 100 °C in a forced-air oven (DHG-9067A, Shanghai Jinghong Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd., China) until complete moisture removal. The resulting agglomerates were manually pulverized in an agate mortar and then calcined at 500 °C for 120 min in a muffle furnace (MF-1100C-S, BEQ, China) to obtain phase-pure β-Li2TiO3 nanopowder. For comparative sintering studies, commercially available β-Li2TiO3 powders (98%, Shanghai McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) were directly utilized without further purification.

### 2.2 Fabrication of Li<sub>2</sub>TiO<sub>3</sub> ceramics via CSP

For the CSP (Fig. 1(b)), 0.2 g of synthesized Li2TiO3 nanopowders (denoted as nm-LT) and commercially sourced Li2TiO3 micropowders (denoted as μm-LT) were separately homogenized with deionized water (25 wt%) via an agate mortar until a uniform consistency was achieved. Approximately 0.15 g of each wetted powders was then carefully loaded into a tungsten carbide die with an internal diameter of 6 mm. The loaded die was immediately transferred to the sintering apparatus for the CSP procedure (Fig. 1(c)).

The cold sintering experiments were conducted in three sequential optimization phases to systematically investigate the influence of key processing parameters on the densification behavior. In the first phase, various sintering temperatures ranging from 150 to 300 °C were evaluated while maintaining a constant uniaxial pressure of 700 MPa and a fixed holding time of 60 min. In the second phase, the sintering temperature was maintained at 300 °C with a fixed holding time of 60 min, while the uniaxial pressure was systematically varied (400, 500, 600, 700, 800, and 900 MPa) to determine the optimal compression conditions. On the basis of these results, the optimal pressure was selected for the second phase of the experiments, where the holding time was varied (0, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 min) to establish the ideal dwell duration for densification. For both nm-LT and μm-LT, identical experimental parameters were applied to enable a direct comparison of their sintering behaviors.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The crystalline structure and phase evolution of the as-prepared samples were identified via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation (wavelength: 1.54184 Å) in the 2*θ* range of 10–80 with a step size of 0.02. The powder repose angle (*θ*) was obtained after nm-LT and μm-LT passed through the funnel. A transmission electron microscope (TEM; F200, JEOL, Japan) was used to characterize the morphology of the powders and the microstructure of the as-prepared ceramic samples. The cross-sectional microstructure of the as-prepared Li2TiO3 ceramics was observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JIB-4700, JOEL, Japan). In addition, the average grain size was estimated via the standard linear intercept method (ASTM E112) by numbering intercepting grains per unit length. The Vickers hardness of the ceramic samples was measured via a micro-Vickers hardness tester (DUH-211S, SHIMADZU, Japan) under a test force of 0.5 kg. The elastic modulus of the samples was measured via a nanomechanical tester (Nano Test Vantage, Micro Materials, UK) equipped with a Berkovich indenter under the conditions of a peak load of 500 mN and a strain rate of 0.2 s−1. The electrical properties of the samples sintered via different processes were analyzed via an impedance analyzer (WK-6500B, Shenzhen Wenke Electronic Instrument Co., Ltd., China) in the frequency range of 40 Hz–20 MHz. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) galvanostatic charge‒discharge experiments were conducted to evaluate the specific discharge capacity of the Li2TiO3 ceramics. A conventional three-electrode glass cell (Pt/saturated Li2SO4 aqueous solution/Li2TiO3) with Ag/AgCl reference electrodes was used. The CV test was performed at room temperature with a scanning voltage range from −0.2 to 1.2 V and a scanning rate of 10 mV/s. The bulk density of the prepared Li2TiO3 ceramics was accurately determined via the Archimedean method. The relative density was then calculated as the ratio of the measured bulk density to the theoretical density of Li2TiO3 (3.43 g/cm3 obtained from JCPDS:33-0831).

### 3.1 Characterizations of raw powders

The TEM micrographs of the synthesized nm-LT and the commercially acquired μm-LT are presented in Figs. 2(a) and 2(d), respectively. The synthesized nm-LT exhibited a homogeneous morphology with well-dispersed particles, whereas the μm-LT displayed significant agglomeration and irregular particle packing. Quantitative particle size distribution analysis, performed via Digital Micrograph software (Figs. 2(b) and 2(e)), revealed a stark contrast in the dimensions of the two powders. nm-LT has a narrow size distribution ranging from 15 to 20 nm, with an average particle size of 19.71 nm. In contrast, μm-LT has a wider size distribution, with an average particle size of 996.79 nm. The phase purity and crystallographic characteristics of both powders were systematically investigated through XRD analysis (Figs. 2(c) and 2(f)). The diffraction peaks of both powders are consistent with the characteristic peaks of standard β-Li2TiO3 (JCPDS#33-0831), and no second phase or impurity-related peaks are detected, indicating that nm-LT has good crystallinity and phase purity.

The friction coefficient (μ*) was measured as 0.331 and 0.715 for Li2TiO3 nm-LT and μm-LT, respectively. This stark contrast underscores the superior flowability of the nanoscale powder, attributed to its uniform morphology and reduced interparticle friction. In contrast, the larger particle size and irregular agglomerates of μm-LT enhance mechanical interlocking and severely hinder particle movement. The low friction coefficient of the nm-LT facilitates efficient particle rearrangement under applied pressure, enabling homogeneous packing and enhanced stress transmission through the powder bed. These characteristics are essential for achieving high green density prior to transient liquid phase activation, as improved particle mobility minimizes void formation at grain boundaries.

### 3.2 Effect of particle size on CSP behavior

To elucidate the influence of cold sintering temperature on the densification behavior of Li2TiO3 ceramics and considering that CSPs typically operate below 300 °C to maintain their low-temperature advantage, we systematically investigated the cold sintering behavior of μm-LT and nm-LT powders at 150, 200, 250, and 300 °C. Figures 4(a)–4(h) present SEM micrographs of Li2TiO3 ceramics prepared via CSP from μm-LT and nm-LT powders at different temperatures. At 150 °C, the ceramics derived from both powders exhibited porous structures with low relative densities (Figs. 4(a) and 4(e)), indicating that the sintering behavior was not fully activated and that the limited densification could be attributed to simple physical compaction. With increasing temperature, the μm-LT samples showed minimal improvement, reaching only 78.93% relative density at 300 °C (Fig. 4(d)), suggesting an inadequate densification driving force for micron-sized particles. In contrast, the nm-LT ceramics exhibited significantly enhanced densification at higher temperatures, resulting in a dense microstructure at 300 °C (Fig. 4(h)). This temperature effectively promotes densification while maintaining the low-temperature advantage of cold sintering, particularly for nm-LT. Therefore, 300 °C was selected for all subsequent investigations.

Figure 5 shows the relationship between the uniaxial pressure and relative density of the Li2TiO3 ceramics prepared from the two powders via CSP. The relative density of the micron ceramic sample first tends to increase but then stabilizes with increasing pressure. The relative density is approximately 72% at 400 MPa, reaches approximately 78% when the pressure increases to 700 MPa, and then remains stable in the range of 700–900 MPa. The relative density of the nanoceramics increases significantly with increasing pressure, reaching approximately 74% at 400 MPa, reaching a maximum of approximately 92.23% at 700 MPa, and then decreasing slightly in the range of 700–900 MPa. The decrease in relative density caused by excessive pressure may be caused by local overcompaction or microcracks, which are often observed in nanoscale ceramics under extreme stress. Through comparative analysis, under the same pressure, the relative density of the ceramic samples prepared with nm-LT is always greater than that of the samples prepared with μm-LT, indicating that, under the same cold sintering conditions, nm-LT is more likely to achieve a higher density.

The cross-sectional microstructural evolution of Li2TiO3 ceramics cold-sintered from μm-LT under varying pressures is presented in Figs. 6(a)–6(f). The microstructures of all the samples retained a wide particle size distribution (3–100 μm) and a sharp polyhedral morphology inherited from the original powder. The marginal density increase observed between 400 and 500 MPa (Figs. 6(a) and 6(b)) likely arises from physical particle repacking rather than genuine sintering, as evidenced by the absence of grain boundary rounding or pore elimination. Extensive interparticle porosity persists, even at 900 MPa, confirming limited densification despite pressure escalation (Fig. 6(f)). This is consistent with the inherent challenge of using water as a transient liquid phase to prepare water-insoluble ceramics via CSP. That is, insufficient interfacial dissolution limits particle rearrangement and densification.

In sharp contrast, Figs. 7(a)–7(f) show the transformative role of nanopowders in achieving effective densification in the CSP process. All the samples prepared from the nm-LT sample exhibit a uniform microstructure and polygonal grain morphology, and the grain size remains at the nanoscale, confirming that CSP can significantly inhibit grain coarsening. Many uniformly distributed pores and clear particle boundaries were observed at 400–500 MPa (Figs. 7(a) and 7(b)), corresponding to a lower relative density. Notably, at 600 MPa (Fig. 7(c)), gradual structural evolution was observed, characterized by the local coalescence of particles into continuous regions with indistinct boundaries, while isolated pores persisted throughout the microstructure. Upon further increasing the pressure, a dense microstructure with more blurred particle edges was present at 700–900 MPa (Figs. 7(d)–7(f)), and no obvious pore or defect structures were found. These results preliminarily prove that although Li2TiO3 is not water soluble, reducing the size of the raw material powders to the nanoscale can effectively circumvent the solubility limitation and thus achieve water-mediated densification behavior.

### 3.3 Effects of holding time and TLP on densification behavior

To further understand the densification behavior of nm-LT during CSP, it is necessary to carry out relevant studies with different holding times under specific conditions. The specific CSP program is shown in Fig. 11(a), where the uniaxial pressure is 700 MPa and the holding temperature is 300 °C. All the programs took 20 min to warm from room temperature to the target temperature of 300 °C; then, they were maintained for 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, or 100 min; and finally, they were allowed to cool naturally. Figure 11(b) shows the relative density of ceramics prepared from nm-LT at holding times of 0–100 min. To highlight the role of the transient liquid phase, comparative data of the water medium and the dry medium are given. The results show that appropriately extending the holding time can effectively improve the densification level of Li2TiO3 ceramics, and the density of ceramics prepared in aqueous media is significantly greater than that in dry media, with a density of 94.33% after being held in aqueous media for 80 min. This divergence highlights the critical role of water as a transient liquid phase, even for water-insoluble Li2TiO3. We attribute this to water-enabled interfacial lubrication and capillary-driven particle rearrangement, which mitigate intergranular friction and activate pressure-assisted viscous flow—mechanisms absent in dry environments. The Vickers hardness (Fig. 11(c)) is linearly related to the densification level, and the Vickers hardness reaches a peak of 905.0 HV in water-mediated ceramics with a relative density of 94.33%, which is significantly greater than the hardness of dry medium sintered ceramics (429.8 HV). The poor performance of ceramics obtained in dry media further emphasizes that the mobility of nanoscale particles is insufficient to overcome the inherent densification bottleneck in the absence of TLP. In summary, although water does not dissolve Li2TiO3, after the raw material powders are refined to the nanoscale, water can serve as a key densification medium, enabling CSP to break through the intrinsic sintering barrier of water-insoluble oxides.

### 3.4 Densification mechanism

Considering the intrinsic insolubility of Li2TiO3, this study achieved cold sintering densification with water as the TLP, which revealed a unique densification mechanism different from that of the traditional dissolution‒precipitation mode. The observed densification behavior combines dislocation-mediated plastic deformation with nanoscale localized dissolution phenomena. This synergistic effect overcomes the solubility limitation and enables water to serve as an effective TLP for the densification of non-water-soluble ceramics. Initially, the densification process begins with transient liquid phase lubrication and particle rearrangement. Under high pressure, deionized water enters a high-density compressed state, infiltrating interparticle gaps via capillary forces. This reduces the effective friction coefficient between particles, facilitating the sliding and rotation of nanoparticles to achieve preliminary close packing. Compared with their micron-sized counterparts, nanosized Li2TiO3 particles demonstrate superior rearrangement capabilities because of their significantly reduced contact areas and higher surface energies, which minimize interparticle friction.

As densification progresses, deformation mechanisms become increasingly important. It is generally recognized that abundant grain boundaries in nanoceramics typically impede dislocation slip rather than facilitating it. However, in this study, the intrinsic layered crystal structure of β-Li2TiO3 exhibited significant anisotropic characteristics, with interlayer bonding substantially weaker than intralayer bonding, facilitating the formation of preferential slip systems along the basal planes. Under high pressure, this structural anisotropy enables dislocations to slip preferentially along the interlayer interfaces. Additionally, nanoscale effects further enhance this process: the stress concentration at nanoparticle contact points is significantly amplified under high pressure, generating local stresses potentially far exceeding the macroscopically applied pressure, thus providing a sufficient driving force for dislocation nucleation and slip. These mechanisms collectively enhance plastic deformation, which effectively fills interparticle voids and accelerates densification. In the final stage of densification, the synergistic effect of contact stress concentration and nanoscale phenomena facilitates localized dissolution at Li2TiO3 grain interfaces. The dissolved ionic species subsequently reprecipitate along the grain boundaries to form an amorphous transition layer. This amorphous phase exhibits glass-like viscous behavior, filling residual pores through stress-driven viscous flow, complementing dislocation-mediated densification and yielding a highly dense ceramic.

### 3.5 Comparison of CSP and various sintering methods

To demonstrate the superior efficiency of the cold sintering process over conventional sintering (CS), we conducted a comparative study using identical Li2TiO3 raw material powders. For CS, the powders were dry-pressed and sintered in air at 1000 °C for 4 h, while CSP was performed at 300 °C. As summarized in Table 1, CSP achieved a comparable density to that of CS despite the significantly lower processing temperature. Furthermore, microstructural analysis revealed that CS led to substantial grain coarsening, with an average grain size of 1.3 μm, which directly influenced the resulting mechanical properties. While CSP-processed ceramics exhibited superior Vickers hardness attributed to their refined microstructure, nanoindentation measurements revealed a lower elastic modulus (114.1 GPa) than that of the CS samples (140.0 GPa). This difference in mechanical properties is consistent with previous reports in Ref. [42]. That is, fine-grained ceramics often demonstrate increased hardness, but the elastic modulus is reduced due to the increased grain boundary density. These findings illustrate that CSP offers significant energy savings through reduced processing temperatures while producing Li2TiO3 ceramics with distinctive mechanical characteristics that may be advantageous for specific applications requiring increased hardness at the expense of some elastic stiffness.

Both samples exhibit two capacitive arcs, which are closely related to their internal structure and composition. The semicircular area in the high-frequency band is generally considered to be caused by the conduction of body charges, whereas the arc in the low-frequency band is considered to be caused by the conduction of surface charges (polar charges) [43]. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data were fitted via the equivalent circuit shown in Fig. 15(b) via ZSimDemo software, and the results are shown in Table 2. Here, *R*s represents the solution resistance, *R*1 represents the bulk resistance, *R*2 represents the charge transfer resistance, *C*1 represents the double-layer capacitance at the sample‒solution interface, and *C*2 represents the capacitance associated with the sample response. The parameter *n* is correlated with surface conditions, where a higher *n* value indicates increased surface roughness. CSP-LT exhibited lower bulk and charge transfer resistance than did CS-LT, indicating enhanced charge exchange capability and thus superior conductivity. Conventionally, grain boundaries impede charge transfer, and CSP-LT with more abundant grain boundaries should theoretically have a higher impedance. For further illustration, Figs. 15(c) and 15(d) show the Bode plots of the impedance‒frequency and capacitance‒frequency relationships for both samples. Generally, increased impedance manifests in two ways: (1) higher impedance modulus (|*Z*|) and phase angle in the low-frequency region and (2) broader phase angle width in the medium-frequency region. On the basis of these standards, it is once again proven that CSP-LT has a lower impedance. We tentatively attribute this phenomenon to a possible qualitative change in the grain boundary properties when the grain size decreases below a critical value, where the grain boundaries in nanocrystalline materials may form continuous fast transport channels, or the defect states at the grain boundaries may create new conduction pathways, thereby increasing the electrical conductivity of the materials [44]. In tritium breeding applications, the enhanced ionic conductivity facilitates rapid tritium release and transport, improving operational efficiency. Additionally, when employed as solid electrolyte materials, improved lithium-ion conductivity promotes enhanced charge transfer kinetics, directly contributing to superior electrochemical performance in all-solid-state batteries and other energy storage systems [45,46].

Figure 15(e) shows the CV curves of Li2TiO3 prepared by the two different sintering methods, with all the curves exhibiting distinct anodic and cathodic peaks, confirming the occurrence of redox reactions during the electrochemical processes. Notably, CSP-LT demonstrated higher absolute current density values than did CS-LT across most potential ranges, indicating enhanced redox capabilities. This improved electrochemical activity can be attributed to increased surface energy and abundant active sites resulting from grain refinement, enabling the CSP-LT samples to participate more rapidly in electrochemical reactions under identical initial potential conditions. As the potential gradually increases, both samples exhibit similar current density evolution patterns: an initial rise, followed by a decrease, and a subsequent increase, suggesting that multistage electrochemical reactions occur within the scanned potential window. Specifically, CS-LT displays a relatively small peak at approximately 0 V and begins to rise again at approximately 0.2 V. This phenomenon further confirms that the nanoceramic CSP-LT possesses superior electrochemical reactivity, thus showing enhanced electrochemical response performance.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, water was successfully demonstrated as an effective TLP for cold sintering of water-insoluble Li2TiO3 ceramics by leveraging nanoscale effects. Through systematic optimization of the processing parameters, dense Li2TiO3 nanoceramics (94.33% relative density) were achieved at 300 °C under 700 MPa pressure, with grain sizes maintained at ~26 nm. High-resolution TEM analysis revealed a unique densification mechanism combining dislocation-mediated plastic deformation with localized dissolution at nanoparticle interfaces. Compared with their conventionally sintered counterparts, the resulting nanoceramics exhibited superior mechanical properties (905 HV) and enhanced ionic conductivity.

The key breakthrough lies in overcoming the solubility limitation of conventional CSP through nanoscale effects. The dramatically increased surface energy of the nanoparticles, combined with the stress concentration at interparticle contact points, enables localized dissolution and plastic deformation despite the material’s bulk insolubility. This work establishes a water-mediated cold sintering strategy for water-insoluble ceramics, significantly broadening the applicability of CSP technology and enabling nanoceramics with enhanced functionalities. While demonstrated effectively in Li2TiO3 systems, further validation in other water-insoluble systems is needed to confirm the universality of this strategy.
