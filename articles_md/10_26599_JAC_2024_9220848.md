## 1 Introduction

Phillips–van Vechten–Levine (P–V–L) theory has been widely used in the field of microwave dielectric ceramics since Xia *et al.* [1] evaluated microwave dielectric properties by chemical bond properties in 2012. In 2023, Zhou *et al.* [2] found that Gd–O bonds in Gd2Mo3O12 ceramics dominate their microwave dielectric properties based on the P–V–L theory. In the same year, Wang *et al.* [3] obtained that the ionicity, lattice energy, and bonding energy of chemical bonds in MnMoO4 ceramics were dominated by Mo–O bonding, which had a greater influence on the microwave dielectric properties compared to the Mn–O bonding. In 2022, Wu *et al*. [4] concluded that the Ti–O and Nb–O bonds in Ba3Ti4Nb4O21 ceramics altogether played a significant role in microwave dielectric properties. However, previous reports mainly focused either on the calculation of chemical bond properties using the P–V–L theory or the discussion of relationships between the microwave dielectric properties and the chemical bonds, and there were few investigations on the change mechanism of chemical bond properties.

In addition, CeO2 ceramics with a cubic fluorite structure have high stability and are good matrix materials for high entropy materials [5,6]. In 2017, Djenadic *et al.* [5] successfully synthesized entropy-stabilized oxide containing up to seven rare earth elements in a single-phase CaF2-type (*Fm** 3¯m*) structure, and found that CeO2 played a crucial role in stabilizing the multicomponent rare earth oxide. In the field of microwave dielectric ceramics, the CeO2 ceramics are also extensively studied due to their structural stability. In 2020, Wang *et al*. [7] studied the microwave dielectric properties of the CeO2 solid solution with light rare earth oxides. In 2021, Wang *et al*. [8] investigated the structure and microwave dielectric properties of CeO2-type ceramics with a cubic fluorite structure using the solid solution method. In 2023, Wang *et al*. [9] utilized four light rare earth ions to substitute Ce4+ ions in CeO2 and investigated the microwave dielectric properties. Obviously, the structure of CeO2 has high stability for different substitution ions, but there is no quantitative parameter to characterize the stability of the CeO2-type cubic fluorite structure, which limits the in-depth analysis of its structure and properties.

To investigate the problems mentioned above and consider the small difference (1.5%) of ionic radius between Yb3+ ions (0.985 Å) and Ce4+ ions (0.97 Å) [10], the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0–0.8) ceramics were designed and successfully prepared in this paper. The bond ionicity and lattice energy were calculated to evaluate the stability of the CeO2-type cubic fluorite structure. The change mechanisms of bond ionicity and lattice energy were investigated in detail both experimentally by oxygen vacancy and theoretically by electronic localization function through first principles. Moreover, the microwave dielectric properties of the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0–0.8) ceramics were also investigated by phase composition, bond ionicity, lattice energy, grain growth, and bond valence.

## 2 Experimental

The Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0–0.8) ceramics were synthesized through a traditional solid-state reaction route using raw materials: Yb2O3 (99.99%) and CeO2 (99.99%). The powder was weighed in the required stoichiometric ratio, mixed, and ball-milled in deionized water for 6 h. The dried mixes were calcined at 1200 ℃ for 4 h after drying at 100 ℃. The sintered powders were re-milled for 12 h, and then repeated the steps of drying, crushing, and sieving. The final powders were pressed into a cylinder with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 5 mm. The cylindrical samples were sintered at 1500–1650 ℃ for 4 h with a heating rate of 5 ℃/min.

The phase composition was examined via X-ray diffraction (XRD) with Cu Kα radiation. The microstructure was observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The content of oxygen vacancies and different valence ions in ceramics was characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Also, the microwave dielectric properties of the sintered ceramics were conducted by Hakki–Coleman method using a microwave network analyzer (N5234A, Agilent Co., USA) [11,12]. The temperature coefficients of resonant frequency were calculated at 25–85 ℃ in  TE01δ mode by Eq. (1) [13,14]:

(1) τf=1f(T0)f(T1)−f(T0)T1−T0

where  f(T1) and  f(T0) represent the resonant frequency at  T1 (85 ℃) and  T0 (25 ℃), respectively.

The crystal structure model of CeO2 was established based on the XRD refinement results, as shown in Fig. 1(a). After the Yb3+ substitution, Yb3+ ions occupy Ce4+ ion site in the CeO2 structure, and the crystal structure model of Yb0.25Ce0.75O1.875 under complete disorder was calculated using cluster unfolding method, Monte Carlo method, and special quasi-stochastic structure method in ATAT software package [15,16], as shown in Fig. 1(b). The electronic properties were calculated by means of density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) [17] with the projector augmented-wave method [18]. Their electronic exchange and correlation energy were accounted for through the generalized gradient approximation of Perdew, Bruke, and Ernzerhof (PBE) [19]. The energy cutoffs for the plane-wave expansion are 500 eV.

According to the refinement data of the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0–0.8) ceramics, the cell volume of the CeO2-type phase and the content of the two phases (CeO2-type phase and Yb2O3 phase) were calculated and shown in Fig. 3. With the doping amount of Yb3+ ions increases from 0 to 0.5, the cell volume of the CeO2 type-phase decreases, owing to the changes in the effective valence electron charge and bond ionicity (see Fig. 3). When the Yb2O3 phase appears (*x* ≥ 0.5), the cell volume of the CeO2-type phase does not change significantly as the increase of *x*, indicating that the Yb3+ ion reaches its maximum limit solution in CeO2 (which is between *x* = 0.4 and *x* = 0.5). Based on the data of the phase composition at *x* = 0.5, the solubility limit of the Yb3+ ion in the CeO2 structure is determined to be *x* = 0.47. Obviously, when *x* > 0.47 in the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* ceramics, the Yb2O3 phase appears and continues to increase as the *x* value increases, which becomes the main crystalline phase of the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0–0.8) ceramics when *x* = 0.7.

To further investigate the changes of bond ionicity and lattice energy, O 1s and Ce 3d of the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0, 0.3) ceramics were characterized by XPS as shown in Fig. 5. AO and ACe3+, which are calculated by the average relative area of each component, are defined as the content of oxygen vacancies and Ce3+ ions, respectively. According to Figs. 5(a) and 5(b), the main peak at 529 eV is assigned to the excited electron of oxygen ions binding with the closest metallic ions (Ce–O) in the crystal lattice; the site at 530 eV is derived from the electrons knocked from ambient oxygen ions of oxygen vacancies; the peak at 532 eV is originated from the excited electrons from oxygen ions of oxygen in hydroxide (–OH), caused by H2O in the atmosphere that attaches to the surface of the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* ceramics [23]. Evidently, oxygen vacancies occur in the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0, 0.3) ceramics (AO ≠ 0), and the content of oxygen vacancies in the ceramics increased when Yb3+ ions replace Ce4+ ions (AO–Yb > AO), which is attributed to the difference in valence states between Ce4+ and Yb3+ ions. The bonding energy for Ce4+ ions in the 882, 888, 898, 901, 907, and 916 eV energy regions and Ce3+ ions in the 881, 885, 897, and 903 eV energy regions is shown in Figs. 5(c) and 5(d) [24]. It is noted that ACe3+ increases from 37.77% to 42.32% when the *x*-value changes from 0 to 0.3, which suggests that the increase in oxygen vacancies leads to a decrease in the total amount of Ce3+ ions oxidized by oxygen ions. The increase in the content of Ce3+ ions reduces the charge transfer between Ce ions and O ions (corresponding to the change in the charge of the effective valence electron), leading to a decrease in the ionicity of Ce/Yb–O bonds. Therefore, it is reasonably hypothesized that the oxygen vacancies are to be responsible for the variation of ionicity and lattice energy in the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* ceramics.

To verify the reasonableness of the above speculations, the electronic local distribution of the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* ceramics was investigated. The electronic local distribution can be characterized by the electron localization function (ELF), which can be also used to confirm the bond types. ELF is calculated by Eq. (7) [25]:

(7) ELF=1/[1+(D(r)Dhr)2]

where *D*(r) is the real gas density; *D*hr is the uniform electron gas density. ELF values are between 0 and 1. ELF = 1 represents the complete electron localization in the region; ELF = 1/2 indicates a uniform electron gas state; ELF = 0 refers to the complete electron delocalization in the region [26]. The ELF values of the (101) plane for the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0, 0.25) ceramics are shown in Fig. 6. It can be observed that the ELF values between Ce atoms and O atoms are in the region of 0.2–0.4, which indicates that the main bonding type in the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0, 0.25) ceramics is ionic bonds. Importantly, after the appearance of oxygen vacancies, the ELF values (ELF ≈ 0.33) between the Ce atoms and the O atoms are significantly larger than that (ELF ≈ 0.23) with no oxygen vacancy, which demonstrates that the presence of oxygen vacancies enhances the covalency of the Ce–O bonds. These results above are cross-corroborated with the analysis of the P–V–L theory and XPS measurement. To sum up, when Ce4+ ions are replaced by Yb3+ ions, oxygen vacancies in the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* ceramics increase, giving rise to the Ce3+ ions increase, which leads to the electron transfer between the Ce/Yb atoms and the O atoms change. Also, continually, the effective valence electron charge of O atoms decreases, which finally contributes to the decrease in ionicity and lattice energy.

In addition, to analyze the non-intrinsic factors of microwave dielectric properties, the SEM micrographs and the corresponding grain size distribution of the Ybx*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0–0.8) ceramics were investigated, as shown in Fig. 7. As shown in Figs. 7(a) and 7(j), it is obvious that the grain size of CeO2 ceramics is large, up to 15.90 μm. However, the grain distribution uniformity of the CeO2 ceramics is poor, with the standard deviation being 7.80. When Ce4+ ions are partially replaced by Yb3+ ions, the grain size of the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0.1–0.8) ceramics decreases, and the grain distribution uniformity is greatly optimized. As shown in Figs. 7 (b)–7(e), the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* ceramics are the single phase (see Fig. 2), and the grains appear smooth overall with distinct grain boundaries. When the *x* value is 0.1–0.4, the average grain size increases from 2.67 to 5.14 μm, and the standard deviation increases slightly, changing from 1.33 to 2.29. With the further substitution of Yb3+ ions, the second phase is detected in the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* ceramics. At this stage, the grain surface starts to be rough, and the grain size decreases significantly, indicating that the grain boundaries increase. Moreover, pores can be observed when the *x* value increases to 0.7 and 0.8, which manifests that the relative density of the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* ceramics decreases. As a result, the Yb3+ ion substitution can optimize the grain growth of the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0–0.4) ceramics, but when the second phase appears (0.5 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.8), the grain growth will be inhibited, and the relative density will decrease.

In the single-phase region (0 *x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* ceramics increase significantly after the Yb3+ ion substitution, mainly because the uniformity of grain size distribution is greatly optimized (see Figs. 7(a)–7(e)). ## 4 Conclusions

Novel high-performance Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0–0.8) microwave dielectric ceramics were successfully designed and prepared via a solid-state reaction method. According to P–V–L theory, when the Yb3+ ions replace Ce4+ ions, the effective valence electron charges of oxygen ions in CeO2 ceramics decrease, which leads to the weakening of Ce/Yb–O bond ionicity and lattice energy, and then finally results in the deterioration of structural stability in a cubic fluorite structure. Consequently, the minimum lattice energy for a CeO2-type cubic fluorite structure to maintain stability is 4142 kJ/mol, corresponding to the maximum solution limit of Yb3+ ions in CeO2 as 0.47. The XPS tests were performed to analyze the cause of changes in the effective valence electron charge, which indicates that the increase of oxygen vacancies with increasing substitution of Yb3+ ions lead to the increase of Ce3+ ions, giving rise to the change of effective valence electron. ELF calculated via the first principles also proves that the change of charge transfer caused by oxygen vacancy is the cause of the change in chemical bond properties. Another advantage of replacing Ce4+ ions by Yb3+ ions is that the grain distribution of Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0–0.8) is significantly optimized. For microwave dielectric properties of the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* (*x* = 0–0.8) ceramics, in a single-phase area (*x* = 0–0.4), the relative permittivity is mainly influenced by bond ionicity, the *Q*×*f* values are determined by the grain distribution and lattice energy, the *τ**f* values are affected by bond valence. In a multi-phase area (*x* = 0.5–0.8), the relative permittivity, *τ**f* values, and *Q*×*f* values are mainly determined by phase compositions and relative density. When *x* = 0.1, the Yb*x*Ce1−*x*O2−0.5*x* ceramics exhibit excellent microwave dielectric properties (*ε*r = 25.15, *Q*×*f* = 97,828 GHz, *τ**f* = −37.44 ppm/℃).
