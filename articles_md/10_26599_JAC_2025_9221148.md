## 1 Introduction

Scintillator materials have been widely used in various applications where the detection of ionizing radiation is needed. Various novel scintillators have been discovered and studied in depth within the last two decades [1−4]. Among these scintillators, Ce3+-doped Lu3Al5O12 (LuAG) single crystals have attracted much attention because of their high stopping power, high light output (~26,000 ph/MeV), and short decay time (55–65 ns) [5−8]. However, the low segregation coefficient of Ce3+ ions in the LuAG lattice and the presence of antisite defects (ADs) can severely decrease the homogeneity [9] and scintillation performance [10] of single crystals. Moreover, advanced optical ceramic preparation technology has been rapidly developed [11−21]. Compared with single crystals, the ceramic preparation process does not need to melt the raw material, which means that the lower sintering temperature results in a lower concentration of intrinsic defects such as AD [22,23] and that the dopants are distributed homogeneously without a segregation process. In addition, LuAG:Ce ceramics show excellent radiation hardness against both γ-rays [24] and hadrons [25], making them promising candidates for use as scintillators in future high-energy physics (HEP) experiments with harsh radiation environments, such as high-luminosity large hadron colliders (HL-LHCs) and future circular colliders (FCC-*hh*).

One crucial issue of LuAG:Ce ceramics and single crystals, however, is the slow scintillation component of ~1 µs decay time caused by intrinsic defects, especially AD, which act as shallow traps, thus delaying the energy transport towards Ce3+ emission centers [10,26]. The suppression of the slow component is of great importance to achieve LuAG:Ce scintillators with high performance. Strategies such as “bandgap engineering” and “defect engineering” have been proposed. The multicomponent garnets Gd3(Ga,Al)5O12 (GGAG:Ce), with high light yields (LYs) reaching 70,000 ph/MeV, were developed via the “band-gap engineering” strategy [27,28]. However, a slow component still exists in these high-LY scintillators. On the other hand, “defect engineering” was proposed by codoping alkaline-earth metal ions, such as Mg2+ and Ca2+, to introduce stable Ce4+ ions into LuAG:Ce [7,14,29,30], which has a beneficial effect on slow component suppression. In the optimized Mg2+ co-doped LuAG:Ce ceramics, the fast component of scintillation decay constituted approximately 60% of the total intensity [31]. In a later work, the fast-total ratio (F/T), defined as the ratio between an LY of 200 ns and an LY of 3000 ns, reached 74% for Mg2+ co-doped samples and 90% for the Ca2+-co-doped samples [24]. Recently, we found that an F/T ratio, defined as the ratio between LY of 500 ns and LY of 3000 ns, of 97% can be achieved by Ca2+ co-doping, but in sacrifice of the light yield of the scintillator [32]. Previous studies have demonstrated that, for LuAG:Ce ceramics, Mg2+ co-doping maintains the LY but can only result in a modest F/T ratio [14,31], whereas Ca2+ codoping can result in a high F/T ratio but at the expense of the LY [7,15,30,32−34]. In short, previous works did not show Mg2+ co-doped LuAG:Ce ceramics with a high F/T ratio. In addition, the reason for the different behavior between Mg2+ and Ca2+ co-doping is unclear. Recently, the proposed model of {Ce3+–Mg2−-} pairs and {Ce3+–Ca2−} pairs [35−39] may explain the difference between Mg2+ and Ca2+ co-doping in LuAG:Ce ceramics.

In this work, Mg2+ co-doped LuAG:Ce ceramics were carefully designed. To obtain a high F/T ratio, we increased the Mg/Ce ratio to 3 : 1, which is the highest value ever reported. The optical and scintillation performances of the samples were investigated. Low-temperature thermoluminescence (TSL) was also studied to understand the effect of slow component suppression. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations on {Ce3+–Mg2−} pairs were performed in comparison with those on {Ce3+–Ca2−} pairs. The discussion includes the results of material optimization and future work concerning various oxide scintillators.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

High-purity commercial oxides CeO2 (99.99%), Lu2O3 (99.995%), α-Al2O3 (99.99%), and MgO (99.998%) were used as the raw powders. The raw powders were weighed according to the formula (Lu0.999−*x*Ce0.001Mg*x*)3Al5O12 (LuAG:Ce,Mg, *x* = 0, 0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.3 at%). The LuAG:Ce,Mg ceramics were fabricated by the solid-state reaction method. The ceramics were successfully fabricated by vacuum pre-sintering at 1825 °C for 5 h combined with hot-isostatic pressing (HIP) post-treatment at 1700 °C for 3 h under an Ar atmosphere with a pressure of 176 MPa. The ceramic samples were further subjected to a high-temperature (1450 °C×10 h) air-annealing process to remove the oxygen vacancies. Round ceramic plates with dimensions of *ϕ*14.4 mm × 1 mm were cut and polished for subsequent characterization.

### 2.2 Sample characterization

Phase identification was identified by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance PC, Bruker, German) using Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 0.15406 nm) with a scanning speed of 5 (°)/min in the 2*θ* range of 10°–80°. The in-line transmittance and absorption spectra were characterized with an ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared (UV–VIS–NIR) spectrophotometer (Cary-5000, Varian, USA). The microstructure was characterized by using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; S-8220, Hitachi, Japan). The ceramic samples were thermally etched in a muffle furnace at 1450 °C for 3 h to expose the grain boundaries. The average grain sizes of the ceramic samples were determined by the linear intercept method. The photoluminescence (PL) decays of 535 nm emission were measured under excitation at 452 nm (nanoLED) using a 5000 M Horiba Jobin Yvon fluorometer (UK) equipped with a TBX-04 photon counting detector. X-ray excited luminescence (XEL) spectra were recorded by a homemade XEL spectrometer using an F30III-2 X-ray tube (W anode; operated at 75 kV/1.3 mA) coupled to an Ocean Optics QE65000 spectrometer (USA). For the TSL study, ceramics were irradiated by the same X-ray tube at 77 K for 20 min. The measurement temperature was controlled by a cryostat Optistat DN-V2 (Oxford Instruments, UK) with a temperature range from 77 to 500 K. The samples were glued to a copper sample holder. A heating rate of 0.1 K/s was adopted. The wavelength-resolved TSL measurements were collected with the same spectrometer used for the XEL measurements. Scintillation decays were recorded with a U7600 fast photomultiplier (Hamamatsu, Japan) coupled to a TDS3052 digital oscilloscope (Tektronix, USA) under excitation of a 137Cs γ-ray source. The LY and energy resolution (ER) under 662 keV γ-ray excitation across varying shaping time were measured with a hybrid photomultiplier (HPMT; Delft Electronic Products (DEP), the Netherlands) PPO 475B connected to a multichannel analyzer for pulse-height spectra acquisition. By applying a thin layer of silicon grease for optical coupling, the samples were attached to the HPMT window and fully wrapped with Teflon tape to increase the light collection efficiency.

### 2.3 Calculation procedure

First-principles calculations on LuAG were performed by using the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) code [40] with the projector augmented-wave method [41,42] and a cell model consisting of 160 atoms. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) [43] generalized gradient approximation was used as the exchange and correlation functionals. Convergence of the plane wave basis set energy cutoff (550 eV) and *k*-point sampling (2×2×2 *k*-point meshes of the Monkhorst–Pack type) [44] were implemented. Lu (5p65d16s2), Al (3s23p1), O (2s22p4), Ce (5s25p64f15d16s2), Mg (3s2), and Ca (3p64s2) electrons were described as valence electrons. All geometry optimizations were performed via a conjugate gradient method with a force tolerance of 0.01 eV/Å. For better treatment of the 4f states of Ce, PBE+U calculations were adopted with *U* = 7 eV [45]. The calculated lattice constant of LuAG is 11.972 Å, which is very close to the experimental value of 11.906 Å [46].

The FESEM micrographs of the LuAG:Ce,Mg ceramics with various Mg co-doping concentrations are shown in Fig. 4. Residual pores can be observed in all the ceramics with different Mg co-doping concentrations. No secondary phase was detected in any of the samples. The average grain sizes of LuAG:Ce,Mg ceramics with different Mg2+ concentrations of 0, 0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.3 at% are 4.5, 3.9, 3.3, 3.1, 2.9, and 5.1 µm, respectively. With increasing Mg2+ co-doping concentration, the grain size initially decreases but then increases, which is in accordance with the observations of Mg2+-doped Y3Al5O12 (YAG) ceramics [48]. The co-doping of Mg2+ acts as an effective grain growth inhibitor in LuAG ceramics, which decreases the average grain size and eliminates abnormal grains. However, a further increase in the Mg2+ co-doping concentration leads to an increase in the average grain size [48]. Excessive Mg2+ co-doping largely decreases the formation energy of oxygen vacancies [49]. According to Wagner theory, the diffusion coefficient of oxygen is enhanced, and grain size growth is promoted.

A two-exponential function rather than a single exponential function was used because, unlike the photoluminescence decay curves, which involve only the luminescence process, the scintillation decay curves also involve delayed emission due to the transport process. In particular, intrinsic defects, such as ADs, act as shallow traps and can delay energy transport to Ce3+ emission centers [10,26]. In addition, the slow component could be partly attributed to Gd3+ impurities, as shown in Fig. 7. As shown in Fig. 6, the intensity *I*1 increases from 36.0% to 88.5% with increasing Mg2+ co-doping level. Moreover, the fast component decay time decreases from 73.4 to 56.3 ns with increasing Mg2+ content, which can be attributed to faster delivery of electrons to Ce3+ centers. In addition, the slow scintillation decay time also becomes shorter with increasing Mg2+ content, mainly because of the suppression of the slow scintillation component.

Interestingly, when the Mg2+ doping level reaches 0.3 at%, an F/T ratio as high as 99.8% can be achieved. Although the XEL intensity decreases dramatically with increasing Mg2+ content, the LY at shaping time of 0.5 µs increases slightly and then decreases slightly. The XEL spectra were measured in direct current mode with the slow scintillation component included, while the LY was measured at fast shaping time, which included only fast scintillation light. This indicates that the decrease in the XEL intensity is caused mainly by the suppression of the slow component. The variation trend of LY at shaping time of 0.5 µs with increasing Mg2+ content can be attributed to the competition between the positive effect of Mg2+ co-doping by introducing Ce4+ and the negative effect of introducing the {Ce3+–Mg2−} pairs (see below for detailed discussion). When the Mg2+ content is low, the {Ce3+–Mg2−} pairs are difficult to form because of the large average Ce and Mg ion distance, so the negative effect of the {Ce3+–Mg2−} pairs is insignificant. When the Mg2+ content is high, the negative effect of the {Ce3+–Mg2−} pairs increases, resulting in a decrease in the LY. In addition, unlike what we observed in the Ca2+ co-doped samples [32], the LY of the materials at shaping time of 0.5 µs slightly decreases (~6%) compared with that of the undoped sample. In comparison, the Ca2+ co-doped samples show a 43% drop in LY at shaping time of 0.5 µs, resulting in an F/T ratio of 97.4% [32].

The ER presented here is worse than the values reported in Ref. [51], where an ER of 12% was obtained for LuAG:Ce ceramics. It is well known that it is hard to mix the dopants, such as Ce and Mg, homogeneously via the solid-state reaction method. Powders obtained from the wet-chemical routes, such as flame spray pyrolysis [51] and co-precipitation [33], have the advantage of mixing at the atomic level. In our future work, LuAG:Ce,Mg ceramics will be fabricated with powders prepared by the co-precipitation method to address this issue.

### 3.2 Calculation results

To understand why only a slight loss of LY is observed in Mg2+ co-doped ceramic samples, we perform DFT calculations on the excited states of LuAG:Ce, LuAG:Ce,Mg, and LuAG:Ce,Ca. Figure 10 shows the charge density difference between the excited state and ground state of these three materials. To simplify the calculation, both co-dopants replace the nearest Lu sites around the luminescence center Ce3+. Interestingly, for LuAG:Ce and LuAG:Ce,Mg, the charge density variation is localized around the Ce ions, which indicates efficient Ce emission [52]. However, for the Ca2+ co-doped LuAG:Ce, the charge density delocalizes to nearby oxygen ions, indicating Ce emission quenching. The {Ce3+–Mg2−} pair-related emission quenching has been extensively reported in multicomponent garnet scintillators [35−39]. Unlike LuAG, where Mg2+ ions mainly replace Lu3+ sites, in multicomponent garnet scintillators, such as GGAG:Ce, Mg2+ ions (with ionic radii of 0.57 and 0.72 Å for coordination numbers (CNs) of 4 and 6) can replace Ga3+ sites (with ionic radii of 0.47 and 0.62 Å for CNs of 4 and 6) because of their similar ionic radii, which may also cause emission quenching.

*
Fig. 10Charge density difference between excited state and ground state of (a) LuAG:Ce, (b) LuAG:Ce,Mg, and (c) LuAG:Ce,Ca. Electrons are depleted in the blue region while in yellow region electrons accumulate.

To verify this assumption, we perform DFT calculations on the excited state of LuAG:Ce,Mg with Mg2+ replacing two Al3+ sites (Al3+ 16a site with a CN of 6 and Al3+ 24d site with a CN of 4). Figure 11 shows the charge density difference between the excited state and ground state of these two types of LuAG:Ce,Mg. Notably, when Mg2+ replaces the Al3+ 16a site, the charge density difference is located around Ce ions, similar to the result of Mg2+ replacing Lu3+ site. However, when Mg2+ replaces the Al3+ 24d site, the charge density difference delocalizes to oxygen ions, similar to the result of Ca2+ replacing Lu3+ site. The emission quenching during the process of Mg2+ replacing Al3+ 24d site may explain the result observed in the multicomponent garnet. This finding also proves that for Mg2+ co-doped LuAG:Ce, emission quenching is unlikely to occur since Mg2+ (0.57 Å) can hardly replace Al3+ 24d sites (0.39 Å) because of the mismatched ionic radius.

Alkaline-earth metal ions co-doping is a strategy that has been extensively studied in recent years [7,14,29,30,53−56]. It is very efficient in improving the scintillation performance of various Ce-doped oxide scintillators, such as LuAG:Ce [31,32], GGAG:Ce [53−55], and (Lu,Y)2Si2O5:Ce (LYSO:Ce) [56]. It has long been considered that Mg2+ and Ca2+ co-doping in LuAG:Ce results in different behaviors, namely, that Mg2+ co-doping maintains the LY but can only result in a modest F/T ratio, whereas Ca2+ co-doping can result in a high F/T ratio but at the expense of the LY. Our work demonstrates that by using the advanced ceramic fabrication process, samples with high Mg2+ co-doping are achievable. These highly Mg2+ co-doped ceramics may have superior performance to their single-crystal counterparts. In particular, the LuAG:Ce,Mg ceramics have a high F/T ratio with a slight loss of LY even when the Mg/Ce ratio reaches 3 : 1. Table 2 shows a comparison between this study and previous studies on the F/T ratio of LuAG:Ce,Mg materials. The F/T ratio and τ*eff obtained in this study are the best ever reported. We plan to apply this strategy in other oxide ceramic scintillators, such as GGAG:Ce, to see whether we can obtain materials with high LYs, fast decay time, and high F/T ratios. For Mg2+ or Ca2+ heavily co-doped with GGAG:Ce, the main issue, however, is an obvious decrease in the light yield [37]. A possible method to avoid this issue is to decrease the Ce doping level while keeping the Mg/Ce ratio above 3 : 1. The trade-off between high light yield and short decay time should be considered.

In addition, in contrast to earlier findings, the difference between Mg2+ and Ca2+ co-doping lies mainly in the LY. As evidenced by the DFT calculations, Ca2+ co-doping can induce Ce3+ emission quenching, which will deteriorate LY. Therefore, a slight LY loss was observed in our Mg2+ co-doped samples, while a LY drop of 43% was observed for the Ca2+ co-doped samples [32].

## 4 Conclusions

Ceramic scintillators of LuAG:Ce,Mg with various Mg co-doping levels were designed and fabricated by the solid-state reaction method with vacuum sintering and HIP post-treatment. The influence of Mg2+ co-doping concentration on the optical and scintillation properties was investigated in detail. By carefully adjusting the Mg/Ca ratio to 3 : 1, the F/T ratio, defined as LY0.5µs/LY10µs, reaches 99.8%, with slight variation in the fast component intensity, indicating significant suppression of the slow component. The slow component suppression phenomenon was further confirmed by the TSL glow curve measurement, where all the TSL glow peaks disappear in the samples heavily co-doped with Mg2+. The slight decrease in the LY value was explained by DFT calculations focused on the {Ce3+–Mg2−} pairs.
