## 1 Introduction

Microwave dielectric ceramics are key materials required for signal propagation in passive devices [1,2]. In the context of the rapid advancement of millimeter wave technology and 5G/6G communication, current communication equipment must meet demanding criteria, including high frequency, low loss, high stability, and the need for miniaturization and integration [3]. There is an urgent need to further increase the *Q×f* value of dielectric ceramic materials through material design, particularly in terms of frequency selectivity and signal transmission accuracy [4−8]. This is crucial for producing components such as filters, oscillators, and antennas with superior and stable performance [9−13]. Owing to their unique structural distortions and compositional characteristics, double perovskite-structured materials exhibit excellent performance and remarkable potential in electronic materials such as ferroelectric devices and microwave resonators [14−17]. In our previous research, Y₂MgTiO₆ (YMT) ceramics sintered at 1450 °C exhibited microwave dielectric properties: relative permittivity (*ε*r) = 20.8±0.8, quality factor (*Q*×*f*) = 42,060±310 GHz, and temperature coefficient of resonant frequency (*τ*f) = −52±3 ppm/°C [25], which provides a foundation for further optimizing the dielectric performance. The principle of ion doping modification, which exploits the differences in ion radius, polarizability, electron distribution, and ion charge to adjust the microstructure and lattice vibration of crystals, is a common method for tuning the properties of microwave dielectric ceramics [26−28]. Research has shown that codoping Ba2GdMO6 (M = Nb and Ta) ceramics with 10 mol% Eu3+ and up to 50 mol% B3+ improves the crystallinity and dielectric properties, peaking at dielectric constants of 33.5 and 35.4 before decreasing due to structural strain, whereas higher B3+ concentrations reduce dielectric loss by suppressing oxygen vacancies [29]. Replacing La sites with Y3+ in LaZn1/2Ti1/2O3 significantly increased the *Q×f* value, and the solid solution exhibited complex long-range ordering and distortion behavior [30]. Our previous research indicated that the Y–O bond, as a strong ionic bond, plays a crucial role in lattice stability, and its parameter changes directly affect the *ε*r and *Q×f* values of YMT ceramics. Theoretically, ion doping substitution at the Y site can improve and enhance the dielectric properties.

The Phillips‒Van Vechten‒Levine (P–V–L) theory provides a fundamental framework for understanding the relationship between the characteristics of chemical bonds and the macroscopic dielectric properties of materials. By quantitatively analyzing key chemical bond parameters, this theory establishes correlations with material polarization, the dielectric constant, dielectric loss, and thermal stability. Specifically, the ionicity of chemical bonds directly influences a material’s ability to polarize, thereby determining its dielectric constant. Moreover, the bond energy and lattice energy collectively reflect the stability of the crystal structure, which significantly affects the dielectric loss [31−33]. The rare earth ions Nd3+ and Sm3+ were selected for the partial substitution of YMT in this study. YMT ceramics were successfully synthesized via the conventional solid-phase sintering method within the solubility ranges of *x* = 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, and 0.3. The effects of ion substitution on the phase composition, microstructure, and microwave dielectric properties of the ceramics were systematically investigated through X-ray diffraction (XRD) structural refinement combined with the P–V–L complex crystal chemical bond dielectric theory. Furthermore, the contribution of phonon vibration modes to dielectric loss was quantified via Raman spectroscopy and far-infrared reflection spectroscopy fitting. The results demonstrate that doping at the Y sites can effectively regulate the microwave dielectric properties, providing a theoretical foundation for material design and performance optimization.

## 2 Experimental

The raw materials are high-purity Y2O3 (AR, 1314-36-9, Macklin, China), TiO2 (AR, 12065-65-5, Macklin, China), Nd2O3 (AR, 1313-97-9, Macklin, China), Sm2O3 (AR, 12060-58-1, Macklin, China), and (MgCO3)4·Mg(OH)2·5H2O (AR, 39409-82-0, Xilong, China), which are mixed according to the molar ratio of Y2−*x*A*x*MgTiO6 (A = Nd, Sm; *x* = 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, and 0.3). The materials were combined with deionized water and zirconia balls at a mass ratio of 1 : 2 : 5 and then ball milled for 5 h. After drying in an oven, the mixture was sieved through a 60-mesh screen to achieve the desired powder fineness. The powder subsequently underwent presintering in an air atmosphere at temperatures ranging from 900 to 1250 °C with holding time of 4 h. Next, the powder, which has undergone preliminary chemical reactions, is subjected to secondary ball milling and then dried. A binder composed of 6 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) is used to granulate the powder, which is then pressed into cylinders of *ϕ*12 mm × 6 mm and discs of *ϕ*12 mm× 2 mm under a pressure of 20 MPa. The samples were sintered in a muffle furnace and initially held at 550 °C for 2 h to remove any residual binders. This was followed by heating at a rate of 3 °C/min to temperatures between 1400 and 1525 °C in an air atmosphere, with holding time of 4 h to complete the sintering process.

A vector network analyzer (E5071C, Agilent Technologies, USA) was used to assess the microwave dielectric properties of the samples. The microstructures and sintering densities of the samples were examined by a scanning electron microscope ( Pro X 6G, Phenom, the Netherlands). The volume density of the samples was determined via a density balance. Following the performance tests, the cylindrical samples were crushed into a powder for X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis by an X-ray diffractometer (X’Pert Pro MPD, PANalytical, the Netherlands) to characterize the composition of the samples and refine the unit cell parameters. Raman spectroscopy, which used a He‒Ne laser with a wavelength of 532 nm and covers a spectral range of 100–800 cm−1, was employed to analyze the lattice vibrations of the materials. Further insights into the crystal structure were obtained from the XRD results. To eliminate interference from diffuse reflection during the far-infrared reflection spectroscopy tests, the dense disc samples were polished to a mirror finish, with the light source provided by the Spectroscopy and Imaging Experimental Line Station of the National Synchrotron Radiation Laboratory at the University of Science and Technology of China.

### 3.2 Phase and crystal structure analysis of ion-doped Y<sub>2</sub>MgTiO<sub>6</sub> ceramics

The XRD patterns of the highest-performing YMT-Nd and YMT-Sm ceramics (*x* = 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, and 0.3) are presented in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b). With different doping contents, the XRD diffraction peaks of all the samples align with the peak positions of the pure-phase Y2MgTiO6 ceramics, which correspond precisely to the diffraction peak positions of the DyMgTiO6 standard card (ICDD#04-021-1637). The phase purity of all the samples indicates the formation of a Y2MgTiO6 solid solution upon doping with Nd and Sm ions. To further characterize the changes in the unit cell parameters, crystal structure refinement was conducted on ceramic samples with varying doping concentrations. Figures 3(a) and 3(b) compare the refinement patterns of the test spectra of YMT Nd doped at *x* = 0.1 and YMT Sm doped at *x* = 0.05. The results of the refined fitting match well with the tested XRD results, and the error lines after fitting are relatively flat.

### 3.3 Analysis of microwave dielectric properties of Y<sub>2</sub>MgTiO<sub>6</sub> ceramics modified by ionic implantation

P–V–L chemical bond theory is utilized to compute the properties of diverse chemical bonds within the doped system. According to the binary bond formula, only the values of *m* and *n* in the binary bond formula for Y–O required adjustment in comparison with those of the undoped system because the samples were Y-site-doped solid solutions. For varying doping amounts, the corresponding values of *m* and *n*, along with the oxidation states and effective valence electron counts, are tabulated in Table S3 in the ESM. On the basis of the calculation results, the relationships between the alterations in the *Q×f* values and the pertinent chemical bond properties of the YMT-Nd and YMT-Sm ceramics at their optimal sintering temperatures for different doping levels are shown in Fig. 4. The disparities in the dielectric properties of the ceramics stem from the dissimilar impacts of Nd and Sm ions upon substitution. When Nd ions are incorporated, the optimal *Q×f* value of 74,821 GHz is attained at *x* = 0.1; in contrast, for Sm ions, the dielectric constant *Q×f* value of the ceramic system reaches 67,068 GHz at *x* = 0.05. In Figs. 4(a) and 4(b), the introduction of dopant ions at the Y site triggers an overall increase in the ionicity of the Y–O bond under both doping circumstances, resulting in a similar variation as the measured *ε*r. This consistency indicates that the theoretical calculation model is reliable and that the ionicity of Y–O bonds is one of the key factors affecting the dielectric properties of materials. Tables S4 and S5 in the ESM present the ionicity values computed via P–V–L theory. With Nd ion doping, the ionicity of the Mg‒O bond decreases overall, whereas that of the Ti–O bond tends to increase. Conversely, with Sm ion doping, both the Mg–O and Ti–O bonds display an overall increase in ionicity. Figures 4(c) and 4(d) imply that the fluctuations in the total lattice energy of the doped crystal follow a trend analogous to that of the *Q×f* values. Moreover, the lattice energies and bond energies of the various chemical bonds are shown in Figs. 5 and 6. As the doping content increases, the lattice energy of the Y–O bond decreases, whereas that of the Nd/Sm–O bond progressively increases. Moreover, the trends in the lattice energy and bond energy of the Ti–O and Mg–O bonds remain consistent.

### 3.4 Lattice vibrational spectroscopy analysis of ion-doped Y<sub>2</sub>MgTiO<sub>6</sub> ceramics

Dielectric loss is mainly attributed to the vibrations of the lattice in light of intrinsic factors. Raman and far-infrared reflectance spectra are valid for analyzing the vibrational modes of crystals. The Raman spectra of the YMT-Nd and YMT-Sm ceramics at different doping levels are shown in Fig. 7. For the samples doped with Nd, the primary differences in peak position and shape compared with those of the undoped samples are observed in the wavenumber range of 600–750 cm−1. Within this range, two distinct peaks appear, possibly because the doped atoms enter the lattice to form a solid solution, which changes the lattice parameters and symmetry. The decrease in symmetry leads to the degeneracy of degenerate phonon modes, resulting in peak splitting or the appearance of new peaks [34−37]. In contrast, for the samples doped with Sm, the measured Raman peak shape and position are consistent with those of the undoped Raman spectra. The Raman results further identified the solid solution in the doped samples.

The far-infrared reflectance spectra of YMT-Nd (x* = 0.1) and YMT-Sm (*x* = 0.05), along with the fitted spectral lines, are compared in Fig. 8. In both sets of doped far-infrared reflectance spectra, the shapes and reflections of the two samples are similar, suggesting that the solid solution of YMgTiO6 is consistent with the results obtained from XRD and crystallographic refinement [38,39].

(2) ε″=2Nk

where N* is the refractive index, and *k* is the extinction coefficient. As plotted in Fig. 9, the calculated dielectric constant of the YMT-Nd ceramics aligns well with the real value, as well as the results from the terahertz transformation. In contrast, the measured dielectric constant of the YMT-Sm ceramics is slightly greater than the fitted values derived from the far-infrared reflectance spectrum, but it matches the results obtained from the terahertz time-domain spectrum. The contributions of each phonon vibrational mode to the dielectric constant and dielectric loss for the YMT-Nd (*x* = 0.1) and YMT-Sm (*x* = 0.05) ceramics, after fitting the far-infrared reflectance spectra, are listed in Table 4. The contribution percentages of each phonon mode to dielectric loss indicate that the top five vibrational modes in YMT-Nd ceramics account for as much as 93.87% of the dielectric loss, whereas the top five modes in YMT-Sm ceramics contribute 80.93%. This further highlights the significant role of low-frequency modes in the perovskite structure on the microwave dielectric properties.

## 4 Conclusions

This study investigated the effects of Nd and Sm ion doping on Y2MgTiO6 microwave dielectric ceramics. Solid solution formation was confirmed upon doping, with a linear increase in the unit cell volume, which is consistent with ionic radius variations. The optimally sintered YMT-Nd and YMT-Sm ceramics achieved peak *Q×f* values of 74,821 GHz (*x* = 0.1) and 67,068 GHz (*x* = 0.05), respectively. Raman and far-infrared analyses revealed that low-wavenumber phonon modes dominated the dielectric loss contributions (93.87% and 80.93%), aligning with the P–V–L theory, which posits that increased lattice energy stabilizes atomic vibrations and reduces loss. This correlation underscores how rare-earth doping strengthens lattice rigidity, thereby suppressing phonon scattering and improving quality factors. The tailored dielectric properties position YMT-based ceramics as promising candidates for next-generation communication systems, particularly in low-loss 5G/6G resonators, filters, and antennas. Additionally, their stable lattice and tunable performance support applications in miniaturized microwave devices, enabling compact, energy-efficient designs.
