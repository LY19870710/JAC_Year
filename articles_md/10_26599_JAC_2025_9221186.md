## 1 Introduction

As far back as the Neolithic Age, our ancestors had already mastered the simple technique of using fire to manually sinter natural clay models into hard and durable ceramics [1−3]. Since then, ceramic sintering technologies have been continuously evolving. Among them, traditional thermal sintering (TTS) was the earliest proposed method and remains the most widely applied sintering technology. However, TTS has inherent drawbacks. TTS typically requires high sintering temperatures exceeding 1000 °C and prolonged processing times, which are not ideal for energy conservation and low carbon emissions [4,5]. In 2024, global CO2 emissions are expected to increase by one gigaton to reach a new record high of 41.6 gigatons. The ceramic industry, as an energy-intensive industry, has total CO2 emissions of 19 million tons annually in Europe, 83% of which result from fuel combustion and electricity production during sintering [6,7].

In recent decades, a series of innovative sintering technologies, such as liquid-phase sintering, hot press sintering, microwave sintering (MW), spark plasma sintering, and flash sintering, have promoted energy savings in ceramics [8−13]. Among them, MW has attracted attention since it was proposed by Garnault *et al*. [14] and Katz [15] in 1968 because of its advantages, such as low sintering temperature, fast heating rate, high efficiency, and ability to improve material properties, and it has been applied to a wide variety of ceramics. Microwave energy is widely used in daily life, such as in home cooking, wood drying, pharmaceutical product drying, and rubber vulcanization. During the microwave sintering process, samples in the form of powders can be densified by energy conversion rather than traditional thermal transmission, as the absorbed microwave energy is converted into the kinetic and thermal energies of internal molecules at relatively high conversion rates. Recently, the cold sintering process (CSP) has been extensively investigated and has the advantages of ultralow sintering temperatures and rapid densification rates, leading to the development of new ceramic-based composites and the ability to sinter metastable materials [16−19]. CSP has been demonstrated to have potential in the preparation of high-performance ceramics by simply utilizing a transient liquid phase and uniaxial pressure [20−22]. However, the external uniaxial pressure applied during the sintering stage may restrict its application. Thus, it is interesting to further develop a cold sintering process without externally applied pressures.

The dissolution‒precipitation process is common in natural phenomena, such as pearl formation, salt caking, and sedimentary rock formation, and serves as the fundamental mechanism in cold sintering. Inspired by the phenomena of microwave resonance and energy dissipation from microwave excitation coupled with the dissolution‒precipitation process, a microwave cold sintering process (MW-CSP) is proposed in this work [23−26]. A small amount of aqueous solution is utilized as the transient liquid phase to enhance the kinetics of dissolution and mass transport. In addition, the transient liquid phase resonates with the microwave signal to provide thermal energy inside the bulk samples. Moreover, under a microwave field, the polar molecules within the transient liquid phase resonate at the frequency of the applied field, effectively converting microwave energy into thermal energy. The heating efficiency of a dielectric material is determined by the imaginary part (* ε″) of the complex permittivity ( ε∗), according to Eqs. (1) and (2):

(1) ε∗=ε′−jε′′

(2) Qe=PdVs=2πfmwε″E2

where  ε∗,  ε′, and  ε″ represent the complex dielectric constant, the real part of the complex dielectric constant, and the imaginary part of the complex permittivity, respectively. Q*e represents the power density converted into heat, and *P*d represents the dissipated power. *V*s, *f*mw, and *E* represent the sample volume, microwave frequency, and electric field strength, respectively. The introduction of the transient liquid phase significantly enhances *ε*″, thereby improving the heating efficiency and enabling rapid densification at low temperatures. Thus, the combination of dissolution‒precipitation and microwave resonance is expected to markedly increase the sintering efficiency and reduce energy consumption.

Chlorides, oxides, phosphates, and molybdates are selected in this study to demonstrate the feasibility and potential of microwave cold sintering. Surprisingly, all of these materials can be successfully densified with excellent mechanical, ferroelectric, or microwave dielectric properties via pressureless sintering technology, i.e., MW-CSP. Notably, the energy consumption of microwave cold sintering is estimated to be only 1%–3% that of other pressureless sintering technologies because of the lower sintering temperatures and shorter processing times. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and phase-field simulations were performed to demonstrate the existence and effect of the transient liquid phase during the densification process of microwave cold sintering. Moreover, a wide range of inorganic ceramics with excellent performance is promising for densification via the microwave cold sintering process with low energy consumption.

### 2.1 Preparation of ceramic powders

The NaCl (99.5%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), KH2PO4 (KDP; 99.5%, Aladdin, China), Li2MoO4 (99+%, Alfa Aesar, USA), and TiO2 (99.8%, Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) raw powders were provided by commercial companies. The MgMoO4 powders were synthesized via the solid-state reaction method. Proportional amounts of reagent-grade starting materials of MgO (98.5%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) and MoO3 (99.95%, Zaibang Chemical Co., Ltd., China) were mixed according to the stoichiometric formula. The mixtures were subsequently ball-milled in ethanol with 2 mm diameter zirconia ball media for 4 h via a planetary ball mill (Nanjing Machine Factory, China). After the slurry was dried in an oven, the ball-milled powders were calcined at 800 °C for 4 h at a heating rate of 3 °C/min, followed by natural cooling in a furnace (LT 9/11/P330, Nabertherm GmbH, Germany). The calcined powders were second ball milled for 4 h and then dried to obtain fine powders.

### 2.2 Synthesis of microwave cold sintered ceramics

The NaCl, KDP, MgMoO4, and Li2MoO4 powders were weighed and homogenously mixed with 5– 20 wt% deionized water via a mortar and pestle. The wetted powders were then placed in a steel mold and pressed into cylindrical pucks (10 mm in diameter and 1–5 mm in thickness) under a uniaxial pressure of 250–300 MPa for 3 min. Finally, all the green pellets were pressurelessly sintered at a heating rate of 30 °C/min and naturally cooled in a microwave furnace (CY-R01000C-S, Hunan Change Microwave Technology Co., Ltd., China) to obtain the final microwave cold sintered ceramics. The sintering temperatures of the NaCl, KDP, MgMoO4, and Li2MoO4 microwave cold sintered ceramics are 100–200, 100–150, 650–875, and 100–200 °C, respectively. The TiO2 powders were homogenously mixed with 20% tetrabutyl titanate (≥ 98%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) and 10% deionized water. Then, the wetted TiO2 powders were placed in a steel mold and pressed into cylindrical pucks (10 mm in diameter and 4–5 mm in thickness) under a uniaxial pressure of 250–300 MPa for 3 min. Finally, all the green pellets were pressurelessly sintered at 900–1025 °C and naturally cooled in a microwave furnace (CY-R01000C-S, Hunan Change Microwave Technology Co., Ltd., China). The experimental details of the microwave cold sintered, traditional thermal sintered and microwave sintered ceramics are available in Fig. S1 and Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

### 2.3 Characterizations

The phase compositions of the microwave cold sintered ceramics were investigated via an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 Advance, Bruker Co., Germany) with Cu Kα radiation in the 2*θ* (10°–70°) range. The sintered ceramics were ground into fine powders before testing. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Zeiss Sigma300, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) and TEM (JEM-2100 and JEM-ARM200F, JEOL, Japan) were employed to observe the microstructures of the microwave cold sintered ceramics. The surfaces of the sintered samples without polishing were used to study their morphologies. Prior to TEM observation, the samples were prepared with a focused ion beam (FIB; Helios 5 UX, Thermo Scientific, USA). The bulk densities of the microwave cold sintered samples were measured via Archimedes’ method. The temperature dependence of the relative permittivity was measured at different frequencies of 1, 10, and 100 kHz via an LCR meter (IM 3536, HIOKI, Japan) at a heating rate of 2 °C/min. The dielectric behaviors at microwave frequencies, including relative permittivity and the *Q*×*f* value (where *Q* is quality factor, and *f* is resonant frequency) were measured via a network analyzer (8720ES, Agilent, USA) according to the TE01δ shielded cavity method.

Prior to the Vickers microhardness, Raman, and infrared (IR) measurements, the microwave cold sintered samples were polished with 1200 mesh, 2000 mesh, and 4000 mesh sandpapers, followed by polishing with polishing agents. A microhardness tester (Shanghai Taiming Optical Instrument Co., Ltd., China) was utilized to collect the Vickers microhardness with a load of 0.25 N and a dwell time of 15 s. The Raman spectra at room temperature were recorded in the range of 50–1200 cm−1 with a Raman spectrometer (Labram HR Evolution, HORIBA Scientific, Japan) using a solid-state laser (532 nm) with an effective power of 100 mW as the excitation source. The infrared reflectivity spectra at room temperature were measured in the range of 50–1200 cm−1 via a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR; Optik IFS 66v, Bruker, UK) on the infrared beamline (BL01B) at the National Synchrotron Radiation Lab (NSRL), China. The characterization methods used for the traditional thermal sintered samples were the same as those described above.

### 3.1 Densification of microwave cold sintered ceramics

Traditional thermal sintering typically requires complex experimental procedures (such as granulation, binder removal, and annealing), low heating rates, and long holding times that prolong the fabrication time. The microwave cold sintering process is both simple and highly efficient, making it feasible to densify ceramics at high heating rates and short holding times. As shown in Fig. 1(a), selected polar molecules (e.g., H2O and tetrabutyl titanate) from the added transient liquid phase are used to uniformly moisten the powders, and the mixtures are then pressed under uniaxial pressure to fabricate initial compacted pellets. Afterwards, all the moistened pellets are directly placed in the microwave oven to be sintered into dense ceramics.

*
Fig. 1Densification process of microwave cold sintered ceramics. (a) Schematic illustration of MW-CSP. (b) Major densification mechanism of the microwave cold sintering process, including dissolution–precipitation process and microwave resonance process.

On the basis of the theories of liquid-phase sintering and microwave sintering, the moistened pellets undergo two major densification mechanisms during the microwave cold sintering process, as shown in Fig. 1(b): (i) Dissolution–precipitation process. In the initial stage, the particles are partially dissolved in the added transient liquid phase. As the sintering temperature increased, the evaporation of the liquid phase accelerated. The dissolved particles in the supersaturated solution precipitate to form an intermediate phase, and the precipitated particles contain crystalline and amorphous phases [27,28]. (ii) Microwave resonance process. The conversion efficiency of dielectric materials is related mainly to the imaginary part ε*′′ of the complex dielectric constant *ɛ** [23,24,29]. During the microwave cold sintering process, since the value of  ε″ of the added transient liquid phase is 104 times greater than that of ceramics, the converted thermal energy of moistened samples is significantly improved to increase the driving force for densification, even for dielectric materials with low thermal conductivity (Table S2 in the ESM). As the sintering temperature increases, the amorphous phase induced by the dissolution‒precipitation process dominates the resonant molecules.

Three typical inorganic materials, NaCl, KDP, and MgMoO4, are selected as examples to study the sintering behavior of MW-CSP. When the sintering parameters are appropriately varied, the microwave cold sintered NaCl, KDP, and MgMoO4 ceramics are well densified at low temperatures with short holding times. The optimal relative densities of the MW-CSP NaCl (200 °C), KDP (150 °C), and MgMoO4 (850 °C) ceramics are 97.9%, 94.2%, and 94.7%, respectively (Table S3 in the ESM). Homogeneous microstructures, well-packed grains, and low porosities can be clearly observed in SEM micrographs (Figs. S2–S4 in the ESM). The grains of NaCl, KDP, and MgMoO4 grow from 1.35±0.02, 19.59±0.74, and 0.85±0.01 to 14±0.25, 121.2±6.12, and 1.61±0.03 µm, respectively. In contrast, the TTS MgMoO4 ceramics have a larger grain size of 2.16±0.1 µm, indicating that the grain growth of MgMoO4 is limited during MW-CSP (Fig. S5 in the ESM). Additionally, the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns show that a pure ceramic phase without any impurities can be detected, revealing that the transient liquid phase has not formed impurities after microwave cold sintering (Figs. S6–S8 in the ESM) [17,30,31].

### 3.2 Dissolution–precipitation process of microwave cold sintered ceramics

To investigate the mass transfer mechanism during the microwave cold sintering process, the microstructures of the MgMoO4 ceramics sintered at 700 °C were analyzed via high-resolution TEM (HRTEM). As shown in Fig. 2(a), localized amorphous phases appear at the grain boundaries, which is consistent with the dissolution‒precipitation process during MW-CSP densification. Clear grain‒grain boundaries are observed in Fig. 2(b), such as between grains 4 (G4, [241]) and 5 (G5 [ 1¯12]), and the high crystallinity is confirmed by clear fast Fourier transform (FFT) results. The crystal‒amorphous interface in Fig. 2(c) exhibits a unique terrace-ledge structure, suggesting that layer-by-layer crystal growth is governed by the terrace-ledge-kink (TLK) model. In this model, the terraces end at one ledge and extend downward to the next, with missing atoms forming kink points in the ledges. These ledges and kink points are the thermodynamically lowest locations of surface free energy, which provide favorable attachment sites. Ionic species bound to these sites can form numerous chemical bonds with the crystal surface, helping to prevent redissolution. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 2(d), nanometer-sized precipitates are found at the terrace-ledge arranged crystal‒amorphous interface, directly demonstrating precipitation at these step-like interfaces. The FFT results, which show diffuse scattering for R3 and sharp diffraction spots for R4, demonstrate the coexistence of amorphous and crystalline phases, highlighting the role of the TLK mechanism in mediating microstructural evolution during MW-CSP.

A natural explanation for our results is that the driving force of the dissolution–precipitation process depends on the surface energy of the particles. With the assistance of a sufficient amount of the transient liquid phase, the surfaces of the ceramic particles are wetted and lubricated, the sharp edges of the particles are dissolved into the liquid phase, and the capillary forces between the particles are beneficial for particle rearrangement. From the perspective of energy, nanometer-sized crystals are more inclined to precipitate at the ledges and kinks of the interfaces as well as at the contact points of the particles because of difference in chemical potential between these regions and the transient liquid phase. The induced chemical potential gradient enhances atomic diffusion and mass transfer in the liquid phase, leading to further grain growth and densification. With increasing temperature, the amount of added liquid decreases, and the formed amorphous phase further enhances the densification process through the conversion of thermal energy under microwave resonance conditions. Moreover, the crystallization process is accelerated by the coupling of dissolution‒precipitation and microwave resonance processes, and clear grain‒grain boundaries can be observed in most areas.

### 3.3 Phase-field simulation of the sintering process

To study the sintering behavior, phase-field simulations of MW-CSP and TTS are performed, as shown in Fig. 3. The phase-field simulation of the TTS is constructed by setting heat transfer as the only energy source, whereas a model with liquid phase interfaces is introduced in the case of the MW-CSP [32−34]. Theoretically, the diffusion behavior of elements in ceramic particles and the liquid phase is described by the evolutionary kinetic Cahn‒Hilliard equation with a flat flow term ***v***adv. The phase transformation behavior of the phase-field order parameters governing the sintering process is described by the Allen–Cahn equation augmented with a plane flow term [35−37]. Herein, temperature (*T*) is taken as the variable that affects the surface diffusion coefficient (*D*) to verify the difference between the MW-CSP and TTS. The phase-field equation and the heat transfer equation are strongly coupled by linking the phase-field order parameters with the thermal conductivity (*D*T). The classical form of the heat transfer equation can be presented as Eq. (15):

(15) ∂T∂t=∇⋅DT∇T+Qh

For MW-CSP, the value of *Q*h is different at each position because of the different absorption strengths of the liquid phase and ceramic particles in the microwave field. (19) X=∑i=1nηiXη+φXφ+(1−∑i=1nηi−φ)Xgas

where *η* and *φ* are the phase-field order parameters of the ceramic particles and liquid phase, respectively. *n* represents the number of ceramic particles. *X* is used to represent *D*T, *C*, *μ*0, and *ε*0, which are the thermal conductivity, heat capacity, permeability and relative permittivity of the ceramic particles (*X**η*), liquid phase (*X**φ*), and sintering atmosphere (*X*gas), respectively (Tables S2–S4 in the ESM).

Figure 3(a) shows that during the microwave cold sintering process, the ceramic particles gradually evolve into dense, larger ceramic grains as the sintering temperature increases. With decreasing porosity, particle contact and sintered neck growth can be observed. To verify the prediction of the microwave resonance process, phase-field simulations of the temperature distribution during the microwave cold sintering process are performed, as shown in Fig. 3(b). In a microwave field with a frequency of 2.45 GHz and a simulated temperature of 700 °C, the temperature of the transient liquid phase increases significantly, and heat is transferred to the nearby ceramic phase. The temperature of the MW-CSP samples is ultimately distributed in the range of 620–690 °C, with the highest and lowest temperatures belonging to the transient liquid phase and the ceramic phase, respectively. Correspondingly, it can be observed in Fig. 3(c) that the pores in the transient liquid phase gradually decrease due to mass transfer, further confirming the densification process during MW-CSP.

In the case of traditional thermal sintering, the ceramic grain maturity is lower and the porosity is greater than those of microwave cold sintering, as shown in Fig. 3(d). When the simulated ambient temperature increased to 800 °C, as shown in Fig. 3(e), the temperature of the TTS ceramic gradually increased and was finally distributed in the range of 600–650 °C. Under the solid-state diffusion mechanism, the growth of the sintered neck is driven mainly by the temperature difference between the grain boundaries and internal grains. In summary, phase-field simulations demonstrate that the application of a microwave field effectively enhances the driving force of densification with the assistance of a transient liquid phase. However, the sintering mechanism of MW-CSP still needs further exploration. Future research can combine experimental results with *in situ* characterization, such as temperature mapping and *in situ* synchrotron radiation, Raman, IR, or XRD, to directly study the densification mechanism of MW-CSP in detail.

### 3.4 Performances of microwave cold sintered ceramics

Another piece of evidence demonstrating the feasibility of microwave cold sintering is that NaCl, KDP, and MgMoO4 MW-CSP ceramics exhibit comparable or even better performances than those fabricated by other pressureless sintering methods [17,30,31]. In terms of the mechanical properties, Fig. 4(a) shows that the Vickers microhardness (HV) of the NaCl ceramics is significantly improved when MW-CSP is used, as the average HV value of the MW-CSP samples (0.41 GPa) sintered at 200 °C is 95.2% greater than that of the TTS samples (0.21 GPa) sintered at 700 °C (Table S5 in the ESM). In addition, the average HV value of the MW-CSP MgMoO4 ceramics (2.32 GPa) sintered at 825 °C is 24.1% greater than that of the TTS samples (1.87 GPa) sintered at 950 °C (Fig. S9 in the ESM). In terms of ferroelectricity, KDP is a typical ferroelectric material that generally takes several weeks to densify via traditional fabrication methods. However, in this work, KDP ceramics can be highly densified by MW-CSP in a few minutes. As shown in Fig. 4(b), the temperature-dependent dielectric behavior of the MW-CSP KDP ceramics sintered at 150 °C shows an obvious dielectric abnormality at −152 °C with a relative permittivity of 63, demonstrating the occurrence of a ferroelectric-to-paraelectric phase transition, which is consistent with previous works using traditional fabrication methods [38,39]. In terms of the microwave dielectric properties, Fig. 4(c) shows that the MW-CSP MgMoO4 ceramics sintered within 1.5 h present distinct advantages, with a relative permittivity of 5–7 and a *Q*×*f* value ranging from 23,840 to 120,894 GHz. At 1.47 h, the MW-CSP samples achieve an optimal *Q*×*f* value of (120,894 GHz), which is 52.8% greater than that of the TTS samples (79,100 GHz) sintered at 14.1 h in previous works and 113.4% greater than that of the TTS samples (56,656 GHz) sintered at 20.3 h in this work (Tables S6 and S7 in the ESM) [40,41]. The optimal *Q*×*f* values of the MW-CSP Li2MoO4 and TiO2 ceramics are also comparable to those of the TTS and MW samples. The improved *Q*×*f* value of the MW-CSP samples is of interest for meeting the demands of 5G/6G millimeter wave communication [42−45]. Furthermore, the long-term structural and functional stability of MW-CSP ceramics under ambient conditions has been demonstrated through long-term aging studies (Fig. S10 and Table S8 in the ESM).

The infrared reflectivity analysis of the MW-CSP MgMoO4 ceramic was performed to elucidate the underlying mechanism affecting the dielectric properties, which can be analyzed via the classical harmonic oscillator model based on the standard Lorentzian equation (Eq. (20)) and the Fresnel equation (Eq. (21)):

(20) ε∗(ω)=ε∞+∑i=1nωpi2ωoi2−ω2−jγiω

(21) R(ω)=|1−ε∗(ω)1+ε∗(ω)|2

where *ε*∞ is the dielectric constant caused by electronic polarization at high frequencies. *ω*p*i*, *ω*o*i*, and *γ**i* are the plasma frequency, transverse frequency, and damping factor of the *i*th Lorentz oscillator, respectively. *n* is the number of transverse phonon modes. *R*(*ω*) is the IR reflectivity. The infrared spectrum of the MW-CSP MgMoO4 ceramic is well fitted by 30 resonant modes (Table S9 in the ESM). In Fig. 4(d), the calculated result (solid line) is in good agreement with the measured data (hollow circle) in the range of 50–1200 cm−1. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 4(e), the calculated * ε′ (relative permittivity, 6.71) is close to the measured  ε′ (6.86) obtained by the TE01δ shielded cavity method at 10.8 GHz, demonstrating that the microwave dielectric properties of the MW-CSP MgMoO4 ceramics are caused mainly by polar optical phonons. The calculated  ε″ (5.81×10−4) for the MW-CSP MgMoO4 sample is slightly lower than the measured  ε″ (6.34×10−4), indicating that extrinsic contributions, such as grain size and pores, increase the measured dielectric losses in the ceramic. In contrast, the difference is greater for the TTS sample, where the calculated  ε″ (4.03×10−4) is lower than the measured value (1.45×10−3) (Figs. S11–S13 and Table S10 in the ESM), indicating more extrinsic losses.

MgMoO4 has a monoclinic wolframite structure and belongs to the space group C*2/*m* (*C*2*h*3). A group theory analysis predicts that there are 69 different vibrational modes in MgMoO4, which are described in the following irreducible representations (Eq. (22)) [41]:

(22) ΓMgMoO4=19Ag+14Au+17Bg+19Bu

where Ag and Bg are Raman-active modes, and Au and Bu are infrared-active modes. In the range of 50–500 cm−1, the vibrational modes belong to the ν2, ν4, and lattice modes. In the 700–1000 cm−1 range, the vibrational modes belong to the ν1 and ν3 modes. The remaining modes are external lattice modes (8Ag + 10Bg + 6Au + 9Bu). Different vibration modes are associated with different structures, which significantly affect the dielectric properties. Further investigation is performed by analyzing the calculated *ε*′ and tan*δ* contributions of all infrared modes in Fig. 4(f). Notably, the infrared modes of the ν2, ν4, and lattice groups dominate the contributions of *ε*′ and tan*δ*; in particular, the infrared mode at 301.7 cm−1 has the highest contribution to the relative permittivity (14.17%), and the infrared mode at 168.7 cm−1 has the highest dielectric loss (24.27%) (Table S11 in the ESM). In addition, 33 Raman modes from a total of 36 Raman modes (19Ag + 17Bg) are clearly observed from the room-temperature Raman spectrum of the MW-CSP MgMoO4 ceramic, which is consistent with previous works. All the Raman modes of the MW-CSP samples can be ascribed to MgMoO4 without any impurity phase, which is the same as that of the TTS samples, further confirming the feasibility of using MW-CSP to densify ceramics (Figs. S14 and S15 in the ESM).

### 3.5 Energy efficiency comparisons

Compared with other pressureless sintering approaches, the use of MW-CSP results in high relative densities (> 90%) under mild conditions (Table S12 in the ESM). In comparison, Fig. 5(a) shows that the sintering temperatures of the MW-CSP MgMoO4, TiO2, Li2MoO4, and NaCl samples are reduced by 225, 500, 390, and 500 °C, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5(b), the sintering times of the MW-CSP samples, including heating and holding times, are dramatically shortened by 18.5 h (MgMoO4), 21.5 h (TiO2), 13.7 h (Li2MoO4), and 12.8 h (NaCl). Consequently, as revealed in Fig. 5(c), the energy consumption of the MW-CSP samples is significantly reduced by 97.3%–99% (Figs. S16–S20 in the ESM**)**. The detailed calculation procedure of energy consumption is provided in the ESM. Given that energy-related production costs can account for up to 30% of the total ceramic manufacturing expenses [7], MW-CSP offers a promising pathway for substantial energy and cost savings.

Chlorides, oxides, phosphates, and molybdates with excellent performance were successfully prepared via microwave cold sintering, as shown in Fig. 5(d). With a greater than 97% reduction in energy consumption, the performance of the MW-CSP samples is comparable to (or even better than) that of samples fabricated by other pressureless sintering technologies. Surprisingly, the HV value of the MW-CSP NaCl samples (0.41 GPa) is 95.2% greater than that of the TTS NaCl samples. The T*C (Curie temperature) of the MW-CSP KDP ceramics is approximately −152 °C, which is in good agreement with previous works in which saturated solutions were prepared via the isothermal evaporation method (ITE). The relative permittivities of the MW-CSP MgMoO4, TiO2, and Li2MoO4 ceramics are consistent with those of previous works, and the optimal *Q*×*f* values of the MW-CSP TiO2 (38,020 GHz) and Li2MoO4 (20,323 GHz) ceramics are comparable to those of ceramics fabricated by other pressureless sintering technologies [46,47]. Moreover, the optimal *Q*×*f* value of the MW-CSP MgMoO4 ceramics (120,894 GHz) is 52.8% greater than that of the TTS samples.

In addition to these selected materials, MW-CSP has the potential to be applied to the densification of a wider range of materials, such as BaTiO3 and ferrites. We anticipate that more research groups worldwide will expand the range of applicable material systems with energy-efficient sintering. The energy consumption of MW-CSP is comparable to that of the cold sintering process (CSP). However, the pressureless nature of MW-CSP offers significant advantages in scalability, enabling the fabrication of larger or thicker components with improved structural uniformity, which is crucial for practical applications. In summary, as a multistep densification process involving coupled chemical potential and multiphysics fields, MW-CSP has significant advantages in both energy efficiency enhancement and performance optimization.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, we demonstrate the energy-efficient densification of a series of high-performance ceramics, including NaCl, KDP, MgMoO4, Li2MoO4, and TiO2 ceramics, via the microwave cold sintering process (MW-CSP). All the samples sintered with the MW-CSP mixture exhibited densities exceeding 90%. TEM and phase-field simulations reveal that the fundamental densification mechanisms are dominated by the coupling of the transient liquid phase and microwave resonance. Nanometer-sized precipitates are observed at the terrace-ledge arranged crystal‒amorphous interfaces, demonstrating the dissolution and precipitation process during MW-CSP. The thermal energy converted from microwaves enhances the sintering driving force, thus accelerating the grain evolution process for the formation of dense ceramics. Compared with other pressureless sintering technologies, MW-CSP significantly reduces sintering temperatures by 225–500 °C, shortens sintering times by 12.8–21.5 h, and decreases energy consumption by more than 97% while maintaining comparable or better performance. For example, the Vickers microhardness of NaCl MW-CSP ceramics shows a 95.2% improvement over that of TTS samples, accompanied by a 99% reduction in energy consumption and a 500 °C decrease in sintering temperature. Among the KDP ceramics, the MW-CSP samples achieve temperature-dependent dielectric behavior comparable to that reported in previous studies, with a 97.3% reduction in energy consumption. For the MW-CSP MgMoO4 ceramics, the optimal *Q*×*f* value is 52.8% greater than that of the TTS samples, along with a 98.2% reduction in energy consumption and an 18.5 h decrease in sintering time. In conclusion, microwave cold sintering holds great promise for efficiently densifying a wide range of high-performance ceramics under mild conditions with substantially reduced energy consumption, offering a sustainable and effective approach for ceramic material preparation. In the future, MW-CSP is expected to be expanded to include more material systems and larger-scale samples.
