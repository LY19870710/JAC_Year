## 1 Introduction

As novel energy-saving materials, infrared (IR) radiation ceramics have attracted great attention in high-temperature industries, such as hypersonic vehicles and thermal furnaces. IR ceramic coatings can be applied to high-temperature equipment to increase heat radiation, leading to energy savings [1−9]. In recent decades, Cr-containing perovskites (ABO3) have demonstrated great potential as IR ceramics because of their excellent IR performance, which is characterized by high emissivity [10,11]. The high emissivity can be attributed to the following factors. On the one hand, the presence of multiple Cr valence states (Cr3+, Cr4+, and Cr6+) introduces impurity energy levels into the bandgap, reducing the forbidden bandwidth and improving impurity energy level absorption [12]. On the other hand, oxygen vacancies usually form *in* *situ*, which could promote free carrier absorption. Owing to the above characteristics, many studies have focused on LaCrO3-based IR ceramics.

To improve emissivity, an effective method is to partially substitute La3+ with alkaline earth metal ions, which promotes the transition of Cr3+ to higher valence states (Cr4+ and Cr6+), introducing more impurities into the bandgap and increasing the emissivity. However, a debate exists regarding the roles of Cr4+ and Cr6+ in enhancing emissivity. While Cr4+ has been shown to improve emissivity, Cr6+ has not, as confirmed in our previous study [13]. Furthermore, Cr6+ poses significant environmental and health hazards during preparation, such as through Cr6+ leaching in water-based coatings and CrO3(g) volatilization during high-temperature service. Therefore, the development of novel environmentally friendly materials with high emissivities and low Cr6+ concentrations is urgently needed.

Compared with LaCrO3 ceramics, LaAlO3 ceramics are nontoxic and nonhazardous, and are capable of achieving high emissivity and low Cr6+ content with minimal Cr doping. Thus, many studies have focused on Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 ceramics with high emissivities in recent years. For example, Han *et* *al*. [14] synthesized Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 ceramics with emissivity of 0.92 in the 1–5 μm band via solid-phase reaction technology. Similarly, Wang *et* *al*. [15] reported emissivities higher than 0.9 in the near-infrared band for Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3, whereas Ye *et* *al*. [16] reported that the emissivity is as high as 0.93 in the near-infrared band. However, introducing Ca2+ at high temperatures in an oxidizing atmosphere still leads to Cr3+ oxidation, resulting in the formation of highly hazardous and water-soluble Cr6+ [17]. In our previous study [13], by adjusting the Ca2+/Cr3+ ratio (0 ≤ *n*(Ca2+)/*n*(Cr3+) ≤ 0.75), La0.9Ca0.1Al0.6Cr0.4O3−*δ* displayed the highest emissivity (0.9181) in the near-infrared band and a relatively low Cr6+ content (62.53 mg/kg). However, the above Cr6+ content exceeds the EU standard (20 mg/kg) [13] and remains toxic. Thus, further reducing the Cr6+ content in the Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 ceramics is extremely necessary.

*In* *situ* detoxification of Cr6+ can be realized by introducing acid oxides such as SiO2, Fe2O3, and TiO2 [18−22]. These oxides facilitate the reduction of Cr6+ to Cr3+ through interactions with O2− anions. Mao *et* *al*. [23] simulated the inhibition mechanism of Cr6+ formation via FactSage and reported that acidic oxides, such as Fe2O3 and SiO2, effectively reduce Cr6+ to Cr3+. Xu *et* *al*. [24] achieved similar results by introducing TiO2 into magnesium-chromium bricks, significantly reducing the Cr6+ content as the TiO2 concentration increased from 2% to 4% and lowering Cr6+ from 35 to 11 mg/kg. In addition, Xu *et* *al*. [25] introduced SiO2-containing zeolite into a CaO–Al2O3–Cr2O3 castable, achieving a Cr6+ detoxification rate of over 96.3%. Moreover, Nath *et* *al*. [26] synthesized a new compound CaAl2Cr2O7, which reduced Cr6+ leaching to 14.8 mg/kg in castables.

On the basis of the above data, it is feasible to introduce acidic oxides into Ca2+/Cr3+-codoped LaAlO3 ceramics to reduce the Cr6+ content. Typically, for the LaAlO3 ceramics, SiO2, Fe2O3, and TiO2 may worsen the high-temperature properties and IR performance due to their reactions with LaAlO3. In contrast, Al2O3, a primary component of LaAlO3, is chemically compatible with LaAlO3 ceramics. In fact, Al2O3 and Cr2O3 can form continuous solid solutions with good chemical stability, which may effectively inhibit both the volatilization of CrO3 and the formation of Cr6+, with the main forms of Cr6+ being CaCrO4 and Ca4Al6CrO16 [27]. Moreover, compared with other acid oxides SiO2 and Fe2O3, Al2O3 more favorably reacts with CaO at 1600 °C, reducing the risk of Cr3+ oxidation in the presence of CaO. Furthermore, the reduction of CaCrO4 by Al2O3 is also more feasible at this temperature, indicating its role in facilitating the reduction of Cr6+ [28–34]. On the basis of the above analysis, Al2O3 appears to be a suitable additive for Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 ceramics to simultaneously achieve high emissivity and detoxification of Cr6+.

In this work, the effects of the addition of Al2O3 on the emissivity and Cr6+ detoxification of Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3-based ceramics were investigated. Specifically, we focus on achieving *in* *situ* Cr6+ detoxification and high emissivity by incorporating residual Al2O3 into the ceramic matrix.

### 2.1 Ceramics fabrication

In this work, La2O3 (99.99%, Shanghai Maclean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China), Al2O3 (99.99%, Shanghai Maclean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China), CaO (99.9%, Aladdin Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), and Cr2O3 (AR, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd., China) were used as raw materials.

The ceramic fabrication, performance test, and energy-saving experimental process are shown in Fig. 1. As shown in Step 1 in Fig. 1, La0.9Ca0.1Al0.6Cr0.4O3−*δ* ceramics were prepared from the above raw materials and were designated as LA-0. Then, LA-5, LA-10, LA-15 and LA-20 were then fabricated by introducing residual Al2O3 at concentrations of 5, 10, 15 and 20 wt%, respectively. The powder mixtures were obtained via ball milling (300 r/min, 6 h), followed by drying and sieving (75 μm) (Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). The mixtures were subsequently pressed into cylindrical samples under 20 MPa. The final Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3-based composite ceramics were obtained by heating the samples at 1600 °C for 3 h in air.

### 2.2 Hexavalent chromium (Cr<sup>6+</sup>) content evaluation

In practical applications of Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3-based IR ceramic coatings, Cr6+ forms primarily during the following two stages: (i) leaching during the preparation of water-based IR ceramic slurries and (ii) gaseous CrO3(g) volatilization during high-temperature service. Thus, two methods were used to quantitatively evaluate the Cr6+ concentration during the above two processes: the leaching method and the heating volatilization method.

1) Leaching method

In this work, the Cr6+ leaching test was conducted according to the EU standard (TRGS613: 2002). As shown in Step 3 in Fig. 1, the ceramic samples were ground to 200 mesh (in Step 2), and slurries were subsequently obtained by stirring 1.5 g of ceramic powder in 30 mL of deionized water at 300 r/min for 15 min. The slurry was subsequently filtered through a membrane, and the Cr6+ concentration in the leachate was determined by measuring the absorbance at 540 nm via a Vis spectrophotometer (722, Jinghua Instruments, China) via the 1,5-diphenylcarbazide method.

2) Heating volatilization method

Typically, gaseous CrO3(g) volatilization usually occurs in Cr-containing materials at high temperatures and is hazardous to both the environment and human health. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate CrO3(g) volatilization during high-temperature service to guide the design of ceramics with high emissivity and low CrO3(g) volatilization. In this study, CrO3(g) volatilization was conducted by subjecting the ceramics to 1600 °C for 3 h to record mass loss.

### 2.3 Characterization

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X’Pert Pro, Philips, Eindhoven, the Netherlands) was employed to characterize the phase composition of all the ceramics. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; EVO-10, ZEISS, Germany) combined with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS; Xplose30, Oxford, UK) was used to analyze the microstructure and element composition. Furthermore, an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; AXIS Ultra DLD, Shimadzu-Krayos, Japan) was utilized to determine the chemical valence state of Cr, and the presence mode of O. The absorbance of the ceramic samples was measured in the 0.2–0.8 μm band via an ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared (UV‒Vis‒NIR) spectrophotometer (Lambda 750 S, PerkinElmer, USA). The IR performance of the ceramics was evaluated by testing the absorptivity in the 0.8–2.5 μm band and the emissivity in the 2.5–14 μm band via an emissivity tester (IS50, Thermo-Scientific Nicolet, USA). Moreover, this emissivity tester was also applied to measure the high-temperature emissivity of the ceramic LA-15 at 800 and 1200 °C.

A supercell of La0.9Ca0.1Al0.6Cr0.4O3−*δ* was established. The optimized crystal structures were subsequently established in Materials Studio, and the crystal structure and spin polarization DF were computed via the gradient approximation of the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof formalism (GGA-PBE) function and Vienna *ab* *initio* simulation package (VASP) code [35,36]. A cutoff energy of 400 eV and Monkhorst–Pack 4 × 4 × 4 *k*-point meshes with a grid spacing of 0.04 Å−1 were used for the optimization calculation.

### 3.1 Phase composition

XRD patterns of the Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3-based composite ceramics are shown in Fig. 2. Notably, only a single perovskite phase forms in the LA-0 sample, and no other oxide peaks (CaO/Cr2O3) appear in the curves of the ceramics, indicating that both CaO and Cr2O3 were successfully incorporated into the LaAlO3 lattice. Moreover, the strongest diffraction peaks of (110) lattice plane (33°–34°) are consistent across all the samples, indicating that perovskite is the main phase.

*
Fig. 2XRD patterns of all ceramics.

Interestingly, LA-5 (5 wt% residual Al2O3) shows weak diffraction peaks corresponding to the CA6 phase. As the residual Al2O3 content increases from 5 to 20 wt%, the intensity of the CA6 peaks gradually increases, confirming the progressive formation of this impurity phase. Additionally, the main diffraction peak of LaAlO3 (33°–34°) shifts toward lower angles with increasing residual Al2O3 content. According to Bragg’s equation (2d*sin*θ* = *λ*),the diffraction angle (*θ*) of a material is inversely related to the interplanar distance (*d*). The formation of CA6 reduces the CaO content in the LaAlO3 lattice. Because the ionic radius of Ca2+ (0.99 Å) is smaller than that of La3+ (1.06 Å), this substitution increases the interplanar distance, causing the diffraction peak to shift. Notably, the peak of CA6 shifts toward lower angles, suggesting an increased interplanar distance and possible formation of CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19 due to the larger ionic radius of Cr3+ (0.62 Å) than of Al3+ (0.55 Å).

### 3.2 Microstructure

Figures 3(a)–3(g) show SEM and EDS images of the Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3-based composite ceramics. The SEM images revealed that all the ceramics exhibited irregular particle morphologies. Notably, the introduction of residual Al2O3 significantly increased the porosity within the ceramics. The impurity phase CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19 appeared after the introduction of Al2O3. As the Al2O3 content increased from 5 to 20 wt%, the CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19 phase content increased accordingly, whereas the size of the LaAlO3 grains gradually decreased. This reduction in grain size can be attributed to the “pinning” effect of CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19, which inhibits the growth of LaAlO3 grains. From the EDS data in Fig. 3(f), sample LA-0 (Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3) contains La, Al, O, Ca, and Cr (Spectrum 1), which confirms that both Ca and Cr entered the LaAlO3 lattice. In Fig. 3(e), the presence of 20 wt% residual Al2O3 reveals that the CA6 impurity phase is composed of Ca, Al, O, and Cr (Spectrum 2), indicating that Cr has entered the CA6 phase to form a CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19 solid solution, which is in agreement with the XRD results.

*
Fig. 3SEM images, grain size distributions and gaussian fitting curves of all ceramics: (a) LA-0, (b) LA-5, (c) LA-10, (d) LA-15 and (e) LA-20; EDS data for ceramics: (f) LA-0 and (g) LA-20.

Additionally, by increasing the residual Al2O3 content from 5 to 20 wt%, the large grains gradually diminish, and the average grain size gradually decreases from 2.376 to 1.807 μm. According to the Gaussian curves, the position of the peak gradually moves toward the smaller grains. These results directly demonstrate that CaAl12−x*Cr*x*O19 inhibits the growth of Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 grains. In general, this grain refinement phenomenon typically leads to improved emissivity [37,38]. According to infrared physics theory, an increase in grain size enhances infrared radiation scattering in the near-infrared band, resulting in reduced emissivity in this band. Therefore, to achieve high emissivity, the grain size of the ceramics must be controlled to lower values [39,40]. Furthermore, the IR performance of the composite ceramics can be enhanced because of the multilayered lamellar structure of CaAl12−*x* Cr*x*O19 and the increased porosity of the material. Specifically, CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19 has a multilayered lamellar structure, which can increase the light path and enhance emissivity. Moreover, with CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19 generation, more nano/micropores formed *in* *situ* in the composite material, which increased light refraction/reflection and increased emissivity.

### 3.3 Bandgap analysis

The UV‒Vis‒NIR absorbance curves in the 200–800 nm band and the bandgap values of all the ceramics are shown in Fig. 4. The bandgap (Fig. 4(b)) was derived from the plots based on the absorbance curves via Eq. (1):

*
Fig. 4(a) Absorbance spectra in 200–800 nm band, (b) bandgap, (c) urbach plot of codoped LaAlO3-based composite ceramics, and (d) crystal structure schematic of Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3.

(1) (αhv)2=K(hv−Eg)

where α* is the normalized parameter, *h* is Planck’s constant, *v* is the frequency, *K* is the material-related constant, and *E*g is the bandgap.

According to the absorbance curves in Fig. 4(a), LA-0 has the highest absorbance, while the introduction of Al2O3 decreased the absorbance in the 200–800 nm band. Furthermore, sample LA-0 had the lowest bandgap (1.834 eV), and the bandgap increased with the addition of Al2O3, from 2.009 eV (5 wt%) to 2.541 eV (20 wt%). This increase can be attributed to two factors: the formation of the impurity phase CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19 and the lower proportion of Ca2+ and Cr3+ entering the lattice of LaAlO3 (Fig. 4(d)). The increased bandgap is detrimental to electron hopping, thus reducing the emissivity in the near-infrared band [41,42]. The results are consistent with the data in IR performance.

Figure 4(c) shows the Urbach energies of Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3-based composite ceramics, which provide a measure of disorder in solids [43] and reflect the degree of lattice distortion. The Urbach energies were derived from the absorbance curves in the 200–800 nm wavelength band via Eq. (2):

(2) lnα=lnB+hvΔE

where *B* is a constant, and Δ*E* is the width of the band tails of the electronic states.

Higher Al2O3 contents lead to decreased lattice distortion, as indicated by decreasing Urbach energies for LA-0, LA-5, LA-10, LA-15, and LA-20 (2.79, 1.43, 1.02, 0.85 and 0.67, respectively). Therefore, as the degree of lattice distortion decreases, the mid-infrared emissivity also decreases with increasing Al2O3 content, which is in agreement with the data in IR performance.

### 3.4 XPS analysis

The high-resolution XPS spectra of the Cr 2p orbitals for the Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3-based composite ceramics are displayed in Fig. 5. Two different peaks, Cr 2p3/2 and Cr 2p1/2, correspond to lower and higher binding energies, respectively. Moreover, both Cr 2p3/2 and Cr 2p1/2 are fitted by three subpeaks, corresponding to Cr3+, Cr4+, and Cr6+, with binding energies as follows: Cr3+ at 575.60 and 585.50 eV, Cr4+ at 575.60 and 590.00 eV, and Cr6+ at 579.51 and 590.00 eV, respectively [44].

*
Fig. 5XPS spectra of Cr 2p orbitals for samples (a) LA-0, (b) LA-5, (c) LA-10, (d) LA-15 and (e) LA-20; O 1 s orbitals for samples (f) LA-0 and LA-20.

In the present work, the Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 ceramics have a typical perovskite structure (ABO3), with Ca2+ and Cr3+ occupying the A- and B-sites, respectively. The introduction of Ca2+ promotes the transformation of Cr3+ to higher oxidation states (Cr4+ and Cr6+) for charge compensation. However, Cr6+ is detrimental to both the environment and human health. Furthermore, Cr6+ does not contribute to emissivity in the near-infrared band [13].

To directly and clearly characterize the IR performance of various Cr oxides, the emissivities of Cr3+, Cr4+, and Cr6+-related oxides in the near-infrared band were measured and are shown in Fig. S1 in the ESM. Notably, Cr3+-related oxides (Cr2O3) and Cr6+-related oxides (CrO3) display very low emissivities, with average emissivity values of 0.31 and 0.27, respectively. In contrast, the Cr4+-related oxide (CrO2) displays extremely high emissivity greater than 0.98, which is nearly similar to that of a black body. Therefore, in* *situ* detoxification of Cr6+ is essential for practical applications. Figure 5 shows that increasing the Al2O3 content reduces the Cr6+ content in the ceramics [45]. When the percentage of Cr6+ in LA-15 decreased to 8.09%, a 4.27% reduction from LA-0 was observed. Thus, the introduction of Al2O3 can significantly reduce the Cr6+ percentage and maintain a relatively high Cr4+ percentage (> 31%), which could decrease the bandgap and enhance the IR performance. A higher concentration of Cr4+ in a material can significantly enhance the emissivity, whereas Cr6+ has no such effect.

The high-resolution O 1s XPS spectra (Fig. 5(f)) reveal two fitted peaks corresponding to lattice oxygen (529 eV) and oxygen vacancies (531.3 eV) for LA-0 and LA-20. Generally, the percentage of the fitted peak areas of lattice oxygen and oxygen vacancies can be used to estimate the content of oxygen vacancies in a material. With the addition of Al2O3 from 0 to 20 wt%, the percentage of oxygen vacancies increases from 74.96% to 80.94% because of the evaluation of the valence state and concentration of Cr [46,47].

### 3.5 IR performance

Emissivity is a key parameter for evaluating the IR performance of materials. The absorptivity spectra of the Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3-based composite ceramics were obtained in the near-infrared band (0.8–2.5 μm), as shown in Fig. 6(a). LA-0 had the highest average absorptivity of approximately 92%. Increasing the amount of Al2O3 from 5 to 20 wt% caused a slight decrease in absorptivity, with LA-20 having the lowest absorptivity of approximately 90%. According to Kirchhoff’s law, the emissivity of a nontransparent material is equal to its absorptivity. The average emissivity in the near-infrared band, which was calculated from absorptivity curves (Fig. 6(b)), revealed that LA-0 had the largest average emissivity value of 0.918. However, as the Al2O3 mass fraction increased from 5 to 20 wt%, the emissivity decreased slightly. The average emissivity values of LA-5, LA-10, LA-15 and LA-20 are 0.916, 0.903, 0.901 and 0.896, respectively, which are only 0.22%, 1.63%, 1.85% and 2.39% lower than that of LA-0. According to Wien’s displacement law, the related radiation wavelength is located mainly in the 0.8–2.5 μm band for thermal equipment above 1159 K, suggesting that Al2O3 has a minimal impact on radiation heat transfer [48,49]. Figures 6(c) and 6(d) show the emissivity spectra and average emissivities of the Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3-based composite ceramics in the mid-infrared band (2.5–14 μm). Notably, specimen LA-0 exhibited the highest emissivity of 0.896, whereas the addition of residual Al2O3 decreased the emissivity from 0.814 (5 wt%) to 0.781 (20 wt%). This indicates that the introduction of residual Al2O3 can affect the emissivity in the mid-infrared band due to CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19 component formation, which reduces the Cr concentration in the Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 component.

### 3.6 Hexavalent chromium (Cr<sup>6+</sup>) content

Typically, leaching tests were performed multiple times until the leaching solutions became completely colorless [50]. Initially, the first and second original leaching solutions appeared colorless (Fig. 7(a)), indicating a relatively low Cr6+ concentration. After the addition of a chromogenic agent (1,5-diphenylcarbazide), all the leaching solutions turned purple (Fig. 7(b)), indicating the presence of Cr6+. The intensity of the purple color varied, with the second leaching solutions being noticeably lighter than the first ones, indicating a much lower Cr6+ concentration. Thus, only two rounds of leaching were necessary. Additionally, with increasing Al2O3 content, the color of the leaching solutions gradually becomes lighter, with the solution of sample LA-20 appearing colorless, demonstrating that the addition of Al2O3 effectively decreases the Cr6+ concentration in the Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 ceramics.

Figure 7(c) displays the total Cr6+ concentration from two leaching tests. Notably, the specimen LA-0 had the highest Cr6+ concentration of 85.77 mg/kg. In contrast, all ceramics containing residual Al2O3 presented Cr6+ concentrations less than 48 mg/kg. Specifically, the Cr6+ concentration continuously decreased from 47.53 to 18.44 mg/kg as the residual Al2O3 content increased from 5 to 20 wt%. Similarly, the detoxification ratio increased from 44.58% to 78.50%. Notably, specimen LA-20 has the lowest Cr6+ concentration of 18.44 mg/kg, which is less than the EU standard of 20 mg/kg [51,52]. This reveals that the introduction of Al2O3 can inhibit the formation of Cr6+. The above data are highly important for the actual application of Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 IR coatings. Specifically, the design of IR ceramics, especially for water-based IR ceramic slurries, must balance low Cr6+ content with high emissivity simultaneously.

Combining the total Cr6+ concentration (Fig. 7) and phase evolution (Figs. 2 and 3), the possible reasons for the in* *situ* detoxification of Cr6+ could be proposed as follows. On the one hand, part of the Cr6+ could be physically absorbed and immobilized by CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19 due to its lamellar structure and high porosity. On the other hand, CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19 formation derives from a chemical reaction between Al2O3 and Cr6+ containing compounds, which decreases the amount of Cr6+ containing compounds (CaCrO4, Ca4Al6CrO16, etc.). To further analyze the suppression of Cr6+ formation, the Gibbs free energy of the reactions was calculated. As shown in Fig. 7(d), in the Ca–Cr–O system, the oxidation of Cr2O3 to CaCrO4 occurs between 200 and 1600 °C (Eq. (3)). However, when Al2O3 is added, CaO is preferentially consumed to form calcium hexaluminate (CA6) at temperatures higher than 1195 °C (Eq. (4)), which can suppress CaCrO4 formation. When the temperature is higher than 1296 °C, CaCrO4 is reduced by Al2O3 because of the lower Gibbs free energy value of the reaction between CaCrO4 and Al2O3 (Eq. (5)). Consequently, Al2O3 reacts with Cr6+ containing compounds, leading to a reduction in the Cr6+ phase and the formation of a CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19 solid solution [53]:

(3) CaO+0.5Cr2O3+0.75O2=CaCrO4

(4) CaO+6Al2O3=CaAl12O19

(5) 2CaCrO4+12Al2O3=2CaAl12O19+Cr2O3+1.5O2

Usually, CrO3 volatilization occurs for Cr-containing ceramics at high temperatures, which is harmful to the environment and human health. To balance high emissivity with low CrO3 volatilization, it is necessary to examine the CrO3 volatilization behavior of Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 containing Al2O3. Figure 8 shows the CrO3 volatilization of these ceramics after heating at 1600 °C for 3 h. The results clearly indicate that adding Al2O3 significantly suppresses the volatilization of CrO3 [54]. As the Al2O3 content increases from 0 to 20 wt%, CrO3 volatilization consistently decreases from 0.2282% to 0.0505% due to the generation of CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19. Moreover, the rate of CrO3 detoxification increases from 23.86% to 77.87%. Therefore, by controlling the Al2O3 content, it is possible to achieve both low CrO3(g) volatilization and high emissivity. The Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3-based composite ceramics clearly display lower Cr6+ concentrations and higher emissivities than other Cr-containing IR ceramics do [55].

### 3.7 Energy savings

The temperature distributions of the surface of the porous media burner were captured by an infrared thermal imager, as depicted in Fig. 9. According to Figs. 9(a) and 9(b), the REF has a lower surface temperature of 767.1 °C. However, the LA-15 sample with a high-emissivity IR coating has a higher surface temperature of 790.7 °C. By covering with an IR coating (LA-15), SiC-mullite porous ceramics have excellent IR performance on the surface because of their greater roughness and higher emissivity [56]. This indicates that the high-emissivity IR coating enhances radiation heat transfer, thus increasing the surface temperature of the burner. According to the heat-up curves of water (Fig. 9(c)), 116 and 104 s are needed for the REF and LA-15 samples, respectively, when the water temperature increases from 30 to 90 °C. The heating rate for the REF sample is 31.7 °C/min, approximately 14% lower than that of the LA-15 sample (34.6 °C/min). The high-emissivity IR coating (LA-15) can significantly enhance the radiation heat transfer and improve heat utilization efficiency [57]. The above results indicate that the prepared Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3-based composite ceramics are promising candidates for energy-saving applications at high temperatures.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, novel environmentally friendly Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 ceramics with high emissivities were fabricated by introducing residual Al2O3 (0–20 wt%) into the system. The conclusions are summarized as follows:

1) The introduction of residual Al2O3 into Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 effectively inhibited Cr6+ formation in the material. For ceramics containing 20 wt% Al2O3, the Cr6+ concentration in leaching solutions and CrO3 volatilization after heating at 1600 °C for 3 h are less than 18.44 mg/kg (lower than the EU standard of 20 mg/kg) and 0.0505% (77.87% lower than that of Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 ceramics without Al2O3), respectively. This detoxification effect can be attributed to the formation of CaAl12−*x*Cr*x*O19.

2) All the Ca2+/Cr3+co-doped LaAlO3 ceramics containing residual Al2O3 exhibit excellent IR performance, with emissivity values exceeding 0.896 in the 0.8–2.5 μm band and 0.781 in the 2.5–14 μm band. These high emissivity values can be attributed to impurity energy level absorption, free carrier absorption, and lattice vibration absorption.

3) The IR coating applied to the SiC-mullite porous ceramics clearly enhances radiant heat transfer. The IR coating (LA-15) increases the burner surface temperature from 767.1 to 790.7 °C and increases the heating rate of the burner from 31.7 to 34.6 °C/min during water heating (30–90 °C).

This work provides valuable insights for designing novel environmentally friendly Ca2+/Cr3+ co-doped LaAlO3 IR ceramics with high emissivities that are suitable for energy-saving applications in high-temperature industries.
