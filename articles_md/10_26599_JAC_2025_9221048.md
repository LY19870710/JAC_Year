## 1 Introduction

Electric field-induced strain (electrostrain) enables precise displacement of piezoelectric materials, rendering them highly valuable for use in actuators, transducers, sensors, and other devices [1−3]. The highest recorded electric field-induced tensile (electrotensile) strain, reaching 1.7%, was achieved in 0.92Pb(Zn1/3Nb2/3)O3–0.08PbTiO3 relaxor ferroelectric single crystals in 1997 [4]. The nature of electrotensile strain is the stretching of the lattice, so the magnitude of electrotensile strain is small, typically no more than 0.4% in polycrystalline ceramics. Since 2022, a series of ultrahigh electrostrains have been reported in lead-free piezoceramics [5−11]. Notably, the sample thicknesses in these studies are typically very thin (12] reported a dramatic increase in the measured electrostrain with decreasing sample thickness, particularly when the thickness was less than 300 μm. Under these conditions, giant electrostrains of 4%–5% have been readily observed in some systems, such as 0.62Na0.5Bi0.5TiO3–0.28SrTiO3 and K0.47Na0.47Li0.06NbO3 ceramics.

In our previous work, we demonstrated that the observed giant “electrostrain” arose from the bending of ceramic samples under an electric field [13]. However, limitations in the current measurement methods often lead to confusion between the electric field-induced bending (electrobending) strain and electrotensile strain. This ambiguity presents a significant challenge in accurately assessing the true levels of electrotensile strain in piezoelectric materials. Therefore, it is essential to accurately differentiate between electrobending strain and electrotensile strain based on the current measurement techniques employed.

In this study, we distinguish between electrotensile strain and electrobending strain in the K0.5Na0.5NbO3 (KNN) system in terms of their underlying mechanism, sample thickness, deformation response, temperature and frequency dependency, and polarity effect. Given that electrobending is influenced by multiple factors, including composition, defect type, and geometric dimensions, we focus on the KNN system (with ( VA′−VO⋅⋅) defect dipoles and a sample diameter of 8.5 mm) to illustrate the distinction between these two strain types. Our findings indicate that the threshold sample thickness for the occurrence of electrobending is approximately 600 μm for a sample diameter of 8.5 mm, providing a preliminary estimate based on the various influencing factors. When the sample thickness exceeds 600 μm, the observed strain predominantly arises from the contribution of electrotensile strain. However, when the sample thickness falls below 600 μm, the influence of electrobending becomes significant and must be considered. Notably, electrobending is present in samples of any thickness, but it dominates the strain response when the thickness is below 600 μm and cannot be neglected. Furthermore, we introduce parameters such as curvature, bending coefficient, and hysteresis of electrobending strain and compare them with electrotensile strain. These efforts are expected to advance both the development and application of electrobending and electrotensile strains.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

The (K0.48Na0.52)0.99NbO2.995 ceramics were fabricated by a conventional high-temperature solid-state sintering method. Na2CO3 (99.8%, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd., China), K2CO3 (99.5%, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd., China), Nb2O5 (99.5%, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd., China), and CuO (99.5%, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd., China) powders were selected as raw materials. The raw materials were mixed and ball-milled in ethanol for 12 h. Then, the mixed powders were calcined at 850 °C for 3 h and then ball-milled for another 12 h. The calcined powders were uniaxially pressed into pellets with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 1 mm under 200 MPa. Polyvinyl butyral (6%, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd., China) was added as a binder and burnt off at 500 °C after pressing. The samples were sintered in air at 1100 °C for 4 h and buried in the self-source. Both sides of the sample were polished and fired with silver on the surfaces as electrodes. The thickness of each sample ranged from 0.2 to 1 mm. The samples were poled in an oil bath at room temperature for 30 min with an electric field of 40 kV·cm−1. The direction of polarization in samples was from bottom to top.

### 2.2 Electrical measurements

The polarization*–*electric field (*P–E*) loops and strain–electric field (*S–E*) curves were measured by a ferroelectric tester (aix-ACCTF Analyzer 2000E, Aachen, Germany) with a high-voltage amplifier (Trek 610E, TREK, USA). A temperature controller was used to produce a thermal field during the measurement process. The sample was secured between a 3 mm diameter bottom holder and a curved top holder to measure both the electrotensile and electrobending strains.

## 3 Results and discussion

As shown in Fig. 1(a), pure KNN ceramics exhibit an orthorhombic phase with [110]-oriented spontaneous polarizations. The electrotensile strain primarily arises from the inverse piezoelectric effect [1,14,15] and the electrostrictive effect. Additionally, reversible non-180° domain switching [16,17], field-induced phase transitions [2,18−20], and polarization rotation [4,21] also contribute to the observed deformation. Owing to the material’s homogeneity, this electrotensile strain usually leads to uniform deformation, manifesting as a consistent expansion, as illustrated in Fig. 1(b).

*
Fig. 1Comparison of mechanisms of electrotensile strain and electrobending strain in KNN ceramics: (a) spontaneous polarization in KNN ceramics; (b) schematic diagram of electrotensile strain; (c) ( VA′−VO⋅⋅) defect dipoles in KNN ceramics; (d) schematic diagram of electrobending strain.

For piezoceramics with defect dipoles (for example,  VA′−VO⋅⋅, as shown in Fig. 1(c)), the ceramics are divided into two surface layers and a bulk layer, with defect dipoles more likely to form on surface layers after sintering. The defect dipole is resistant to switching under an electric field and exerts a significant pinning effect on the ferroelectric domains. As shown in Fig. 1(d), defect dipoles oriented inward have a lower formation energy; thus, most of them are inclined to orient towards the interior [13]. The defect dipole has a large electromechanical response to the electric field, exhibiting stretching in the same direction as the electric field and compression in the opposite direction of the field. When an electric field is applied, the defect dipoles in the top and bottom surface layers experience stretching and compression, respectively. This induces in-plane compression on the top surface and stretching on the bottom surface (illustrated by purple arrows in Fig. 1(d)) due to the Poisson effect of ceramics. These contrasting responses result in differential stresses on the top and bottom surfaces, leading to bending deformation in the ceramics under the electric field. Importantly, the stress differentials are consistently present in ceramics. When the thickness of a sample with a given diameter is sufficiently large, the rigidity of ceramics resists bending. However, significant bending deformation occurs only when the thickness of the sample falls below a certain threshold, allowing a degree of flexibility. Owing to their distinct mechanisms, electrotensile strains are ideal for applications such as microactuators and transducers, while electrobending strains offer unique potential for flexible electronics and smart devices.

Since electrobending occurs primarily in thin samples, it is essential to specify the threshold thickness and dimensions at which this phenomenon manifests. Limitations in current measurement techniques often lead to misinterpretation, as bending deformation in a sample produces a pronounced asymmetric S–E* curve, which can be mistaken for “giant strain”. The setup includes a sample fixture with a bottom holder (a platform with a larger surface area) and a top holder (a smaller-area probe) (Fig. 2(a), aix-ACCTF Analyzer). The measured deformation magnitude is based on the displacement of the top holder. As illustrated in Fig. 2(b), the top holder moves upwards to record a significant positive “strain” only when the sample bends downward under a negative electric field. Conversely, under a positive electric field, when the sample bends upwards without substantial displacement of the top holder, no apparent “strain” is observed. Occasionally, a “negative strain” is detected under positive electric fields because of the pre-poling of the sample. After the pre-poling process, the sample may retain bending deformation. If placed in the fixture in a convex state (yellow dashed box in Fig. 2(b)), the sample flattens under a positive electric field, causing the top holder to shift downward, which is referred to as “negative strain”.

*
Fig. 2Investigation of thickness dependence: (a) schematic diagram of measurement holder; (b) illustration of asymmetric S–E* curves due to electrobending-induced deformation; (c, d) Bipolar *S–E* curves with varying thicknesses; (e, f) unipolar *S–E* curves with varying thicknesses; (g) variation in apparent strain values (bipolar and unipolar) with thickness in KNN ceramics; (h) *P–E* hysteresis loops with varying thicknesses.

Figures 2(c) and 2(d) show the bipolar *S–E* curves of the sample as a function of sample thickness. As shown in Fig. 2(c), when the sample thickness exceeds 600 μm, the strain remains largely unaffected by thickness. At a thickness of 600 μm, the bipolar *S–E* curve begins to show pronounced asymmetry. For samples thinner than 600 μm, the apparent strain under a negative electric field intensifies as the thickness decreases, especially when the thickness decreases below 300 μm, and the apparent strain increases sharply (Fig. 2(d)). This effect is due to the increased ease of bending in thinner samples, which leads to substantial apparent strain enhancement as the thickness decreases.

Figures 2(e)–2(g) further illustrate the notable thickness dependence of the unipolar strain for samples thinner than 600 μm. Thus, the estimated threshold thickness for electrobending in KNN ceramics is approximately 600 μm, based on preliminary observations. For samples thinner than this threshold, where significant asymmetry appears in the bipolar *S–E* curves, the influence of electrobending must be considered. Importantly, this threshold value is an initial estimate based on the ceramic samples in this experiment and can be adjusted with further research on different ceramic systems. Finally, Fig. 2(h) displays the *P*–*E* loops for samples of varying thicknesses, showing that, owing to the pinning effect of defect dipoles, domain switching in the surface layer is restricted. As the sample thickness decreases, the surface layer proportion increases, leading to a decrease in the remanent polarization (*P*r).

In addition to the thickness dependence, electrobending also exhibits significant frequency and temperature dependence. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the strain response of a 1000 μm thick sample remains relatively stable across different frequencies, with no substantial variation observed. Conversely, for a 200 μm thick sample, the apparent strain decreases considerably as the frequency increases (Fig. 3(b)). This occurs because electrobending deformation arises from the stress difference between the two surfaces of the ceramics. The time required for stress redistribution to balance these forces limits the ceramic’s ability to bend under high-frequency electric fields. Similarly, the electrotensile strain shows minimal dependence on temperature (Fig. 3(c)). However, as the temperature increases, thinner samples are more susceptible to bending deformation, leading to an enhancement in the electrobending strain (Fig. 3(d)). This phenomenon results from two main reasons. First, ceramics are more flexible at high temperatures, leading to greater bending deformation. Second, the residual bending state of the ceramics is partially removed at high temperatures (approaching a flat state, as indicated by the yellow dashed box in Fig. 2(b)), allowing the bending of the sample under an electric field to lift the top holder to a greater height, which contributes to the larger apparent strain values observed at high temperatures.

Notably, after a poling-aging process, the defect dipoles in Cu2+-doped KNN samples become uniformly distributed throughout the material. Even thick samples (> 600 μm) exhibit an asymmetric *S–E* curve due to asymmetric domain switching (Fig. 3(e)). Moreover, because the defect dipoles are uniformly oriented within the material, the *S*–*E* curve direction reverses when measured along different sample thickness directions (Fig. 3(e)) [22]. In contrast, for samples without a poling-aging process, the defect dipoles in the top and bottom layers remain inwardly oriented, resulting in no preferred directionality. Consequently, when measured along different sample thickness directions, the direction of the *S–E* curve does not reverse (Fig. 3(f)).

Another important consideration is that when the holder’s area exceeds that of the sample, the *S–E* curve direction may reverse if measured along different sample directions owing to variations in pre-bending (i.e., the initial concave or convex state of the sample). Since the deformation responses of the electrobending strain and electrotensile strain differ significantly, new parameters are needed to assess the electrobending strain effectively. Given current measurement limitations, directly measuring a sample’s bending performance is challenging, while measuring axial displacement is more feasible. The electrotensile strain is calculated as the displacement of the top probe (*d*) divided by the initial sample thickness (*t*) (Fig. 4(a)). However, this approach is unsuitable for electrobending, which involves three-dimensional deformation. ## 4 Conclusions

In summary, this study clearly differentiated between electrotensile strain and electrobending strain, establishing a ~600 μm threshold thickness for electrobending in KNN ceramics with an 8.5 mm diameter. Samples thinner than 600 μm that exhibit asymmetric bipolar strain curves must be assessed carefully for electrobending contributions. We also recommend the use of point-to-point contact holders for electrotensile strain measurements to mitigate the impact of electrobending effects. We further distinguish these two kinds of strains by examining their frequency and temperature dependence and directionality and propose new parameters—deflection, curvature, bending coefficient, and electrobending hysteresis—to evaluate bending performance. In the future, standardizing sample thickness and measurement conditions will be crucial in clarifying electrostrain phenomena, allowing electrobending strain to emerge as a distinct research field.
