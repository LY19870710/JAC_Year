## 1 Introduction

Due to their high fracture toughness, good chemical inertness, and white aesthetic translucency [1,2], yttria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia polycrystal (Y-TZP) ceramics have been widely used in a variety of engineering applications, such as optical fiber connectors, grinding media, and dental restorations [3−6]. The properties of Y-TZP ceramics are sensitive to the grain size. Compared with their coarse-grained counterparts, the benefits of refined microstructures include (i) enhanced mechanical properties [7,8], (ii) improved optical transparency [9,10], (iii) increased stability of the tetragonal phase [11,12], and (iv) decreased thermal conductivity [13].

Defect engineering is an important method for the preparation of fine-grained ceramics. In the case of bulk zirconia, the strategy commonly involves plastic deformation-induced defects under an external force. The preparation of Y-TZP nanoceramics was first achieved by Munir’s group [14,15]. They developed a new densification technique using high-pressure spark plasma sintering (SPS). With the simultaneous application of the brutal force (up to 1 GPa) and high heating rates, the samples could be densified to relative densities above 98% within several minutes. Plastic (or superplastic) deformation was speculated to play a very important role in this densification technique. Ji *et* *al*. [16] demonstrated the coexistence of two types of plastic deformation, dislocations and sliding, in nanocrystalline 3 mol% Y-TZP (3Y-TZP) ceramics prepared under a pressure of 1.5 GPa. The results confirmed the dominant role of plastic deformation in sintering. Zhu and Xie [17] prepared fine-grained 3Y-TZP ceramics with enhanced mechanical properties by the novel oscillatory pressure sintering (OPS) technique. The introduction of the oscillatory pressure was proposed to be effective for defect engineering by enhancing grain rotation and/or sliding and activating plastic deformation during sintering. In addition, defects such as dislocations, stacking faults, and point defects were also observed in zirconia ceramics prepared by flash sintering [18].

In practice, zirconia ceramic products usually have complex geometries. It is determined that sintering is preferred for conventional pressureless sintering routines, especially for mass manufacturing. The addition of small amounts of alumina is recognized as an effective approach for preparing fine-grained Y-TZP ceramics. Although the strategy has been commercialized, the underlying mechanism is not fully understood. The grain growth was proposed to be impeded by grain-boundary drag due to the segregation of Al3+ at the grain boundaries [19]. However, why alumina doping can lower the sintering temperature is still under debate [20,21]. In the present work, fine-grained dense 147 nm 3Y-TZP ceramics were prepared by pressureless sintering of commercial zirconia powders containing 0.25 wt% Al2O3. A novel discovery was reported in which substantial defects were observed in the grain interiors of the sample. The possible reasons for the defect formation were investigated. For the first time, the dependence of the lattice distortion on low-temperature sintering of alumina-doped zirconia ceramics was discussed.

## 2 Experimental

Two kinds of zirconia powders commercialized by Jiangxi Size Materials Co., Ltd., China were used as raw materials. The grade MS-FS-3.0 powders (labeled MS) were synthesized by a hydrothermal method and mixed with 0.25 wt% alumina powders, while the grade SZ-DT-SE2A powders (labeled SZ) were alumina-free. The yttria content in the two powders was 5.2±0.2 wt%. The specific surface areas were 14.05 and 10.85 m2/g for the MS and SZ powders, respectively. TZ-3Y-E powders (labeled TZE, provided by Tosoh Co., Japan) were also used for comparison. The as-received TZE powders were in the form of spherical granules (as shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) and contained 0.25 wt% alumina. The yttria content in the TZE powders was 5.2±0.5 wt%, and the specific surface area was 16 m2/g. More detailed information on the three raw powders is summarized in Table S1 in the ESM according to the manufacturers’ data.

The bulk samples were prepared by consulting the routine for Wieland dental blocks. The raw powders, without any further treatment, were poured into a stainless steel mold and pressed under an uniaxial pressure of 10 MPa. The obtained disks were further compacted under cold isostatic pressing (CIP) at 250 MPa. After CIP, the compacts were partially densified by 2 h of sintering at 1000 ℃ to obtain Wieland zirconia blocks with dimensions of *ϕ*98 mm × 16 mm. The blocks had a density of 3.128 g/cm3 and can be machined using dental milling machines (as demonstrated in Fig. S2 in the ESM). The pellets with dimensions of *ϕ*18 mm × 2 mm were sliced from the dental blocks and further sintered at the target temperature with soaking time of 2 h. Sintering was performed in muffle furnaces (L(T) 3/12, Nabertherm, Germany for temperatures not exceeding 1000 ℃ and 3F, Sinosteel Luoyang Institute of Refractories Research, China for temperatures above 1000 ℃). The heating rate was 10 ℃/min before 1000 ℃ and then decreased to 5 ℃/min until the end of the sintering cycles. Finally, the sintered bodies were naturally cooled to room temperature in the furnace. The powder samples were sintered under the same conditions. For the TZE powders, the granules were slightly crushed in a mortar before sintering.

The theoretical density of the alumina-doped 3Y-TZP ceramics was calculated to be 6.072 g/cm3 by taking 6.08 and 3.98 g/cm3 for the theoretical values of zirconia and alumina, respectively. The density of the sample was measured using Archimedes method.

Phase identification was performed via X-ray diffraction (XRD). The XRD patterns were collected within the 20°–90° range at step sizes of 0.005° and 20 s per step using an X-ray diffractometer (Panalytical Empyrean, Almelo, the Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. The cubic phase was identified according to Refs. [22,23]. Accurate cell parameters were calculated by Rietveld refinement method using the software HighScore Plus (version 4.0). The initial values of cell parameters, such as space group and atom coordinates, were taken from matching reference patterns from the crystallography open database (COD). The pseudo-Voigt function was chosen for peak shapes because it is a linear combination of Lorentzian and Gaussian functions and can be used to resolve strain and size contributions to peak broadening [24]. The goodness of fit (GOF) was evaluated in terms of profile *R* factors. The tetragonality (*c*/*a*) was calculated as a function of *X* by consulting Ref. [25] (Eq. (1)):

(1) ca=1.44289−0.0016X

where *a* and *c* are the lattice parameters in the *a*-axis and *c*-axis directions of the tetragonal unit cell obtained by Rietveld refinement, respectively. *X* (mol%) is the concentration of YO1.5. The Y2O3 content of the tetragonal phase, represented by *M* (mol%), was obtained by the conversion in Eq. (2):

(2) M=(X/1002−X/100)⋅100

The microstructure was observed using both a scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU8010, Hitachi, Japan) and transmission electron microscopes (TEM; JEM-2100, JOEL, Japan and Talos 200X, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with X-ray energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) detectors. Before SEM observations, bulk samples were mirror-polished using a diamond suspension followed by 30 min of thermal etching at temperatures lower than the densification temperature of 50 ℃. An Au film was coated on the etched surfaces to avoid surface charging under electron beam irradiation. Based on the SEM images, the average grain size of the samples was determined by the linear intercept method. The bulk TEM sample was prepared using a focused ion beam (FIB; Model Helios 5UX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to eliminate possible influences from mechanical forces.

The reconstructed one-dimensional images of the different areas in the grain interior are presented in Figs. 3(b)–3(e). Many defects, such as edge dislocations [16], can be identified in the images. To eliminate the influences of stress, the abnormal phenomenon was further examined using samples, and possible reasons for defect formation were investigated. Figure 4 shows the XRD patterns of the powder samples sintered at 700–1300 ℃. The data were collected within the 27°–32° range, where the strongest diffraction peaks of the tetragonal and the monoclinic phases can be distinguished. For the TZE powders, as shown in Figs. 4(a-1) and 4(a-2), the monoclinic to tetragonal phase (m → t) transformation is completed at 1000 ℃. Moreover, the tetragonal (101) peak shifts to the low-angle region with increasing temperature. For the MS powders, the t → m transformation occurred at 900 ℃, as shown in Figs. 4(b-1) and 4(b-2). The tetragonal (101) peak shifts first to the high-angle region at temperatures up to 1100 ℃ and then shifts to the low-angle region at 1200 ℃. A similar peak shift is also observed for the SZ powders, as shown in Figs. 4(c-1) and 4(c-2), and the t → m transformation of the powders is completed at 1000 ℃.

With the suspicion that the defects might have resulted from the t → m transformation, HRTEM analysis of the SZ powders sintered at 1000 ℃ was performed. As shown in Fig. 5(a), edge dislocations are hardly observed in the alumina-free sample after the t → m transformation. Large-scale powder manufacturing usually involves particle-size refinement processes, for instance, sand grinding and/or jet milling. Defects might be induced in the raw powders and be inherited within the grains of the final products. Figure 5(b) shows the HRTEM image of the initial MS powders, in which edge dislocation is rarely observed. By comparison, a large amount of edge dislocations can be easily found in the grain interiors of the MS powders sintered at 1000 ℃, as shown in Fig. 5(c). These findings indicate that neither the stress nor the t → m transformation is the main cause of defect formation. The absence and presence of large amounts of edge dislocations inside the grains are also observed in the TZE powders before and after sintering at 1000 ℃, according to the HRTEM images shown in Figs. 5(c) and 5(d), respectively. The results of the HRTEM analysis confirm the essential dependence of defect formation on alumina addition.

Rietveld refinement of the powder samples sintered at 1000–1300 ℃ was further performed, as presented in Fig. 6. The refined unit-cell parameters and compositional analysis of the samples are summarized in Table 1. The relatively lower values of R*p and *R*wp are considered to indicate good profile refinement, where *R*p is the residual difference between observed and calculated plots, and *R*wp uses a weighting function to place more emphasis on good fit between high intensity data points (such as intense peaks) and less emphasis on low intensity data points (such as background). The changes in the values of the unit-cell parameters are rather limited (to the third or fourth decimal place). Previous studies [22,26] reported that the tetragonality (*c*/*a*) of Y-TZP ceramics was dependent on the yttria content. For the SZ powders, the yttria content of the tetragonal phase gradually reached a maximum at 1200 ℃, and the value decreased with increasing temperature. For the TZE and MS powders, the yttria content always decreased within 1000–1300 ℃. Y3+ migration from grain interiors to grain boundaries (or vice versa) leads to a change in the chemical composition of the tetragonal phase. Matsui *et* *al*. [27−29] reported that Y3+ slightly segregated at grain boundaries at 1100 ℃ and became further enriched at higher temperatures. In their works, only Tosoh zirconia powders were used. Zhang *et* *al*. [30] reported the Rietveld compositional analysis of 3Y-TZP ceramics with and without the addition of alumina. In their work, 3Y-TZP ceramics were sintered at 1350–1550 ℃ using powders provided by Sigma-Aldrich, Germany and Tosoh, Japan. Their results showed that the yttria content in the Tosoh samples always decreased with increasing temperature, while the maximum yttria content in the Sigma‒Aldrich samples occurred at 1400 ℃. This result is rather similar to that of the present study. These findings indicate that the behaviors of Y3+ migration are also influenced by the different raw zirconia powders. In addition, adding a larger (or smaller) atom/ion into an existing lattice will strain that lattice. The change in the strain was in good accordance with that of the yttria content. Considering that the doping concentration of alumina is much lower than that of yttria, the predominant effects with respect to the change in the unit-cell parameters can be ascribed to the Y3+ migration rather than that of the Al3+ migration. An increase/decrease in the yttria content in the tetragonal phase results in an increase/decrease in the *d*-space according to Bragg’s law. This fact offers a possible explanation for the diffraction peak shifting in Fig. 4. On the other hand, it makes the possible dissolution of Al3+ even more difficult to distinguish by Rietveld refinement method.

## 4 Discussion

The sintering behaviors of alumina-doped Y-TZP ceramics have been studied previously. An issue of controversy is related to the dissolution of Al2O3 in ZrO2. Matsui *et* *al*. [20] investigated the sinterabilities of fine zirconia powders containing 5 wt% Y2O3. Based on calculations, they reported that the addition of 0.25 wt% Al2O3 decreased the activation energy, and the mechanism at the initial sintering stage changed from grain boundary diffusion (GBD) to volume diffusion (VD). This change in the sintering mechanism was interpreted as a result of the dissolution of Al2O3 in ZrO2 by speculation. Suárez and Sakka [21] studied the effect of 0–10 wt% Al2O3 addition on the initial sintering of 8 mol% Y2O3 cubic ZrO2 (8YSZ). In their work, the mechanism changes from GBD to VD in the initial sintering stage of 2O3–8YSZ was also reported, but they ascribed this phenomenon to the migration of Al3+ ions segregated to grain boundaries.

Al2O3 and ZrO2 (without Y2O3) are known to exhibit rather limited mutual solubility. Previous studies [31,32] have confirmed the existence of 4-, 5-, and 6-fold aluminum coordination in ZrO2 by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). The phase diagram suggested that the solubility of Al3+ in ZrO2 decreased to immeasurable levels below 1200 ℃ under equilibrium conditions [33,34]. The absolute level of Al3+ in the Y-TZP matrix has not been quantified, and the reported values are inconsistent. Hodgson *et* *al*. [35] reported that the solubility of Al3+ in Y-TZP ceramics increased with increasing temperature. However, the solubility limit of Al2O3 in 3Y-TZP ceramics sintered at 1350 ℃ estimated by Yang [36] was approximately 0.2 wt%, while the value was approximately 0.1 mol% (corresponding to 0.13 wt%) in the 3Y-TZP ceramics sintered at 1500 ℃, as evaluated by Matsui *et* *al*. [37].

Hwang and Chen [38] reported that trivalent cations, such as Y3+ and Al3+, were segregated at grain boundaries in zirconia ceramics (Fig. S5 in the ESM). A driving force for the segregation was proposed to be the size mismatch between the dopant and the host cations [39]. Matsui *et* *al*. [40] investigated the microstructural development of the 3Y-TZP ceramics doped with 0.25 wt% Al2O3 and sintered at 1100–1650 ℃. They reported that Al3+ ions started to segregate at grain boundaries at 1100 ℃ and that the segregation increased as the sintering temperature increased. Ross *et* *al*. [41] used high spatial resolution EDS, electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS), HRTEM, and secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) to determine the location of trace quantities of Al2O3 in 0.15 wt% Al2O3-doped 3Y-TZP ceramics sintered at 1450 ℃. In their work, the issue of whether trace amounts of Al3+ were present in the grain interiors remained unclear, and the authors proposed that the absolute levels of Al3+ in the tetragonal zirconia were small and that the dominant effect with respect to the microstructural properties was the segregation of Al3+ at the grain boundaries. Matsui *et* *al*. [40,42] interpreted the increased grain growth rate at 1300–1500 ℃ by taking into account the effect of Al3+ segregation along grain boundaries. In addition, Zhang *et* *al*. [43,44] ascribed the enhanced low-temperature degradation (LTD) resistance of Y-TZP ceramics to the segregated Al3+ at the grain boundaries of the zirconia grains. In their works, the samples were prepared at temperatures higher than 1300 ℃.

Based on the reports above, it can be inferred that Al3+ ions dissolve into the zirconia matrix at relatively low temperatures (below 1100 ℃) and then migrate from grain interiors to grain boundaries as the temperature increases. This phenomenon might be rather similar to that of Y3+ [27−29]. Analogous to Y3+ and other trivalent cations that form solid solutions with zirconia, the local charge balance can be maintained by creating one oxygen vacancy for every two aluminum ions introduced. The process can be expressed using Kröger–Vink notion [45] as shown in Reaction (3):

(3) Al2O3→ZrO22AlZr'+VO..+3OO×

The radius of Zr4+ (0.079 nm) is smaller than that of Y3+ (0.093 nm) but much larger than that of Al3+ (0.050 nm). Based on the HRTEM results, the authors proposed that the relatively large difference (~36.7%) between Zr4+ and Al3+ was the probable reason for the formation of substantial lattice distortions in the alumina-doped samples. The presence of high-density defects enhances sintering kinetics [46] and favors the low-temperature sintering of alumina-doped zirconia ceramics. An increase in the temperature accelerates the diffusion rate of atoms and the annihilation of dislocations and substructures, resulting in a decrease in lattice distortion. The authors also found that defects could hardly be observed in the 3Y-TZP ceramics prepared at 1400 ℃ using MS powders (as shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM). This fact offers a possible explanation for the absence of defects in the 3Y-TZP ceramics prepared at relatively high temperatures in previous studies. In addition, the authors previously reported the presence of hard agglomerates in the initial granules of the TZE powders [47]. The powders could be densified to a relative density greater than 99.9% by 1 h of sintering at 1350 ℃, yielding an average grain size of ~220 nm in the final products. Matsui *et* *al*. [48] investigated the influences of doping methods on the sintering behaviors of alumina-doped zirconia powders during the initial sintering stage. They concluded that the sintering mechanism did not change with the alumina addition, irrespective of the powder mixing or chemical process. They also reported that alumina addition by chemical processes, e.g., homogenous precipitation and the hydrolysis of alkoxide, tended to enhance the grain growth of zirconia. Alumina was added by powder mixing and refined hydrolysis to the MS and TZE powders, respectively. This phenomenon also contributed to the even finer microstructure of the MS powders.

## 5 Conclusions

The present work reported a novel discovery that large amounts of defects were present in the grain interiors of 0.25 wt% Al2O3-doped 3Y-TZP ceramics prepared by pressureless sintering at 1300 ℃. This phenomenon was also confirmed for two types of alumina-doped powders but was absent for the alumina-free samples sintered at 1000 ℃. The HRTEM analysis results showed that neither the stress nor the t → m transformation was the main cause of the inner-grain defects and that the defect formation was essentially dependent on the alumina addition. The authors attributed the defect formation to the relatively large difference between Zr4+ and Al3+ during the dissolution of alumina into zirconia. It was proposed that the sintering kinetics were affected by the presence of substantial defects, which contributed to the low-temperature sintering of alumina-doped zirconia ceramics.
