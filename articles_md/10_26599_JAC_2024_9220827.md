## 1 Introduction

Ultra-high temperature ceramics (UHTCs) contain transition metal borides, carbides, and nitrides, which are a kind of high-temperature materials with melting points above 3000 ℃ due to the unique strong covalent bonds [1−4]. UHTCs have excellent strength, hardness, oxidation resistance, wear resistance, and ablation resistance. They can maintain chemical and structural stability in extreme environments such as high temperatures, low oxygen, and airflow erosion [5]. Therefore, UHTCs can be used in the form of coating materials to protect the hot-end components with refractory alloy material of extreme equipment such as nuclear power equipment, rocket launchers, and hypersonic vehicles [6,7]. And studies of UHTC coatings on the surface of hot-end components are of great significance and necessary for long-life service.

Tantalum-based alloys, as a class of refractory alloys, have high melting point, excellent processing plasticity, strength under high-temperature, making them a key material for hot-end components [8]. However, the poor oxidation resistance of the alloys significantly restricts their practical application in high temperatures environments [9]. To solve this problem, the preparation of UHTC coating on the surface of Ta-based alloy has become one of the best strategies to improve its high-temperature performance. ZrC is a kind of UHTC materials with a high melting point of over 3500 ℃, low density (6.64 g·cm–3), good oxidation resistance, and high-temperature creep strength. Many studies have shown that ZrC has the ability to maintain high strength and form refractory ZrO2 in high temperature environment [10]. Thus, it is a good choice to use ZrC as a surface coating material for Ta-based alloy.

At present, the main preparation technologies of UHTC coatings on refractory alloy substrates mainly include plasma spray [11], chemical vapor deposition (CVD) [12], and other methods. However, the plasma sprayed coatings have the disadvantages of high porosity, weak adhesion to the substrate, and lamellar stacking characteristics [2,11]. Besides, the CVD-UHTC coatings have limited thickness (usually no more than 50 μm for each layer) [13]. The low deposition efficiency accompanied with the expensive product for CVD further limits its industry application [2]. Until now, the current UHTC coatings fabricated by the above methods are still difficult to achieve a stronger ability of ultra-high temperature thermal protection. Therefore, it is urgent to develop a new preparation technology for UHTC coatings.

For years, the laser surface technology is rarely mentioned to fabricate UHTC coatings as the extremely high energy density of laser and the inherent brittleness of UHTC materials. A representative work from the research group of the US Air Force Research Laboratory (AFRL) [14] has made an attempt to prepare ZrB2–ZrC–B4C composite ceramic coating on tungsten substrate by selective laser melting (SLM) technology for the first time. However, the thermal expansion mismatch between the ceramic coating and the substrate was proved to be detrimental to coating integrity after cross-sectioning. Besides, other researches also confirm that the laser surface technology is difficult to produce UHTC coatings [15,16]. Actually, the laser surface technology is usually used to prepare ceramic phase-reinforced metal matrix composite coatings. For example, Zhang *et al.* [17] conducted *in*-*situ* synthesis between graphene and Ti occurred by laser cladding, and the feathery TiC was formed. The coating is metallurgically bonded to the substrate without pores, but the content of the TiC ceramic phase in the coating is low. Lv *et al.* [18] used laser cladding and self-propagating high-temperature synthesis reaction to prepare ZrB2–ZrC reinforced Cu matrix composite coatings on a copper substrate. Although the microhardness of the composite coating (410 HV0.2) is significantly higher than that of the substrate, the ceramic phase content in the coating is extremely low as the dilution rate is high during the laser cladding process. Therefore, the critical challenge during the preparation of laser-formed UHTC coatings is how to simultaneously realize the high content of UHTC phase in the coating and low cracking susceptibility.

In 2017, an innovative laser surface coating technology, high speed laser cladding (HSLC), was developed by the Fraunhofer Institute for Laser Technology ILT in Aachen and RWTH Aachen University in Germany [19,20]. By adjusting the relative position of laser focus and powder focus, the best coupling between the laser and powder would be achieved. According to the basic forming mechanism during the HSLC process, most of the laser energy of HSLC directly acts on the powder, which can easily melt the UHTC powder with a high melting point [21]. The remaining small amount of laser energy acts on the substrate to form a micro-molten pool, and then solidifies in a short time, limiting the diffusion of substrate elements in order to produce a lower dilution rate [22−24]. Therefore, it is expected to prepare UHTC coatings with metallurgical bonding to the substrate and high ceramic content by HSLC technology. By contrast, the total laser energy of the traditional laser-formed coating preparation technology is almost acted on the substrate surface/molten pool. Thus, a large number of longitudinal penetrating cracks, transverse cracks, and severe delamination would appear inside the ceramic coating [14−16].

Recently, we have prepared ZrB2–SiC ceramic–metal coating by HSLC technology on 304 stainless steel [25]. The microstructure and elements of the coating exhibit a continuous gradient distribution from bottom to top. The bottom of the coating exhibits a desirable metallurgical bonding with the substrate while the top of the coating with high contents of ZrB2 and SiC phases exhibits excellent mechanical properties (hardness up to 1810 HV0.5). Then we used HSLC technology to prepare ZrB2 and ZrB2 + TiB2 ceramic coatings on 304 stainless steel [26]. The results showed that additional TiB2 was melted sufficiently during the preparation process and reacted with ZrB2 to generate (Zr*x*,Ti1–*x*)B2 solid solution with an acicular structure. Due to the large lattice distortion inside the (Zr*x*,Ti1–*x*)B2, the hardness reached 1130 HV0.5, which is about 1.5 times that of the HSLC ZrB2 ceramic–metal coating.

In this study, we intended to make further research on demonstrating the promise of using HSLC technology to prepare UHTC coatings on the Ta–10W refractory alloy substrate. By tailoring the most important process parameter (i.e., powder feeding rate), the evolution of microstructure, phase compositions, and the corresponding mechanical properties of HSLC-UHTC coatings were systematically investigated.

### 2.2 HSLC process

The Ta–10W alloy was cleaned with alcohol to remove oil stains from its surface, and the ZrC powder was dried at 120 ℃ for 1 h prior to the start of the preparation experiment. The coating was prepared using an RC-LCD-4000-IPG HSLC system (Ipg (Beijing) Fiber Laser Technology Co., ltd., China) equipped with a synchronous powder feeder. The maximum output power of the laser system is 4000 W, and the spot diameter of the adjustable spot laser cladding head is 1–5 mm. Before the start of HSLC, the laser spot was adjusted to 2 mm and the distance from the laser/powder focus to the substrate surface was adjusted to 1 mm. The working diagram of HSLC is shown in Fig. 2(a). The nozzle was vertically 18 mm from the substrate surface and moved in a “bow” shape relative to the substrate. The specific process parameters are shown in Table 1, where HSLC-1, HSLC-2, and HSLC-3 represent coatings prepared at powder feeding rates of 1.8, 3.0, and 4.2 g·min–1, respectively. It should be noted that after the process optimization of HSLC, we found that compared with the other important parameters (laser powder, scanning speed, lapping rate, etc.), the powder feeding rate exhibited the most influencing parameter to form an ideal HSLC-UHTC coating that we expected. Detailed process exploration can be found on pages 1–4 of the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

### 2.3 Coating characterization

The coatings prepared at different powder feeding rates were cut along the cladding path, and the coating section was polished. The surface morphology and roughness of the coating were measured using an optical profiler (Contour GT-K, Bruker, Germany). The cross-section morphology and microstructure of the coating were observed by the scanning electron microscope (SEM, Apreo 2C, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and the element analysis was carried out by energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS, Oxford Ultim Max65, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). In order to test the phase compositions inside the coating, the surface of the coating was polished with 400#, 800#, 1200#, and 2000# sandpaper in turn. The phases of the coatings were tested by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, XRD-6100, Shimadzu, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation. The data was collected at a scattering angle (2*θ*) ranging from 10° to 90° with a scanning speed of 5 (°)·min−1, and the step size is 0.02°. The coating with a length of 5 μm was cut on the sample using a focused ion beam (FIB, Helios 5 CX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and characterized by the transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos F200S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

The cross-section nano-hardness, elastic modulus, and fracture toughness (* KIC) of the coating were measured using a nanoindentation machine (UNHT, Anton Paar, Austria). The nanoindentation tests were all carried out in the middle regions of the cross-sections of the three polished coatings (Fig. 2(b)) with a load of 4.9 N and a loading time of 10 s. Six test points were selected to get an average value. The fracture toughness was calculated according to Eq. (1) [27]:

(1) KIC=0.0295H0.6E0.4a1.04l−0.54

where H* is the hardness of the coating (GPa), *E* is the elastic modulus of the coating (GPa), *a* is the half-length (m) of the nanoindentation diagonal, and *l* is the length of the crack (m).

The hardness of the coating surface was tested at room temperature (RT, about 25 ℃), 500, 750, and 1000 ℃ under argon atmosphere using high-temperature Vickers hardness test system (ZD-HVZHT-30, ZONE-DE, China). The sample was kept at the required temperature for 10 min before the tests began. The loading time was 10 s, and the loading force was 0.5 kg.

### 3.2 XRD analysis of the HSLC coatings

Figure 5 shows the XRD patterns of the coatings with different powder feeding rates. It can be identified that the main phases in the coating are (Zr,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phase with face-centered cubic (FCC) structure and Ta(W) metal solid solution phase with body-centered cubic (BCC) structure. During the preparation process of HSLC technology, the ZrC feedstock powder and Ta(W) material on the (sub)surface of the substrate were fully melted under the effect of high-density laser. Meanwhile, Ta(W) melt from the substrate would diffuse into the molten pool through the Marangoni effect [29,30]. Then, chemical diffusion and mixing between the ZrC melt and Ta(W) melt is expected to occur. Due to the rapid solidification characteristic of HSLC technology with the cooling rate of higher than 106 K·s–1, relatively sufficient mixing melt would solidify in a very short time. Besides, due to that the atomic radii of tantalum atom (146 pm [31]) is close to that of zirconium atom (160 pm [32]), the tantalum atoms are prone to occupy node positions of FCC-ZrC crystal lattice, resulting in the formation of (Zr,Ta)C ceramic solid solution. At the same time, it is found that ZrC ceramic phase presents in HSLC-2 and HSLC-3 when the powder feeding rate increases from 1.8 to 4.2 g·min–1, which proves that few content of ZrC does not dissolve with the substrate elements during the cladding process. Compared with the standard diffraction peaks of ZrC phase (PDF#73-0477), the 2*θ* angle values of the diffraction peaks of the (Zr,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phases of the coatings shift to higher angles. With the Bragg equation 2*d*sin*θ* = *λ*, the lattice constants of the (Zr,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phases of the three coatings are calculated to be 4.539, 4.568, and 4.557 Å, respectively, which are smaller than that of ZrC (*a*ZrC = 4.669 Å). This is because the radius of Ta atom is smaller than the radius of Zr atom. When Ta atom is dissolved into the ZrC lattice to form a solid solution, the generated stress would cause lattice distortion. Hence, the lattice constant would reduce, leading to a shift of the diffraction peak position to a higher angle [33−35].

### 3.3 Cross-section microstructure of the coatings

Figure 6 shows the cross-section morphologies of the HSLC coatings fabricated at different powder feeding rates. Longitudinal cracks and metallurgically bonded interface between the coating and the substrate are obvious for each HSLC coating. It is hard to observe the unmelted ZrC feedstock powder in all coatings. Figure 7 gives a quantitative comparison of the values of the coating thickness, crack width, and number density of longitudinal cracks for each coating. It is found that with the increase of the powder feeding rate, the three values of the coating showed an increasing trend. For example, when the powder feeding rate increases to 4.2 g·min–1, the coating thickness range of HSLC-3 is between 294 and 420 μm with an average thickness of 345 μm. The crack width range is obviously increased to 1.57–30.24 μm while the number density of longitudinal cracks increased to 2.69.

Formation of the longitudinal cracks in the HSLC coatings can be speculated as follows: (1) High speed laser cladding process with rapid heating and cooling characteristics produces high thermal stress inside the coating. (2) The difference in thermophysical properties between the UHTC coating and refractory alloy substrate causes large mismatch stress [36]. (3) The cracking tendency of the coating with intrinsic brittle property is aggravated under the action of high energy laser beam. Therefore, when the content of the ceramic phase in the coating is higher, the brittleness of the coating is greater. That is, HSLC-3 exhibits the most serious cracking behavior.

In order to further study the effect of powder feeding rate on the element distribution and microstructure of the coating, EDS line scanning was performed on HSLC-1, HSLC-2, and HSLC-3 (Fig. 10). The three coatings contain Ta, W, Zr, and C elements. From the perspective of element distribution, the Ta, W, and Zr elements of HSLC-1 and HSLC-2 are evenly distributed. At the interface between the coating and the substrate, Zr decreases while Ta and W contents increase. During the HSLC process, the substrate elements (Ta, W) are diffused into the molten pool of the coating through the Marangoni effect [37,38]. However, the cooling rate of the HSLC process is extremely fast (more than 106 K·s–1), which makes the molten pool solidify in a very short time and the substrate elements diffuse incompletely in the coating [39]. Unlike HSLC-1 and HSLC-2, HSLC-3 has a large amount of Zr element at the top from the coating to the substrate. From the surface of the coating to the distance of ~50 μm, the content of Zr element tends to be stable. At a distance of approximately ~220 μm away from the surface, the Zr content gradually decreases while the Ta and W contents increase. It is inferred that when the powder feeding rate reaches a relatively high value (i.e., 4.2 g·min–1), excess ZrC powder would be melted on the surface of the main coating. Meanwhile, the substrate elements cannot diffuse to the upper part of the coating. Therefore, HSLC-3 has a lower dilution rate and forms a ceramic phase (ZrC) surface layer with a thickness of about 50 μm at the top of the coating to construct a continuous gradient coating from pure ceramic, solid solution ceramic, to refractory metal, which can significantly improve the bonding properties of the coating.

The morphology of Ta(W) phase gradually transits from the coarse planar & irregular cellular dendritic structures at the bottom of the coating (transition zone), flake-like structure near the transition zone, to the shot-rod structure near the ZrC top layer. According to the classic rapid solidification theory for laser deposition [37,40], temperature field significantly impacts the solidification behavior of deposited track. Combined with the above XRD and EDS analysis, it can be concluded that the content of (Zr,Ta)C ceramic phase in the coating increases correspondingly, which will be beneficial to improve the mechanical properties of the coating.

Figure 14 gives a detailed investigation of the newly formed ZrC top layer. As shown in Figs. 14(a) and 14(b), the brightness of the ZrC phase presents darker, indicating lower content of Ta and W elements. Although few content of Ta element is detected near the interface, the EDS result shown in Fig. 14(c) can also confirm the presence of the ZrC top layer. It is analyzed that when the powder feeding rate reaches a relatively high value, the substrate elements in the microscale molten pool cannot diffuse to the upper part of the coating. Besides, the excessive melting ZrC powder in the upper part of the molten pool crystallizes rapidly, forming a ZrC layer at the top of the coating. Then, a TEM specimen (Fig. 14(d)) cut across the interface with the FIB-SEM technology was made to investigate the crystalline structures of different phases. As shown in Figs. 14(e) and 14(f), the FCC-(Zr,Ta)C solid solution phase in the main coating and FCC-ZrC phase in the top layer were identified with the SAED patterns of areas A and B, respectively. Furthermore, the HRTEM image (Fig. 14(g)) presents the lattice spacing of ~0.165 nm, corresponding to the (220) plane of the FCC-ZrC phase. Besides, a mushy interface with no crack between the ZrC top layer and the main coating is clearly observed, as shown in Fig. 14(b) with SEM mode and Fig. 14(d) with TEM mode. Actually, this phenomenon is significant as the coating–substrate system accomplishes the metallurgical bonding from the top layer, the main coating to the substrate. That is, the whole coating would have a strong bonding ability with the substrate to protect the substrate from ultra-high temperature thermal ablation. Compared with the work on the SLM fabrication of a eutectic UHTC coating reported by King et al.* [14] from AFRL, the HSLC process not only accomplishes the preparation of UHTC coatings with relatively integrated microstructure, but also a strong bonding with the substrate, which paves a new way for fabricating UHTC coatings. Besides, Tan *et al.* [41] prepared Ta–Hf–W–C solid solution coating with the plasma spraying technology on the refractory tungsten substrate. However, the porosity of the coating is as high as 10.6% due to that the UHTC powder with ultra-high melting point is difficult to be melted in the plasma jet. And the mechanical bonding characteristic for the plasma sprayed coating means a lower bonding strength when compared with that of the HLSC coating that presents metallurgical bonding with the refractory substrate.

### 3.5 Mechanical properties of the HSLC coatings

Figure 15(a) shows the load–displacement curves of the substrate and HSLC coatings fabricated at different powder feeding rates. Under the action force of 4.9 N, the maximum indentation displacement of HSLC-1 decreases to nearly half of that of the substrate. Compared with HSLC-1, HSLC-2 exhibits stronger mechanical properties due to that HSLC-2 has a lower content of Ta(W) metal solid solution phase and more content of carbide ceramic solid solution phase. As the powder feeding rate increases, the content of the soft metal phase further decreases. And the hardness of HSLC-3 increases accordingly. So, the maximum indentation depth is minimized under the same loading condition [42]. It is analyzed that the (Zr,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phase leads to the local inhomogeneity of its lattice structure due to the difference of its internal metal elements, hinders the movement of dislocations, and produces a solid solution strengthening effect [43−45], improving the mechanical properties of the coating. Therefore, as the content of carbide solid solution gradually increases, the deformation generated during the loading and unloading process gradually becomes smaller.

Figure 15(b) shows the nano-hardness and elastic modulus of the three coatings and the substrate. The average nano-hardness values of the HSLC-1, HSLC-2, and HSLC-3 are 11.05, 13.86, and 15.09 GPa, respectively, which are significantly higher than that of the substrate (3.42 GPa). The reasons are as follows: (1) With the increase of powder feeding rate, (Zr,Ta)C solid solution ceramics with high hardness in the coating gradually increase while the soft metal phase decreases; (2) Ta solid solution in ZrC causes lattice distortion and hinders dislocation movement, which plays a solid solution strengthening effect. As for the elastic modulus, the values of the three coatings increase first and then decrease. Actually, the elastic modulus is mainly determined by the content of solid solution ceramic and metal phases in the coating. Besides, the width and number density of the original longitudinal cracks in the coating will also affect the elastic modulus. That is to say, the value of the elastic modulus is affected both by the coating composition and the crack defects. In this study, it is shown that the Ta element in the HSLC-2 is more dissolved in the ZrC lattice compared with the element in the HSLC-1. And the metal phase content is reduced accordingly. Hence, HSLC-2 exhibits a stronger ability to resist elastic deformation. Compared with the HSLC-2, however, the HSLC-3 with a higher content of carbide solid solution phase presents lower elastic modulus. It is speculated that the number density and width of the original longitudinal cracks in the coating increase, weakening the ability to resist crack propagation and deformation. The previous study also confirmed that cracks in brittle material could affect the elastic modulus of the material [46]. In summary, the increase of powder feeding rate leads to the increase of the relative content of (Zr,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phase in the coating, which further enhances the solid solution strengthening effect and the hardness.

Figure 16 is the SEM morphologies of the representative indentations of the three coatings. It is clearly shown that there is no crack around the indentation and its four tips of the substrate (Fig. 16(h)), indicating its good ductility. The cracks at the tips of the indentation of HSLC-1 are relatively short (about 3 μm, the yellow arrow in Fig. 16(a)). And the crack arrest occurs at the metal phase (black arrow in Fig. 16(b)). Besides, some small microcracks are generated along the indentation edge (green dashed line in Fig. 16(b)). Except for the four cracks of HSLC-2 extending outward along the tips of the indentation, the side crack near the indentation edge (the green dashed line in Fig. 16(c)) is clearly observed. Besides, crack deflection occurs at the metal phase during the crack propagation process (Fig. 16(d)). As for HSLC-3, there are numerous microcracks near the indentation tip, and the crack propagation length increases significantly. It can be clearly observed that the crack propagates through the carbide solid solution zone straightly while deflects at the metal phase zone. Due to the plasticity and toughness of the metal phase, the crack propagation energy is dissipated and terminated at the metal phase (Figs. 16(f) and 16(g)). According to Eq. (1), the average values of the fracture toughness of HSLC-1, HSLC-2, and HSLC-3 are 4.98, 3.73, and 3.59 MPa·m1/2, respectively. It is analyzed that the energy dissipation capacity of crack propagation decreases with the decrease of metal phase content and the increase of carbide solid solution phase content. Hence, the crack propagation length of HSLC-3 is the longest among the three coatings under the action of the indentation load. The corresponding fracture toughness is the lowest. Therefore, as the powder feeding rate increases, the fracture toughness of the coating decreases.

## 4 Conclusions

The ZrC-based UHTC composite coatings were successfully prepared on Ta–10W alloy substrate by HSLC technology. The relationships between the powder feeding rate, the resulting microstructure, and their mechanical properties were systematically investigated. The following conclusions are drawn:

1) The coating is mainly composed of (Zr,Ta)C ceramic solid solution phase, Ta(W) metal solid solution phase, and a small amount of ZrC phase. With the increase of powder feeding rate, the relative content of (Zr,Ta)C solid solution increases. When the powder feeding rate was 4.2 g·min–1, a ZrC UHTC top layer with a thickness of about 50 μm was successfully fabricated.

2) The coating is relatively dense with relatively few longitudinal cracks. The morphology and constituent elements present a gradient distribution from top to bottom in the coating. Both the substrate–main coating interface and the ZrC top layer–main coating interface exhibit desirable metallurgical bonding.

3) Higher content of (Zr,Ta)C phase accompanied with its solid solution strengthening effect contributes to stronger hardness of the HSLC coating (maximum hardness of up to 15.09±1.88 GPa@4.2 g·min–1 powder feeding rate). However, the fracture toughness decreases from 4.89 to 3.59 MPa·m1/2 with the increase of powder feeding rate. Besides, the elastic modulus of the coating increases first and then decreases (maximum elastic modulus of 201.28±1.29 GPa@3 g·min–1 powder feeding rate) which is determined both by the coating compositions and crack defects.

This study provides a new idea for the preparation of UHTC coatings by HSLC technology. And a comprehensive work on the ablation properties of the HSLC ZrC-based coatings will be reported in the near future.
