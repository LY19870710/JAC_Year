## 1 Introduction

Conventional sintering (CS) of ceramic components is typically performed at high temperatures to accelerate diffusion kinetics. However, this approach is both time-consuming and energy-intensive [1]. Figures 1(a)–1(c) show the process of CS, where the current passing through the heating element generates Joule heat, which is transferred to the porous green body via thermal radiation. As the temperature increases, the density of the sample increases, resulting in a dense sample. Researchers have introduced electric fields into the sintering process of ceramics and found that electric fields can not only facilitate efficient sintering but also modify the microstructure and properties of the resulting materials. The primary sintering methods involving electric fields include spark plasma sintering (SPS) [2], ultrafast high-temperature sintering (UHS) [3], flash sintering (FS) [4], and flash spark plasma sintering (FSPS) [5]. In SPS, electric current heats the graphite die, which transfers heat to the green body via thermal conduction, thereby improving the heating efficiency and enabling rapid sintering (Figs. 1(d)–1(f)). Similarly, at the UHS, the current primarily flows through the graphite felt surrounding the green body. However, its heating rate of 103–104 °C/min is significantly higher than that of SPS. The FS applies a high electric field directly to the green body. Once the green body is heated to a certain temperature by the furnace, it becomes conductive, generating substantial Joule heat and resulting in rapid sintering (Figs. 1(g)–1(i)). For example, nanosized zirconia can be sintered in just 5 s at 850 °C [4], which is the onset temperature [6]. Interestingly, FS can sometimes occur even at room temperature [7−10], making the furnace unnecessary in such cases. FSPS is simple flash sintering performed within an SPS furnace [5]. In conclusion, during the FS process, electric current inevitably passes through the green body, directly affecting its internal mass transfer. This heating method is the most efficient among the techniques discussed. Furthermore, the introduction of electric current can induce various effects, such as enhanced sintering [11,12], the inhibition of grain growth [13−15], an improved compression strain rate [16], and increased electrical conductivity and dielectric properties [17].

Previous research on flash sintering has focused mainly on exploring in which material systems flash sintering can occur. Although FS offers numerous potential advantages, the characterization of the mechanical properties of sintered products is limited. Moreover, when such characterization does exist, it is typically only at the microscopic level [16,18−20]. This lack of data can be attributed to the fact that FS often leads to the formation of defects in the sintered ceramic bulk, such as cracks, voids, and inhomogeneity (Table 1). These issues arise from the excessively fast sintering rate (thermal runaway) and uneven current distribution (leading to dielectric breakdown). Similar issues were observed in our flash sintering experiments (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). Consequently, testing the mechanical or other properties of samples, which require large samples, becomes problematic. Clearly, the difficulty in preparing large bulk samples is a significant bottleneck limiting the application and research of FS.

In addition, the current flowing through a sample induces an atherrmal effect, i.e., despite the temperature rise caused by Joule heating, kinetic analysis indicates that the rates of FS should still be orders of magnitude higher than those of conventional sintering [26]. This suggests that electric fields and/or currents have athermal effects on the ionic diffusion of ceramics in addition to their thermal effects [27]. To determine whether the changes in sintering behavior and material properties caused by FS are due to athermal effects, eliminating the interference of temperature factors is essential. For example, the athermal effect on sintering behavior was investigated by controlling the furnace temperature to ensure that the temperatures of flash-sintered and conventionally sintered samples were comparable [11]. The athermal effect on grain growth was studied via the use of a very small electric field to minimize Joule heating effects and subsequent temperature increases [13]. In this work, we also aim to study the athermal effect through an experimental design that excludes the interference of temperature changes caused by Joule heating. Therefore, how to study the athermal effect is another key challenge.

To address the aforementioned issues, a novel sintering technique, designated ECS (Figs. 1(j)–1(l)), is introduced. This method employs BN paper to serve simultaneously as both an electrical insulator and a thermal insulator. By ensuring that the current flows exclusively through the sample, the ECS guarantees optimal energy utilization. Because excessive heating rates can induce critical defects, ECS improves the densification of the green body by carefully controlling the current through programmed regulation. In other words, the current can be adjusted to achieve a uniform and gradual increase in temperature at a slow rate. Notably, the experimental setup used in the ECS is highly similar to that used in the FSPS. However, in both the FS and FSPS methods, the sample temperature abruptly increases from the set temperature to the required sintering temperature within seconds. In contrast, the ECS method prevents excessive temperature escalation. To mitigate issues related to nonuniform current distributions, materials with electrical conductivities exceeding 106 S/m are selected for this study. Notably, the process operates without the need for external heating devices, starting from room temperature. The resulting bulk materials exhibit uniform density and are free from macroscopic defects. Furthermore, the samples are sufficiently large to undergo mechanical and other characteristic analyses, demonstrating the potential of this method for industrial applications. Additionally, to study the athermal effect, we designed a control group in which the current does not pass through the sample. Unlike the ECS experiment, the control group utilized graphite paper instead of BN paper, with a BN sheet placed between the sample and the indenter to block current flow through the sample. This ensures that both samples experience the same temperature and heating rate, allowing for a direct investigation of the effects of athermal factors on the sintering behavior and material properties.

### 2.1 ECS process

The ECS process involves loading the powder into a graphite die and performing electric field-controlled sintering (ESC; Figs. 2(a)–2(c)). To ensure that the current flows exclusively through the powder, boron nitride (BN) paper (Shandong Industrial Ceramics Research & Design Institute Co., Ltd., China) was placed between the sample and the graphite lining. All the samples (detailed material information can be found in the ESM) were sintered in a vacuum furnace (HHPD 10 FL, FCT, Germany) under a uniaxial pressure of 30–60 MPa, with sample temperatures ranging from 1100–1800 °C and heating rates ranging from 100–300 °C/min. The power source was a direct current pulse power supply, with a maximum voltage of 9 V and a maximum current of 3000 A. The power supply operated for 12 ms and paused for 2 ms, with each individual pulse lasting for 1 ms. Throughout the sintering process, the voltage, current, and specimen thickness (based on this, the volume and density of the sample can be calculated), specimen temperature, and furnace temperature were recorded. Notably, the specimen temperature was measured via infrared light through the thermometer hole in the upper pressure head. The thermometer hole in the upper punch is not a through-hole, so the measured temperature does not represent the sample temperature but rather the temperature of the graphite located close to the sample (10 mm away). Given that graphite has a high thermal conductivity, its thermal relaxation time constant is orders of magnitude shorter than the characteristic timescale of temperature evolution during sintering. The measured temperature can be approximated such that it is equivalent to the specimen temperature.

In this setup, current control is implemented on the basis of real-time specimen temperature feedback. Initially, upon applying a voltage, the current rapidly surges, resembling FS, leading to thermal runaway. Once the sample reaches 300 °C, the system subsequently begins restricting the current ramp rate, causing the current to increase gradually. As a result, the temperature increases at a controlled pace. Throughout this process, the instrument dynamically adjusts the current on the basis of real-time temperature readings from the thermometer hole and the preset heating rate, thereby maintaining precise control over the heating rate.

### 2.2 Process without current

To examine the athermal effect on the sintering process and eliminate the influence of the heating rate, a control group named “without current” was included in the study. The control group (Figs. 2(d)–2(f)) employed 3 mm thick BN pads as insulating layers on both the upper and lower surfaces of the green body. Instead of using BN paper, graphite paper was utilized to enable the current to pass through the graphite die rather than the green body. The temperature measurement of the sample was conducted in the same manner as the ECS experiment, with the test point located in a thermometer hole situated 10 mm away from the sample. Although a BN spacer was added between the sample and the upper punch in this set of experiments, given that BN has high thermal conductivity, it can be approximated that the measured temperature is equivalent to the sample temperature. By comparing the results of the control group with those of the ECS, researchers can isolate and analyze the athermal effect.

### 2.3 Material characterization

In this study, the phase composition at different temperatures was determined via X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, USA) with Cu Kα radiation. The bulk density of the sintered samples was measured via the Archimedes method. This involved immersing the samples in deionized water at room temperature and measuring the resulting buoyant force to calculate the density. The microstructure evolution was characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Merlin, Zeiss, Germany). An energy dispersive X-ray detector (EDX; Merlin, Zeiss, Germany) was used in conjunction with SEM to analyze the element distribution. The dislocation and the low-angle boundary were characterized by an electron back-scattered diffractometer (EBSD; SIGMA500, Zeiss, Germany). To prepare the samples for EBSD analysis, they were mechanically polished with a 2000-grit diamond disc until no visible scratches remained. The samples were subsequently subjected to vibration polishing at a frequency of 67.5 Hz for 2 h using a silica suspension as the polishing liquid. The average grain size was determined by statistically analyzing the grains in SEM or EBSD images.

The flexural strength of the sintered ceramics was measured at room temperature via a three-point bending test (AG-IC, Shimadzu, Japan). The specimens used in the test were chamfered rectangular bars with dimensions of 1.5 mm × 2 mm × 25 mm. The span between the supports was set at 20 mm. The loading rate during the test was set at 0.2 mm/min. Both hardness testing and indentation testing of the material were conducted via a Vickers indenter (2500B, Instron, USA). The indenter was applied with a pressure of 10 kg, and once the desired pressure was reached, it was maintained for dwell time of 15 s.

The central region (1.5 mm × 2 mm × 1 mm) of the bar, which is the stress concentration region of the three-point bending test, was tested via a three-dimensional X-ray microscope (XRM; Xradia620 Versa, Zeiss, Germany). The pixel size was 2.6 μm, the voltage was 160 V, the source filter was HE6, the exposure time was 2.5 s, and the optical magnification was 4 times.

### 3.1 The advantages of the ECS process

The method employed in this study, ECS (Figs. 1(j)–1(l)), builds on FS by using highly electrically conductive materials and controlling the current increase rate through a programmed process for controlling the temperature increase. This enables the production of large samples without cracks. The implementation of ECS uses boron nitride paper for the isolation of the current, and a thermometer hole is employed for monitoring the sample temperature, with the objective of adjusting the current accordingly.

The specifics of the alterations in relative density, current, voltage, and specimen temperature throughout the course of ECS are illustrated in Fig. 4(a). In the onset stage, both the voltage and current increase simultaneously, resulting in an increase in the specimen temperature. This behavior is fundamentally different from the thermal runaway phenomenon observed in FS, where conductivity gradually increases with temperature, further promoting current enhancement. In our approach, the temperature increase is driven solely by the rapid current increase, without the involvement of conductivity changes due to temperature. The pyrometer on top of the sample, positioned through the thermometer hole, rapidly increased the temperature to 253.9 °C, which is the detection limit of the pyrometer. Subsequently, according to the preset program, the current is controlled to achieve the desired temperature rise on the basis of the feedback from the sample temperature testing. During the sintering stage, the current reached 2400 A, leading to significant specimen contraction. In the quenching stage, the applied voltage is terminated, causing the sample temperature to rapidly decrease from 1350 to 253.9 °C. Finally, a large sample without cracks was sintered (Figs. 4(b) and 4(c)). The entire ECS process, from voltage application to sample heating initiation and subsequent cooling to 253.9 °C, is completed in less than 22 min, which is faster than hot-pressing sintering (Fig. S3 in the ESM). Furthermore, at room temperature, the cooling rate is extremely fast, which may assist in preserving the changes in the microstructure of the material caused by the electric field, such as vacancies, interstitial ions, dislocations, etc. [28,29].

In this work, ECS effectively sinters various materials, including MAX phases, cemented carbides, ultrahigh-temperature ceramics, and refractory metals, producing monolithic samples with densities exceeding 99%, as shown in Fig. 5. The sintering parameters are shown in Table S1 in the ESM. To investigate their mechanical strength and structural uniformity, six bars were extracted from each sample for three-point bending tests (Figs. S4 and S5 in the ESM). The cross-sectional morphologies of the different samples are shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM. The strength of the ECS sintered samples lies between the reported minimum and maximum values in the literature, with small error bars. Notably, individual ECS sintered samples, such as Ti3SiC2, Ti2AlN, and Mo, exhibit strengths exceeding the maximum values reported in the literature. Given that all the experiments were performed at room temperature, it was essential for all the samples to exhibit room temperature conductivity, with values exceeding 106 S/m (Table 2). Materials with a conductivity less than 104 S/m at room temperature exhibit low conductivity at room temperature and are unsuitable for sintering through the ECS method (Fig. S7 in the ESM). Furthermore, a higher conductivity enhances the ability to conduct current, resulting in a lower resistance to current transfer within the sample and a more uniform distribution across its cross-sectional direction. This improves the heating uniformity and reduces the number of defects caused by an inhomogeneous current distribution.

The primary advantage of the ECS method over the FS method lies in its ability to maintain a sufficiently low sample heating rate, enabling the production of large samples without macroscopic defects. To confirm that thermal runaway (an excessively rapid heating rate) is the primary cause of defects in FS sintered samples (Table 1), FS experiments should have been performed and compared with ECS in this study. However, the equipment used in this research was incapable of performing FS experiments, such as the uncontrolled scenario depicted in Fig. 7(a), which necessitates a high-power source. Therefore, instead of the uncontrolled experiment, ECS experiments with different heating rates (up to 300 °C/min) were performed for WC–6Co (Fig. 7(a)). Figure 7(b) shows that slower heating rates yield higher strengths. Similar experiments were conducted on two other materials, Mo (Fig. 7(c)) and Ti3SiC2 (Fig. 7(d)). For Mo, the flexural strength decreases sharply when the heating rate reaches 300 °C/min. However, the flexural strength of Ti3SiC2 is almost unaffected by the heating rate, probably due to the superior thermal shock resistance and toughness of the MAX phases [49] compared with those of other materials. Therefore, for most brittle materials, thermal runaway can lead to a decrease in their mechanical properties. Additionally, a higher strength generally corresponds to smaller error bars, which may be attributed to the more uniform current and temperature distributions achieved at slower heating rates, thereby minimizing the occurrence of hot spots.

The decrease in mechanical properties results from changes in the microstructure of the sample. To investigate this, a tomography scan was conducted on the middle region of the WC–6Co bars (Fig. 7(e)) used for the three-point bending tests to detect internal defects. A statistical analysis of the average size, quantity, and distribution of defects within the same volume (Figs. 7(f) and 7(g)) reveals that a faster heating rate results in a greater number of defects and a more inhomogeneous distribution. It can be concluded that in the case of thermal runaway—where the heating rate exceeds 104 °C/min [50], which is much higher than 300 °C/min—more voids and even cracks are likely to form. This conclusion applies to most brittle materials, such as ultrahigh-temperature ceramics. Thus, controlling the temperature increase rate is necessary to achieve a monolithic structure.

In this setup, the BN paper was replaced with graphite paper, and insulating BN spacers were inserted above and below the sample. 1) **WC–6Co**: Under identical temperature, pressure, and heating rate conditions, the application of an electric current resulted in faster shrinkage of the green body (Fig. 9(a)). When the densities were comparable, the sample subjected to current exhibited greater flexural strength (Fig. 9(b)) and hardness (Fig. 9(c)). However, the grain size of the sample with current is larger than that of the sample without current. Comparative analysis of the composition revealed the formation of a new phase, Co3W3C, in the sample with current, whereas the sample without current contained only Co (Fig. 9(e)), which was present in the raw powder (Fig. 6(d)). A semitransparent substance was observed on the grain surfaces of the sample with current, which was absent in the sample without current (Fig. 9(f)). This substance may be Co3W3C. WC–6Co is a liquid phase sintered material that relies heavily on Co as a sintering aid. The application of current leads to localized heating due to increased resistance at grain boundaries [4], making Co or its compounds more prone to melting (Fig. S9 in the ESM). Consequently, the application of current enhances the densification rate of WC–6Co. Furthermore, to investigate the reason for the formation of Co3W3C, WC–6Co powder was tested via XRD at different temperatures in a vacuum environment (Figs. 9(g) and 9(h)). Two kinds of η phases (Co6W6C and Co2W4C) were formed by the reactions among Co, W, and C at approximately 850 °C without current. As Co3W3C is also a kind of η phase, Co3W3C was formed at 850 °C with or without current. The η phase formed in the ECS sample is slightly different from that formed in the characterization. This discrepancy may be attributable to the fact that ECS was carried out in a carbon-rich environment within a graphite die. The η phase remains in the sample with the current until the sintering is finished. In contrast, without current, Co3W3C continues to react with C to form Co and WC at approximately 950 °C (Reaction (1)) [51]. Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that the current suppresses the reaction of Co3W3C and C, resulting in the retention of Co3W3C in the sample with the current, which may increase the flexural strength and hardness of WC–6Co.

Except for Ti3SiC2 (Fig. S11 in the ESM), the current has an athermal effect on most materials. The application of electric current can increase the densification rate, promote the generation of low-angle grain boundaries, and alter the direction of specific chemical reactions, thereby affecting the mechanical properties. These effects are highly dependent on the inherent structure and properties of the materials. Notably, quenching after ECS helps preserve the microstructural changes induced by the current, enabling the investigation of athermal field effects [55]. This study offers a straightforward experimental approach to investigate the effects of various factors on the sintering process, microstructure, material properties, etc.

## 4 Conclusions

This article introduces a novel electric field-assisted sintering method based on flash sintering for the preparation of monolithic materials. The method achieves significant improvements in the mechanical properties of some materials. Compared with other sintering methods, this approach offers the following advantages:

1) High energy utilization efficiency: The current flows through the sample, resulting in high energy efficiency, as the sintering process relies solely on the Joule heat generated by the sample itself.

2) Improved manufacturing efficiency: This method enables rapid heating and cooling, significantly enhancing manufacturing efficiency.

3) Without macroscopic defects, by controlling the current, the temperature is increased slowly and uniformly, facilitating the production of large samples without macroscopic defects.

While this sintering method requires materials to be electrically conductive at room temperature, limiting its application, its broad applicability has been demonstrated for MAX phases, cemented carbides, ultrahigh-temperature ceramics, refractory metals, and potentially other conductive materials. For high-resistivity ceramics, achieving room-temperature conductivity can be accomplished by incorporating conductive materials to form a conductive network within the ceramics. Thus, this sintering method has the potential for widespread application across a range of materials. Additionally, this approach serves not only as a sintering method but also as a heat treatment method.

Finally, distinguishing athermal effects from those of Joule heating on the sintering behavior and properties of materials remains challenging, as the electric field is often associated with Joule heating. However, in this study, a set of controlled experiments was specifically designed to investigate the athermal effects. These experiments have proven to be both highly effective and straightforward.
