## 1 Introduction

With the vigorous development of power Internet of Things (power IoT), real-time online state perception of distributed devices in electrical transmission power lines is an essential support for power grid stability [1−4]. The existing challenges related to the energy supply, high cost, and accuracy of traditional sensors have long been pressing issues [5−7]. Thus, the development of self-powered real-time online monitoring and relevant high-accuracy fault diagnosis technology is urgently needed to ensure the reliable operation of transmission power lines. Flexible composite-based piezoelectric nanogenerators (PENGs) are sensitive to mechanical deformation and can harvest mechanical energy while capturing vibration data, enabling the construction of self-powered sensor nodes or monitoring systems in power IoTs [8−10]. Compared with triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs), which offer higher output performance, PENGs have their own unique advantages, such as small volume, high sensitivity, and stable output in extreme environments, making them highly suitable for monitoring remote power lines [11,12]. However, the current output performance of PENGs is generally low, coupled with limited research on energy management circuitry and constrained application scenarios in sensor technologies. These factors collectively impede their practical implementation and technological advancement [13−15]. Therefore, if the output performance of a PENG can be enhanced through rational piezoelectric ceramic microstructure design, the PENG could still offer a strong competitive advantage and market value within the intelligent sensing layer of power IoTs.

Generally, flexible composite-based PENEs are composed of piezoelectric ceramic fillers (BaTiO3 (BT), PbZrTiO3 (PZT), (K0.5Na0.5)NbO3 (KNN), BiFeO3, etc.) and polymer materials (polyvinylidene fluoride, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), polyimide, etc.), as the composites combine the favorable piezoelectric response of ceramics with the excellent flexibility of polymers [15−20]. The primary reason for the low output of the flexible PENG can be attributed to the generally low effective polarization electric field acting on the piezoelectric ceramic filler. Compared with inorganic piezoelectric fillers, polymer materials, which are typically used as good insulators, have higher resistance. As a result, the polymer matrix consumes most of the polarization voltage, leading to insufficient polarization for these piezoelectric fillers. Thus, the key to improve the output performance of PENGs lies in how to design microstructures of piezoelectric fillers to enhance the effective polarization electric field as much as possible [15,19,21]. This study focuses on low-cost BaTiO3 and its solid solution (Ba,Ca)(Zr,Ti)O3 (BCZT), which are considered among the most promising lead-free ceramics because of their excellent piezoelectric response and environmentally friendly characteristics. The electrical properties of BCZT can be tuned by adjusting the ratios of Ca2+/Ba2+ and Zr4+/Ti4+ in the ABO3 structure [22−24]. In addition, poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) stands out as an ideal candidate owing to its structural flexibility, ease of processing, and superior piezoelectric properties compared with those of most other polymer materials [25]. Thus, the performance of BCZT/PVDF composites-based PENGs needs to be further investigated through microstructural engineering of piezoelectric ceramic fillers, and their applications in self-powered sensing and monitoring systems also need to be further developed.

Previous reports have shown that doping conductive materials into the matrix can improve the transport mechanism of internal charge carriers in PENGs and reduce the internal resistance of composites [15,19,26−28]. Zhou *et al*. [26] used Ag nanoparticles coated onto the surface of BCZT to create a heterojunction interface between Ag and BCZT, and the results demonstrated that the Schottky barrier formed at the heterojunction interface improved the unidirectional mobility of charge carriers, thereby increasing the polarization charge density and piezoelectric potential (electrical outputs of 20 V, 15 µA, and 272 mW·m−2). Wang *et al*. [19] introduced nano-Cu rods into composite materials with an oriented arrangement of KNbO3 to prepare PENGs, which can generate electrical outputs of 64 V and 32 μA when energy was collected from human walking. Das *et al*. [27] used carbon nanotube-modified BT to prepare PENGs with electrical outputs of 42 V, 9 µA, and 31.5 µW. Mahanty *et al*. [28] prepared PENGs using polyaniline-coated BT/PVDF spun fibers, and the electrical outputs of the PENGs were 68 V, 3 µA, and 225 mW·m−2. Inspired by these studies, we design a new piezoelectric ceramic fiber with a new branch-heterostructure involving the grafting of Ag-coated BCZT nanoparticles (BCZT-SP@Ag) onto BCZT fibers (BCZT-NF) to form a branch heterostructure (BCZT-NF@BCZT-SP@Ag) with multiple interface regions. The high surface area of BCZT-SPs on the surface of BCZT-NF creates more interfaces and promotes carrier movement near piezoelectric fillers. Additionally, coating with Ag forms multiple heterogeneous interface regions within the matrix, enhancing the induced polarization in the PENG. In practical applications, recent studies on sensing systems built with nanogenerator technology in power IoTs have relied primarily on TENGs, given their significantly higher output performance than PENGs [29−32]. Few attempts have been made to investigate PENG-based self-powered monitoring systems. However, TENGs generate electrical signals through mechanical friction, and the friction and electrode layers will inevitably wear out over time, making it challenging to maintain a long-lasting and stable electrical signal output. This is often the primary obstacle to its application in remote transmission line monitoring [1]. To address this concern, designing high-performance PENGs that are free from wear and tear could be an effective solution for addressing such challenges.

In this work, we successfully enhanced the output performance of the PENG by designing a new branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber, where BCZT@Ag nanoparticles are grown on the surface of BCZT fibers to form a branch-heterostructure (BCZT-NF@BCZT-SP@Ag). This piezoelectric ceramic fiber can induce an enhanced effective polarization electric field and improve the charge transport mechanisms within the PENG during the polarization and compression processes. The branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber-based PENG results in significantly increased outputs of 96.4 V and 15.52 µA in tapping mode, which are approximately 3 and 6 times greater than those of traditional BCZT-NF ceramic fiber-doped PENGs. The output performance of our PENG exceeds most reported results from similar studies in recent years [17,26−28,33−37]. On the premise of ensuring high-performance output, on the basis of our designed PENG combined with a communication circuit design and machine learning recognition, we have developed a self-powered monitoring system for antivibration devices in power lines, as shown in Fig. 1. A flexible PENG is synthesized using branch-heterostructure fibers doped with PVDF. Au electrodes are sprayed on both sides of the PENG, which is then encapsulated in polyimide (PI) to form an approximately 2.5 cm × 7 cm sandwich structure, as shown in ① in Fig. 1. The working states of the antivibration device (i.e., the vibration damper) can be monitored by recording the vibration information of the transmission power line using the PENG, and then the signal is transmitted to the receiving and mobile ends. After machine learning analysis, if any abnormalities are detected, warning results are promptly communicated to on-site personnel for maintenance and intervention to prevent further damage. To the best of our knowledge, there are currently no reports on the development of a monitoring system for the working states of vibration dampers in transmission power lines based on PENGs. More importantly, this work will promote the practical process of piezoelectric ceramics in the scope of self-powered power IoT systems, which offers a new solution for the safe monitoring of transmission power lines.

## 2 Experimental

The fabrication process for branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber-based PENGs is briefly outlined in Fig. 2. The branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers were prepared through coaxial electrospinning and chemical reduction technology, as shown in Figs. 2(a)–2(c). The raw materials were thoroughly mixed to prepare BCZT-NF in a solvent to prepare solution No. 1. Additionally, a specific amount of BCZT-SP was dispersed in another solvent and labeled as solution No. 2. Both solutions were transferred into two plastic syringes, and a coaxial electrospinning process was subsequently performed to synthesize low-humidity preform piezoelectric fibers (Fig. 2(a)). After sintering treatment, a ceramic fiber structure in which BCZT-SPs were attached to BCZT-NFs (BCZT-NF@BCZT-SPs) was obtained (Fig. 2(b)). Next, we employed a chemical reduction method to deposit nano-Ag crystallites onto the surface of BCZT-SP; subsequently, a strong reducing agent was used to promote the growth of the Ag crystallites, resulting in BCZT-NF@BCZT-SP@Ag fibers, in which BCZT-NF served as the backbone, while BCZT-SP@Ag was similar to a branch growing from it (Fig. 2(c)). The branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers were evenly mixed with PVDF powders in dimethylformamide (DMF) (Fig. 2(d)), and a composite film was then prepared using a chemical coating method (Fig. 2(e)). Two Au layers were sprayed as the top and bottom electrodes of the composites. After the polarization process, the composite film was encapsulated in PI and assembled into the PENG (Fig. 2(f)). The detailed process of the experimental synthesis is provided in Sections 2.1–2.3.

### 2.1 Materials

PVDF powder (*M*w ≈ 275,000), DMF, and acetone were purchased from Sigma-aldrich Co., Ltd. AgNO3, SnCl2, and CH2O were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. BaCl2·H2O, ZrOCl2·8H2O, CaCl2, and tetrabutyl titanate were purchased from Aladdin Industrial Corporation. Ba(OH)2·8H2O, Ca(OH)2, Zr(C5H7O2)4, and polyvinyl pyrrolidone were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd.

### 2.2 Fabrication of branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers

The first step is to synthesize 47(Ba0.7Ca0.3)TiO3–53Ba(Zr0.2Ti0.8)O3 nanoparticles (BCZT-SPs). The predetermined amounts of BaCl2·H2O, CaCl2, and ZrOCl2·8H2O were dispersed in deionized water with adequate stirring (approximately 1–2 h). Then, NaOH was added to the mixture as a pH regulator, and the molar ratio of Ba2+ to OH− was regulated at approximately 1 : 5. The final mixtures were enclosed in a Teflon-lined autoclave, and a hydrothermal reaction was subsequently carried out at 200 °C for 24 h and naturally cooled to room temperature in air. Finally, the product was collected and dried for 12 h to obtain BCZT-SPs.

The second step is to synthesize BCZT-NF@BCZT-SP fibers. The predetermined amounts of Ba(OH)2·8H2O, Ca(OH)2, Zr(C5H7O2)4, and tetrabutyl titanate as raw materials were dissolved in a mixed solvent of acetic acid and ethylene glycol methyl ether (volume ratio of 1 : 1) to obtain solution No. 1. In addition, a certain amount of BCZT-SP was dispersed in the other mixed solvent to obtain solution No. 2, and moderate polyvinyl pyrrolidone was selected to adjust the solution viscosity. Subsequently, both No. 1 and No. 2 solutions were transferred into two plastic syringes, and a coaxial electrospinning process was carried out to prepare low-humidity preform piezoelectric fibers (Fig. 2(a)), which was performed under a high voltage of 18 kV with a rotating drum at a speed of 200 r/min and a ﬂow rate of approximately 20 μL/min. After the abovementioned process, the low-humidity prefabricated fibers were sintered at 800 °C for 4.5 h in a muffle furnace to obtain BCZT-NF@BCZT-SP fibers (Fig. 2(b)).

The third step is to prepare branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers. The predetermined amounts of BCZT-NF@BCZT-SP fibers were fully mixed in deionized water, and HCl was selected to regulate the pH value to approximately 5. Subsequently, SnCl2 (0.08 mol/L) was added to the mixed solution to induce the adsorption phenomenon of Sn2+ coated on the surface of BCZT-SP. Next, these Sn2+-coated BCZT-NF@BCZT-SP fillers were transferred to a silver ammonia solution (0.3 mol/L AgNO3) with stirring, which deposited Ag seeds on the surface of BCZT-SP. Finally, these piezoelectric fibers were placed in a mixed solution consisting of a formaldehyde solution, a silver ammonia solution, and alcohol (volume ratio of approximately 1 : 4 : 8) and agitated for approximately 12 h, after which the Ag seedlings coated on the surface of BCZT-NF@BCZT-SP gradually grew to form nanoparticles. After rinsing and stoving, these BCZT-NF@BCZT-SP@Ag branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers were obtained (Fig. 2(c)).

### 2.3 Preparation of PENGs

Branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber-doped PVDF composites with different concentrations were prepared by the conventional solution casting method. A predetermined amount of branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers and PVDF powders was gradually added to the DMF solution with full stirring at room temperature for 12 h to prepare a casting solution (Fig. 2(d)). The product was then poured onto the coating equipment and dried at 80 °C until the solvent evaporated (Fig. 2(e)). The desired film can be obtained with a thickness of approximately 30 μm (Fig. 2(f)). The composite film of branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers doped with PVDF was diced into pieces 2 cm × 2 cm in size. After that, an Au layer with a radius of approximately 0.85 cm was sprayed on the upper and lower surfaces of the composite film as electrodes, and a strong external direct current (DC) electric ﬁeld of approximately 40 kV/mm for 24 h at 80 °C was used to align the polar dipoles of the composite film. After the polarization process, the composite film was encapsulated in PI to assemble into the PENG.

### 2.4 Characterization and measurement

The micromorphology of the piezoelectric fillers and composite films was detected via a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Nova Nano SEM 450, FEI, USA). The micromorphology of the piezoelectric fillers was characterized via a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-2100, JEOL, Japan). The crystal structures of the samples were evaluated via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X’Pert PRO, PANalytical, the Netherlands; D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation. The chemical structure and functional groups of the composite films were characterized by a Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrometer (Vertex 70 V, Bruker, Germany). The dielectric constant and dielectric loss of the composite films were measured via a precision impedance analyzer (Alpha-A, Novocontrol, Germany). The ferroelectric polarization−electric field (*P−E*) loops and leakage current characteristics of the samples were characterized by a comprehensive electrical performance tester (Multiferroic, Radiant Technologies, USA). The piezoelectric charge coefficient *d*33 of the composite films was measured with a quasistatic piezoelectric meter (ZJ-3AN, Institute of Acoustics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China). The output performance of a series of PENGs was measured by an electrometer (Keithley 6514, USA). Piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) was employed to explore the piezoelectric response of the composite fibers using an Asylum Research MFP-3D (Oxford Instrument, UK).

### 3.1 Structure and morphology

The structure and morphology of synthesized branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers were characterized by XRD, TEM, and SEM, as shown in Fig. 3. For comparison, the morphology of BCZT-NF prepared via traditional processes is shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), which features typical one-dimensional (1D) materials with smooth surfaces. Furthermore, these spherical BCZT@Ag particles are attached to BCZT-NF, resembling branches growing on the main trunk, as shown in Fig. 3(a). These smaller Ag nanospheres can be observed on the BCZT-SP in the inset of Fig. 3(a), forming a BCZT-NF@BCZT-SP@Ag structure, which is referred to as a branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber. The synthesized branch heterostructure ceramic fibers have an average size of approximately 300–500 nm. As shown in Fig. 3(b), the characteristic diffraction peaks at 2*θ* = 21.9°, 31.7°, 38.1°, 45.2°, 55.7°, and 65.4° correspond to the (100), (110), (111), (200), (211), and (220) crystal planes of BCZT (Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) card No. 89-1428), which are labeled ◆. The other four crystalline peaks at approximately 38.5°, 44.2°, 64.2°, and 77.5° belong to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) crystal planes of the face-centered cubic (fcc) Ag phase (JCPDS card No. 4-0783), which have been labeled by ♥. The XRD pattern indicates that BCZT has a perovskite-type ABO3 structure and that the introduction of Ag nanoparticles does not disrupt its crystal structure to generate additional secondary phases. Figure 3(c) shows a TEM image of a single randomly selected branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber, and the intact structure is consistent with the SEM studies. The insets in this figure show that BCZT-SP@Ag particles are attached to the surface of the main trunk BCZT-NF, with a clear heterogeneous interface between Ag and BCZT-SP observed via high-resolution TEM. The crystal lattice spacing of 0.231 nm corresponds to the (111) crystal plane of cubic Ag, whereas the lattice spacing of 0.291 nm corresponds to the (110) crystal plane of BCZT, further confirming the successful formation of the Ag and BCZT heterojunction. These Ag nanoparticles, with an average diameter of 5–7 nm, are uniformly coated on the surface of BCZT-SP. Figure 3(d) shows the elemental mapping spectral analysis for a single branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber, with the four main elements Ba, Ti, O, and Ag distributed homogeneously throughout the branch structure fiber, confirming that this is the desired structure of the filler as intended in our design. Moreover, Fig. 3(e) shows the atomic ratios of the four elements in a randomly selected local area around the branch heterostructure ceramic fiber, revealing that the percentage (at%) of Ag is approximately 7.21%. In addition, the ratio of Ba : Ti is approximately 1 : 1, which is almost consistent with the calculated stoichiometry.

The degree of crystallinity of the electroactive β-*phase is an important factor affecting the piezoelectric performance of PVDF-based composites [38,39]. To investigate the crystalline structure of the materials, a series of FT-IR spectral analyses and XRD patterns were carried out, as shown in Fig. 4. In addition, these vibrational bands near 838 cm−1 for CH2 rocking, 1070 cm−1 for C–C stretching, and 1411 cm−1 for CH2 bending are typical β*-*phase characteristics of PVDF [39,40]. To analyze the phase composition of the composites more accurately, 2*θ* angles ranging from approximately 15° to 21° were increased, as shown in Fig. 4(b). When these 20 wt% piezoelectric fillers are introduced in sequence (BCZT-NF, BCZT-NF@SP, and branch-heterostructure), the area of the diffraction peak corresponding to the β crystalline phases is gradually increased. Both the FT-IR and XRD results indicate that the introduction of suitable doped fillers can increase the intensity of the β peak and reduce the intensity of the α peak. All the coefficients of matching are better than 96.8%, indicating a good correlation between the fitting and experimental curves. After Gaussian fitting, independent diffraction peaks can be observed, in which the peaks near approximately 18.4° and 20.3° in the composites correspond to reflections of the respective nonpolar α crystalline phase and electroactive β crystalline phase (JCPDS Card No. 38-1638). By comparing the XRD patterns of the composite films with different fillers, the β and α peak integral areas of the composite films can be estimated and are shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM; the β crystalline phases of pure PVDF, BCZT-NF, and branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers doped with PVDF are approximately 41%, 56%, 61%, and 70%, respectively. Among them, the peak integral β in the branch heterostructure ceramic fibers doped with PVDF is the largest, which proves that its β phase content is the highest. Although there is a slight difference in the quantitative data among the calculations, the overall trend remains consistent in the FT-IR results. With the addition of BCZT-NF, β crystallinity begins to rise compared with that of pure PVDF because ferroelectric BCZT can act as a nucleating agent to promote dipole orientation [44]. For doped BCZT-NF@SP, more interface areas introduced by BCZT-SP are beneficial for turning trans-gauche–trans-gauche (TGTG) conformation into the all-trans (TTTT) conformation, leading to the formation of β crystals in PVDF. After Ag is coated on BCZT-SP, the increased dipole moment of the fillers and the electrostatic interaction between the piezoelectric particles and –CH2–CF2– chains in the PVDF matrix further promote the formation of more local polar groups [26,45]. The experimental results demonstrate that the optimal design of piezoelectric fillers can significantly promote β-phase formation and stabilization, consequently improving the piezoelectric output performance.

Figure 4(e) shows the cross-sectional SEM morphology of 20 wt% branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers doped with PVDF. These piezoelectric fillers are uniformly distributed in the PVDF matrix, and no obvious agglomeration can be found. In addition, the composite films exhibit a dense and smooth cross section, and no pores or defects can be discovered, which shows good compatibility between the piezoelectric fillers and the polymer matrix. The thickness of the composite film is approximately 30 μm and presents a flat and neat surface, as depicted in the inset of Fig. 4(e). The elemental mapping spectral analysis of branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers dispersed in PVDF is provided in Fig. 4(f). The element mapping clearly reveals that four main elements, Ba, Ti, O, and Ag, are detected in the area where the piezoelectric fillers appear without obvious agglomeration. Overall, the branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber-doped composite film has a uniform and stable structure with good compatibility with the matrix, which is beneficial for ensuring the piezoelectric output of PENGs.

### 3.2 Electrical performance measurements of materials

The effects of introducing branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers on the ferroelectric, piezoelectric, and dielectric properties of composites, which are closely related to the output performance of PENGs, are investigated. The *P–E* hysteresis loops of pure PVDF, traditional BCZT-NF, BCZT-NF@SP, and branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber-doped PVDF composites were measured at 100 kV/cm with a frequency of 100 Hz to study the effect of the filler structure on basic polarization behavior, as shown in Fig. 5(a). The remanent polarization (*P*r) and maximum polarization (*P*max) of pure PVDF, BCZT-NF/PVDF, and BCZT-NF@SP/PVDF composites are 0.006 and 0.043 μC·cm−2, 0.019 and 0.068 μC·cm−2, and 0.022 and 0.091 μC·cm−2, respectively. After branch-heterostructure design, the *P*r and *P*max of the composites reached 0.040 and 0.154 μC·cm−2, respectively, and significantly enhanced induced polarization was observed. Figure 5(b) shows the leakage current density of samples with a maximum electric field of approximately 200 kV·cm−1. The leakage current density of all samples increases with increasing electric field, which shows a strong electric field dependence on the leakage current density. The leakage current density of a sample is also strongly affected by the microstructure of the filler. The leakage current densities of the pure PVDF, BCZT-NF/PVDF, and BCZT-NF@SP/PVDF composites are 4.60×10−8, 7.69×10−8, and 8.71×10−8 A·m−2, respectively, at 200 kV·cm−1. The leakage current in the branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber-doped PVDF composites suddenly increased to 1.58×10−7 A·m2 at 200 kV·cm−1, indicating that many charge carriers were present in the composites after the introduction of the Ag shell. As a whole, even in branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber-doped PVDF composites, the leakage current of the composite still remains relatively low, and the good insulation performance of the sample can guarantee superior piezoelectric performance. Moreover, the dielectric permittivity and loss tangent of the four samples were measured at a frequency of 100 Hz, as shown in Fig. 5(c). The dielectric permittivity of pure PVDF is 8, and it increases to 13, 15, and 24 with the doping of BCZT-NF, BCZT-NF@SP, and branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers, respectively. The remarkable increase in the dielectric permittivity can be attributed to two main factors. On the one hand, the BCZT filler has a higher dielectric constant than does PVDF. On the other hand, many blocked charge carriers at internal interfaces generate interfacial polarization in composites under an electric field, namely, the Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars (MWS) effect [45,46]. The introduction of an Ag shell in branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers further increased the intensity of the local electric field at these interfaces and promoted the migration and accumulation of carriers, leading to a higher dielectric constant of approximately 24 in the composites. In addition, as shown in Fig. 5(c), the loss tangent of the composites also clearly increases in branch-heterostructure fiber-based composites compared with that of the other composites, in which the loss tangents are 0.037, 0.045, 0.049, and 0.080, corresponding to pure PVDF, BCZT-NF/PVDF, BCZT-NF@SP/PVDF, and branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber doped composites. This is attributed to the increased relaxation polarization loss and conduction loss after the introduction of BCZT and Ag. Figure 5(d) shows the *d*33 values of four types of fibers corresponding to 9, 22, 28, and 55 pC·N−1, which shows that the branch-heterostructure design can significantly enhance the piezoelectric performance of composites. ### 3.3 PENG output performance measurements

The deformation of the PENG generates piezoelectric output primarily through tapping and bending modes, and the two working mechanisms are illustrated in Fig. 7. At the initial state in both modes, these electric dipoles within the PENG are highly oriented after polarization treatment (Figs. 7(a1) and 7(b1)). When a vertical pressure force is applied on the PENG in tapping mode, the deformation changes the orientation of these electric dipoles, generating a piezoelectric potential on the surface of the piezoelectric material. Free electrons subsequently flow from one side electrode to the other side in the external circuit to neutralize the piezoelectric potential (Fig. 7(a2)). When the external force is released, the accumulated free charges then flow back in the opposite direction (Fig. 7(a3)). Additionally, a lateral strain is induced in the PENG during bending mode, which also compresses the electric dipole moments and alters the polarization state of the PENG. The piezoelectric potential formed on the surface of the composites then drives electrons from the elongated side to the compressive side through the external circuit (Fig. 7(b2)). Similarly, when the strain is released, the opposite piezoelectric signal can be observed (Fig. 7(b3)**)**. According to the working principle, a regular piezoelectric output is obtained by periodically tapping/bending and releasing the PENG.

*
Fig. 7Schematic diagrams of working mechanism of PENGs under (a) tapping and (b) bending–releasing cycles.

After sufficient polarization under a high DC electric field, the output voltage and current of the PENGs were tested under a periodic pressure of 30 N at a frequency of 2 Hz at room temperature. Optimizing the weight ratio of the BCZT-NF and BCZT-SP in the PENG is a significant task for maximizing the output performance of the device; thus, output tests of a series of PENGs with different weight ratios of BCZT-NF and BCZT-SP of 4 : 1, 3 : 1, 2 : 1 and 1 : 1 were carried out, as shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. As the BCZT-SP component increases (BCZT-NF : BCZT-SP from 4 : 1 to 2 : 1), the output performance of PENGs gradually increases, with open-circuit peak-to-peak voltage (V*pp) and short-circuit peak-to-peak current (*I*pp) ranging from 31.6 V and 2.39 μA to 46.2 V and 3.1 μA, respectively, in tapping mode. This occurs because the nano BCZT-SPs attached to the BCZT-NF surface provide a larger interfacial area, thereby increasing the polarization potential of these piezoelectric fillers. However, at a ratio of 1 : 1, the output performance of the PENG decreases to 40.7 V and 2.88 μA. An excessive amount of BCZT-SP will cause agglomeration on the surface of BCZT-NF, resulting in a decrease in the piezoelectric output of the PENG.

Since the BCZT-NF@SP filler with a 2 : 1 ratio (BCZT-NF : BCZT-SP) has the best piezoelectric output performance, it has been selected to fabricate branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers for preparing PENGs. As shown in Figs. 8(a)–8(d), the output performances of PENGs based on 10, 20, and 30 wt% branch heterostructure fillers were determined in tapping mode (30 N, 2 Hz). Figures 8(a) and 8(b) show the *V*oc and *I*sc of a series of PENGs measured under a periodic press force. Compared with those of neat PVDF-based PENGs, the outputs are significantly greater with increasing concentrations of branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers. As the filler concentration increases from 0 to 20 wt%, both the *V*oc and *I*sc of the PENGs gradually increase from 5.7 V (*V*pp ≈ 9.8 V) and 0.73 μA (*I*pp ≈ 1.35 µA) to 52.3 V (*V*pp ≈ 96.4 V) and 8.96 μA (*I*pp ≈ 15.52 µA), respectively. The increasing tendency can be understood in that more electroactive materials of piezoelectric BCZT fillers induce a larger piezoelectric response, and more interfacial polarization between inorganic particles and the organic matrix can be generated in composites. However, once the filler concentration reaches 30 wt%, the output performance of the PENG reaches saturation and slightly decreases to 38.3 V (*V*pp ≈ 69.5 V) and 5.76 μA (*I*pp ≈ 10.48 µA). In bending mode (120° bending angle, 2 Hz), the output performance of the PENG has a similar trend to that in tapping mode. As shown in Figs. 8(c) and 8(d), the 20 wt% branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber-doped PENG has the optimum electrical output, in which the *V*oc and *I*sc are 27.1 V (*V*pp ≈ 49.47 V) and 4.86 μA (*I*pp ≈ 8.97 µA), respectively. As the filler concentration further increased to 30 wt%, the *V*oc and *I*sc of the PENG decreased to 21.3 V (*V*pp ≈ 38.6 V) and 3.24 μA (*I*pp ≈ 6.12 µA), respectively. This behavior can be attributed to the following factors: (i) The incorporation of high-content inorganic filler significantly increases the solution viscosity during processing, resulting in filler agglomeration and inhomogeneous dispersion; (ii) excessive Ag particles facilitate conductive pathway formation, consequently increasing the leakage current density and thereby neutralizing the induced piezoelectric charges in PENGs. Thus, a moderate concentration of branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers can significantly increase the electrical output of PENGs; nevertheless, an excessively doped concentration decreases their output performance.

To investigate the effects of the branch-heterostructure filler design on the electrical output of PENGs, three types of PENGs with different fillers were prepared: BCZT-NF, BCZT-NF@SP, and branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers. A comparison of the electrical outputs of different types of PENGs is presented in Figs. 8(e) and 8(f). With the same 20 wt% concentration, the *V*pp values of traditional BCZT-NF, BCZT-NF@SP, and branch-heterostructure filler-doped PENGs are 30.6 and 18.3 V, 46.2 and 26.1 V, 96.4 and 49.5 V, and the *I*pp values of the above three types of PENGs are 2.40 and 1.83 µA, 3.10 and 2.25 µA, 15.52 and 8.97 µA under tapping/bending working modes, respectively. Among these three types of PENGs, the branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber-doped PENG possesses optimum piezoelectric output performance both in tapping and bending modes; its output voltages (96.4 and 49.5 V) are approximately 2.7–3.2 times greater, and its output currents (15.52 and 8.97 µA) are approximately 4.9–6.5 times greater than those of the BCZT-NF-doped PENG and BCZT-NF@SP-doped PENG. Compared with traditional BCZT-NF fillers, these nano BCZT-SPs, which have large surface areas on the surface of BCZT-NF, can increase the interface area to promote the flow of carriers near the filler and thus improve the polarization efficiency. Then, Ag coated on BCZT-SPs on the surface of branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers will induce a high localized field concentration to form a “boundary layer capacitor effect”, contributing to efficient dipole polarization and consequently enhanced piezoelectric outputs [45,48]. Furthermore, during the compressive state of the PENG, the interface between nano-Ag and BCZT-SP can be considered a Schottky barrier, and the transport mechanism of free charges in branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers is shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM. The microdeformation of BCZT fibers generates an internal piezoelectric potential. Assuming that nano-Ag has a nearly zero potential (*V*Ag = 0), at the positive potential surface (*V*B+) of BCZT-SP, the interface between Ag and BCZT-SP is negatively biased for Δ*V* = *V*Ag – *V*B+ B−), the Ag and BCZT-SP interface behaves like a positively biased Schottky diode for Δ*V* = *V*Ag – *V*B− > 0, leading to a sudden increase in the flow of free electrons from the BCZT-SP to the nano-Ag [26,49]. The polarization charges can be trapped and aggregated in the nano-Ag, which results in the enhancement of piezoelectric outputs at the macroscopic scale.

Notably, we also compared the output performances of the branch-heterostructure fiber-based PENG and BCZT-NF@Ag fiber-based PENG, as shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM. According to the mechanistic conclusions derived above, both piezoelectric fiber structures can enhance the effective polarization field and form a Schottky heterojunction of Ag and BCZT. However, with the same 20 wt% doping composition, the output voltage of the branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber-based PENG (~96 V) is significantly better than that of the BCZT-NF@Ag-based PENG (~66 V). The reason for this is that the BCZT-SP attached to the surface of BCZT-NF in the branch-heterostructure fibers provides a larger surface area, offering more contact with nano-Ag than the BCZT-NF@Ag fiber structure does, thus forming a larger positively biased Schottky diode region that further promotes the flow of free electrons from the BCZT to the nano-Ag.

These smaller balls that coat the surface of larger spheres are given the features of Ag, and BCZT-SP@Ag can be simulated. Then, they are put in a cuboid, which is set as the physical feature of PVDF, to simulate PENGs with different piezoelectric fillers doped. A vertical polarization voltage of 50 V is applied to the top surface of the PENG, and its bottom position is set as the zero-electric potential. ### 3.4 Real-time power line self-powered monitoring system based on PENGs

Vibration dampers can effectively reduce the vibration amplitude of transmission power lines and prevent the occurrence of conductor galloping or aeolian vibration, thereby effectively ensuring the safety of transmission power lines [1,3,4]. Therefore, monitoring the working status of the vibration damper is necessary. The vibration of the transmission power line is driven by a vibration exciter operating at approximately 3 Hz with a force of 50 N. Our designed high-performance PENG can be regarded as a self-powered perception layer in a power line monitoring system. The PENG was cut into 2.5 cm × 7 cm pieces and fixed near a vibration damper to efficiently capture the vibration information of the power line originating from piezoelectric signals. The practical fundamental structure of this system is schematically summarized in Fig. S9 in the ESM. Through the signal processing circuit, which consists primarily of a differential amplification circuit, an in-phase proportional amplification circuit, and a second-order voltage-controlled low-pass filter circuit (Fig. S10 in the ESM), the signal captured by the PENG is transmitted to a wireless communication circuit based on the STM32 series microprocessor MCU (Fig. S11 in the ESM). After the above circuit system, the data can be transmitted to the intelligent receiver terminal. An intelligent receiver terminal, such as a computer or phone, can harvest, process, and display vibration data for subsequent analysis and judgment. For different states, 500 signal data points for each channel are recorded for the machine learning data. Subsequently, 100 sets of data samples were collected, of which 80 sets were used for model training and 20 sets were used for testing. Owing to the high-performance branch-heterostructure ceramic fiber-based PENG design, clear and precise monitoring signals for the vibration state of the power line can be obtained via this self-powered system.

An application photograph of this monitoring system based on the PENG is presented in Figs. 11(a) and 11(b), which represent normal and invalid operation of the vibration damper device, respectively. The states of the power line at the receiving end are shown in Fig. S12 in the ESM. A live demonstration and more details are also displayed in Video S1 in the ESM. Once an anomaly occurs in the vibration damper, an alarm is triggered at the receiving end (computer or mobile phone) after data capture and model processing. As shown in Figs. 11(c)–11(e), the characteristic electrical signals of three different vibration states of the power line were captured, indicating whether the vibration damper functions normally, experiences looseness, or has failed. Figures 11(f) and 11(g) show the training of the deep residual network concerning the change process of accuracy and the loss function. With increasing training epochs, the accuracy gradually improves, the error decreases, and it eventually stabilizes. In the confusion matrix shown in Fig. 11(h), the system achieves an average recognition accuracy of approximately 96% in detecting the normal, loose, and faulty states of the vibration damper. When the vibration damper becomes loose, the self-powered sensing device will detect this condition and alert onsite personnel to fix the damper, preventing potential sliding wear on the power line. Moreover, if the vibration damper falls and fails, the system will alert maintenance personnel to replace it at the designated node, which can be further integrated with a commercial global positioning system module. Although it is only a preliminary model, PENG-based self-powered sensing systems can be expanded to monitor and alert various abnormal conditions of power lines, demonstrating significant potential for ensuring the safe operation of power grids.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, this work presents a new strategy to increase the electrical output of PENGs by designing branch-heterostructure ceramic fibers. This new structure can significantly improve the charge transport mechanism and the effective polarization electric field, thereby enhancing the PENG output, as confirmed by both simulation and experimental results. More importantly, this PENG can be combined with a management circuit and learning-assisted data analytics to construct a vibration monitoring system for transmission power lines, offering high sensing recognition accuracy and self-powered operation. This work not only provides an effective method for preparing high-performance PENGs but also extends their potential application to enrich the perception layer and monitoring system of power IoTs.
