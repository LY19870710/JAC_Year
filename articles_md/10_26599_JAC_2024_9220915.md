## 1 Introduction

Porous ceramics are highly desirable for a myriad of applications, such as photonics [1], electrodes [2,3], and catalyst [4] and membrane  support [5,6]. Due to the porous and brittle characteristics of porous ceramics, high mechanical strength is the most important prerequisite among the fundamental requirements, such as permeance, surface roughness, and chemical stability, especially when these materials are used as supports [7,8]. Increasing the processing temperature is a common way to improve the mechanical strength of porous ceramics [9−12]. However, this will result in an increase in energy consumption and microstructural densification, thus reducing the porosity. The trade-off between the mechanical strength and porosity is well recognized in the field of porous ceramics, and attempts have been increasingly devoted to overcoming this issue [13]. Among them, introducing ceramic fibers or whiskers has been demonstrated to be an effective way to improve both the mechanical strength and porosity of porous ceramics [14,15]. However, the cost of these whiskers is usually high, and the reinforcement effect can be reduced once these whiskers are not uniformly mixed. A particle grading strategy with fine particles filling the interspace of coarse particles could maximize the packing density of the powder mixture [16] and thus improve the final density and mechanical strength of the bulk counterparts, which has been widely adopted in the fields of cement [17,18] and ceramics [19].

In view of this, the particle grading strategy has been intensively extended in the preparation of porous ceramics to improve the mechanical strength [20,21]. Unfortunately, this approach is usually accompanied by a notable decrease in porosity [22]. For example, Wei *et al*. [23] introduced fine SiC powders (*d*50 = 25 µm) into coarse SiC aggregates (*d*50 = 200 µm) to fabricate supports, and the porosity of the sintered aggregates decreased from 35% to 26% as the content of fine powders increased from 5 to 40 wt%. Since high porosity is also a key target for porous ceramics, a well-adopted particle grading strategy is not the best pathway for preparing porous ceramics.

In addition to experimental attempts, mathematical models have been explored to understand and predict the packing density (or porosity) of graded mixed powders by considering the particle size [24,25], volume fraction, particle shape, and packing density of each constituent [26−28]. Among them, the linear packing model has become one of the most popular analytical models [29], in which the packing density first increases to its highest value when the volume fraction of the fine component increases to 0.7. With a further increase in the volume fraction to 1.0, the packing density decreases and finally becomes equal to that of the fine component. In fact, the packing density of fine components is lower than that of coarse components due to the enhanced effects of van der Waals forces and electrostatic repulsive forces [30]. Therefore, unlike the conventional particle grading strategy, it is worth exploring the reverse grading strategy—the incorporation of coarse components into fine components in the preparation of porous ceramics—aiming at improving the packing density and mechanical strength through the relatively high porosity of fine particles.

In this work, the proposed reverse grading strategy is adopted for the first time to prepare porous tubular SiC supports as a case study to improve the mechanical strength, which is the fundamental requirement for the subsequent fabrication of ceramic membranes [10]. Two types of SiC powders with different average particle sizes (*d*50 = 16 and 5 μm) were selected, and the packing density of the binary mixture was firstly simulated based on the linear packing model. Then, the content of coarse powder was deliberately adjusted to prepare a porous SiC support by using the extrusion technique. Finally, extruded green bodies were sintered by *in*-*situ* reaction bonding [9,31,32] among various sintering methods [33,34] due to the advantages of relatively low processing temperatures. The effects of coarse particles on the rheological properties and moldability of ceramic pastes and the microstructure and physical properties of porous SiC supports were investigated. Additionally, the mechanism of the enhanced bending strength and microstructure evolution was discussed. In addition, the performances of such SiC membrane supports in terms of pure water permeability, surface roughness and corrosion resistance in acidic and alkaline environments were also examined. Finally, the pilot-scale fabrication of 19-channel porous SiC ceramic supports was successfully demonstrated.

### 2.1 Raw materials

Two types of SiC powders with average particle sizes of 16 and 5 μm (Nantong Yuda SiC Co., Ltd., China) were labeled W16 and W5, respectively. NaA zeolite powder (*d*50 = 5 μm, Jiangxi Xintao Technology Co., Ltd., China) was used as a sintering aid. Hydroxypropyl methyl cellulose (HPMC, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., China) and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA; *M*w: 1750±50, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) served as the plasticizer and binder, respectively. Oleic acid (Shanghai Lingfeng Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), glycerol (≥ 99.5% in purity, Xilong Scientific, China) and paraffin liquid (Shanghai Lingfeng Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) were used as lubricants. Sulfuric acid (Yangzhou Hubao Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), and sodium hydroxide (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) were used for acidic and alkaline corrosion testing, respectively. Deionized water was produced by a homemade purification system.

With an increase in the amount of fine powder from 0 to 20 wt%, the packing density increased to a maximum (a → b in Fig. 1) because of the filling effect of the fine powders and then decreased due to the loosening effect [30]. On the other hand, from the point of view of fine powders, the increase in coarse powder allows more coarse particles to occupy the space between fine particles and their interstices, thus increasing the packing density (i.e., occupying effect, c → b in Fig. 1) [35]. Once all available space is occupied by coarse particles, the packing density will decrease (i.e., wall effect, b → a in Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 1, this trend in density is also applicable in binary mixtures with particle size ratios ranging from 2 to 10. The addition of fine powder into a coarse powder matrix increases the density, which then remains relatively high due to the high initial packing density; however, this approach is not desirable for porous ceramics. From the point of view of fine powder, although the packing density will also increase with increasing coarse powder, it will remain at a relatively low level because of the much lower initial packing density, as shown in Fig. 1. Therefore, it is assumed that such a powder mixture will be advantageous for achieving high mechanical strength and maximized porosity at the same time.

### 2.3 Fabrication of SiC membrane supports

Based on the modeling results, powder mixtures containing graded SiC powders with various contents of coarse SiC particles (0–30 wt%), NaA zeolite, and HPMC (plasticizer) were mixed at a speed of 30 r/min in a mixing machine (QLJ-50L, Hebi Yuchuang Machine Co., Ltd., China). Then, PVA aqueous solution (5 wt%), oleic acid, paraffin liquid, and water were added to the matrix and further mixed for 2 h. Afterwards, the aged pastes were extruded by a twin-screw extruder (FM-P50, Miyazaki Iron Works Co., Ltd., Japan) using two kinds of stainless-steel pressure dies to obtain the green body of single-channel tubular ceramic supports (outer diameter: 12 mm, inner diameter: 8 mm) and 19-channel tubular ceramic supports (diameter: 30 mm, channel diameter: 4 mm). Next, the green body was dried at 25 °C for 48 h and then transferred to a furnace (DHG-9078A, Shanghai Jinghong Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd., China) at 120 °C for 12 h. Finally, the green body was sintered at 1100 °C in an air atmosphere for 2 h with a ramping rate of 2 °C/min.

### 2.4 Characterizations

The particle size distribution of the SiC powders was measured by using a laser particle size analyzer (PSA; M3000, Malvern, UK). The phase of the powders was characterized using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; MiniFlex 600, Rigaku, Japan). Thermogravimetric analysis and differential thermal analysis of the paste sample were conducted using a thermal analyzer (thermogravimetry–differential scanning calorimetry (TG–DSC); STA449, Netzsch, Germany) from 25 to 1200 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C/min under an air atmosphere. The rheological properties of the ceramic paste were measured by using a rheometer (MCR-102e, Anton Paar, Austria). The nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms of the SiC powders were measured by an auto-adsorption analyzer (ASAP2020, Micromeritics Instrument, USA), and the specific surface areas were obtained from the Brunauer‒Emmett‒Teller (BET) model. The bending strength of the sintered tubular supports was measured from the maximum loads using a three-point bending test (DR028G-1000, Wenzhou Darong Textile Instrument Co. Ltd., China) at the tubular membrane supports using Eq. (1):

(1) σ=8π×FLDD4−d4

where *σ* (MPa) is the bending strength; *F* (N) is the maximum load on the membrane sample during the test; *L* (mm) is the spacing of the support blade; *D* and *d* (mm) are the outer and inner diameters of the membrane sample, respectively. In this work, the length of the supports we used was 60 mm, and the span distance (*L*) was 40 mm. The microstructures of the powders, green bodies, ceramic supports, and membranes were characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; S-4800, Hitachi, Japan). The open porosity was measured by the Archimedes method using Eq. (2):

(2) ϵ=G2−G1G2−G3×100%

where *G*1 (g) and *G*2 (g) are the masses of dry supports and water-saturated supports in air, respectively, and *G*3 (g) is the mass of water-saturated supports in water. Fast GPU matrix computing of the discrete element method (MatDEM) was used to establish stacking models with the introduction of coarse particles and to predict changes in porosity. The pore size distribution and average pore size were measured by a pore size distribution analyzer (Ipore 1500, PMI, USA) via the bubble pressure method. The permeability of the ceramic supports was measured by the pure water flux using a homemade setup. The pure water flux (*J*, L/(m2·h), LMH) of the ceramic supports at different transmembrane pressures (*P*, bar) was calculated by Eq. (3):

(3) J=VA⋅t⋅Δp

where *V* (L) is the volume of the permeate; *A* (m2) is the effective area of the membrane surface; *t* (h) is the filtration time, and * Δp (bar) is the TMP. The surface roughness of the ceramic supports and membrane layers was measured by a high-precision surface profiler (VK1000, KEYENCE, Japan).

Tubular SiC ceramic membrane supports were immersed in slightly boiled 20 wt% H2SO4 and 1 wt% NaOH solution (~100 °C) to investigate their acidic and alkaline corrosion resistances, respectively. The supports were removed from solution at 1, 2, 4, and 8 h and then washed with a large amount of water for 1 h. Finally, the samples were placed in a furnace at 120 °C for 6 h to dry. The mass loss rates (R*) of the supports after corrosion were determined by Eq. (4):

(4) R=M0−M1M0×100%

where *M*0 (g) and *M*1 (g) are the masses of the supports before and after corrosion, respectively.

A comparison of the TGA curves of the ceramic pastes with different W16 contents revealed that the mass rapidly increased from 900 to 939 °C, after which the rate of increase decreased above 939 °C. To understand the rate of increase after 939 °C, we also characterized the ceramic paste without any W16 or NaA (labeled S0′ in Fig. 3(b)). In the same temperature range (> 900 °C), the mass of S0′ increased gradually, as shown in Fig. 3(a), which is largely different from that of the other samples.

Further, the thermal behavior of NaA, SiC, and their mixture was analyzed. As shown in Fig. 3(c), the DTA curve of the mixture went up from 700 to 870 °C, suggesting that the reaction of NaA with surface oxidation generated SiO2. There is an endothermic peak between 870 and 939 °C on the DTA curve of NaA, indicating the emergence of NaAlSiO4 (NaA → NaAlSiO4). The curve of the mixture went up rapidly after 939 °C. This can be attributed to the reaction between NaAlSiO4 and SiO2 generated by surface oxidation (NaAlSiO4 + 2SiO2 → NaAlSi3O8) [39]. As a result, the rate of increase in mass decreased compared with that of the paste without NaA or W16 (i.e., S0’).

Particle grading can affect the rheological properties of ceramic paste and generate pressure during extrusion [22,40−42]. Figure 3(d) shows the molding pressure in the extrusion process as a function of the W16 content. With the addition of 0 to 30 wt% W16, the pressure gradually decreased from 5.6±0.3 to 1.5±0.1 MPa. To clarify the trend of the extruding pressure, the rheology of samples with different contents of W16 was measured, as shown in Fig. 3(e). With increasing shear rate, all the samples exhibited a typical shear-thinning behavior, and the sample with 30 wt% W16 grain showed the lowest viscosity, suggesting that it can be extruded more easily than the other samples. Previously, Zheng *et al*. [41] reported that as the volume fraction of fine alumina particles increased from 10% to 25%, the viscosity of ceramic paste increased under a shear rate of 30 s−1 because the addition of fine particles increased the amount of resin restricted between particles and thus decreased the free liquid phase, which increased the probability of particle collisions. Therefore, the reverse particle grading strategy is advantageous for smoothening the extrusion formation process.

As evidenced by the N2 adsorption/desorption results in Fig. 3(f), the specific surface areas of W5 and W16 were 1.39 and 0.63 m2/g, respectively. Therefore, it can be inferred that the amount of the liquid phase surrounded by fine particles would be greater than that surrounded by coarse particles. With the addition of coarse particles, the amount of the adsorbed liquid phase decreased, and the amount of the interstices in the liquid phase increased. Correspondingly, the amount of free liquid phase in the paste increased, thereby decreasing the probability of particle collisions. As a result, the viscosity of the ceramic paste prepared with more W16 decreased gradually, and thus, the extrusion pressure decreased.

However, when the content of W16 further increased to 30 wt%, the bending strength slightly decreased to 42.5±6.5 MPa. Optical photographs of the sintered supports with 20 wt% W16 are shown in Fig. 4(b). The single-channel tubular supports maintained their configuration and dimensions well, as illustrated by the dashed circles.

To interpret the changes in bending strength, cross-sectional SEM images of these supports were captured. As shown in Figs. 4(d)–4(f), several fine particles connected with a single coarse particle once the coarse particles were introduced. Among them, some necking areas were observed, which mainly contributed to the increased bending strength. With increasing W16 content, the possibility of coarse grains connecting with each other increased, so the bending strength increased further. When a force was exerted perpendicularly on the surface of the support, the neck connections experienced a crack. As shown in Figs. 4(c)–4(f), the fracture in connecting necks (yellow arrows) presented transgranular features, and the route of cracks approached along the neck connections between coarse grains and fine particles. As illustrated in Fig. 4(g), with increasing content of coarse particles, the route of crack deflection became longer, which could increase the resistance of crack propagation. This process requires more fracture energy, which is conducive to increasing the bending strength [43]. However, when the W16 content increased to 30 wt%, the resistance of the crack declined because the number of neck connections in the route of the crack decreased, which can be attributed to the lower oxidation degree of the coarse SiC particles [36].

### 3.3 Porosity and pore size distribution

As shown in Fig. 5(a), the porosity of the SiC ceramic supports decreased from 48.9%±1.3% to 45.1%±1.6% with increasing the W16 content. It could be understood that a W16 particle can replace the space that is occupied by several W5 grains as well as their interstices, resulting in a reduction in porosity. Previously, Deng *et al*. [44] also reported that with the addition of coarse particles (0–30 wt%) to a fine particle matrix, the porosity of the supports decreased from 42.1% to 33.2%. The rapid decrease in porosity was mainly attributed to the large difference in the particle size of the used SiC powders (*d*50 = 0.6 and 6 μm), in which the fine particles could easily occupy the voids among large particles. Compared with previous porous SiC ceramics [9,20], the porosity of the porous SiC supports in this work was notably greater because of the higher porosity of the fine powders.

As shown in Fig. 5(b), the average pore size increased from 0.95 to 1.2 μm with increasing W16 content. This was ascribed to the irregular stacking of these particles. The pores formed by small particles were smaller than those formed by large particles [45−48]. The pores formed by the W5 grains were smaller than the pores formed by W16 and W5. The random stacking behavior of these particles was modeled by discrete element method (DEM), as illustrated in Figs. 5(c)–5(f). Correspondingly, the change in porosity with increasing W16 content was simulated, as shown in Fig. 5(a). The porosity decreased from 38.9%±0.1% to 36.9%±1.1% as the W16 content increased from 0 to 30 wt%. The simulation was based on the random and free stacking of sphere-like particles, and the values were close to those reported for alumina porous ceramics [21,49]. For example, the porosity of the sintered supports was maintained at 32%–37% regardless of the mass ratio between the medium and fine powders [21]. Compared with the experimental values in this work, the simulated values of porosity were lower, but the decreasing trend was the same. According to our previous results [12,50], the porosity of porous SiC supports prepared by the reaction-sintering technique is greater than that of traditional oxide porous ceramics, largely because of the enlarged connecting necks.

It is worth noting that the supports prepared with 30 wt% W16 had the lowest porosity, but their bending strength was not the highest. According to the Rice equation [51], the mechanical strength of porous materials is related to their porosity, as described in Eq. (5):

(5) δ=δ0exp(−bp)

where δ* and *δ*0 represent the bending strengths of porous and nonporous materials, respectively; *p* represents the porosity of the porous material; *b* is a constant that depends on the pore characteristics. Therefore, ceramic samples with low porosity have a higher mechanical strength. However, in this work, the decrease in porosity was mainly attributed to the replacement of fine grains by coarse grains. The low oxidation level of coarse SiC grains resulted in less SiO2, and the number and size of neck areas thus decreased. Therefore, the decreased bending strength at 30 wt% W16 can be related to fewer neck connections and weaker bonding strength [20], as shown in Fig. 4(f).

For example, Li *et al*. [20] used three sizes of SiC powders (*d*50 = 30, 3, 0.5 µm) and changed the weight ratio between intermediate and fine powders to prepare substrates. The bending strength was high (49.8 MPa), but the porosity was relatively low (34%) when the weight ratio was 0.7 : 0.24 : 0.06. Compared with the ceramics fabricated with the conventional particle grading strategy, the porous ceramics fabricated with the reverse particle grading strategy in this work could simultaneously achieve a high mechanical strength (49.1 MPa) and maintain a maximized porosity (45.1%).

The relationship between the coarse particle content and pure water flux at a transmembrane pressure of 1 bar is shown in Fig. 6(a). Therefore, *R*2 can be used as an effective factor for evaluating the mechanical stability of ceramic supports.

### 3.5 Surface roughness

Porous ceramic supports can be used either directly as filters [47] or indirectly as supports for preparing ceramic membranes [55,56], and both require a smooth surface. The surface roughness of ceramic supports is an important factor that can affect the filtration performance [57] and the quality of the subsequent coating layer [44,58]. For the former, a smooth surface can retard the accumulation of foulants on the surface, while for the latter, a smooth surface can benefit the formation of a thin defect-free membrane layer. Generally, SiC micro-filtration tubes have rarely been used as supports to directly deposit fine separation layers due to their high surface roughness, which can increase the difficulty of fabricating defect-free membranes [59].

As presented in Figs. 7(a)–7(d), after adding 10 and 20 wt% W16, the roughness of the inner surface gradually decreased from 3.2±0.3 to 2.68±0.2 and 2.00±0.2 μm, respectively. The surface roughness did not change significantly when the content of W16 further increased to 30 wt%. The change in surface roughness could also be evidenced by the surface SEM images of the supports, as shown in Figs. 7(e)–7(h). Ceramic membranes fabricated by adding coarse grains into the matrix with fine grains showed greater surface roughness than those fabricated without coarse grains, as reported by Deng and coworkers [44]. However, in this work, the ceramic paste experienced a kneading process before extrusion, which is helpful for improving the plasticity of the paste and arrangement of grains. In other words, the extrusion process has a smooth effect on the surface of the green body, especially when the fluidity of the ceramic paste is good. These two factors could be conducive to the smooth surface of the supports. In addition, with the addition of coarse particles, the top surface of the coarse particles could form a flat layer independently, which can help to reduce the surface roughness.

### 3.6 Chemical stability

The loss in bending strength and mass was used to evaluate the chemical stability of ceramic supports in acidic and alkaline environments [49,60]. The bending strength of the supports fabricated with 20 wt% W16 decreased from 49.1±4.5 to 29.8±0.5 MPa after 8 h of corrosion using 1 wt% NaOH (boiled slightly at ~100 °C), and the mass loss rate of the supports gradually increased to 0.88%±0.17%. However, for the supports immersed in 20 wt% H2SO4 (boiled slightly at ~100 °C), the bending strength decreased to 31.0±1.6 MPa after 8 h of corrosion, which is slightly greater than that of the supports in an alkaline environment. Correspondingly, the mass loss rate increased to 0.7%±0.17%, which is relatively lower than that in the alkali environment, as shown in Figs. 8(a) and 8(b). The XRD patterns of the sintered supports fabricated with various contents of W16 showed the characteristic peaks of SiO2, NaAlSiO4, and SiC, and their intensities were similar, as shown in Fig. 8(c). This suggests that the influence of coarse particles on the phase components is negligible. According to the XRD patterns of the supports before and after acid and alkali corrosion treatments, as shown in Fig. 8(d), the characteristic peak of SiO2 decreased after alkali corrosion, indicating that the content of SiO2 slightly decreased. During alkali corrosion treatment, SiO2 would react with NaOH in solution so that the connecting necks between SiC particles were damaged, resulting in reduced bending strength. While in acid corrosion environment, some neck connections formed directly by melted NaA zeolite particles reacted with H2SO4. The connections were also broken to some degree, so the bending strength slightly decreased.

### 3.7 Pilot-scale fabrication of 19-channel tubular supports

Based on the reverse particle grading strategy and the successful preparation of single-channel tubular supports, the reverse particle grading strategy was further extended in the preparation of 19-channel tubular supports with a diameter of 30 mm and a channel diameter of 4 mm, as illustrated in Fig. 9(a), which is one of most common dimensions of commercial ceramic membranes [62]. It was found that the extruding pressure decreased from ~11 to ~8 MPa compared with that of paste without particle size gradation, which is conducive to energy-saving and prolonging the service life of the machine. Green bodies of 19-channel SiC ceramic tubular supports 1.4 m in length were fabricated at the pilot scale, as presented in Fig. 9(b). After sintering, as presented in Fig. 9(c), the diameter of the supports slightly increased to ~31.05 mm, and the size of the channels decreased to ~3.95 mm, which was mainly attributed to the expansion behavior of the SiC particles induced by surface oxidation [36]. In addition, the shapes of the green body, sintered body, and their channels were well maintained.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, a reverse particle grading strategy was developed based on the linear packing model to prepare porous ceramics with both high porosity and high mechanical strength. As an example, tubular SiC ceramic supports with enhanced bending strength were prepared at 1100 °C by using this strategy, in which coarse SiC powder with an average particle size of 16 μm was incorporated into the powder matrix that is mainly composed of SiC powder with an average particle size of 5 μm. The results showed that the addition of coarse SiC powders could favorably reduce the extrusion pressure from 5.6±0.3 to 1.5±0.1 MPa. Compared with the conventional particle grading strategy, which requires much smaller and more expensive powders, the cost of large-sized powders is lower, and thus, the overall fabrication cost of porous ceramic supports is lower. When 20 wt% coarse grains were added, the bending strength of the ceramic supports reached a high value of 49.1±4.5 MPa due to the presence of strong connecting necks and promoted crack deflection. Meanwhile, the porosity, average pore size, and pure water flux at a TMP of 1 bar were 45.1%±1.6%, 1.2 μm, and 7163±150 LMH, respectively. Importantly, the ceramic supports had a smooth inner surface, which is beneficial for their application in membrane fabrication and membrane separation. Moreover, the supports exhibited good acid and alkali corrosion resistances at ~100 °C, enabling them to be highly competitive in harsh environments. Finally, the feasibility of the reverse particle grading strategy for the pilot-scale fabrication of 19-channel porous SiC ceramic supports was successfully demonstrated. This work paves a cost-efficient and scalable pathway for preparing porous ceramic supports with both high mechanical strength and porosity.
