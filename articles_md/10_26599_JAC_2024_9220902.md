## 1 Introduction

Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) are clean energy conversion devices capable of converting chemical energy stored in fuels directly into electrical energy without being limited by the Carnot cycle, resulting in high energy conversion efficiency [1−3]. Since the operating temperature of SOFCs is usually higher than 600 °C, they have a wide range of fuel flexibility [4,5]. Hydrogen (H2) is the simplest fuel for SOFCs, resulting in high performance and zero emission [6−8], but its production, transportation, and storage are not convenient for large-scale applications [9−11]. In contrast, hydrocarbon fuels, such as natural gas containing mainly methane (CH4), have abundant reserves, and their direct utilization is preferred for stationary power generation using SOFCs [12,13].

Considering that both CH4 and carbon dioxide (CO2) are greenhouse gases and are plentiful, dry reforming of CH4 (DRM) is a promising approach for consuming these two greenhouse gases to produce H2 and carbon monoxide (CO) for the use of SOFCs. SOFCs with on-cell DRM can simplify the power generation system and balance the heat released by the electrochemical reactions on the electrodes. However, despite the excellent catalytic activity of nickel (Ni) cermet anodes [14], the direct use of CH4–CO2 gas as fuel in state-of-the-art setups may result in carbon deposition [15,16]. This can lead to rapid degradation of cell performance, consequently reducing the durability of the cell and shortening the lifetime of the stack and system [17].

Studies have been conducted to improve the long-term performance stability of cells for on-cell DRM, such as adding Fe or Mo–Au into NiO–(Gd0.6Ce0.4)2O3.2 [18] and CaO into NiO–Ce0.8Sm0.2O1.9 [19] cermet anodes. However, this approach will reduce the overall electrocatalytic activity of the anode while enhancing its resistance to carbon deposition. In addition, an additional DRM layer of a doped perovskite material has been applied on the surface of Ni-cermet anodes [20,21], such as Ba0.9(Zr0.8Y0.2)1−*X*Ni*X*O3−*δ* (*X* = 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, and 0.2) [22], La0.6Sr0.2Cr0.85Ni0.15O3−*δ* [23], and Sr2Co0.4Fe1.2Mo0.4O6−*δ* [24]. The added DRM layer significantly improved the conversion efficiency and reduced carbon deposition in the Ni-cermet anodes. Coating the DRM layer on the outside of the anode can indeed enhance its resistance to carbon deposition in the short term. However, as the operating duration increases, carbon-containing fuels may penetrate the interior of the anode. This penetration will lead to irreversible carbon deposition in the anode functional layer.

Different from the commonly used porous anode structure [25−27], a through-hole structured anode with solid-state aerogel catalysts was developed in this study, consisting of a phase inversion-formed Ni−GDC scaffold and fine and large BaCe0.8Fe0.1Ni0.1O3−*δ* (BCFN) particles via impregnations of BCFN solution and suspension. This is in contrast to the conventional approach of applying a DRM catalyst layer externally onto the anode. This integration enables comprehensive contact and reaction between the entire anode and the fuel gas. Moreover, this configuration facilitates the conduction of oxygen ions and electrons while effectively shielding the functional layer of the internal anode. Consequently, it significantly enhances both the electrochemical performance and carbon resistance of the single cell. The phase inversion causes the Ni−GDC to have a structure of straight pores perpendicular to the surface, and the pore size increases from the inside to the outside. BaCeO3 is a strong water-absorbing material [28] doped with Fe and Ni, and Fe–Ni alloy nanoparticles exsolve from the BCFN matrix in the reducing atmosphere of the anode [29,30], acting as an active DRM component with strong resistance to carbon deposition and promoting the electrochemical performance of the anode [31,32]. The water adsorbability of the BCFN matrix can further enhance the carbon deposition resistance by increasing the water content around the nanoparticles. The solution-impregnated small BCFN particles cover the whole inner surface of the Ni–GDC scaffold; the suspension-impregnated large BCFN aerogels with a high specific surface area fill the Ni–GDC scaffold to a thickness with large pores, forming an additional seamless on-cell DRM layer with strong carbon deposition resistance and reforming CH4–CO2 into CO–H2 for power generation.

## 2 Experimental

BCFN powder was prepared by a conventional sol–gel method by dissolving stoichiometric amounts of the constituent nitrates (Sino-pharm) in deionized water with the addition of ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (Sinopharm) and citric acid (Sinopharm) for complexation. The final concentration of the BCFN solution was 0.25 mol·L−1. Some solutions were air-dried and calcined at 1000 °C for 3 h to obtain BCFN powder, and some were used for the preparation of BCFN aerogels; the rest were used for solution impregnation.

The BCFN aerogel was prepared as follows: (1) The BCFN solution was dried at 300 °C for 4 h to form a powdery intermediate compound, which was dispersed in a solution of hexane and isopropanol in a 2 : 1 volume ratio and agitated ultrasonically for 1 h; (2) carbon powder (charcoal activated granular; Sinopharm) was added to the suspension at a weight ratio of 4 : 1 to the intermediate compound, followed by ultrasonic agitation for 2 h to obtain the BCFN aerogel suspension; (3) the suspension was dried in air and calcined in a microwave furnace (HY-MF1512) with rapid heating (10 °C∙min−1 to 1000 °C) for 1 h to obtain the BCFN aerogel. The BCFN powder (BCFNP) and aerogel (BCFNG) were reduced in H2 at 800 °C for 4 h for the exsolution of the Fe–Ni alloy particles, which are denoted as R-BCFNP and R-BCFNG, respectively.

Ba0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3−*δ* powder (BSCF) was prepared by a similar sol–gel method with calcination at 950 °C for 2 h and mixed with GDC powder (Sofcman, China) in a 7 : 3 weight ratio for the preparation of BSCF–GDC composite cathodes.

The cell support (anode) was made of a NiO–GDC powder mixture by a phase inversion method according to the following procedure. First, an organic solvent was prepared by dissolving a certain amount of polyethersulfone (PES) in N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) under agitation. Then, NiO, GDC, and polyvinyl-pyrrolidone (PVP) in a designed proportion were added to the organic solvent, followed by ball milling at 250 r·min−1 for 48 h to form a slurry. Finally, the slurry was injected into a mold and covered with deionized water for phase inversion until the solidification process was completed.

A GDC suspension consisting of GDC powder, anhydrous ethanol solvent, polyvinyl butyral binder, polyethylene glycol plasticizer, and triethanolamine/dimethyl phthalate surfactant was drop-coated onto the solidified NiO–GDC support. It was dried at room temperature and sintered at 1550 °C in air for 4 h to form a half-cell with densified GDC electrolyte on the fine-pore side surface of the support with a straight-pore structure.

BCFN solution was impregnated into the support from the large-pore side, and dried and calcined at 1000 °C in air for 3 h. The impregnation process was repeated until the small BCFN particles reached 10 wt%, covering the entire inner surface of the NiO–GDC scaffold. Then, the BCFN suspension (1 wt%) was impregnated and calcined in the microwave furnace with rapid heating (10 °C·min−1 to 1000 °C) for 1 h to form a BCFN aerogel, completing the BCFN-impregnated NiO–GDC anode. After reduction, NiO–GDC was converted to Ni–GDC, while the Fe–Ni alloy nanoparticles exsolved from the BCFN matrix.

A slurry containing BSCF–GDC composite powder, ethyl cellulose binder, and terpineol solvent was screen-printed onto the surface of the densified GDC of the half-cell prepared above. After drying, the sample was sintered at 950 °C in air for 2 h to form a cathode with an effective area of 0.2 cm−2. The cell with a conventional Ni–GDC anode without BCFN impregnation is designated Cell-C, and the cell with a BCFN-impregnated anode is designated Cell-I.

The phases in R-BCFNG were identified by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; XRD-7000). The specific surface areas and water adsorbabilities of BCFNP and BCFNG were measured by physical surface adsorption (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET); Autosorb-iQ) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA; Netszch STA 449 C), respectively. The surface chemistry of BCFNP and BCFNG was analyzed by an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Nexsa). A scanning electron microscope (SEM; GeminiSEM300) with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS) was employed to observe the microstructure of the cell and the elemental distribution of the anode. The exsolved Fe–Ni nanoparticles in R-BCFNP and R-BCFNG were examined by a transmission electron microscope (TEM; FEI-TALOS-F200X) and EDS (Super-X). A scanning electron microscope–Raman coupling system (RISE-CLARA) was employed to detect deposited carbon in the posttest anodes.

For evaluation of electrochemical performance, the cell was painted on the anode and cathode with Pt paste (Sino-Platinum Metals), and Pt mesh and wire were attached as the current collector and measuring lead. A glass sealant (Ceramabond, Aremco Product) was used to seal the cell onto a cell holder. Pure H2 was fed into the holder to reduce the anode *in situ* at 800 °C for 4 h before the electrochemical measurements. The electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) and current density–voltage–power density (*I*–*V*–*P*) curves of the cells in atmospheres of dry H2 and dry 50% CO2–50% CH4 (CO2–CH4) were obtained at 750, 700, 650, and 600 °C. The cell performance stability was evaluated in dry 45% CO2–45% CH4–10% N2 at 400 mA·cm−2 and 700 °C for more than 400 h, and the exhaust gas composition of the anode was analyzed by a gas chromatograph (PANNA A91).

### 3.1 Physical characterizations

Figure 1 shows the physical characterizations of the prepared materials. After 4 h of reduction at 800 °C in H2, FeNi3 alloy phases formed on the BaCeO3-based BCFN matrix in R-BCFNG and R-BCFNP, as evidenced by the XRD peaks in the range of 42°–46° (Fig. 1(a)), indicating successful synthesis of BCFNG and BCFNP in perovskite structures capable of exsolving FeNi3 alloy particles in H2 atmosphere. The BCFN and GDC powders were mixed and cocalcined at 1000 °C for 2 h. The XRD pattern (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) confirms the excellent compatibility between them. The specific area of BCFNG was 9.468 m2·g−1, 2.5 times larger than 3.863 m2·g−1 of BCFNP (Fig. 1(b)), confirming that the specific surface area of the prepared BCFN was significantly increased by aerogel formation. This also suggests that the impregnated high-specific-area BCFNG with more catalytically active sites will promote the DRM reactions and, in turn, the efficiency of the anode support. The high specific surface area of BCFNG was also supported by morphology examination (Fig. 1(c)) and pore size distribution analysis. The BCFNG particles were evenly fine and uniformly connected, forming an open three-dimensional network with a high porosity. In contrast, BCFNP was sintered with a continuous irregular morphology. The pores in BCFNG were small, mainly below 5 nm, while those in BCFNP were larger than 50 nm (Fig. S2 in the ESM).

The water adsorbabilities of BCFNG and BCFNP were evaluated by TGA by recording the weight change of the sample during heating to 800 °C at 5 °C·min−1 in dry air and then cooling to 200 °C at the same rate in wet (5% H2O) air (Fig. 2). The sample weight of BCFNP decreased more than that of BCFNG during heating, indicating that more substances were adsorbed on the surface of BCFNP before heating (Fig. 2(a)). During cooling in wet air from 800 to 200 °C, the weight gain of BCFNG (0.55 wt%) was greater than that of BCFNP (0.17 wt%) (Fig. 2(b)), demonstrating that the greater water adsorbability of BCFNG benefited from its high specific surface area. Combining the adsorption and desorption curves of BCFNG in Fig. 1(b), it can be seen that due to the unique structure of the solid aerogel, the adsorbed gas molecules cannot completely detach, leading to a certain degree of nonclosure in the adsorption and desorption processes. Similarly, due to the porous structure of the aerogel, BCFNG exhibited excellent water adsorption performance. The nanoscale porous three-dimensional network structure formed by the aerogel can effectively adsorb and capture water vapor molecules in the atmosphere while preventing their loss with sweeping gas flow, ultimately resulting in less mass loss of the aerogel powder compared to BCFNP.

The chemical states of the surface Ce and O in BCFNG, BCFNP, R-BCFNG, and R-BCFNP were analyzed by XPS, which revealed a Ce 3d spectrum with five pairs of 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 doublets (Figs. 3(a) and 3(c)) and an O 1s spectrum (Figs. 3(b) and 3(d)) [33]. The ratio of the areas beneath the Ce3+ and Ce4+ peaks reflects the concentration ratio of Ce3+/Ce4+, and the concentration of Ce3+ is positively correlated with the number of oxygen vacancies on the surface. The Ce3+/Ce4+ ratios of BCFNG and BCFNP were 83.03% and 76.44%, respectively, and the ratios decreased to 72.84% and 68.39% after reduction, indicating that BCFNG has a higher surface oxygen vacancy concentration than BCFNP, regardless of whether it was reduced or not (Table S1 in the ESM). The difference was caused by the fact that the particle size of BCFNG was smaller with a greater specific surface area and more crystal surfaces exposed to the external atmosphere, increasing the chance of a redox reaction between Ce3+ and Ce4+ and thus increasing the concentrations of Ce3+ and oxygen vacancies. After reduction, FeNi3 exsolved from the lattice, increasing the concentration of Ce4+ to maintain electrical neutrality. By fitting the O 1s spectrum with chemisorbed oxygen (OC), surface oxygen vacancy (OV), and lattice oxygen (OL) data, the ratio of OV/(OV+OL) of BCFNG was determined to be 70.60%, which is greater than that of BCFNP (65.43%), which increased in the reduced form due to the exsolution of FeNi3 particles (Table S1 in the ESM). The chemical information of the Fe and Ni in BCFNG and R-BCFNG further revealed the presence of Fe0 and Ni0 peaks in R-BCFNG after reduction, accompanied by a decrease in the intensity of the Fe3+ and Ni2+ peaks compared to those of BCFNG (Fig. S3 in the ESM). Such FeNi3 nanoparticles and increased oxygen vacancies are expected to enhance the resistance to carbon deposition, as the exsolved FeNi3 nanoparticles are not as susceptible as Ni to carbon deposition and have a strong interaction with the BCFN matrix, preventing carbon formation on their surfaces. Additionally, the oxygen vacancies in the matrix adsorb water molecules to oxidize the deposited carbon.

### 3.2 Morphology and element distribution

Figure 4 shows the morphologies and element distributions of the BCFN-impregnated anode, R-BCFNP and R-BCFNG. As stated above, the NiO–GDC scaffold was prepared by a phase inversion method in which straight pores gradually increased in size from the inside to the outside. After the initial BCFN solution impregnation treatment, the NiO–GDC scaffold was covered uniformly by small BCFN particles on the surfaces of the pores, even the tiny inner pores (Figs. 4(a) and 4(b)), as evidenced by the distribution of Ba and Ce. The subsequent BCFN suspension impregnation formed relatively large BCFN aerogels, filling the large pores near the outside surface of the NiO–GDC scaffold to the depth marked by the dashed line (Figs. 4(a) and 4(c)), as shown by the Ba and Ce distributions. Small BCFN particles are anticipated to enhance the electrochemical performance and carbon deposition resistance of the anode, and large aerogels in the outside layer of the scaffold are expected to promote DRM reactions and carbon deposition resistance. After reduction at 800 °C in H2 for 4 h, the NiO–GDC scaffold was reduced to Ni–GDC, while Fe–Ni alloy (identified as FeNi3) particles exsolved in both R-BCFNP and R-BCFNG, as proven by the morphologies (Fig. S4 in the ESM and the distributions of Ni and Fe (Figs. 4(d) and 4(e)). The FeNi3 particles in R-BCFNG were smaller and more evenly distributed than those in R-BCFNP because of the difference in their specific surface areas. The exsolved Fe–Ni nanoscale alloy particles demonstrated remarkable catalytic activity due to their small size [34,35]. Moreover, their strong interaction with the matrix material effectively enhanced their resistance to carbon deposition [21], leveraging the water absorption properties of the matrix material.

### 3.3 Cell performance in H2 and CO2–CH4

Figure 5 shows the electrochemical impedance of Cell-C and Cell-I using H2 and CO2–CH4 as fuel, respectively. As usual, the electrochemical impedance of the cells (Figs. 5(a)–5(d), derived from Fig. S5 in the ESM) increased with decreasing the temperature. However, the ohmic and polarization resistances (*R*Ω and *R*P) of Cell-C were much greater and decreased more with decreasing temperature than those of Cell-I. *R*Ω and *R*P of Cell-I with H2 ranged from 0.047 to 0.099 and 0.005 to 0.039 Ω·cm2, respectively, at temperatures between 750 and 600 °C, more than 1 time lower in *R*Ω and nearly 5 times lower in *R*P than those of Cell-C. With CO2–CH4, *R*Ω and *R*P ranged in 0.085–0.23 and 0.033–0.53 Ω·cm2 for Cell-C, respectively, which decreased significantly, ranging in 0.054–0.15 and 0.01–0.19 Ω·cm2, respectively, for Cell-I. The increase in *R*Ω and *R*P with the DRM fuel containing H2 and CO compared to H2 was caused by the decrease in fuel reducibility, increasing the electrical resistance of GDC [36] and the increase in activation energy for CO electrochemical oxidation.

Figure 6 shows the electrochemical performances of Cell-C and Cell-I using H2 and CO2–CH4 as fuels, respectively, and air as the oxidant at different temperatures. Due to the decrease in the electrochemical impedance of the anode, Cell-I demonstrated a significantly greater maximum power density (P*max) than Cell-C in the temperature range between 750 and 600 °C regardless of whether it was fuelled by H2 (Figs. 6(a) and 6(b)) or CO2–CH4 (Figs. 6(c) and 6(d)). With H2, *P*max of Cell-I ranged from 1.44 to 0.92 W·cm−2, while that of Cell-C ranged from 0.83 to 0.33 W·cm−2; with CO2–CH4, *P*max of Cell-I ranged from 1.09 to 0.50 W·cm–2, in contrast to that from 0.68 to 0.23 W·cm−2 for Cell-C. At low temperatures of 650 and 600 °C, the difference in the performance between the two cell types increased. As characterized above, in the BCFN-impregnated anode, small BCFN particles covered the entire pore surface of the anode, and large BCFN aerogels with a high specific surface area filled the large pores near the anode surface. After reduction in H2, FeNi3 nanoparticles formed on the BCFN matrix with abundant oxygen vacancies. The performance improvement of Cell-I over Cell-C with H2 stemmed mostly from the solution-impregnated small BCFN particles with exsolved FeNi3 nanoparticles. They reached the inner region of the anode in the vicinity of the GDC electrolyte, promoting the electrochemical oxidation reactions of H2. When exposed to CO2–CH4, the suspension-impregnated large BCFN aerogels in the region of the anode near the surface are considered to play a critical role in catalyzing the reactions of DRM and promoting the resistance to carbon deposition, enhancing the performance of Cell-I. At low temperatures, the activity of the Ni-GDC anode decreased significantly, and the contribution of the impregnated BCFN particles was more noticeable.

### 3.4 Performance durability in CO2–CH4

Durability tests were conducted for Cell-C and Cell-I at 400 mA·cm−2 and 700 °C with 45% CO2−45% CH4−10% N2 to further demonstrate the advantage of the BCFN-impregnated anode for on-cell DRM. The performance stability and posttest analysis results are shown in Fig. 7. The voltage of Cell-C fluctuated significantly and decreased continuously for 120 h, followed by an abrupt decrease from nearly 0.5 to 0.2 V (Fig. 7(a)). On-line analysis of anode exhaust gas revealed that although the traditional Ni–GDC anode is a good catalytic material, its susceptibility to carbon deposition hinders its effective conversion of CH4 in CO2 into CO and H2, with more than 17% of CH4 still present in the exhaust gas and a CO/H2 ratio lower than 40% (Fig. 7(c)). In contrast, Cell-I maintained a stable performance above 0.55 V during the long-term test for 400 h without noticeable voltage fluctuations (Fig. 7(b)). The fluctuations at approximately 160 and 260 h were caused by the instability of the atmosphere following the replenishment of fuel gas. CH4 was fully converted by DRM with a CO/H2 ratio higher than 60% in the exhaust gas (Fig. 7(d)), suggesting a much-improved catalytic activity of the BCFN-impregnated anode for DRM, with most of the produced H2 selectively consumed for power generation and a significant amount of CO remaining. ## 4 Conclusions

An SOFC anode was developed with a new structure using a phase-inversion-prepared Ni–GDC scaffold, which was impregnated sequentially with a BCFN solution and a BCFN suspension. The performance of the anode was evaluated in cells with H2 and CH4–CO2 fuels. After reduction, FeNi3 nanoparticles exsolved on a BCFN matrix with abundant surface oxygen vacancies. FeNi3 was less susceptible to carbon deposition compared to Ni, and the surface oxygen vacancies adsorbed H2O, which increased the resistance to the carbon deposition. Small BCFN particles formed by BCFN solution impregnation covered the entire pore surface of the phase-in-version prepared Ni–GDC scaffold; large BCFN aerogels produced by BCFN suspension impregnation with a high specific surface area filled the large pores of the scaffold near the surface, serving as a catalytic layer for on-cell DRM. The cell with the BCFN-impregnated Ni–GDC anode demonstrated a much greater electrochemical performance with H2 and CH4–CO2 as fuels. This is because the solution-impregnated BCFN particles in the anode increase the electrical conductivity and electrochemical activity. The cell with BCFN-impregnated Ni–GDC anode demonstrates long-term stable performance with 45% CO2–45% CH4–10% N2 as fuel because the BCFN-impregnated Ni–GDC anode is more active for on-cell DRM and more resistant to carbon deposition than the conventional Ni–GDC anode.
