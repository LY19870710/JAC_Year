## 1 Introduction

Transparent ceramics have extensive applications due to their special properties [1−3]. Since neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet ceramics were first prepared by Ikesue [4,5], highly transparent polycrystalline Y3Al5O12 (YAG) ceramics have been extensively studied [6−8]. When the Y3+ sites in YAG are substituted by Lu3+, another kind of garnet with the same structure, Lu3Al5O12 (LuAG), is formed [9−11]. For laser systems operating at room temperature, Nd:YAG is one of the most commonly used gain media [12−14], but its relatively low saturation fluence limits its scaling performance for high-power laser applications. Recently, Nd:LuAG has shown exceptional scaling performance, with a saturation fluence of 1.93 J/cm², which is three times greater than that of Nd:YAG [15]. Compared with Nd:YAG (84 cm−1), Nd:LuAG has been shown to exhibit a smaller splitting of the 4F3/2 state (67 cm−1) [16], resulting in greater amplification at the 1064 nm lasing wavelength. On the other hand, the thermal conductivity of YAG is greater than that of LuAG. However, in the case of YAG, a very large mass difference between Nd3+ (144 g/mol) and Y3+ (88.9 g/mol) exists. In contrast, the atomic mass of Lu3+ (175 g/mol) is much closer to that of Nd3+. By Nd3+ doping, the thermal conductivity of YAG decreases greatly, but that of LuAG declines only moderately [17,18]. Wagner *et al*. [19] first fabricated Nd:LuAG transparent ceramics and investigated the microstructures of the samples in 2012. In conclusion, Nd:LuAG ceramics have shown great potential as gain media in high-energy amplifiers with strong energy storage capacity [20−22].

In high-power laser systems, thermal effects fundamentally constrain the achievable power density within the gain medium because of localized heat accumulation [23−25]. Increasing the size of the gain medium can enhance heat dissipation and partially alleviate these limitations [26−28]. However, when the gain medium has a high aspect ratio, the spontaneous fluorescence can be amplified to extremely high power. The spontaneous fluorescence that propagates perpendicular to the main beam results in amplified spontaneous emission (ASE) and parasitic oscillation (PO), which depletes the upper laser level population and gives rise to undesirable POs [29,30]. Therefore, cladding on the side surface of the gain medium is an efficient way to suppress lateral reflection and improve laser performance [31−33]. Based on the design requirements, cladding layers must meet the following functional criteria: (1) a high absorption coefficient at the laser emission wavelength (1064 nm), (2) refractive index matching with the gain medium, (3) seamless integration with the gain medium to minimize optical losses, and (4) excellent transparency (> 80%) at the pump wavelength (808 nm), which is particularly important in edge-pumped configurations where lateral photon leakage plays a significant role in energy dissipation [34,35]. In 2006, Sm:YAG-Nd:YAG composite ceramics with a volume of 120 mm × 120 mm × 20 mm were reported by Yagi and Yanagitani [36], and a high output power of 67 kW was achieved. In 2013, Gonçalvès-Novo *et al*. [37] used Yb:YAG AM Lucia laser amplifier and achieved 14 J at a 2 Hz laser output. In 2017, Ma *et al*. [38] reported a 10.3 mJ amplified pulse using Nd:LuAG ceramics seeded by a 5.2 mJ Nd:YAG Q-switched oscillator, corresponding to a pulse peak power of 1.47 MW. In 2019, a 10.3 J, 10 Hz output was achieved via a Nd:LuAG ceramic nanosecond laser amplifier [13]. Kong *et al*. [39] also utilized Sm:YAG cladding ceramics to suppress the ASE of parasitic ring modes at 1064 nm, achieving a single-pulse energy of 1.66 mJ with a repetition rate of 10 Hz.

To minimize interfacial reflection losses, cladding materials must have a refractive index that precisely matches the optical properties of the gain medium. This requirement inherently favors compositional continuity between the cladding and gain materials. Ceramic-based solutions offer distinct advantages over single-crystal alternatives, enabling cost-effective production of large-scale components while maintaining comparable optical performance. Their scalability in bulk manufacturing helps overcome critical cost barriers in high-power laser systems without sacrificing spectral integrity [26]. Thus, ceramic materials are suitable for high-power lasers as well as for cladding. For the cladding materials used in Nd:LuAG high-power solid-state lasers, Sm:LuAG ceramics exhibit superior characteristics, such as a high absorption coefficient at 1064 nm and high transparency at a pump wavelength of 808 nm, making Sm:LuAG ceramics very effective in suppressing the ASE and PO effects. Compared with Sm:YAG ceramics, Sm:LuAG ceramics have a higher cost. However, for solid-state lasers using Nd:LuAG ceramics as the gain medium, Sm:LuAG ceramics have a refractive index closer to that of Nd:LuAG ceramics, allowing for better integration with the gain medium. This helps reduce the difficulty of preparing clad composite ceramics and minimizes optical losses.

In general, RE:LuAG ceramics can be fabricated by two main methods. For the conventional solid-state reaction method, which starts with commercial oxides, high sintering temperature and prolonged sintering time are necessary to gain fully dense ceramics. In our previous studies, high-quality transparent 5 at% Sm:LuAG ceramics were fabricated by the solid-state reactive sintering method. However, the ceramic samples had relatively large grain sizes (> 10 μm). Reducing the grain size of ceramics can improve the bending strength and thermal shock resistance of ceramic material [40]. The thermal effect is considered to be the most common failure mode in high-power solid-state lasers. The co-precipitation method exhibits considerable advantages, such as homogeneous mixing of the starting materials, excellent chemical homogeneity, and high sintering activity. In 2023, Li *et al*. [41] synthesized Tb(1+*x*)3(Al0.996255Si0.003745)5O12.0093625+3*x*/2 nano-powders by a co-precipitation method, which produced transparent terbium aluminum garnet (TAG) ceramics that exhibited exceptional optical quality with an in-line transmittance of 82.6% at 1064 nm. Thus, the co-precipitation method is considered a promising route to prepare high-optical-quality and fine-grained ceramics.

In the present work, Sm:LuAG transparent ceramics were fabricated from nano-powders synthesized by co-precipitation method with different Sm3+ concentrations. The influences of Sm3+ concentration on the microstructure and optical transmittance of Sm:LuAG ceramics were systematically evaluated.

## 2 Experimental

Lu(NO3)3 and Sm(NO3)3 solutions were prepared by dissolving Lu2O3 (99.999%, Sheeny Metal Material Co., Ltd., China) and Sm2O3 (99.99%, Alfa Aesar, USA) in diluted nitric acid under stirring and heating, respectively. Al(NO3)3 solution was prepared by dissolving Al(NO3)3·9H2O (99.99%, Aladdin, China) in deionized water. The above nitrate solutions were mixed together to obtain a mixed solution of 0.48 mol/L for the metal ions. The precipitant solution was prepared by dissolving ammonium hydrogen carbonate (99.995%, Aladdin, China) and ammonium sulfate (analytical grade, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) in deionized water. The concentrations of ammonium carbonate and ammonium sulfate in the precipitant were 1.5 mol/L. The excess amount of ammonium bicarbonate relative to the metal salts is approximately 5%. The mixed salt solutions were dripped into the precipitant solution at a speed of 20 mL/min with different stoichiometric ratios of (Sm*x*Lu1−*x*)3Al5O12 (*x* = 0.03, 0.05, 0.07, 0.09). The pH of the reaction medium was 6.2. The as-prepared precursor was aged for 1 h and then washed several times with deionized water and ethanol. Then the precursor was dried at 70 °C for 48 h. After sieving through a 200-mesh screen, the precursor was calcined at 1100 °C for 4 h to obtain 3–9 at% Sm:LuAG nano-powders. The powders were then uniaxially pressed into pellets at 20 MPa, followed by cold isostatic pressing (CIP) at 250 MPa. The green bodies were vacuum pre-sintered at 1550 °C for 3 h. The pre-sintered ceramics were subjected to high-temperature hot isostatic pressing (HIP) post-treatment at 1550 °C for 3 h under a 200 MPa argon atmosphere. Finally, the ceramics were mirror polished to a thickness of 1.5 mm and annealed at 1100 °C for 10 h in air for subsequent characterization. The Sm:LuAG ceramics underwent thermal etching at 1100 °C for 5 h, and micrographs of the ceramics were obtained.

The micrographs of the starting powders and polished ceramics were observed by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; SU8220, Hitachi, Japan) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDS; X-MaxN 80, Oxford Instruments, UK). The phase composition of the nano-powders was identified by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Bruker D8 Focus, Germany) with Cu K*α*1 radiation (*λ* = 0.15405 nm) in the range of 2*θ* = 10°–80° using nickel-filtered Cu-K*α* radiation. Before the FESEM observations, the ceramics were thermally etched at 1200 °C for 3 h in air. The average grain sizes were measured via common linear intercept analysis (more than 200 grains were counted) according to the equation GS = 1.56*L*, where *L* is the mean intercept. The in-line transmittance and absorption curves of the mirror-polished samples were measured by an ultraviolet–visible–near infrared (UV–VIS–NIR) spectrophotometer (Cary-5000, Varian, USA). The optical scattering sources of the polished ceramics were observed by a transparent microscope (optical microscope; BX51TF, Olympus, Japan).

Figure 1 displays the XRD patterns of 3–9 at% Sm:LuAG nano-powders calcined at 1100 °C for 4 h. XRD analysis demonstrates that all samples, regardless of Sm concentration, exhibit a pure LuAG phase after calcination under an air atmosphere. No secondary phases were detected within the instrumental resolution limit of the XRD measurements.

Figure 2 presents the lattice parameter a* of the Sm:LuAG nano-powders as a function of Sm concentration. Compared with those of undoped LuAG (11.9064 Å), the lattice parameters of Sm:LuAG nano-powders with different doping amounts exhibit a systematic increase, which can be attributed to the ionic size effect. At a coordination number of 8, the ionic radius of Sm3+ (1.079 Å) significantly exceeds that of Lu3+ (0.977 Å). Consequently, the substitution of Lu3+ by Sm3+ in the LuAG lattice induces lattice expansion, resulting in increased lattice parameters. As the Sm3+ concentration increases from 3 to 9 at%, the lattice parameters increase, with measured values of 11.9247, 11.9315, 11.9446, and 11.9504 Å, respectively.

Figure 7 shows a photograph and the in-line transmittance curves of the Sm:LuAG ceramics with different doping amounts pre-sintered at 1550 °C for 3 h and subjected to HIP post-treatment at 1550 °C for 3 h. All the ceramic samples were double-polished to a thickness of 1.5 mm. After HIP post-treatment, all the Sm:LuAG ceramics are transparent, and the words below the ceramics can be clearly observed. The optical loss in transparent ceramics mainly consists of absorption loss (due to impurity ions, oxygen vacancies, etc.) and scattering loss (due to pores, second phases, surface flatness, etc.). The intensity (I*) of transmitted light can be expressed by Eq. (1):

*
Fig. 7(a) Photograph and (b) in-line transmittance of Sm:LuAG ceramics with different doping amounts.

(1) I=I0(1−R)2exp[−2(α+Sp+Sb)t]

where I*0 is the incident light intensity; *R* is the reflectance; *α* is the absorption coefficient of the sample; *S*p and *S*b are the scattering coefficients due to pores and secondary phases, respectively. To obtain high-optical-quality transparent ceramics, it is necessary to reduce the sources of optical losses within the ceramic material. Owing to the significant difference in ionic radii between Sm3+ and Lu3+, as the concentration of Sm3+ increases, the lattice distortion in Sm:LuAG ceramics becomes more severe, which enhances light scattering and subsequently reduces the optical quality of the ceramics. This phenomenon is likely the main reason for the decrease in the linear transmittance of Sm:LuAG transparent ceramics with increasing doping concentration. As the Sm3+ concentration increases from 3 to 9 at%, nearly fully dense ceramics are achieved after HIP post-treatment, and the scattering loss caused by residual pores is reduced. The 3 at% Sm:LuAG ceramics possess the best in-line transmittance of 83.9% at 808 nm. Furthermore, the high in-line transmittance in the absorption band of Nd:LuAG suggests that Sm:LuAG ceramics are well-suited as cladding materials for edge-pumped lasers.

Figure 9 shows the absorption curves of the Sm:LuAG ceramics (1.5 mm thick) with different doping amounts pre-sintered at 1550 °C for 3 h and subjected to HIP post-treatment at 1550 °C for 3 h. Distinct absorption features in the near-infrared spectrum are observed at approximate wavelengths of 1070, 1229, 1360, and 1467 nm. The prominent peak centered at 1070 nm is attributed to the 6H5/2 → 6F9/2 electronic transition of Sm3+ ions. With the Sm3+ concentration increasing from 3 to 9 at%, the absorption coefficients of the ceramics at 1064 nm are 2.44, 3.36, 4.53, and 5.99 cm−1, respectively. Importantly, the 1064 nm absorption coefficient of Sm:LuAG ceramics closely aligns with reported values for Sm:YAG systems [8], suggesting comparable photon attenuation mechanisms. The dominant electronic transition of Sm3+ ions occurs within the 1045–1085 nm spectral range, and its spectral overlap with the characteristic 1064 nm emission band of Nd3+ ions. ## 4 Conclusions

Sm:LuAG transparent ceramics with different doping amounts were fabricated by vacuum pre-sintering and HIP post-treatment with the co-precipitated powders. The nano-powders calcined at 1100 °C for 4 h showed dendritic agglomerations but relatively small particle sizes and high uniformity. The Sm:LuAG transparent ceramics with different doping amounts all had a clear and uniform microstructure. All the ceramic samples show a uniform grain size distribution after HIP post-treatment at 1550 °C for 3 h. The 3 at% Sm:LuAG ceramics exhibit an excellent in-line transmittance of 83.9% at 808 nm and a fine grain size of 909 nm. The high in-line transmittance in the pump band of Nd:LuAG suggests that the 3 at% Sm:LuAG ceramics are highly suitable as cladding materials for edge-pumped lasers, significantly reducing pump light losses. Additionally, the optimal 3 at% Sm:LuAG ceramics demonstrate an absorption coefficient of 2.44 cm−1 at 1064 nm, effectively suppressing the PO and ASE effects.
