## 1 Introduction

With the rapid advancement of wireless communication technologies and electronic devices, the issue of electromagnetic interference (EMI) has become increasingly critical, significantly affecting the stability and reliability of electronic systems [1−3]. This challenge is especially prominent in the aerospace industry, where EMI shielding materials are frequently exposed to high-temperature and oxidative environments, such as those encountered in aero engines and reusable launch vehicles. EMI can lead to data loss and misinterpretation during signal transmission, potentially resulting in equipment malfunction or system degradation [4−7]. As electronic devices continue to evolve toward higher performance and miniaturization, the impact of EMI has intensified, necessitating the development of advanced EMI shielding materials with enhanced properties [8−11].

Although conventional metallic shielding materials exhibit high electrical conductivity and demonstrate effective EMI shielding performance, their applicability in modern electronic systems is limited by several drawbacks, including substantial weight, susceptibility to corrosion, and the propensity to generate secondary electromagnetic (EM) reflections [12,13]. In weight-sensitive applications such as aerospace, there is an urgent need for novel shielding materials that combine lightweight characteristics with high strength, thermal stability, and oxidation resistance. To meet this demand, researchers have explored ceramic-based EMI shielding materials [14−16]. While traditional oxide ceramics exhibit excellent thermal stability, their dielectric loss capabilities are often insufficient for effective EMI shielding. Recent efforts have focused on incorporating highly conductive carbon-based materials, such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and graphene, into ceramic matrices [17−19]. Although these composites have shown some promise, they typically suffer from high brittleness and require significant thickness, which limits their practical utility. Additionally, two-dimensional (2D) transition metal carbides and nitrides (MXenes) have attracted considerable attention because of their unique EM properties [20−23]. However, their limited high-temperature stability and mechanical strength remain significant barriers to widespread application.

Compared with purely functional materials, structurally functional integrated materials that combine load-bearing capabilities with EMI shielding performance demonstrate greater potential for practical applications [24−26]. These materials not only reduce manufacturing costs but also enable more compact and durable designs. As a new class of functional materials, EMI shielding ceramics stand out because of their ability to integrate the inherent advantages of ceramics with EMI shielding functionality. These materials typically enhance conductivity by incorporating metal or alloy powders, such as silver, nickel, or copper, thereby enabling efficient reflection or absorption of EM waves [27−31]. The key features of these ceramics include (i) broadband EMI shielding performance, covering a wide frequency range from low frequencies to microwaves; (ii) excellent thermal stability, making them suitable for high-temperature environments; (iii) superior corrosion resistance and environmental adaptability, ensuring reliable performance under harsh conditions; and (iv) flexible structural designability, allowing fabrication into various forms such as films, sheets, and blocks to meet diverse application needs, including EMI shielding windows, packaging materials, and circuit board substrates. These attributes position EMI shielding ceramic materials as promising candidates for use in electronic devices, communication systems, military equipment, medical instruments, and aerospace applications. The development of lightweight, high-strength, thermally stable ceramic materials with superior EMI shielding performance will be crucial for addressing equipment protection challenges in complex EM environments.

## 2 Design principle of ceramic-based EMI shielding materials

Ceramic-based EMI shielding materials are functional materials that mitigate EMI through the reflection and absorption of EM radiation. Their research and application have gained increasing importance with the widespread use of electronic devices in commercial, defense, and scientific research domains. The shielding performance of such materials is governed primarily by three key mechanisms [32−35]. First, reflection loss occurs due to impedance mismatch at the material’s surface, which causes EM waves to be reflected. Metallic materials exhibit pronounced reflective characteristics due to their intrinsic impedance, which is typically on the order of milliohms and therefore markedly distinct from the 377 Ω wave impedance of free space. Second, absorption loss arises from the attenuation of EM waves as they propagate through the material, mainly due to dielectric and magnetic losses. The magnitude of this loss is influenced by material properties such as electrical conductivity (*σ*), magnetic permeability (*μ*), and thickness (*d*). Third, multiple reflection losses become prominent in thin-layer materials whosethickness is less than the skin depth (*δ*), allowing for repeated internal reflections that further dissipate EM energy (Fig. 1(a)).

### 2.1 Theoretical principle

When EM radiation interacts with a protective cover, part of the radiation is absorbed, while another portion is reflected. Simultaneously, a certain amount of energy continues to propagate through the shielding material. The EMI shielding effectiveness (SE), a critical parameter for evaluating the performance of shielding materials, is defined as the ratio of incident EM power to transmitted EM power, typically expressed in decibels (dB) (Eq. (2.1)) [34,36−38]:

(2.1) SE=10log(PinPt)=20log(EinEt)=20log(HinHt)

where *P*in, *E*in, and *H*in denote the power, electric field intensity, and magnetic field intensity incident on the surface of the shielding material, respectively. *P*t, *E*t, and *H*t represent the corresponding strength parameters after transmission through the shielding material. Notably, the EMI SE value is positively correlated with the EMI shielding performance. A higher SE value indicates a lower amount of transmitted EM energy. According to Schelkunoff’s theory [39−41], the total EMI SE (SET) is composed of three primary mechanisms: reflection loss (SER), absorption loss (SEA), and multiple reflection loss (SEM). The combined contribution of these three mechanisms constitutes the overall EMI SE (Eq. (2.2)) [39,42]:

(2.2) SET=SER+SEA+SEM

When the thickness of the shielding material exceeds the skin depth, the reflected wave from the internal surface is substantially attenuated by the conductive medium, rendering the multiple-reflection effects negligible. In contrast, for shielding layers with a thickness smaller than the skin depth, multiple-reflection phenomena become prominent and can significantly compromise the overall EMI shielding performance. For far-field planar EM waves, the SER, SEA, and SEM can be expressed as functions of frequency (*f*) and conductivity (*σ*) (Eqs. (2.3)–(2.5)) [43−45]:

(2.3) SER=20log(Z+Z0)4ZZ0=39.5+10logσ2fπμ

(2.4) SEA=20(dδ)loge=8.7(dδ)=8.7dfμrσ2

(2.5) SEM=20log(1−e−2dδ)=20log(1−10SEA10)

where *μ*, *d*, and *δ* represent the magnetic permeability and thickness of the sample, and the skin depth, respectively. Notably, the absorbed energy increases as the *d* increases, and the *δ* (*δ* = (π*fμσ*)(−1/2)) decreases. Accordingly, the *δ* decreases with increasing *σ*, *μ*, and *f*. For the design of EMI ceramics, several core principles need to be followed.

#### 2.1.1 Electrical conductivity optimization

The development of ceramics with high *σ* is essential for EMI shielding applications. The imaginary part (*ε"*) of the dielectric constant and *σ* are related by *ε"* = *σ*/(2π*ε*0*f*). Here, *ε*0 is the dielectric constant of free space (8.854×10−12 F/m). Owing to the skin effect, there exists a critical volume fraction of the conductive filler in the insulator‒conductor composite, commonly referred to as the percolation threshold. The *σ* and the real part (*ε'*) of the dielectric constant as functions of the conducting phase content exhibit a power-law behavior (Eqs. (2.6) and (2.7)) [46,47]:

(2.6) σm=σc[(Φ−Φc)/(1−Φc)]t(Φ>Φc)

(2.7) ϵm=ϵc(Φc−Φ)−s(ΦΦc)

where *σ*m and *σ*c are the effective direct current conductivities of the composite and the conducting filler, respectively; *ε*m and *ε*c represent the *ε'* of the composite and the insulating component, respectively; *t* and *s* are the conductivity exponent and critical exponent, respectively, which describe the divergent behavior of the conductivity. *Φ* and *Φ*c denote the volume fraction and the critical volume fraction (i.e., percolation threshold) of the conducting component, respectively.

In terms of electrical conductivity optimization, percolation theory suggests that when the concentration of conductive fillers (e.g., CNTs and graphene) surpasses the critical percolation threshold (*Φ*c), the electrical conductivity of the composite increases exponentially [48−52]. At this stage, reducing the filler size can significantly enhance the interfacial utilization efficiency of the material. The primary mechanism for EMI shielding is the reflection of EM waves, which necessitates that the shielding material contains mobile charge carriers (electrons or holes) capable of interacting with EM radiation. Metals, as the most commonly used EMI shielding materials, exhibit an intrinsic impedance on the order of milliohms, which contrasts sharply with the free-space wave impedance of 377 Ω [53,54]. This discrepancy results in a pronounced reflection effect when EM waves encounter the metal surface. In practical applications, a thin metallic foil layer applied to the surface of a structure can effectively reflect incident EM waves, preserving the electrical integrity of the system and preventing EM leakage.

#### 2.1.2 Dielectric property regulation

McLachlan *et al*. [55] proposed a generalized effective medium equation to describe the permittivity of a 2D two-component composite. This model integrates effective medium theory with percolation scaling laws and is referred to as the two-exponent single percolation equation (TESPE) ((Eq. (2.8)):

(2.8) Φ1(ϵ11/s−ϵ1/s)ϵ11/s+[(1−Φ2c)/Φ2c]ϵ1/s+Φ2(ϵ11/t−ϵ1/t)ϵ11/t+[(1−Φ2c)/Φ2c]ϵ1/t=0

The complex permittivity (*ε*) of a two-phase composite is determined by the surface fractions *Φ*1 and *Φ*2, as well as the *ε*1 and *ε*2 of component 1 (continuous matrix) and component 2 (inclusion), respectively. *Φ*2c denotes the surface fraction of the percolation threshold. With respect to dielectric property regulation, TESPE offers a theoretical foundation for accurately predicting the dielectric behavior of composites. *ε'* determines the phase velocity and wavelength of EM waves, whereas *ε"* governs the intensity of dielectric loss. The absorption loss capability depends on *ε* (*ε* = *ε'* + j*ε"*). When EM waves penetrate a material, the conductive network induces dipole and interfacial polarization, converting EM energy into thermal energy. The absorption loss increases exponentially with increasing material thickness, and its efficiency can be quantified by *δ*. For highly conductive materials, the *δ* can be reduced to the micrometer scale within the GHz frequency range. Notably, EMI shielding ceramics require both high *ε'* and high *ε"* values, which has led to the widespread adoption of novel high-conductivity carbon materials such as CNTs and graphene in the development of EMI shielding ceramics [35,56−58]. In summary, to increase *ε'* and *ε"*, EMI shielding ceramics should possess conductivity and may partially incorporate semiconductor or insulating phases. EMI shielding ceramics containing fine conductive secondary phases can exhibit effective shielding performance only when the volume fraction of these secondary phases reaches the percolation threshold.

#### 2.1.3 Structural design criteria

Regarding structural design criteria, it is essential that the selected thickness satisfies *d* ≈ *δ* to minimize the effects of multiple reflections [43,59]. Multiple reflections refer to repeated reflections that occur at various surfaces or interfaces within a material. This phenomenon typically requires the material to possess a large surface area or extensive interfacial regions. Therefore, the occurrence of multiple reflections within the thin shielding layer can lead to a reduction in the overall EMI SE. According to Eq. (2.5), such coherent reflections can degrade the overall shielding efficiency [60]. To mitigate this phenomenon, interface polarization can be enhanced by constructing a three-dimensional (3D) conductive network and introducing heterogeneous interfaces, or a gradient impedance structure can be employed to achieve gradual wave impedance matching (Fig. 1(b)).

### 2.2 Measurement methods for EMI SE

The measurement methods for EMI shielding ceramics are categorized primarily into the coaxial method and the waveguide method, which are based on the frequency range of the EM waves. These methods are applicable to different frequency bands and are grounded in distinct principles of EM wave propagation. The coaxial method is based on coaxial transmission line theory, and the test material is placed between the inner and outer conductors of a coaxial fixture, forming a ring-shaped specimen. This method is typically applicable within the frequency range of 30 MHz–1.5 GHz and is suitable for evaluating the EMI shielding performance of materials under plane wave field conditions. The scattering parameters (*S*11, *S*21, *S*12, and *S*22) before and after the insertion of the material are measured using a vector network analyzer (VNA), and the EMI SE value is subsequently calculated [8,61]. These scattering parameters are directly associated with the absorption coefficient (*A*), reflection coefficient (*R*), and transmission coefficient (*T*) (Eqs. (2.9)–(2.11)):

(2.9) A=1−(R+T)

(2.10) R=|S11|2=|S22|2

(2.11) T=|S12|2=|S21|2

The EMI SE can be formulated in terms of the EMI shielding mechanism coefficients and *S*-parameters as Eqs. (2.12)–(2.14) [62,63]:

(2.12) SET=10log(1T)=10log(1|S21|2)

(2.13) SER=10log(11−R)=10log(11−|S11|2)

(2.14) SEA=10log(11−Aeff)=10log(1−RT)=10log(11−|S11|2)

An SE of 20 dB indicates that 99% of the incident EM waves can be attenuated, which has become a fundamental criterion for commercial applications. The waveguide method involves filling the material into rectangular or circular waveguides and utilizing the waveguide’s cutoff frequency characteristics to support the propagation of a single mode. This method is suitable for high-frequency bands such as millimeter waves and terahertz waves (ranging from 1 to 110 GHz, depending on the waveguide dimensions). Additionally, the free-space method is employed for frequencies in the terahertz range and above. It utilizes antennas and anechoic environments to simulate far-field conditions. The shielded room method is used to directly assess the EMI shielding performance of materials in large-scale EM environments. However, this method is associated with high implementation costs.

In summary, the optimal design of EMI shielding ceramics requires a comprehensive evaluation of the synergistic effects among electrical conductivity, dielectric properties, and microstructural features. By leveraging percolation theory to guide filler distribution, applying the TESPE model to predict dielectric responses, and integrating multi-scale structural design strategies, high-performance EMI shielding materials can be developed to meet the demands of emerging electronic devices.

## 3 Traditional EMI shielding ceramics

In contrast to traditional insulating ceramics such as SiO2, BN, Si3N4, and Al2O3, composite systems containing conductive phases, including graphite, carbon nanotubes, ordered mesoporous carbon, and graphene, have effectively progressed from being electromagnetically transparent to functioning as highly efficient EMI shielding materials [64,65]. These composites not only retain the excellent mechanical properties and processability of the ceramic matrix but also significantly improve EMI SE through the formation of a conductive network. Notably, carbon-based materials have emerged as the most promising conductive reinforcement phases because of their exceptional electrical conductivity ((2–14)×106 S∙m–1) [66,67]. By precisely controlling the morphology, content, and spatial distribution of the conductive phase, researchers can achieve tailored design of the dielectric and conductive properties of the composite, thereby fulfilling the specific EMI shielding requirements across diverse application scenarios.

### 3.1 Oxide ceramics

Although oxide ceramics are not considered traditional primary materials for EMI shielding compared with their metallic counterparts, they play an indispensable role in specific high-end applications because of their unique properties, including high-temperature resistance, corrosion resistance, and functionalization capabilities. For example, Zhou *et al*. [68] fabricated TiO2/Cu/TiO2 multilayer films on quartz glass substrates using the magnetron sputtering technique, and the EMI SE of the TiO2/Cu/TiO2 multilayer film with a thickness configuration of 30/34/30 nm exceeded 19.0 dB. Xiang *et al*. [69] investigated the EMI shielding performance of ZnO ceramics fabricated using conventional processing techniques and high-energy planetary ball milling. The SET reached 26.3 dB at a thickness of 1.8 mm. The *ε'* of ball-milled ZnO is 15, which is greater than that of conventionally processed ZnO (*ε'* ≈ 3).

Given the high electrical conductivity and large aspect ratio of carbon materials, it is anticipated that carbon-reinforced ceramic composites will demonstrate highly promising EMI shielding performance. For example, the percolation threshold of ordered mesoporous carbon (OMC)-filled SiO2 (OMC/SiO2) is approximately 3%. When the volume fraction of OMC reaches 10 vol%, absorption-dominated SE is still observed, resulting in an EMI SE as high as 40.0 dB. In this case, the EMI SE of the OMC/SiO2 composite is 35.0 dB. When the carbon content increases to 33 vol%, the electrical conductivity of the OMC/SiO2 composite reaches 1.47×103 S∙m–1, and the *ε*" is 1.5×103 [70]. Similarly, when 10 vol% CNTs were dispersed in SiO2, the *ε*" reached 31–47. Compared with that of molten SiO2, the *ε*" of the CNT–SiO2 composite is approximately 3×104 times greater than that of pure SiO2. The incorporation of CNTs significantly enhances the electrical conductivity of the SiO2 matrix, thereby endowing the material with a high EMI SE of 34.0 dB [71]. Furthermore, the EMI SE of SiO2-based composites incorporating short carbon fibers (CFs) (*σ* = 3×104–3.5×104 S∙m−1, diameter of 7 μm, and length of 1 mm) exceeds 10.0 dB. In comparison, SiO2–CFs exhibit a *σ* of 4.6×104 S∙m−1 and achieve an EMI SE value of 124.0 dB within the frequency range of 0.3 MHz–1.5 GHz [72]. In particular, Lee *et al*. [73] fabricated ZrO2-embedded CF materials by electrospinning and heat treatment techniques. The average EMI SE reached 31.8 dB within the frequency range of 800–8500 MHz. The EMI shielding performance of these fibers is attributed primarily to the absorption of EM waves, thereby minimizing the secondary EMI by reducing EM wave reflection. When the conductivity of the conductive secondary phase is relatively low, a significantly higher aspect ratio is required to generate a substantial current under an applied EM field.

Moreover, carbon-reinforced ceramic composites offer not only superior EMI SE but also excellent mechanical properties. For example, Al2O3 composites embedded with highly aligned reduced graphene oxide (RGO) can be efficiently fabricated via pressure-assisted sintering of the RGO@Al2O3 nanoplate core‒shell structure. Compared with those of conventional Al2O3 ceramics, the strength and fracture toughness of the highly aligned RGO/Al2O3 composite are significantly enhanced by approximately 26.1% and 60.2%, respectively. Moreover, owing to the enhanced absorption and reflection effects resulting from out-of-plane infiltration, the composite demonstrates a high EMI SE of 34.0 dB in the K band [74]. Coincidentally, Li *et al*. [75] fabricated a Ti4O7/CNT/Al2O3 coating via the plasma spraying method. For Ti4O7 coatings induced with different oxygen vacancy levels, the enhanced *ε*' and *ε*'' within the X-band frequency range are attributed to the synergistic regulation of conductive pathways, structural defects, and interfacial polarization effects (Fig. 2). The EMI SE of the Ti4O7/CNT/Al2O3 sample containing 5 wt% CNTs increased to 20.5 dB, which was attributed primarily to the enhanced electrical conductivity. Notably, the flexural strength of the coating initially increased from 48.6 MPa (0 wt% CNTs) to 67.1 MPa (2.5 wt% CNTs) and subsequently decreased to 38.3 MPa (5 wt% CNTs). This trend indicates that an appropriate amount of CNTs uniformly dispersed within the Ti4O7/Al2O3 ceramic matrix can effectively function as a reinforcing phase [76,77]. Overall, while oxide ceramics exhibit favorable transparency and tunable dielectric properties in specific applications, carbide ceramics (e.g., SiC) present more significant advantages in terms of electrical conductivity, dielectric loss, and structural‒functional integration. These advantages are particularly evident in their ability to enable the development of efficient conductive networks and enhance EMI shielding capabilities.

### 3.2 Carbide ceramics

SiC-based ceramics exhibit excellent EM compatibility, outstanding mechanical strength, and high-temperature resistance, enabling reliable performance in extreme environments [78]. These materials are widely applied in the fabrication of forward cones for stealth cruise missiles and rocket engine casings, and they provide effective EMI shielding while maintaining structural integrity at elevated temperatures [79,80]. Although the intrinsic electrical conductivity of SiC is lower than that of carbon-based materials, SiC ceramics with nanoscale grain structures can effectively attenuate EM waves, thereby increasing EMI SE. For example, NiO nanocrystals loaded onto the surface of SiC powder exhibit a unique ring-like morphology that significantly improves the dielectric properties and enhances the EMI SE within the temperature range of 373–773 K [81]. Yin *et al*. [82] employed a chemical vapor infiltration (CVI) process to deposit SiC into porous yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) felt, forming a continuous SiC matrix around YSZ fibers. With a SiC content of 98%, the real part *ε*' and tan*δ* of the composite ranged from 1.2 to 10.8 and from 0.01 to 1.8, respectively. The EMI SE increased from 0.1 to 20.0 dB, demonstrating high surface impedance and low reflectivity. The dominant EM wave attenuation mechanism in this material is absorption.

Furthermore, SiC fiber-reinforced SiC composites (SiCf/SiC) maintain excellent high-temperature strength, oxidation resistance, and microstructural stability even under neutron irradiation. The *ε*′ and *ε*" of SiCf/SiC composites fabricated via the precursor impregnation and pyrolysis (PIP) process increase with increasing temperature within the range of 25–700 °C [83]. When the thickness is 310 nm, the EMI SE of SiCf/SiC composites containing a continuous pyrolytic carbon (PyC) interfacial phase prepared by CVI can reach approximately 25.0 dB at a frequency of 8.0–18.0 GHz [84]. Sun *et al*. [85] developed a flexible multilayer EMI shielding film based on a SiC matrix reinforced with CNTs (Fig. 3). This film was fabricated using a polycarbosilane/allyl phenolic resin precursor, which was converted *in situ* into SiC during sintering, ensuring strong interlayer bonding and a multilayer toughening effect, thereby significantly enhancing the tensile strength. By optimizing the SiC content and the number of CNT layers, the material achieved an EMI SE of 73.0 dB at a thickness of merely 50.0 μm. After the ablation test, it retained an EMI SE of 30.0 dB, surpassing the commercial requirement of 20.0 dB. This fabrication method is simple and holds potential for large-scale production. In particular, SiC nanowires (SiCnws) also demonstrate superior EMI shielding performance due to their 3D network structure, which facilitates multiple reflections and absorption of EM waves. For example, Ruan *et al*. [86] fabricated a dense SiCnw/SiC ceramic matrix composite by arranging 20 vol% SiCnws into an interlaced structure via filtration, followed by CVI introduction of the PyC interface and SiC matrix. As the PyC content increased from 21.3% to 29.5%, the average EMI SE of the porous SiCnws preform rose significantly from 9.2 to 64.1 dB in the X-band. Liu *et al*. [87] also fabricated a CFs/PyC/SiC–SiCNFe material by incorporating multilayer interfaces and magnetic particles. Multiphase heterogeneous interfaces induce lattice defects and enhance polarization effects, thereby improving EMI shielding performance. CFs contribute to conductance loss, while magnetic particles provide magnetic loss. Under synergistic effects, the EMI SE of the material reaches 62.1 dB in the 8.0–12.0 GHz band and 38.6 dB in the 12.0–18.0 GHz band. Additionally, this composite structure provides excellent mechanical support, with a flexural strength of 444.8±20.9 MPa.

This approach uniformly blends conductive carbon precursors with ceramic precursors, enabling simultaneous carbonization and ceramicization in a single thermal step. The EMI SE is 26.2 dB at 8.2 GHz, and the photothermal conversion performance surpasses that of the original ceramic. This process is scalable and shows promise for applications in Joule heating (e.g., deicing, boiling, and cooking) and EMI shielding. Hou *et al*. [89] produced a lightweight and flexible ZrC/SiC hybrid nanofiber pad using polycarbosilane (PCS) and zirconium acetylacetonate (Zr(acac)3) as precursors via electrospinning and high-temperature pyrolysis. The addition of Zr(acac)3 reduces the viscosity of the PCS solution and enhances its conductivity. After pyrolysis, highly conductive ZrC nanoparticles are uniformly dispersed within the SiC nanofibers, increasing the electrical conductivity from 1.5×10−6 to 1.3×10−1 S/cm. At a density of 0.06 g/cm3, the specific shielding efficiency reaches 315 (dB·cm3)/g, and it remains at 20.1 dB even at 600 °C, demonstrating potential for lightweight, high-efficiency, and high-temperature EMI shielding applications. To summarize, the high-temperature stability and mechanical strength make SiC-based ceramics particularly suitable for extreme environments. However, the limited electrical conductivity has imposed restrictions on practical applications. Compared with carbide ceramics, boride-based ultra-high-temperature ceramics (e.g., TiB2, HfB2, and ZrB2) not only possess higher melting points and enhanced oxidation and corrosion resistance but also demonstrate superior electrical and thermal conductivity [90,91]. These materials are capable of maintaining stable EMI shielding performance, particularly under extremely high-temperature and highly corrosive conditions.

### 3.3 Boride ceramics

Among various ultra-high temperature ceramic materials, boride ceramics exhibit superior electrical and thermal conductivity, along with enhanced corrosion resistance and excellent high-temperature oxidation resistance. These advantageous properties enable them to be used not only as standalone ultra-high temperature structural materials, but also as anti-oxidation coatings for other high-temperature structural materials. As functional materials, boride ceramics such as CrB2, ZrB2, and TiB2 are widely applied in wear-resistant and corrosion-resistant coatings, neutron-absorbing coatings, and self-melting alloys used for reinforcing similar materials and ultra-high temperature coatings [92,93].

For example, Niu *et al*. [94] synthesized hybrid phenolic precursors (BSiTa–phenolic aerogel (PA)) through coordination of metal precursors, followed by the preparation of hybrid phenolic aerogels via sol‒gel processing and ambient pressure drying. Subsequently, hybrid carbon aerogels were fabricated using a volume ablation method. The resulting phenolic aerogel exhibited excellent thermal stability, good mechanical strength, low thermal conductivity (49.6 mW/(m·K)), and strong ablation resistance. After exposure to extreme thermal erosion conditions, the *in-situ* formed carbon aerogel demonstrated superior EMI shielding performance and mechanical load-bearing capabilities. At 1400 °C, the carbon aerogel achieved a peak average EMI SE of 31.6 dB, a maximum electrical conductivity of 473.9 S/m, and a specific modulus of 272.8 (kN·m)/kg. Qiu *et al*. [95] fabricated a series of lightweight B4C–(Ti1−*x*Cr*x*)B2 (*x* = 0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, and 1) composites via reaction sintering (Fig. 4). These materials demonstrate excellent EMI shielding performance within the X-band frequency range (31.9–43.5 dB). Notably, the B4C composite incorporating the (Ti,Cr)B2 solid solution exhibits significantly improved absorption loss. This enhancement can be attributed to the presence of abundant nanotwins and stacking faults within the B4C matrix, as well as the severe lattice distortion and high-density dislocations observed in the diboride phases, which collectively contribute to increased polarization.

### 3.4 Nitride ceramics

Si3N4 ceramics have emerged as a technical benchmark in the field of high-temperature EMI shielding because of their dense atomic structure, inherent EM attenuation capability, and extremely low coefficient of thermal expansion [24,96]. For example, Cheng *et al*. [97] synthesized Ag/Si3N4 composites via an impregnation‒calcination process. As the Ag content increases, the material transitions from semiconductive property to metallic conductivity. The composite achieves an average total SE of approximately 30.0 dB. On one hand, the high-polarity Si–N bonds, with an electronegativity difference of up to 0.8, can induce significant dipole polarization under high-frequency electric fields. As a result, the material exhibits a tan*δ*ε ranging from 0.005 to 0.007 within the 1.0–40.0 GHz frequency band, allowing it to absorb more than 30% of incident EM wave energy. On the other hand, when a porous gradient structure with a porosity ranging from 10% to 30% is designed, the propagation path of EM waves within the material is significantly extended. This structural design enhances the EMI SE by more than 40%. Similarly, Si3N4 ceramics incorporating 5 wt% graphene achieved an EMI SE of 67.2 dB in the X-band, surpassing that of conventional aluminum materials (approximately 40.0 dB), which highlights its superior performance in both EM wave absorption and reflection. The material exhibits a thermal expansion coefficient of merely 3.1×10–6 °C−1, approximately one-fifth that of stainless steel, thereby ensuring that the thermal expansion remains below 0.05% across a temperature range from ambient to 1200 °C and effectively mitigates the deformation induced by thermal stress [98]. In strong radiation environments, such as those encountered in nuclear applications, the high bond energy (4.5 eV) of the Si–N bonds imparts excellent radiation resistance to Si3N4 ceramics. Even after exposure to a gamma-ray irradiation dose of 107 Gy, the material exhibits a volume expansion rate below 0.2%, ensuring long-term structural integrity.

To further increase the EMI SE of Si3N4 ceramics, researchers have employed various structural and compositional optimization strategies, such as gradient pore structure, grain boundary engineering, and surface functionalization techniques [99,100]. For example, sandwich-structured composites feature a high-density outer layer that provides mechanical strength and environmental durability, a low-density middle layer designed to minimize EM wave transmission via pore-induced scattering, and an inner Si3N4–BN layer that improves impedance matching. This architectural design achieves an EMI SE of 72.0 dB within the Ka band while maintaining a flexural strength of 160 MPa [101]. Likewise, a Si3N4/Ag nanocomposite coating fabricated via plasma spraying further improves the surface conductivity and high-temperature oxidation resistance [102]. When a conductive secondary phase with a high aspect ratio is incorporated into the matrix grains, the composite can achieve EMI SE values comparable to those of CNTs or graphene.

In contrast, pyrolytic carbon synthesized via CVI exhibits a denser microstructure and higher graphitization level. For example, Li *et al*. [98] infiltrated PyC into pressureless-sintered porous Si3N4 using CVI and subsequently deposited a dense Si3N4 coating via chemical vapor deposition (CVD), increasing the electrical conductivity to 2.6×104 S/m. The dense CVD-Si3N4 coating also imparts excellent oxidation resistance, enabling the PyC/Si3N4 composite to maintain stable SET after oxidation in air at 1100 °C for 10 h. Coincidentally, Hao *et al*. [103] introduced pyrolytic carbon (PyC) into porous Si3N4 via the precursor infiltration and pyrolysis (PIP) method, achieving an electrical conductivity of 4.2 S/m. The SET of the unmodified porous Si3N4 is only 2 dB. However, when the PyC content reaches 4% and the thickness is 2.0 mm, the SET increases to 13.5 dB. Moreover, the CVI technique enables *in-situ* synthesis of nano-SiC particles within porous Si3N4 [104]. The resulting Si3N4–SiC porous composite exhibits not only superior EMI shielding performance but also enhanced mechanical properties. Similarly, Shi *et al*. [105] constructed a nickel-based network within porous alumina via wet impregnation to create a double-negative material. The negative dielectric behavior arises from plasma oscillations of delocalized electrons within the nickel network, while the negative permeability originates from the demagnetization response induced by current loops in the nickel network. Although natural porous materials typically exhibit reduced mechanical properties due to decreased density and lightweight porous materials commonly suffer from low strength and poor fatigue resistance, the strength degradation caused by low density can be effectively mitigated through structural optimization design of mechanical metamaterials. For example, Tian *et al*. [106] fabricated BN/graphene/BN ceramics featuring a concave concentric structure, negative Poisson’s ratio, high specific strength, and superelasticity by employing a concentric low-temperature casting technique combined with directional ice crystal growth controlled by a vertical temperature gradient (Fig. 5). At an elevated temperature of 700 °C, the material exhibits a shielding efficiency (SSE/*t*) of up to 9416 (dB·cm2)/g, which is two orders of magnitude greater than that of conventional ceramic matrix composites.

Moreover, the inherent brittleness and limited color variety of Si3N4 ceramics have long constrained their broader application. Notably, Xu *et al*. [107] successfully produced colored Si3N4 ceramics by incorporating α-Si3N4 powder, sintering aids, and rare earth oxides (Eu2O3, Dy2O3). Eu2O3 imparts a yellowish-green hue to the ceramics, while Dy2O3 modulates the lightness of this color. The inclusion of rare earth oxides significantly increased the fracture toughness of Si3N4 ceramics. This improvement is attributed primarily to the increased β-Si3N4 phase content and enhanced aspect ratio, which contribute to greater fracture energy absorption. As a result, the introduction of rare earth elements does not significantly affect EMI shielding performance, thereby offering new possibilities for expanding the application of Si3N4 ceramics in emerging technological fields. Overall, nitride ceramics (such as Si3N4) demonstrate superior dimensional stability, lower dielectric loss, enhanced structural design flexibility (including gradient pore structures and composite interface regulation), and more mature fabrication and integration techniques while maintaining excellent high-temperature performance.

### 3.5 Ferrite ceramics

Ferrite ceramics, a class of magnetic materials composed primarily of iron oxide combined with other metal oxides, such as barium, strontium, and nickel, play an essential and irreplaceable role in the field of EMI shielding [108,109]. Their unique EM properties enable effective adaptation to complex EM environments across a wide frequency range, from low frequencies (kHz) to high frequencies (GHz), making them a core component in the EM compatibility design of modern electronic devices [110]. A key advantage of ferrite ceramics lies in their combination of high magnetic permeability, low loss factor, and broadband performance. The high magnetic permeability allows for strong interactions with external EM fields. Through mechanisms such as domain wall displacement and magnetic moment rotation, these materials demonstrate superior magnetic loss properties in the low-frequency range [111]. The low loss factor ensures that the material can absorb EM waves in high-frequency applications without experiencing performance degradation due to excessive heat generation, thereby significantly enhancing the long-term stability of the shielding system [112,113].

For example, Zhu *et al*. [1] fabricated Sb2Te3/Bi2Te3 core–shell structures on porous CFs via electrochemical deposition, achieving a 300-fold improvement in the power factor and an EMI SE of 93.0 dB, alongside high moisture permeability. Sadek *et al*. [109] developed a graphene/hexagonal ferrite nanocomposite that reached an EMI SE of 52.0 dB (25% graphene) and up to 99.0 dB under high-power conditions, demonstrating stable performance from the hard magnetic composite. Mohapatra and Dobbidi [112] enhanced a lithium ferrite/carbon black composite via erbium (Er) doping, improving the saturation magnetization to 66 emu/g and increasing the EMI SE to 33.7 dB through absorption-dominated mechanisms. For high-temperature aerospace applications, Abramovskis *et al*. [110] synthesized lightweight ceramics from cenospheres and CoFe2O4. These materials offer tunable porosity and compressive strength up to 125.0 MPa and integrate EMI shielding with thermal stability and low density. These studies illustrate progress in designing ferrite-based composites that meet demanding EMI shielding requirements across diverse operating conditions. In total, ferrite ceramics offer distinct advantages over oxide, carbide, boride, and nitride ceramics in terms of low-frequency (kHz–MHz) magnetic field shielding and absorption owing to their unique magnetic loss mechanism and broadband response. However, their application in extremely high-temperature environments is limited by relatively poor thermal stability and mechanical strength. Additionally, their high density can impose constraints in lightweight design applications.

For traditional ceramics (Table 1), current research focuses on multi-scale structural design strategies aimed at synergistically enhancing both the mechanical strength and EMI shielding performance. The incorporation of conductive fillers such as CNTs, CFs, and graphene facilitates the formation of conductive networks and enhances the dielectric loss. Concurrently, the addition of magnetic particles (e.g., Fe or Co) allows tuning of the magnetic loss characteristics and broadening of the EAB. Furthermore, by integrating advanced sintering technologies, such as spark plasma sintering (SPS) and hot-press sintering, with interface engineering strategies, including gradient structural design, both the material density and interfacial bonding strength can be substantially improved. Nevertheless, several challenges remain in conventional ceramic-based EMI shielding materials. The traditional CVD process is costly, and coatings are prone to cracking due to thermal expansion mismatch at high temperatures, limiting large-scale applications. Additionally, the absorption efficiency of existing materials in low-frequency bands (e.g., S and C bands) requires improvement, and long-term stability under high-temperature, oxidative, and mechanical load conditions remains a critical issue.

### 4.1 Polymer-derived ceramics

In recent years, polymer-derived ceramics (PDCs) have shown significant promise in the field of EMI shielding. For example, Jia *et al*. [114] reported that a ZrB2/SiOC composite fabricated via SPS demonstrated an EMI SE of 90.8 dB at 39.7 GHz. This exceptional performance is attributed to the formation of a t-ZrO2 interfacial layer, which has a higher electrical conductivity than pure SiOC, thereby enabling a reflection-dominated shielding mechanism. To address the challenges associated with the precision manufacturing of PDCs, Su *et al*. [115] innovatively employed projection micro-stereolithography 3D printing technology and successfully fabricated high-precision SiOC microstructures with a minimum feature size of 52 μm. Inspired by biomimetic principles, Su *et al*. [117] also designed and fabricated bionic honeycomb SiOC microstructures with wall thicknesses less than 50 μm and cell sizes less than 200 μm. This structure demonstrated a transmission rate of less than 1.4% in the 0.2–1.6 THz frequency range, a maximum EMI SE of 64.1 dB, and an absorption rate of 97%–99.8% within the 0.8–1.6 THz range. Wang *et al*. [119] fabricated 3D carbon skeleton/SiCN composites using electroplating combined with polymer-derived methods. The material exhibited EMI SE exceeding 55.0 dB in the X-band and demonstrated excellent resistance to both acidic and alkaline environments. In addition, Jia *et al*. [118] elucidated the phase transformation mechanism of ZrC ceramics derived from polyzirconoxane and found that the C/ZrC core‒shell structure formed after heat treatment at 1700 °C increased the EMI SE of the material to 34.7 dB.

In response to the demands of THz technology applications, Su *et al*. [120] innovatively constructed a SiCw@MXene/SiOC minimal surface superstructure by integrating electrostatic self-assembly with projection micro-stereolithography (PμSL) 3D printing technology (Fig. 6). This material offers three key advantages: (i) The hybrid structure formed by one-dimensional (1D) SiC whiskers and two-dimensional MXene generates abundant heterogeneous interfaces, significantly enhancing EM wave absorption; (ii) the ultrathin curved surface configuration facilitates multiple internal reflections of EM waves, achieving an average EMI SE of 58.6–66.4 dB at thicknesses ranging from 1.3 to 2.7 mm; (iii) the material combines low thermal conductivity (0.23 W/(m·K) at room temperature) with excellent electrothermal conversion performance, offering novel insights for the development of multifunctional THz shielding devices suitable for extreme environments. To further enhance the terahertz shielding performance, Su *et al*. [116] integrated an acrylic-modified epoxy silicone resin precursor with MXene and subsequently subjected the mixture to ultraviolet curing and vacuum pyrolysis, resulting in the formation of a Ti3C2T*x* MXene/SiOC ceramic with a “sandwich” heterostructure. In summary, PDCs are based on the pyrolysis of precursor polymers. Through component regulation and structural design (e.g., biomimetic honeycomb and sandwich heterostructures), the material achieves highly efficient, predominantly absorption-based EMI shielding over a broad frequency range, particularly within the THz band. Additionally, these materials exhibit multiple functional properties, including corrosion resistance and low thermal conductivity. In comparison, MAX phase ceramics (e.g., Ti3SiC2 and Ti3AlC2) exhibit EMI shielding capabilities predominantly via reflection in high-temperature environments (up to 800 °C), attributed to their inherently high electrical conductivity and dielectric loss. The layered structure and tunable composition of these materials offer a foundation for the development and design of advanced materials.

### 4.2 MAX phase ceramics

In 2008, Ti3SiC2 ceramics attracted significant attention in the field of materials science because of their unique combination of metallic and ceramic properties. This ternary layered ceramic material exhibits excellent mechanical, electrical, and thermal properties, which result from the synergistic effects of metallic, covalent, and ionic bonding. Notably, the presence of a polar center and a high-density electronic state near the Fermi level in Ti3SiC2 endows the material with both a high *ε'* (~396) and metal-like conductivity (*σ* ≈ 4.5×106 S/m) [125]. For example, Shi *et al*. [121] fabricated Ti3SiC2 ceramics through hot-pressing sintering, which exhibited remarkable EMI SE (35–54 dB) in the X-band. In the context of high-temperature applications, Li *et al*. [122] systematically investigated the EMI shielding performance of nano-layered Ti3SiC2 ceramics over a temperature range from room temperature to 800 °C. The material achieves an EMI SE of approximately 30.0 dB under ambient conditions and maintains excellent stability at elevated temperatures. Fan *et al*. [126] and Dong *et al*. [127] introduced the Ti3Si(Al)C2 MAX phase into a C/SiC material system through the low-temperature infiltration process of an Al–Si alloy melt at 1300 °C. Specifically, the resulting C/SiC–Ti3Si(Al)C2 composite exhibited a remarkable flexural strength of 556 MPa, a high fracture toughness of 21.6 MPa·m1/2, and an excellent EMI SE of 43.9 dB. The overall performance of this composite significantly surpasses that of conventional C/SiC-Si and C/SiC-Ti3SiC2 systems.

In 2016, a groundbreaking study by Shahzad *et al*. [123], published in *Science*, marked a new era for applications of MXene materials in EMI shielding. Research has demonstrated that a 45 μm thick Ti3C2T*x* film can achieve an EMI SE of 92.0 dB. This study, for the first time, revealed the unique EMI shielding mechanisms of 2D MXenes, which originate from strong reflection caused by highly conductive surfaces, ohmic loss resulting from lattice interactions, and the synergistic effect of multiple reflections and absorptions. These findings laid a crucial theoretical foundation for subsequent optimization strategies in MXene-based materials. Iqbal *et al*. [124] reported a breakthrough involving a novel Ti3CNT*x* MXene material. A 40 μm-thick film, after heat treatment at 350 °C, demonstrated an unprecedented EMI SE of 116.0 dB. Notably, although the electrical conductivity peaked at 250 °C (2375 S/cm) and subsequently declined, the EMI shielding performance continued to improve. In-depth analysis indicated that the enhancement was primarily due to increased porosity and reduced interlayer spacing induced by thermal treatment. Additional mechanisms, including dipole polarization, dielectric properties, and low-dimensional effects, play a significant role in enhancing EMI shielding performance. This finding challenges the conventional assumption that conductivity alone dictates SE and provides new perspectives for the design of advanced EMI shielding materials.

Recent advancements indicate that the performance of MXene-based materials can be further enhanced through innovative structural design [128]. Specifically, a porous MXene/Co–C@MWCNTs material, fabricated via directional freezing, exhibited an electrical conductivity of 849 S/m and EMI SE of 41.7 dB. Additionally, it demonstrated excellent mechanical properties (50.9 kPa compressive stress at 80% strain) [129]. Moreover, an MXene/CNT Janus film achieved an EMI SE of 72.0 dB and an ultra-low infrared emissivity of 0.09 at a thickness of 15 μm and maintaining stable performance across a wide temperature range from −1 to 300 °C [130]. Similarly, a sandwich-structured MrG film (18 μm) exhibited an EMI SE of 89.1 dB, along with outstanding thermal camouflage capabilities [131]. In terms of mechanical properties, an arginine-modified MXene composite film demonstrated a tensile strength of 21.57 MPa and elongation at break of 880% under 8 wt% loading, while maintaining excellent environmental stability [132]. From the perspective of the preparation process, a dense MXene film (2.25 μm) fabricated via centrifugal spraying achieved an EMI SE of 59.0 dB in the terahertz band [133]. Furthermore, Wang *et al*. [134] designed a hollow egg-box structured MXene composite film that increased the absorbance from 0.02 to 0.45. The film achieved an EMI SE of 69.2 dB. Moreover, Zhang *et al*. [135] developed a TiO2–Ti3C2T*x* ball–plate structured material (50 μm) that achieved an EMI SE of 72.0 dB through multiple absorption mechanisms.

For applications in harsh environments, the EMI SE of porous Ti3SiC2 ceramics fabricated via the ice-template method reaches 35.4 dB, with a thermal conductivity of 12.2 W/(m·K). More importantly, the material exhibits excellent thermal stability, maintaining its performance at temperatures up to 700 °C, as well as in both acidic and alkaline environments [136]. Coincidentally, Li *et al*. [122] confirmed that dense Ti3SiC2 ceramics retained an EMI SE of 26.0 dB even at 800 °C. Furthermore, Sun *et al*. [137] developed a 22 μm-thick aramid nanofiber (ANF)/polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) (AP)/MXene-bridging-liquid metal (MBLM) film by forming a liquid metal bicontinuous network bridged by MXene. The AP/MBLM11 film demonstrated stable resistance characteristics over 10,000 bending cycles, as illustrated in Fig. 7(a). Following the bending test, the EMI SE of the film exhibited only a marginal decrease, with the average value maintained at approximately 71.8 dB (Fig. 7(b)). After 1000 folding cycles with a bending radius of 82.7 μm (Fig. 7(c)), the EMI SE of the AP/MBLM11 film slightly decreased to 70.7 dB (Fig. 7(d)). The film retained an EMI SE exceeding 70.0 dB after 1 h of ultrasonic treatment at 300 W (Fig. 7(e)). As shown in Fig. 7(f), the EMI SE of the AP/MBLM11 film remained largely unchanged after immersion in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C for 1 h, and the material preserved excellent flexibility. As depicted in Figs. 7(g) and 7(h), the EMI SE experienced only a minor reduction following heat treatment at 400 °C for 1 h. These results indicate that the AP/MBLM11 film is capable of functioning effectively as a temperature-stable EMI shielding material within an operational temperature range from −196 to 400 °C. Furthermore, the AP/MBLM11 film exhibits outstanding flame-retardant properties. Even after direct exposure to the outer flame of an alcohol lamp for 60 s, its EMI SE remained above 61.0 dB (Fig. 7(i)). In summary, these properties highlight its promising application potential in the field of electronic packaging. These advancements not only expand the performance limits of EMI shielding materials but also enable the coordinated optimization of mechanical, thermal, and EM functionalities through multi-scale structural design and compositional regulation.

### 4.3 Medium-entropy ceramics

Owing to the urgent demand in the aerospace industry for reliable EMI shielding materials capable of functioning in high-temperature and highly oxidative environments, MAX phase ceramics face limitations. To address this challenge, researchers innovatively developed a heterovalent-substituted medium-entropy perovskite ceramic material system. For example, Liu *et al*. [138] achieved a series of breakthroughs in the field of high-temperature EMI shielding materials (Fig. 8). In the early stages of their research, the team successfully developed oxide-based composites, including mullite, aluminosilicate glass, and zinc oxide, through low-temperature sintering technology. These materials not only maintain favorable mechanical properties but also significantly enhance EMI shielding performance, effectively addressing the issue of vulnerability in low-dimensional materials. In prior work, the team designed and synthesized (Sr0.25Ba0.25Ca0.25La0.25)TiO3 ceramics. Through the synergistic effects of entropy stabilization and heterovalent substitution, they created high-concentration, high-temperature-stable titanium/oxygen vacancy defects. This unique defect engineering enables the material to achieve an outstanding EMI SE of 30 dB in the X-band and maintain stable performance even in oxidizing environments reaching 1000 °C.

### 4.4 High-entropy ceramics

High-entropy ceramics (HECs) represent a novel class of inorganic non-metallic solid solution materials. Within their crystal structure, one or more Wyckoff positions are occupied by multiple elements in approximately equal proportions [140,141]. These materials typically contain five or more cations and exhibit a configurational entropy exceeding 1.5*R* (where *R* denotes the gas constant). This material system inherits the fundamental design principle from high-entropy alloys (HEAs) and demonstrates four core effects: the high-entropy effect, the lattice distortion effect, the sluggish diffusion effect, and the “cocktail” synergistic enhancement effect. Collectively, these characteristics significantly expand the compositional design space and performance tunability of ceramics.

The concept of HECs originated from the introduction of HEAs in 2004. Its theoretical foundation lies in enhancing phase stability and performance potential through the maximization of configurational entropy. Owing to the diverse crystal and electronic structures inherent to inorganic non-metallic compounds, HECs enable precise performance engineering via band structure and phonon engineering approaches, thereby overcoming the performance limitations of conventional ceramics [142,143]. A wide range of material systems have been developed, including high-entropy oxides, nitrides, carbides, borides, sulfides, fluorides, and various complex anion systems, such as phosphates, nitrogen oxides, and carbonitrides. These materials can crystallize into multiple typical structures, including rock salt, fluorite, perovskite, and spinel structures. This structural diversity serves as a foundation for performance modulation. In terms of functional performance, HECs not only exhibit the same high strength, high hardness, and low thermal conductivity as high-entropy alloys but also demonstrate unique functional properties, such as an exceptionally high dielectric constant, superior electronic conductivity, pronounced anisotropic thermal expansion, and effective EMI shielding capabilities [144]. Overall, HECs leverage multi-principal element synergy and entropy-stabilization strategies to overcome the performance limitations of traditional ceramics, demonstrating exceptional multiscale performance.

#### 4.4.1 High-entropy carbide ceramics (HECCs)

With the development of advanced systems such as hypersonic vehicles and reusable orbiters, the demand for materials capable of stable operation at temperatures exceeding 2000 °C is increasingly urgent. Currently, only a limited number of refractory alloys, carbon-based composites, and ultra-high-temperature ceramics can partially meet these requirements. Among them, although carbon-based materials exhibit excellent thermal resistance (e.g., C/C composites can withstand temperatures up to 3000 °C), they are prone to oxidation and degradation in oxygen-rich environments. In contrast, the oxidation resistance of ultra-high-temperature ceramics has long been limited to below 3000 °C, restricting their application in thermal protection systems. Compared with traditional carbide ceramics, HECCs exhibit superior oxidation resistance, low thermal conductivity, and favorable high-temperature mechanical properties, indicating great potential for applications in extreme environments.

For example, Schwind *et al*. [145] prepared (Hf,Ta,Zr,Nb)C HECCs via plasma sintering and evaluated their electrothermal properties. The room-temperature resistivity of this material is approximately 80.9 μΩ·cm, demonstrating metallic-like electrical conductivity. Wen *et al*. [146] successfully developed a high-entropy carbide (Hf,Ta,Zr,W)C with exceptional oxidation resistance by designing a multi-component composition and utilizing an independently developed laser oxidation testing platform. The results indicate that (Hf,Ta,Zr,W)C exhibits the lowest oxidation depth across the entire temperature range, with oxidation kinetics following a parabolic law, demonstrating excellent oxidation resistance over a wide temperature range. This performance is attributed to the formation of a high-melting-point tungsten alloy phase on the surface. Tungsten has the highest surface oxygen atom adsorption energy, making it the most resistant to oxidation. Other elements oxidize preferentially, forming a protective layer that further inhibits tungsten oxidation. Dispersed tungsten alloys enhance the viscosity of the oxide layer, reducing high-temperature volatilization and oxygen diffusion. Laser testing confirmed that this material maintains excellent oxidation resistance even at 3600 °C, significantly outperforming other known ultra-high-temperature materials.

Hu *et al*. [147] fabricated BN interface-modified C@BN/(CrZrHfNbTa)C–SiC composites using a slurry impregnation–lamination and precursor impregnation–pyrolysis process. The composite exhibited a density of 2.72 g/cm3 and a porosity of 12.44 vol%, with a flexural strength of 185±13 MPa. The EMI SE of the CFs reinforced high-entropy (CrZrHfNbTa)C ceramic composite material with a thickness of 3 mm in the X band and Ku band reached 88.2 and 90.0 dB, respectively. This composite maintains excellent EMI shielding stability even after multiple oxidation cycles at 400–800 °C, with no significant degradation in performance. Hu *et al*. [148] also designed CF/(CrZrHfNbTa)C–SiC HEC matrix composites with excellent mechanical properties, EMI shielding performance, and ablation resistance. Using a combined slurry impregnation–lamination and precursor impregnation–pyrolysis process, a composite with a density of 2.72 g/cm3, a porosity of 12.44 vol%, and a flexural strength of 185.0±13.0 MPa was successfully fabricated. Owing to the synergistic effect of CF reinforcement and the HEC matrix, the composite exhibited outstanding EMI shielding performance in the X and Ku bands, with EMI SEs reaching 88.2 and 90.0 dB, respectively. After exposure to a heat flux of 5 MW/m2 for 300 s (approximately 2450 °C), the composite exhibited a linear ablation rate and mass ablation rate of only 0.9 µm/s and 1.82 mg/s, respectively, demonstrating excellent ablation resistance. He *et al*. [149] synthesized (Hf0.2Ta0.2Zr0.2Nb0.2W0.2)C via pressureless sintering, incorporating Cr3C2 and C as composite sintering aids to increase densification. Owing to the optimized microstructure (grain size and defect density), this ceramic exhibited excellent electrical conductivity (0.7040 MS/m) and flexural strength (378±11.4 MPa). This ceramic containing a specific proportion of additives achieved an EMI SE of 92.0 dB at a thickness of 1.0 mm, which was attributed primarily to intense interfacial polarization and relaxation effects.

Moreover, 1D high-entropy metal carbide nanomaterials hold significant potential for structural and functional applications because of their unique physicochemical properties. Liu *et al*. [150] utilized waste cotton fabric as both a flexible substrate and a carbon source to synthesize high-entropy (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C nanowires via a precursor hydrolysis and heat treatment process (Fig. 9). Nanowire growth followed a gas–liquid–solid mechanism, driven by the dissolution–precipitation process of metals and carbon-containing molecules in the precursors under the catalytic action of Fe–Ni alloys and the thermal decomposition of cotton fibers. The resulting HEC nanowires, with diameters ranging from 0.05 to 0.1 μm, were randomly distributed without a preferred orientation on the carbonized cotton fiber substrate, forming an interconnected multi-scale conductive network. Owing to the synergistic effects of conductive loss, dipole polarization induced by lattice distortion, and interfacial polarization at heterogeneous interfaces, this material exhibited an outstanding EMI SE of 57.5 dB at a thickness of 1.3 mm.

#### 4.4.2 High-entropy boride ceramics (HEBCs)

HEBCs are solid solution phases formed by the combination of multiple transition metal elements, such as Zr, Ta, and Ti, with boron, typically exhibiting near-equimolar atomic ratios. These materials can adopt various crystal structures, including cubic, hexagonal, or orthorhombic structures, depending on their elemental composition. Their high configurational entropy originates from the random atomic arrangement within the solid solution, which contributes to enhanced properties such as hardness, thermal stability, and electrical conductivity. HEBCs are predominantly composed of metallic bonds, although boron atoms introduce a degree of covalent bonding. This hybrid bonding further improves their thermal and mechanical performance, indicating potential applications in the field of EMI shielding. HEBCs primarily consist of transition metal borides, and boron atoms form a variety of structural units through single bonds, double bonds, planar networks, or 3D frameworks. These complex structural features endow the material with superior physical and chemical properties. For example, Zhang *et al*. [151] synthesized a novel equimolar high-entropy transition metal boride (Cr0.2Mn0.2Fe0.2Co0.2Mo0.2)B, using a technology of high-temperature elemental reaction combined with SPS. This material exhibits a relatively high electrical conductivity of 0.49×106±0.04×106 S/m and an EMI SE of 23.3 dB at a thickness of 3.0 mm at a frequency of 18.0–26.5 GHz.

#### 4.4.3 High-entropy oxide ceramics (HEOCs)

Perovskite-type HEOCs represent one of the earliest developed high-entropy ceramic systems and have since evolved into a diverse range of structural types. Based on differences in crystal structure, these materials can be broadly classified into several major categories, including rock salt, perovskite, fluorite, and spinel structures. Perovskite-type HEOCs typically exhibit an ABO3 crystal structure, wherein the A-site consists of a 12-coordinated cationic sublattice commonly occupied by alkali or rare earth metal elements. The B-site, on the other hand, is a 6-coordinated cationic sublattice located at the center of the octahedra within a cubic close-packed framework and is predominantly occupied by transition metal elements. The B-site cations, which often exhibit multiple valence states, play a crucial role in determining the functional properties of these materials.

Conventional EMI shielding materials often suffer from issues such as thermal degradation, oxidation, and performance deterioration under high-temperature conditions. In contrast, perovskite-type oxide ceramics are promising alternatives because of their non-oxidizing nature and ability to increase electrical conductivity with increasing temperature. To address these challenges, Ma *et al*. [140] synthesized high-entropy perovskite cobalt oxide ceramics (Y0.2La0.2Sm0.2Nd0.2Gd0.2)CoO3 (HE-RECoO3) by using a one-step solid-state reaction method. This material benefits from the high entropy effect, the presence of multivalent Co ions, and a high concentration of structural defects, resulting in a distinctive charge transport behavior. After heat treatment at 1200 °C for 2 h, the material demonstrated an EMI SE exceeding 30 dB across the entire X-band at 500 °C, along with a Seebeck coefficient of 53.56 μV/K. Zhang *et al*. [152] synthesized porous conductive high-entropy (La0.2Y0.2Nd0.2Gd0.2Sr0.2)CrO3 ceramics using a sol–gel method combined with a partial sintering process. The resulting material exhibits high porosity, low resistivity, and low thermal conductivity, making it suitable for use as a novel catalyst carrier, heating element, and thermoelectric material.

With the advancement of nuclear energy technology, there is an urgent need to develop new shielding materials capable of efficiently attenuating both neutrons and gamma rays while offering lightweight and non-toxic characteristics. Rare earth elements are considered ideal alternatives to lead-based shielding materials because of their excellent radiation resistance (e.g., Ce), high thermal neutron capture cross-sections (e.g., Sm, Eu, and Gd), and absence of weak absorption zones. For example, Zhang *et al*. [153] synthesized (La0.2Ce0.2Gd0.2Er0.2Tm0.2)2(WO4)3 powder via a high-temperature solid-state reaction and subsequently fabricated a high-performance radiation shielding composite by incorporating epoxy resin. Neutron radiography experiments revealed that the neutron EMI SE of this material with a thickness of 2.0 cm exceeded 99%. Gamma-ray shielding tests demonstrated that the material exhibited a relatively high lead equivalent (0.434 mmPb) within the weak absorption region of lead (44–80 keV). The linear attenuation coefficient is significantly greater than that of heavy metal oxides such as Ta2O5, Bi2O3, and HfO2 in the low-energy region. As mentioned above, the high-entropy strategy plays a crucial role in the design of high-entropy ceramics (Table 2). The incorporation of large-sized transition metal elements with strong crystal field splitting capabilities significantly enhances the magnetic properties of a material. Furthermore, this strategy enables the material to maintain low magnetic loss even at elevated temperatures. Moreover, the lattice distortion caused by the high-entropy effect effectively reduces the electrical conductivity of the material.

### 5.1 Overview

With the rapid advancement of 5G/6G infrastructure, the Internet of Things (IoT), and aerospace technologies, electronic systems are increasingly subjected to extreme environments characterized by high temperatures and intense EMI. Although conventional metal shielding materials exhibit good electrical conductivity, they suffer from drawbacks such as susceptibility to corrosion and high density. Carbon-based materials offer advantages in terms of low weight and excellent electrical conductivity but tend to oxidize and degrade at elevated temperatures, limiting their thermal stability and long-term reliability. Therefore, the development of a new generation of materials that combine stable EMI shielding performance with excellent thermal stability under high-temperature conditions has become a critical research priority.

In this context, advanced ceramic materials, particularly HECs, have emerged as promising candidates for advancing high-temperature EMI shielding technologies. These materials possess tunable physical and chemical properties, including exceptional thermal stability, high mechanical strength, excellent chemical inertness, and adaptable electrical and dielectric characteristics. As such, they represent a compelling alternative to traditional metallic and polymer-based materials in extreme environments involving high temperatures and severe corrosion. In Fig. 10, this review focuses on the following key research directions. (i) By precisely controlling the microstructure, the EM wave absorption and reflection mechanisms of ceramics under high-temperature conditions can be optimized. (ii) By utilizing advanced additive manufacturing technologies, ceramic components with complex geometries, reduced weights, and enhanced thermal shock resistance can be developed for scalable production. (iii) By leveraging the inherent properties of ceramics, it is possible to achieve the coordinated integration of EMI shielding, thermal management, structural load-bearing capability, and environmental resistance. (iv) By leveraging artificial intelligence technology, this study aims to investigate the accelerated screening of components and the prediction of high-temperature EMI shielding performance in novel refractory ceramics. In summary, this review aims to provide forward-looking design strategies and theoretical foundations for the development of ultra-durable EMI shielding ceramics tailored for extreme operating conditions, such as those encountered in hypersonic vehicles, next-generation propulsion systems, and high-power-density electronic devices.

#### 5.2.1 Microstructure engineering builds high-efficiency EMI shielding ceramics

By precisely designing the microstructures of ceramic materials, their EMI SE can be significantly enhanced.

(i) Low-dimensional nanostructure design

Leveraging the high specific surface area, high electrical conductivity, and abundant defect sites of 1D (e.g., CNTs and SiC nanowires) and 2D (e.g., MXene and graphene) nanomaterials can significantly improve EMI shielding performance. 1D materials form conductive networks, while 2D materials stack to create a “brick-mud” structure that efficiently reflects EM waves. The edges and defect sites of these materials serve as ideal absorption centers. Moreover, the directional assembly of low-dimensional nanomaterials enables the development of anisotropic EMI shielding properties, meeting directional shielding requirements. For example, Hao *et al*. [24] developed a layered porous skeleton composed of Al2O3 and SiO2, followed by *in-situ* growth of defective graphene and Si3N4 nanowires to form an Al2O3/SiO2–graphene–Si3N4 composite. The sintered ceramics achieved a flexural strength of 388.5 MPa and a fracture toughness of 11.3 MPa·m1/2. Additionally, the EMI SE in the X-band reached 31.8 dB, which was attributed primarily to conductance loss, dipole polarization loss, and interface polarization loss.

(ii) Porous and foam structure

The construction of a 3D interconnected porous network can significantly enhance EMI shielding by enabling multiple reflections and scattering of EM waves, thereby increasing their propagation path and consuming EM energy. This approach achieves ultra-lightweight (ultra-low density) materials while maintaining excellent absorption-dominated shielding performance, effectively mitigating secondary reflection pollution. Furthermore, gradient pore structures (from high to low density) enable wideband impedance matching, enhancing shielding performance at low frequencies (below GHz). For example, Liang *et al*. [154] fabricated a lightweight and robust SiC foam with a multi-layer porous structure using dough as a raw material, followed by carbonization and thermal reduction. The SiC foam, with a porosity of 72.8%, demonstrated an EMI SE exceeding 20.0 dB and achieved a specific EMI SE of over 24.8 cm3/g across the temperature range from 25 to 600 °C, which is three times greater than that of dense SiC ceramics. It also demonstrated low thermal conductivity (0.02 W/(m·K)) and high compressive strength (9.8 MPa). Lightweight, high-strength, and efficient EMI shielding materials are urgently needed for aerospace applications such as lunar and Mars rovers. SiC nanowire aerogels, characterized by low density, stable chemical properties, and tunable dielectric performance, are promising candidates for high-performance EM protection. However, developing SiC nanowire aerogels with both robust mechanical properties and high EMI SE remains challenging. Guo *et al*. [155] fabricated SiC@carbon nanowire metamaterials via 3D printing and chemical vapor deposition of graphene-like carbon aerogels. The optimized aerogel exhibited a compressive strength and Young’s modulus of 1.6 and 46.2 MPa, respectively. Additionally, it demonstrated an EMI SE of 38.4 dB.

(iii) Heterogeneous interfaces and multilayer structures

Ceramic matrix composites offer broad application potential but are limited by brittleness and functional limitations. These properties can be enhanced by incorporating conductive/magnetic nanophases (e.g., CNTs, MXenes, and magnetic metal particles) into ceramic matrices (e.g., Al2O3 and Si3N4) to construct numerous heterogeneous interfaces. These interfaces act as polarization centers and charge accumulation regions, triggering intense dielectric relaxation and magnetic losses that convert EM energy into thermal energy. Multilayer structures (e.g., conductive layers/impedance-matching layers/absorption layers) synergistically enhance both reflection and absorption mechanisms. Precise control over the dimension (0D, 1D, 2D), distribution, and orientation of the secondary phase enables customized coupling of loss mechanisms (conductance loss, polarization loss, magnetic loss). For example, Sun *et al*. [85] developed a thin and flexible multilayer carbon/SiC composite film with exceptional EMI shielding performance (Fig. 11). SiC ceramics provide excellent thermal and oxidation stability, protecting the embedded CNTs from degradation. Moreover, CNTs contribute flexibility, high specific strength, and superior EMI shielding properties.

#### 5.2.2 Advanced manufacturing technology for EMI shielding ceramics

Traditional ceramic preparation processes, such as dry pressing and sintering, face significant challenges in achieving precise control over complex microstructures. In contrast, emerging additive manufacturing (AM) and precision forming technologies enable the fabrication of intricate structures and support large-scale production.

(i) Chemical vapor infiltration/deposition (CVI/CVD)

CVI/CVD technology facilitates the *in-situ* growth of conductive coatings, such as pyrolytic carbon, CNTs, and graphene, within porous ceramic preforms (e.g., foams and fiber felts). This method preserves the lightweight nature of the preform while imparting excellent electrical conductivity, thereby integrating structural load-bearing and EMI shielding functionalities. The development of low-temperature CVD techniques enhances compatibility with a broader range of ceramic substrates and enables atomic-level control over coating thickness and crystallinity. For example, Wang *et al*. [156] utilized a low-pressure CVD and CVI technique based on the SiCl3CH3–NH3–BCl3–H2–Ar system to infiltrate SiBCN ceramics into a 3D graphene foam (GF), yielding GF/SiBCN composites that exhibited superior microwave attenuation performance. The total EMI SE of these composites reached approximately 19.0 dB. The composite relies primarily on absorption and shielding mechanisms due to the porous 3D foam structure and the strong attenuation capability of graphene. The EMI shielding performance remains unaffected by the SiBCN content, as the conductive network is already established by the highly conductive graphene. Potukuchi *et al*. [157] incorporated multiple loss media (C, SiC, and SiCnws) into Sc2Si2O7 ceramics and fabricated carbon@SiCnws–Sc2Si2O7 composites through hydrogen reduction and CVI techniques. The composite exhibited a multiple loss dielectric content of 31.2±0.22 wt%, with a total EMI SE of 31.8 dB at a 2.0 mm thickness in the X-band, corresponding to 99.92% attenuation of incident EM waves.

(ii) Additive manufacturing

3D printing technologies, including direct ink writing (DIW), stereolithography (SLA), and selective laser sintering (SLS), enable the direct fabrication of ceramic components with customized complex geometries (e.g., lattice, honeycomb, and fractal structures), achieving lightweight design and structural‒functional integration unattainable by conventional methods [158,159]. For example, printing pre-ceramic polymers (PCPs) via DIW allows for the production of complex-shaped SiOC and SiC ceramics after pyrolysis, with microstructures precisely controlled through ink formulation and printing parameters. Spatial gradient materials can be realized by continuously varying the porosity and composition across different regions of a component, thereby optimizing the broadband EM response. For example, Yang *et al*. [160] reported that 3D-printed polymer-derived SiOC ceramics (PDC-SiOC) modified with SiC nanowires exhibited high *ε*′ (from 16.6 to 28.9) and *ε*" (from 31.7 to 34.2) in the X-band (Fig. 12). SEM analysis revealed that sintering at 1500 °C resulted in numerous pores and cracks in SiOC, while uniformly grown SiC nanowires were observed in the presence of graphite microcrystals. The total EMI SE reached 34.7 dB, with SEA at 29.3 dB and SER at 5.4 dB. Additionally, PDC-SiOC ceramics sintered at 900 °C demonstrated a compressive strength of 12.6 MPa. Recently, Su *et al*. [117] designed a SiOC ceramic biomimetic honeycomb microstructure inspired by the microstructures found in butterfly wings. Using face PμSL 3D printing technology, they fabricated SiOC ceramic microstructures with wall thicknesses less than 50 μm and cell sizes less than 200 μm. Testing revealed a maximum EMI SE of 64.1 dB, with a transmittance below 1.4% across the 0.2–1.6 THz range. Over 97%–99.8% of THz waves were absorbed within the 0.8–1.6 THz range, demonstrating the exceptional terahertz shielding performance of the structure and its potential for future EM wave protection applications.

(iii) Freeze-casting

In freeze-casting, ceramic slurries are solidified under a directional temperature field, where ice crystals grow and separate ceramic particles, forming aligned pore structures. After freeze-drying and sintering, layered ceramic materials with highly directional long pore channels can be obtained. These structures are conducive to multiple reflections and absorptions of EM waves. By adjusting the freezing rate and temperature gradient, the morphology, size, and orientation of the pores can be precisely controlled, enabling the fabrication of anisotropic EMI shielding ceramics with predictable performance [161]. For example, Zhi et al*. [162] developed a novel β-Si3N4 scaffold via directional freezing-casting of α-Si3N4 suspensions with the addition of elongated β-Si3N4 seeds, followed by liquid-phase sintering. The resulting Si3N4 scaffold exhibited compressive strengths ranging from 2.8 to 22.0 MPa, with the porosity decreasing from 94.4% to 88.8% and the density increasing from 175 to 365 mg/cm3. Xue *et al*. [163] fabricated gradient conductive MXene/CNT/polyimide (GCMCP) aerogel frameworks using a multi-feed 3D printing system. MXene/CNT/PAA inks were deposited layer by layer, followed by freeze-drying and imidization to form a hierarchical porous structure. In this framework, the electrical conductivity gradually increases from top to bottom. The top layer serves as an absorption layer (impedance matching), while the bottom layer functions as a reflection layer (impedance mismatch), forming an absorption–reflection–reabsorption interface. The GCMCP aerogel demonstrated excellent EMI SE (68.2 dB) and an ultralow reflection coefficient (*R* ≈ 0.23). In summary, CVI/CVD techniques face challenges related to process complexity, long production cycles, and high costs. Additive manufacturing is constrained by the inherent properties of ceramic materials, such as density and electrical conductivity, which are difficult to balance, and by low printing efficiency. Freeze-casting encounters difficulties in maintaining structural consistency across large-scale products and achieving scalable manufacturing.

#### 5.2.3 Multifunctional integrated EMI shielding ceramic

In the future, EMI shielding ceramics should be developed with multifunctional integration to meet the demands of complex application scenarios. Ceramic materials demonstrate unique advantages in this context.

(i) Thermal management–EMI shielding integration

Achieving simultaneous thermal protection and EMI shielding presents a design challenge, as materials with high electrical conductivity typically exhibit high thermal conductivity, which conflicts with the requirement for good thermal insulation. To address this issue, composite materials combining high-thermal-conductivity ceramics (e.g., SiC, BN, and AlN) as matrices or constituents with highly conductive materials (e.g., graphene) can be employed. These composites effectively dissipate heat generated by electronic devices while providing reliable EMI protection, thereby addressing the integrated thermal and EMI management needs of high-power, high-integration chips. For example, Jia *et al*. [164] reported a multifunctional ceramic composite system for CFs-reinforced polymer composites. The system features a multi-layer structure: yttria-stabilized zirconia fiber-reinforced polymer-derived SiCN ceramics serve as thermal protection and impedance matching layers, while carbon nanotube-stabilized yttria-stabilized zirconia fiber-reinforced SiCN ceramics provide EMI shielding. Compared with that of the CF-reinforced polymer composites, the thermal conductivity of the multilayer ceramic composites is approximately 22.5% lower. This hybrid composite can operate at temperatures up to 300 °C, with an average total EMI SE of 21.4 dB/mm for the four-layer system. Additionally, Yang *et al*. [136] developed a structurally ordered, lightweight porous Ti3SiC2 ceramic using an ice-templated assembly strategy combined with *in situ* reaction methods. At room temperature, the porous Ti3SiC2 ceramic achieves an EMI SE of 35.4 dB and a thermal conductivity of 12.17 W/(m·K), with performance tunable via porosity. Furthermore, owing to its excellent high-temperature oxidation resistance and corrosion resistance, the ceramics can operate stably in environments ranging from room temperature to 700 °C or in corrosive environments rich in acids, alkalis, and salts.

(ii) Mechanical load-bearing–EMI shielding integration

The development of structure‒function integrated ceramic matrix composites, such as CFs/SiC and SiCf/SiC, enables simultaneous structural support and EMI shielding. These materials possess high specific strength, high specific modulus, and ultra-high temperature resistance. The CFs and SiC matrix form an effective conductive/dielectric loss network, making them suitable for aerospace vehicle skins and engine hot-end components, and they can fulfill structural support, thermal protection, and EM stealth/shielding functions. For example, Li *et al*. [88] synthesized a CFs-reinforced metal carbon-containing silicon nitride composite (CFs/PyC/SiC–SiCN(Fe)) by integrating multilayer interfaces and magnetic particles. The multiphase heterogeneous interfaces generate numerous lattice defects and enhance the polarization effects, thereby improving the EMI shielding performance. The EMI SE reaches 62.1 dB in the 8.0–12.0 GHz frequency band and 38.6 dB in the 12.0–18.0 GHz band. Moreover, the CFs and double-layer interface structure provide strong mechanical support, achieving a strength of 444.8±20.9 MPa. Zhu *et al*. [80] fabricated SiCNWs–Fe3Si/SiC(rGO) PDCs with multiple *in-situ* generated dielectric/magnetic nanocomposites through chemical grafting, high-energy ball milling, and re-pyrolysis. The SiCnw network formed under Fe catalysis serves as a carrier for abundant polarization sites and charge carrier pathways, mutually penetrating the SiO*x*C*y* matrix to regulate dielectric properties and toughening the framework via bridging and stress deflection mechanisms. The resulting composite exhibits an EMI SE of up to 68.7 dB, with 90.9% absorption efficiency. It also features a dense framework, low density (1.82 g/cm3), high hardness (6.1 GPa), and compressive strength of 112.0 MPa.

(iii) Extreme environment resistance-integrated EMI shielding

Compared with polymers and metallic materials, ceramics exhibit inherent properties such as high-temperature resistance, corrosion resistance, and radiation resistance, making them ideal candidates for long-term, stable EMI shielding in extreme environments, including nuclear reactors, deep space exploration missions, and hypersonic vehicles. For example, Hu *et al*. [147] developed a mechanically robust (bending strength: 246.0±15.0 MPa) CFs@BN/(CrZrHfNbTa) BN interface phase C–SiC high-entropy ceramic matrix composite with excellent EMI shielding performance and high-temperature resistance (Fig. 13). The average SET of the composite reaches 32.5 dB with a thickness of 3.0 mm in the Ku band. After oxidation at 800 °C, the composite exhibits excellent thermal stability, maintaining a nearly constant SET value over a duration of up to 600 s. Furthermore, Bai *et al*. [165] synthesized four MAX phases (Ti3SiC2, Ti3AlC2, V0.5Cr1.5AlC, and Nb4AlC3) via solid-liquid reaction. Their electrical conductivities are 14.7×103, 15.5×103, 5.1×103, and 8.0×103 S/cm, respectively, with corresponding EMI SE values of 53.9, 69.2, 19.4, and 29.0 dB in the 18.0–26.5 GHz range. Notably, these MAX phases exhibit high corrosion resistance under acidic conditions. Even after exposure to acidic environments with slight conductivity reduction, the corroded MAX phases maintain excellent EMI shielding performance compared with their original state.

(iv) Ceramifiable EMI-shielding rubber

Ceramifiable rubber, also referred to as fire-resistant rubber or porcelain rubber, is a multifunctional high-performance material. At ambient temperatures, it exhibits properties comparable to those of conventional rubber, such as excellent elasticity, sealing performance, and processability. It undergoes a sintering process to form a hard, dense, and mechanically robust ceramic-like protective layer. This class of materials typically consists of four primary components: a polymer matrix, porcelain-forming fillers, fluxing agents, and reinforcing additives. Typically, silicone rubber is the preferred choice because of its inherent thermal stability. Porcelain-forming fillers are generally compound mineral systems, such as glass powder, mica, diatomite, and montmorillonite, which melt and react at elevated temperatures to form a ceramic structure. Fluxing agents are incorporated to lower the ceramicization temperature, enabling the formation of a ceramic layer within the decomposition temperature range of the polymer matrix. Reinforcing additives, such as CFs and zinc borate, are used to enhance the mechanical strength of the ceramic residue and prevent cracking. Ceramifiable rubber has a range of exceptional properties, including superior fire resistance (withstanding temperatures ranging from 800 to 1500 °C or higher), electrical insulation, smoke suppression, fire insulation, and environmental compatibility.

By integrating ceramifiable rubber with EMI shielding capabilities, a new class of “intelligent” multifunctional materials can be developed. These materials can effectively shield EMI under normal operating conditions and maintain structural integrity through ceramic transformation under extreme conditions such as fire, thereby offering critical emergency protection for essential equipment. The prevailing technical approach involves the development of conductive filler-reinforced ceramifiable rubber composites, and conductive components are introduced into the base formulation. Potential conductive fillers include metal-based materials (e.g., silver powder, copper powder, nickel powder, and silver-coated copper powder), carbon-based materials (e.g., CNTs, graphene, carbon black, and CFs), and emerging two-dimensional materials such as MXenes. These fillers establish a conductive network within the rubber matrix, enabling EMI shielding. However, several technical challenges remain: (i) Compatibility between fillers and the matrix: High loading levels of conductive fillers may compromise the processability and mechanical properties (e.g., elasticity) of the rubber, which can be mitigated through surface modification techniques or by selecting a suitable rubber matrix. (ii) Impact of fillers on the ceramicization process: Metallic fillers may oxidize, melt, or chemically interact with the porcelain-forming system at high temperatures, potentially weakening the ceramic layer. Carbon-based materials are susceptible to oxidation in oxygen-rich environments, which may lead to failure of the shielding function. Ceramifiable rubber with EMI shielding functionality has demonstrated significant potential across a wide range of applications, including the sealing and electromagnetic shielding of cable harnesses in aircraft and spacecraft; electronic equipment enclosures; battery pack sealing and EM management in electric vehicles; fireproof sealing strips for server cabinets and communication base stations; and the protection of electronic systems in nuclear power plants, military vehicles, naval vessels, and command centers.

#### 5.2.4 Ai-driven EMI shielding ceramics

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) profoundly transform the paradigm of materials research and development. These technologies provide powerful tools to address the complexity of EMI shielding ceramic design [166]. Compared with traditional materials, the research on ultra-high-temperature and high-entropy ceramics faces more multi-dimensional challenges: their composition space encompasses complex combinations of multiple cations, anions, and defects; the scale spans from macroscopic to mesoscopic; the service environment often involves extreme conditions of multi-field coupling, such as heat, force, and chemical coupling. The microstructural characteristics and performance of such materials rely heavily on extensive experimental data and continuously drive innovations in characterization techniques. Therefore, traditional research methods that depend solely on scientific intuition and trial-and-error approaches are often highly inefficient and time-consuming when high-performance ultra-high-temperature and high-entropy ceramics are developed.

(i) Performance prediction and reverse design

First-principles calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) can approximate electron exchange-correlation effects based on the atomic structure. These calculations not only yield microscopic structural information, such as electron density of states and lattice distortion but also enable the prediction of mechanical and thermophysical properties, thereby facilitating the screening of candidate systems with target performance across a broad compositional space. This significantly improves research and development efficiency [167−170]. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations can directly track particle movement trajectories, revealing the dynamic behavior of materials during phase transitions, deformation, and diffusion. These simulations are of great value for understanding mechanical properties, oxidation and ablation mechanisms at ultra-high temperatures, effectively compensating for the high cost and long cycle of experimental testing in extreme environments [171,172].

By leveraging ML models such as neural networks, it is possible to learn the complex nonlinear relationships among “composition–microstructure–process–performance”. By inputting the target performance, AI can reverse-recommend the optimal material formula and process parameters, significantly shortening the development cycle. For example, Dikshit *et al*. [173] utilized machine learning to predict the EMI shielding effect of (Ba1−*x*Ca*x*)(Ti0.9Sn0.1)O3 (BCTS) ceramics at different Ca doping concentrations (*x* = 0.06–0.09). The results confirm that the EMI SE reached 58.0 dB at *x* = 0.08, which was significantly higher than the pre-optimization value. Shi *et al*. [174] employed a machine learning approach to establish a rapid prediction model for EMI SE and analyze critical design factors and rules, thereby optimizing new material development and reducing the number of required experiments (Fig. 14). Wang *et al*. [175] calculated the electronic density of states of the five-element boride (Hf0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)B2 via DFT. They reported that the density of states at the Fermi level was non-zero, indicating metallic characteristics. Additionally, the Fermi level was located in the pseudo-energy gap valley region, suggesting relatively high structural stability. Xiong *et al*. [176] obtained the equilibrium lattice constant of (TiZrNbTa)C through first-principles calculations (*a* = 0.45 nm) and reported that the calculated density (*ρ* = 8.4 g/cm3) was highly consistent with the experimental values. The influence of pressure on structural parameters and stability was systematically studied, indicating that pressure can reduce the lattice constant and increase the density without causing severe lattice distortion or instability. Ye *et al*. [177] used Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) software based on density functional theory to calculate the elastic constants C11, C12, and C44 of (Zr0.25Nb0.25Ti0.25V0.25)C as 547.9, 114.9, and 170.1 GPa, respectively, verifying its mechanical stability and providing theoretical basis for system screening.

(ii) Multi-scale simulation and data generation

Ultra-high-temperature and high-entropy ceramics exhibit complex characteristics, such as multi-component, multi-phase, multi-scale, and multi-field coupling, generating a vast amount of simulation and experimental data that exceed the processing capacity of traditional statistical methods. By integrating first-principle calculations with machine learning, a high-precision mapping relationship of “composition–structure–performance” can be established, significantly narrowing the range of component screening, forming data-driven design criteria, and enabling efficient directional design of materials [178]. Further integration with multi-scale modeling methods, such as phase field and finite element methods, allows for the generation of large-scale training data. Based on this, artificial intelligence can be used to screen new material systems efficiently (e.g., high-entropy ceramics and defect-engineered MXenes) and optimize their microstructures (e.g., pores and interfaces), thereby significantly enhancing the EM response performance.

For example, Imran et al*. [179] proposed an intelligent data preprocessing mechanism based on ensemble learning to address issues of missing and inconsistent material data. A dedicated data platform for ceramic materials was constructed, and the effectiveness of interpolation techniques was verified through a regression model. Sarker *et al*. [180] calculated 56 five-element carbide systems composed of 8 refractory metal elements (Hf, Nb, Mo, Ta, Ti, V, W, and Zr) using entropy descriptors (EFA). Nine combinations were experimentally verified and found to align with the predictions. Furthermore, the EFA values of 70 new components containing Cr were predicted using machine learning, demonstrating the effectiveness of this method in the initial screening of large-scale components [181]. However, EFA is currently only applicable to five-element refractory carbides and has limited universality. Moreover, there are individual cases where single-phase solid solutions still form even when the EFA value is less than 50 (eV/atom)−1, indicating that its prediction accuracy still requires improvement. Dai *et al*. [182] used a deep learning potential function method to predict the thermodynamic properties of (Zr0.2Hf0.2Ti0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C within the temperature range of 0–2400 °C, including the lattice constant (4.67×10−10 m), coefficient of thermal expansion (10.58×10−6 K−1), and phonon thermal conductivity (0.95 W/(m·K)). The results were consistent with the experimental measurements, indicating that this method has strong reliability and promising application prospects in predicting properties under extreme temperatures. In total, through multi-scale calculation methods such as first-principles calculations, molecular dynamics, and machine learning, the mechanical and thermophysical properties of ultra-high temperature and high-entropy ceramics can be effectively predicted, significantly improving the research and development efficiency of electromagnetic shielding ceramic materials.

## 6 Conclusions

Ceramic-based EMI shielding materials, as the core components of next-generation high-performance shielding systems, have increasingly demonstrated significant potential to replace traditional metallic and polymeric materials. This review systematically examines the mechanisms, performance optimization strategies, and future development directions of ceramic-based EMI shielding materials. Studies indicate that conventional ceramics (e.g., silicon carbide and ferrites) and emerging ceramics (e.g., MXenes and high-entropy ceramics) dissipate electromagnetic energy through dielectric and magnetic loss mechanisms. Their performance is heavily influenced by the synergistic optimization of compositional design, microstructural control, and fabrication processes. Although ceramic materials offer distinct advantages in terms of high-temperature stability, chemical inertness, and structural designability, their limited electrical conductivity and challenges in achieving precise microstructural control remain key obstacles. By designing low-dimensional nanostructures and employing porous or heterogeneous interface engineering combined with advanced fabrication techniques such as additive manufacturing and chemical vapor deposition, the electrical conductivity and electromagnetic performance of ceramics can be significantly enhanced. These strategies also enable lightweight structural design and multifunctional integration.

Future research should focus on the following areas. (i) wideband compatibility design: developing frequency-adaptive ceramic composites to meet the communication demands of 5G/6G and terahertz technologies; (ii) multifunctional integration: integrating electromagnetic shielding with thermal management, mechanical load-bearing, and environmental protection to address the requirements of extreme environments such as aerospace and high-power electronics; (iii) smart responsive materials: investigating ceramics capable of responding to temperature, electric, or magnetic fields for dynamic shielding regulation; (iv) AI-driven innovation: leveraging machine learning and high-throughput computational methods to accelerate material discovery, performance prediction, and process optimization, thereby overcoming the limitations of traditional trial-and-error approaches. In conclusion, ceramic-based EMI shielding materials are transitioning from laboratory research to industrial application.
