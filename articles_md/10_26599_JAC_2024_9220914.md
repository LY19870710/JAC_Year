## 1 Introduction

Ferroelectric–phosphor composite materials have wide applications in optoelectronics and smart sensors [1−3] due to their excellent piezoelectric and luminescent properties. The approach frequently adopted to prepare ferroelectric–phosphor composite materials is to deposit a phosphor layer on the ferroelectric materials [4]. Zhang *et al*. [5] prepared a ZnS:Mn/PMN–PT composite material by depositing a ZnS:Mn phosphor thin film on a PMN–PT ferroelectric crystal via pulsed laser deposition. They observed dual-mode light and ultrasound emissions induced by the piezo–phototronic effect, which cannot be observed in single-phase phosphors or ferroelectric materials. However, the emission intensity is relatively weak due to the weak coupling between the ferroelectric and phosphor materials.

The 0–3 type composite technology is an alternative method for preparing ferroelectric–phosphor composite ceramics. Wang *et al*. [6] reported that 0–3 type BaTiO3–CaTiO3:Pr3+ (BT–CT:Pr3+) composite materials exhibited strong electric field-induced strain, mechanoluminescence, and electroluminescence. However, a pure CT phase was not found, and only two Ba-rich and Ca-rich phases were observed, resulting in a much lower luminescent performance than that of CaTiO3:Pr3+ (CT:Pr3+) [7]. The controllable inter-diffusion between the ferroelectric matrix and the filler phase can significantly enhance the piezoelectric performance and coercive field of the composite ceramics. Zhang *et al*. [8] used a two-step sintering method to defer the thermal depolarization of 0–3 type Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3-based composite ceramics. Gong *et al*. [9] prepared 0–3 type Na0.5Bi0.5TiO3–CaTiO3:Pr3+ ferroelectric–phosphor composite ceramics using a two-step sintering method to reduce the inter-diffusion between Na0.5Bi0.5TiO3 and CT:Pr3+. However, the inevitable chemical reaction between ferroelectric Na0.5Bi0.5TiO3 and the phosphor CT:Pr3+ at temperatures greater than 1100 °C produces a weakly polar phase, resulting in a great reduction in the ferroelectric performance (~0.5 μC/cm2). Our previous work used cold sintering assistance (CSA) to prepare BT–CT:Pr3+ composite ceramics using the NaOH/KOH solution as a sintering aid [10]. This decrease is expected to reduce the sintering temperature and inhibit the mutual diffusion between BT and CT:Pr3+ caused by high temperatures. However, annealing at 900 °C to densify the composite ceramics caused CT:Pr3+ to diffuse into the BT lattice, forming Pr3+-doped Ba1–*x*Ca*x*TiO3, and CT:Pr3+ was not observed in the as-prepared ceramics with a nominal composition of 0.9BT–0.1CT:Pr3+. As a result, only weak luminescence was observed. Fortunately, the cold sintering process (CSP) may be able to solve this issue by effectively inhibiting atomic diffusion.

CSP, which is inspired by the phenomenon of biological mineralization [11], can achieve densification of ceramics at ultralow sintering temperatures, generally below 300 °C. It uses a specific transient liquid and external pressure that allows the evaporation of the transient phase to aid in rearrangement, dissolution–precipitation creep, and grain growth at the interfaces between particles [12]. As an ultralow temperature sintering method, CSP has been used to prepare more than 80 materials since its discovery, such as BT [13−17], NaNO2 [11], CaF2 [18], (Ba,Sr)TiO3 [19], Na2MoO4 [20], (K,Na)NbO3 [21−25], BaFe12O19 [26], and hexagonal faujasite (EMT) glass [27]. In addition, several electronic composite ceramics, such as BT–polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) [16], ZnO–C60 [28], Al2SiO5–NaCl [29], and ZnO–BT [30], have been prepared using CSP. Composite ceramics with water-soluble phases such as NaCl or polymers can be conveniently prepared. However, there are no reports on the successful preparation of all-inorganic ferroelectric–phosphor composite ceramics using CSP, especially composite ceramics with filler and matrix phases that share similar crystal structures, such as perovskite structures. It is feasible to achieve the densification of composite ceramics using an appropriate transient liquid at low temperatures without annealing.

Herein, we employ CSP to prepare dense 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ ceramics with both strong ferroelectric and luminescent properties at an ultralow sintering temperature to demonstrate the feasibility of preparing inorganic‒inorganic composite ceramics with similar structures using Ba(OH)2∙8H2O as a transient flux. Ba(OH)2∙8H2O does not introduce metal cation impurities into composite ceramics solution like NaOH/KOH. The effect of CSP parameters on the microstructure of the prepared ceramics was investigated, and the CSP mechanism was discussed. The present work illustrates that the as-prepared composite ceramics have stronger ferroelectric properties than their counterparts fabricated by the CSA and SSS methods and exhibit strong luminescent performance, which paves the way for the fabrication of ferroelectric–phosphor composite ceramics.

### 2.1 Powder preparation

BT nanoparticles (99.9%, average grain size: ~72 nm) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd., China. Barium hydroxide octahydrate (Ba(OH)2∙8H2O, > 98%) was obtained from Alfa Aesar Chemical Co., Ltd., China. CT:Pr3+ powders with an average grain size of ~1.22 μm were synthesized using a high-temperature solid-state reaction (SSS) method, and the Pr3+ concentration was 0.3 mol% since previous research indicated that CT doped with 0.3 mol% Pr3+ exhibited excellent luminescence properties [9]. The raw materials CaCO3 (99.5%), TiO2 (99%), and Pr2O3 (99.9%) (Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd., China) were weighed to synthesize the CT:Pr3+ powders. Subsequently, they were ball milled using ethanol as the milling medium for 12 h. Afterwards, the dried materials were crushed and sieved through 80 mesh sieves. The prepared powders were calcined at 900 °C for 4 h in air to form the CT:Pr3+ compound, which was then sintered at 1400 °C for 4 h.

### 2.2 Cold sintering protocols

The experimental procedure for CSP is illustrated in Fig. S1(a) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). BT and CT:Pr3+ were weighed according to the chemical formula of 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ and then ball-milled using ethanol as the grinding medium for 12 h to obtain 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ powders. Subsequently, 0.4 g of 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ powder was mixed with 0.045–0.17 g of Ba(OH)2·8H2O solid powder. When employing NaOH/KOH fluxes as a sintering aid, Na+ and K+ cations are introduced into the 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ composite ceramics, which greatly affects the ferroelectric and luminescent properties of the composite ceramics. On the other hand, the fabrication of novel composite ceramics is still limited by the relatively harsh conditions, where the raw powders are exposed to strong alkaline NaOH/KOH fluxes. To address these two issues, a milder sintering flux Ba(OH)2∙8H2O was used for the CSP, which would enable the densification of 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+. The obtained mixtures were loaded into a steel die with 10 mm in diameter and uniaxially pressed into pellets under 400 MPa. Given that the melting point of Ba(OH)2·8H2O is 78 °C [16], a preheating step was carried out at 80 °C for 30 min to ensure the homogeneous distribution of Ba(OH)2 within the 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ powders. Subsequently, both a heater jacket and a press equipped with hot plates were applied to increase the temperature to the target temperature for the CSP reaction, and a uniaxial pressure of 400 MPa was applied. The pressure and temperature profiles are shown in Fig. S1(b) in the ESM. To compare the ferroelectric and luminescent properties of the ceramics prepared using different methods, 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ ceramics were also prepared using CSA and conventional SSS. Further details regarding the preparation can be found in the supporting materials.

### 2.3 Materials characterizations

The bulk densities (*ρ*b) of the ceramics were determined using Archimedes’ principle. The theoretical densities (*ρ*th) of the composite ceramics were calculated based on the percentages of pure BT (6.03 g·cm−3) and CT (5.83 g·cm−3). The relative densities were then calculated by dividing the bulk densities by the theoretical densities (*ρ*b/*ρ*th). The phase structures of the ceramics were analyzed by a powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyzer with Cu-Kα radiation (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany). The fracture surface microstructures of the composite ceramics were measured by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; SUS-700 Hitachi, Japan) operating at an accelerating voltage of 6 kV. The grain sizes were calculated via the line intercept method to determine the distribution of the grain sizes via the nano measurer. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of the samples were obtained with an ion polishing system (Gatan 695, Gatan, USA). High-resolution TEM and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy were performed using a FEI Talos F200X instrument, USA. The local piezoresponse of the ceramics was observed by a commercial atomic force microscope (AFM; Cypher-HV, Asylum Research, USA) via piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM). The current–electric (*I*–*E*) field and polarization–electric (*P*–*E*) field hysteresis loops were recorded via a ferroelectric analyzer (Premier II, Radiant Technologies, USA). The photoluminescence (PL) excitation and emission spectra were recorded using a fluorescence spectrometer (FS5, Edinburgh Instrument, UK).

## 3 Results and discussion

The 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ composite ceramics were sintered at 225 °C for 1 h under 400 MPa for different contents of Ba(OH)2·8H2O (10–30 wt%). As shown in Figs. 1(a)–1(c), the SEM images and corresponding grain size distributions of the composite ceramics were examined to study the effect of the liquid content on CSP. The results show that the composite ceramics prepared with 20 wt% Ba(OH)2·8H2O have a homogeneous microstructure and high densification. Figure 1(a) shows that an excessively low Ba(OH)2·8H2O content (10 wt%) leads to a loose structure. The ceramics with 20 wt% Ba(OH)2·8H2O added show a homogeneous grain size, and no obvious pores are identified in Fig. 1(b). Moreover, a 30 wt% Ba(OH)2·8H2O content results in the appearance of many small grains, as shown in Fig. 1(c). The CSP ceramics with 10, 20, and 30 wt% Ba(OH)2·8H2O have average grain sizes of 84, 101, and 75 nm, respectively (as shown in the inset figure). The largest average grain size of 101 nm is obtained at a Ba(OH)2·8H2O content of 20 wt%. The calculated Christiansen uniformity coefficients [31] of the grain size for the three components with 10, 20, and 30 wt% Ba(OH)2∙8H2O are 87%, 88%, and 82%, respectively. The variation in the relative density with the Ba(OH)2·8H2O content is shown in Fig. 1(d). A medium Ba(OH)2·8H2O content (20 wt%) results in a high relative density of 89%. As mentioned above, the CSP is significantly affected by the content of Ba(OH)2·8H2O. There is an insufficient quantity of Ba2+ ions to promote grain growth and densification through recrystallization when the Ba(OH)2·8H2O content is 10 wt%. Although excess Ba(OH)2·8H2O molecules (30 wt%) promote the dissolution of BT, the excess OH− in the solution may bind to the positively charged surface sites of BT grains [32]. These OH− ions cover a significant part of the BT surface and tend to stay on the surface at elevated temperatures. Thus, this strong adsorption makes recrystallization on the surface of the BT grain more difficult and results in recrystallization induced by homogeneous nucleation in the supersaturated liquid when the temperature is elevated, which results in many small crystals in the ceramics (Fig. 1(c)) and low density. A similar structure is observed in ZnO ceramics [33].

Figures S2(a)–S2(c) in the ESM show the SEM images of the 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ composite ceramics with different sintering time and CSP preparation parameters: 20 wt% Ba(OH)2·8H2O, sintering temperature of 225 °C, and pressure of 400 MPa. As expected, the average grain size of the composite ceramics improves, and the small grains decrease gradually with increasing sintering time. The average grain size of the composite ceramics sintered for 0.5 h is only 83 nm. The average grain size increases rapidly from 83 to 101 nm with increasing sintering time from 0.5 to 1 h, while a slight increase from 101 to 106 nm is observed with a further increase in sintering time to 3 h. During the CSP, the optimal sintering time of 1 h is sufficient to allow Ba2+ and Ti4+ ions to be mass transported and recrystallized for grain growth. The variation in the relative density with sintering time is consistent with the change in the average size, as shown in Fig. S2(d) in the ESM. The composite ceramics sintered for 1 h reached a relative density of 89%. Upon further increasing the sintering time to 3 h, the densification slightly increases to 91%.

The sintering temperature is another important parameter for CSP. Figure 2 shows the SEM image and grain size distribution of the 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ ceramics sintered at various temperatures for 1 h with 20 wt% Ba(OH)2·8H2O. The CSP ceramics sintered at 125, 225, and 325 °C have average grain sizes of 91, 101, and 130 nm, respectively. The ceramics sintered at 125 °C are not dense. As the sintering temperature increases from 125 to 225 °C, the ceramics become much denser. The average grain size increases from 101 to 130 nm as the sintering temperature further increases to 325 °C. Obviously, many small pores can be found in the composite ceramics, resulting in a decrease in the density. Furthermore, the ceramic grains exhibit a polygonal structure. The relative densities of the ceramics are shown in Fig. 2(d). For the investigated sintering temperature, sintering at 225 °C could produce a density of 89%. During CSP, elevated temperatures can enhance dissolution and increase the fraction of ions in the liquid phase, which is beneficial for the recrystallization of ions on grain surfaces. Moreover, the water layer can also be removed at elevated temperatures to improve grain growth and densification. Hence, as the sintering temperature increases from 125 to 225 °C, a significant improvement in densification can be found. At 225 °C, sufficient ions can be transported to and recrystallized on the surfaces of BT and CT:Pr3+ grains for densification. A further increase in temperatures to 325 °C leads to rapid grain growth and increased porosity, which is adverse to the densification of the ceramics. As a result, an optimal temperature of 225 °C can be applied to obtain dense microstructures with no obvious pores.

The above results demonstrate that the samples prepared by CSP with the optimized preparation parameters (Ba(OH)2·8H2O: 20 wt%, sintering temperature: 225 °C; sintering time: 1 h) have excellent density and recrystallization. Their microstructure and properties were further investigated. The XRD patterns of the 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ raw powders and the pellets prepared by CSP are shown in Fig. 3. The ceramics exhibit two main phases, BT and CT, and a new phase appears after CSP. This impurity phase is indexed to BaCO3 according to the inorganic crystal structure database (ICSD), as marked in Fig. 3(a). The formation of BaCO3 is the consequence of the reaction between Ba2+ ions in the basic solution and CO2 in air. This impurity phase is also observed in BT ceramics prepared by CSP using Ba(OH)2·8H2O as a hydrated flux [16]. The (110) peak of BT and the main peak of CT:Pr3+ are magnified in Figs. 3(b) and 3(c), respectively. Compared with that of the raw materials, the (110) peak of BT slightly shifts toward smaller angles after CSP. This result indicates that the crystal lattice of BT increases after CSP. The intensity of the CT:Pr3+ phase after CSP shows little difference from that of the raw powders, which indicates that little CT:Pr3+ has been incorporated into the matrix BT phase. Furthermore, the main peak (121) of CT:Pr3+ slightly shifts toward small angles after CSP, indicating an increase in the lattice. This result may be ascribed to Ba2+ ions being slightly diffused into the CT:Pr3+ lattice to replace Ca2+. To obtain the composition ratio of the sintered ceramics, Rietveld refinement was performed using GSAS II software, as shown in Fig. 3(d). The refinement results show that the molar contents of BT, CT:Pr3+, and BaCO3 are 79%, 15%, and 6%, respectively. By comparing the Rietveld refinement of the sintered ceramics with that of the starting materials, it can be found that the ratio between CT:Pr3+ and BT decreases after CSP, indicating that a portion of the CT:Pr3+ is partially dissolved during CSP.

To confirm the formation of the 0–3 type composite structure in the prepared 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ ceramics, both EDX and TEM were carried out, as shown in Fig. 4. Two phases with obviously different morphologies are observed in Fig. 4(a). According to the EDX mapping results in Figs. 4(b) and 4(c), the grains with much larger sizes are CT:Pr3+, and the smaller grains are BT. Ba2+ ions slightly diffuse into the CT:Pr3+ grains, which is consistent with the XRD results. Furthermore, the Ca2+ ions diffuse into the BT grains (Figs. 4(d)–4(f)), and the Ca2+ ions are not uniformly distributed in the BT matrix. A stronger signal from Ca2+ is detected at the boundaries of the BT grains, as indicated by the dotted lines. The TEM results reveal that some internal defect inclusions exist in the BT grains, as shown in Figs. 4(a) and 4(d). Due to the presence of hydroxyl groups, internal defect inclusions such as intragranular pores are often observed in sintered BT ceramics when low-temperature hydrothermal or solvothermal synthesis is used to prepare raw BT powders [15]. Such internal defect inclusions are probably formed by the concentration of migrating vacancies and water inside the pores [34,35]. The presence of hydroxyl groups in the initial nanopowders used in this work leads to the final existence of internal defect inclusions. This observation is similar to that reported in Ref. [36] for conventionally sintered BT ceramics with hydrothermally derived raw powders. It can be rational to infer that the internal defect inclusions in Figs. 4(a) and 4(d) are not a specific consequence of CSP. In addition to these minor internal defects, the specimens exhibit well-crystallized grain boundaries, which are features of the final stage of the CSP, as illustrated in Figs. 4(g)–4(i).

Figure S3 in the ESM shows the height, piezoelectric amplitude response, and phase images of the polished 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ ceramics, which were recorded in PFM. A point is randomly selected on the BT and CT:Pr3+ grains (marked in Fig. S3(a) in the ESM). The obtained amplitude–voltage loop and phase–voltage loop are shown in Figs. S3(b) and S3(c) in the ESM. The amplitude loop of BT shows a classic butterfly shaped curve, and the phase loop shows a phase difference of ~180°. This indicates that the BT ceramics have good local ferroelectricity. The amplitude loop of CT:Pr3+ does not show a classic butterfly shaped curve, indicating that the CT:Pr3+ grains do not have local ferroelectricity.

The XRD patterns of the 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ ceramics prepared by CSP at 225 °C, CSA at 900 °C and SSS at 1200 °C are shown in Fig. 5. As illustrated in Fig. 5(a), all the specimens have a main phase of BT. Figure 5(b) shows that the main peak of CT (2θ* ≈ 33°) appears in the 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ composite ceramics prepared by CSP. However, compared with the peak of the CSP counterpart, this peak in the SSS ceramics disappears, indicating that a solid solution of (Ba0.8Ca0.2)TiO3 is formed in the prepared ceramics. This is ascribed to the fact that all CT:Pr3+ was incorporated into the matrix BT phase after SSS. It is also noted that the CSA ceramics have the main peak of CT (2*θ* ≈ 33°). However, the peak intensity is much lower than that of the ceramics prepared by CSP, indicating that the ceramics prepared by CSP have a much greater content of CT:Pr3+. The present results show that CSP can significantly decrease the interdiffusion between the filling phase CT:Pr3+ and matrix phase BT, which can greatly preserve the optical properties of the composite ceramics. The peak of BT near 45° is split into two peaks in Fig. 5(c), similar to that of the counterpart prepared by SSS. However, the angles of this peak for the SSS ceramics are larger. The much smaller lattice of the samples prepared by SSS can be attributed to more Ca2+ ions with smaller radii than Ba2+ ions. Furthermore, the split peaks of the ceramics prepared by SSS and CSA are due to the diffusion of Ca2+ into BT and the increase in grain size, forming a tetragonal/orthorhombic solid solution of Ba(1−*x*)Ca*x*TiO3 [37]. The single peak of the (200) reflection indicates that all the CSP ceramics are in the pseudocubic phase, which is similar to a previous work on BT ceramics prepared by CSP [15]. It is known that common XRD cannot sufficiently resolve spontaneous distorted structures at the 100 nm scale. Such grains with sizes of approximately 100 nm should have at least local tetragonal and/or orthorhombic structures, and hence, ferroelectric performances are expected, as confirmed by the PFM test.

The ferroelectric properties of the 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ ceramics prepared by CSP with optimized parameters were characterized via P*–*E* and *I*–*E* loop measurements, as shown in Fig. 6. The *P*–*E* and *I*–*E* loops were recorded at a frequency of 10 Hz. For the *I‒E* loop, two obvious current peaks are observed at the coercive field (±*E*c). This is ascribed to the domain switching of the long-range ferroelectric order under an external electric field. Notably, the 0–3 type 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ composite ceramics prepared by CSP show strong ferroelectric properties, which are even superior to those ceramics prepared with SSS at 1200 °C (Fig. S4 in the ESM). Moreover, the temperature of CSP is far below that of SSS. For a better comparison, Table 1 shows the ferroelectric parameters of the BT and BT–CT ceramic/composite ceramics prepared by different sintering methods. The ferroelectric properties of 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ composite ceramics prepared by CSP are even greater than those of conventional sintered samples.

The PL excitation spectra for the 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ composite ceramics prepared by CSP, CSA, and SSS are shown in Fig. 7(a). Two broad bands of the excitation spectra monitored at 614 nm for the CSP, CSA, and SSS ceramics are centered at ~320 and ~400 nm, which is ascribed to the intervalence charge transfer (IVCT) [40]. Moreover, these two broad bands are not found in the ceramics prepared by SSS. The two broad bands of the excitation spectra are induced by the CT:Pr3+ phase [41]. This characterization also confirms that the composite ceramics prepared by CSP and CSA have a pure phase of CT:Pr3+. Three characteristic peaks of Pr3+ are also found between 430 and 500 nm, as marked in Fig. 7(a). As shown in Fig. 7(b), the PL emission spectra under 326 nm excitation show a strong peak centered at 614 nm for the CSP and CSA ceramics, which is ascribed to the 1D2→3H4 transition of Pr3+. The 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ composite ceramics prepared by CSP have a maximal PL peak at 614 nm, which is ~8 times greater than that of the SSS ceramics and ~1.5 times greater than that of the CSA ceramics. Pr3+-doped CT has strong photostimulated luminescence (PSL) after ultraviolet light irradiation at 980 nm [7]. The emission spectra after stimulation with a 980 nm laser and 407 nm light irradiation for 10 min are shown in Fig. 7(c). The characteristic emission peak of Pr3+ centered at 614 nm is observed, which cannot be observed before irradiation. PSL is not observed for the SSS ceramics, which reconfirms that the ceramics prepared by SSS have no Pr3+-doped CT phase. This is consistent with the XRD results. Moreover, the PSL intensity of the CSP ceramics is more than 2 times that of their CSA counterparts.

Based on the above experimental results, a schematic diagram of the reaction mechanism of CSP for 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ composite ceramics is shown in Fig. 8. Ba(OH)2·8H2O was added to the raw powders as a transient medium. As the temperature increases to 80 °C, the Ba(OH)2·8H2O hydrated flux begins to lose crystalline water [15], eventually forming a Ba(OH)2 alkali solution as the holding time increases. Generally, the CSP can be divided into three stages. First, the contact interfaces of BT and CT:Pr3+ are dissolved in an alkali-based solution into soluble species, such as Ba2+ (or BaOH+), Ca2+ (or CaOH+), and  Ti(OH)x4−x (Eq. (1)). This reaction is enhanced more than in the noncontact areas [16]. Moreover, the particle rearrangement, diffusion, and mass transfer of particles are facilitated by the contact pressure in the local area due to the sharp dissolution of BT and CT:Pr3+. Second, the “solution–precipitation” process, assisted by pressure and temperature, occupies the main position. During this process, heating promotes the dissolution of powder particles. When the temperature is increased to 225 °C, the H2O molecules in the solution continuously evaporate and are discharged from the mold, leading to a supersaturated state. With prolonged sintering time, the solute diffuses in the liquid phase and precipitates at the pores and the gaps between particles. Under the combined action of applied pressure and heat, the liquid phase is eliminated, and the powders are sintered into blocks and densified. The CT:Pr3+ content in the ceramics prepared by CSP decreases, and Ca2+ and Ba2+ are uniformly distributed in the BT grains. One may conclude that the grown layer is epitaxial and that no obvious heterostructures are found. This is also found in BT ceramics prepared by CSP using Sr(OH)2·8H2O as a transient solution [19]. During this process,  Ti(OH)x4−x reacts with the dissolved Ba2+ and Ca2+ ions, forming BT and CT (Eqs. (2) and (3)) [42]. Furthermore, Ca2+ ions can diffuse into the whole BT grain at an ultralow temperature of 225 °C under external pressure. The stronger signal from Ca2+ ions at the grain boundary is because not all the dissolved Ca2+ ions diffuse into the lattice of BT. The observed BaCO3 phase is caused by the reaction of basic Ba2+ with carbon dioxide (Eq. (4)).

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, CSP using Ba(OH)2·8H2O as a transient flux can be used to prepare dense 0–3 type 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ all-inorganic composite ceramics. Compared with CSA and SSS, CSP can significantly reduce the interdiffusion between the BT matrix phase and the CT:Pr3+ filler phase. The investigated CSP parameters, Ba(OH)2·8H2O content, sintering time, and temperature, greatly influence the grain size and density of the ceramics. The optimized CSP parameters (20 wt% Ba(OH)2·8H2O, 1 h sintering time, 225 °C sintering temperature, and 400 MPa pressure) can achieve a relatively high density of 89% for the composite ceramics. The prepared ceramics have strong ferroelectric performance. Of particular significance, the luminescent properties of CT:Pr3+ are preserved. The 0.8BT–0.2CT:Pr3+ composite ceramics have much stronger PL and PSL than their counterparts prepared by the CSA and SSS methods. The present investigation provides a potential method for combining luminescent and ferroelectric properties via 0–3 composite technology using CSP.
