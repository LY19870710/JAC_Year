## 1 Introduction

Since the first report on the photocatalysis of TiO2 by Fujishima and Honda in 1972 [1], many semiconductor-based materials have been extensively explored as photocatalysts for various technical applications [2]. Although different kinds of photocatalysts have been developing continuously [2−4], TiO2 is still considered as the most suitable candidate for potential applications at the industrial level due to its good photoactivity, low cost, high chemical and thermal stability, and nontoxicity [1,5−8]. However, its relatively large band gap and especially its high charge carrier recombination rate are still limiting its application potential [9−11]. To further improve its overall performance, many research efforts have been made in these years, including doping with anions, cations, and their co-doping [8,12], nano-metal cocatalyst deposition [13], formation of heterojunctions and homojunction [14−18], crystal facet engineering [19,20], morphology control and modulation [5], and plasmonic resonance assisted photocatalysis [21].

A recent research progress on photocatalysis is the combination of photoirradiation and piezo-activation on proper photocatalysts, which could not only largely improve the charge carrier separation and transfer from their established internal electric fields but also introduce extra charge carriers to participate in various reactions to further boost their performance [15,22,23]. Quite a few photocatalysts have been developed to take advantages of this synergistic photopiezocatalysis effect [24], all of which must meet the pre-requirement of piezoelectric response. This specific material characteristic largely limits the adoption of photopiezocatalysis for the performance enhancement of photocatalysts that do not generally have the piezoelectric response. Thus, it is no surprise that there was no report available on the photocatalytic performance improvement of TiO2 with the photopiezocatalysis strategy, although the photopiezocatalysis strategy had shown significant effects [25].

There were very limited reports on TiO2 with lattice distortions [26−28], which had been found to affect the X-ray absorption and Raman spectrum through theoretical calculations and the gas sensing properties by experiments. However, no report is available on the creation of lattice distortion to break the structure symmetry of TiO2 for the introduction of piezoelectricity to TiO2. If it is feasible, it could subsequently open the novel approach of photopiezocatalysis to enhance the photocatalytic performance of TiO2. In this work, a low-temperature molten salt etching process was developed with MgTiO3 as raw materials and NH4Cl as molten salt. By etching [MgO6] octahedrons away from MgTiO3 in this specific synthesis process, rutile TiO2 nanoparticles (the ER-TiO2 sample) were created with distorted crystal lattice as demonstrated by the XRD Rietveld refinement analysis. By breaking the structure symmetry of rutile TiO2 through the lattice distortion, the ER-TiO2 sample was endowed with an unusual and interesting piezoelectric response for the first time as revealed by the piezoelectric response force microscopy (PFM) analysis.

As one important kind of emerging contaminant, the contamination from pharmaceutical and personal care products (PPCPs) are raising increasing concerns [29]. Tetracycline hydrochloride (TC–HCl) is a commonly used broad-spectrum antibiotic, and its release into water and soil causes serious environmental pollution problems and poses threats to human beings [30−32]. Through the introduction of piezoelectricity, it was found that the ER-TiO2 sample had an improved photocatalytic degradation effect on tetracycline hydrochloride (TC–HCl) under visible light illumination than its commercially available rutile TiO2 nanoparticle counterpart. More significantly, its TC–HCl degradation efficiency was largely enhanced by 71% than its photocatalytic degradation performance when the synergistic photopiezocatalytic effect was present, which demonstrated clearly that the photopiezocatalysis approach could be a novel strategy for the photocatalytic performance enhancement of TiO2 from the introduction of piezoelectricity into it through the creation of lattice distortion.

### 2.1 Chemicals and materials

Chemicals and materials: Magnesium titanium oxide (MgTiO3, 99%, Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) and ammonium chloride (NH4Cl, 99.99%, Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China) were used as sacrificial raw materials and salt for the molten salt etching process, respectively. Ethanol (EtOH; 99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) and deionized water were used for sample washing. Deionized (DI) water was obtained from a water purification system (ChengDu Pure Technology Co., Ltd., China) with resistivity higher than 18 MΩ·cm. For comparison purposes, a commercially available rutile titanium dioxide sample (CR-TiO2, 99.99%) was obtained from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Tetracycline hydrochloride (TC–HCl; USP, Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China) were used as target organic pollutants in the degradation experiment. Tert-Butanol (TBA; AR ≥ 99%, Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China), p-Benzoquinone (BQ; C6H4O2, 99%, Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China), sodium bromate (NaBrO3, AR ≥ 99%, Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China), and potassium iodide (KI, AR ≥ 99%, Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., China) were used as radical scavengers. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

### 2.2 Synthesis of ER-TiO<sub>2 </sub>sample

A low-temperature molten salt etching method was used to create the rutile phase TiO2 nanoparticle sample with lattice distortion (ER-TiO2). First, magnesium titanate and ammonium chloride were poured into a ball mill tank with a moral ratio of 1 : 30, put into a planetary ball mill, and ball ground for 6 h at a speed of 250 r/min. Then, the ball ground mixture was put into a crucible in a muffle furnace (KSL-1200X-M, HF-Kejing Ltd., China), quickly heated up to 360 ℃ at a heating rate of 10 ℃/min, and kept at 360 ℃ for 10 h to allow the low-temperature molten salt etching process to happen. Next, the etched product was cooled naturally to room temperature, washed with deionized water and ethanol several times alternately, and put into an air-drying oven to dry at 60 ℃ overnight. The etching process was repeated with the same procedure for 7 times to obtain the pure rutile phase TiO2 nanoparticle sample.

### 2.3 Characterization of ER-TiO<sub>2 </sub>sample

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained with an X-ray diffractometer (Empyrean Alpha-1, Malvern Panalytic, the Netherlands). Raman spectroscopy measurement was conducted with a Raman spectrometer (Cora-5200, Anton Paar, Austria). Morphology and element distribution analysis were conducted by a transmission electron microscope (JEM-2100F, JEOL, Japan). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was conducted on an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha+, Thermo Fisher, USA) with a monochromatized Al K X-ray source (1486.6 eV). Optical absorbance spectra were measured by a spectrophotometer (UV-3600PLUS, Shimadzu Co., Ltd., Japan). A piezoresponse force microscope (Cypher, Asylum Research, USA) was used for the piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) measurement.

### 2.4 Electrochemical test

A three-electrode electrochemical system was used to measure the photoelectrochemical performance of the working electrode by submerging the counter electrode (Pt wire), reference electrode (Ag/AgCl), and working electrode in 1 M Na2SO4 (pH = 6.65) electrolyte. To prepare the working electrode, the test sample, acetylene black, and binder (PVDF) were mixed uniformly with the mass ratio of 8 : 1 : 1, and then N-Methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) was added into the mixture to form uniform slurry. The slurry was evenly coated on a titanium mesh substrate with a coating area of 1 cm2, and dried in a vacuum drying oven at 60 ℃ for 10 h to obtain the working electrode. A 300 W xenon arc lamp (Beijing Perfect Light Technology Co., Ltd.) was used as a visible light source to simulate sunlight irradiation. The surface photovoltage spectrum of the working electrode was measured on a homemade surface photovoltage measurement system. Mott–Schottky (MS) analysis was performed at different frequencies to obtain the sample’s flat-band potential. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurement was conducted in the frequency range of 0.01 Hz to 100 kHz.

### 2.5 Photocatalytic TC–HCl degradation experiment

The synergistic photopiezocatalytic performance of the ER-TiO2 sample was investigated by its degradation of an antibiotic TC–HCl. The initial TC–HCl solution concentration was fixed at 10 mg/L to simulate the antibiotic-contaminated water, both DI water and tap water were used as water matrixes, and the pH values of the TC–HCl solution were ~7.0. 100 mg sample was first dispersed into 100 mL TC–HCl antibiotic solution, and the sample/TC-HCl mixture was stirred in the dark for 30 min to reach adsorption–desorption equilibrium. Subsequently, the sample/TC–HCl mixture was put into a photo-reactor equipped with ultrasonication, and the degradation experiment began under both visible light irradiation (400–780 nm) and ultrasonication. Every 20 min, TC–HCl antibiotic solution of 4 mL was taken out from the photo-reactor and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant for the determination of residual TC–HCl concentration in it by the spectrophotometer (UV-3600PLUS, Shimadzu Co., Ltd., Japan). All tests were in triplicate. Total organic carbon (TOC) measurement of the TC–HCl solution treated by the ER-TiO2 sample under the photopiezocatalytic treatment for a series of time was conducted on a total organic carbon analyzer (TOC-LCPH, Shimadzu, Japan).

### 3.1 Crystal structure and morphology of ER-TiO<sub>2</sub> nanoparticle sample

Figure 1(a) shows XRD patterns of samples through the low-temperature molten salt etching process 1–7 times, compared with that of the MgTiO3 precursor before the low-temperature molten salt etching process. MgTiO3 has a rhombohedral structure as shown schematically in Fig. S1(a) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). In MgTiO3, all oxygen atoms are equivalent. Each oxygen atom is connected to two titanium atoms and two magnesium atoms, while each titanium/magnesium atom is connected to six oxygen atoms to form an oxygen octahedron [33]. Figure 1(a) shows clearly that MgO6 octahedra were etched away gradually, and the remaining TiO6 octahedra were reorganized into the tetragonal rutile TiO2 structure during the molten salt etching process. After 7 times of the etching process, all diffraction peaks belonging to MgTiO3 disappeared, and all remaining XRD peaks of the sample could be assigned to the rutile TiO2 phase (JCPDS No. 99-0090) as shown schematically in Fig. S1(a) in the ESM. Thus, the sample after 7 times of the etching process was named as the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample and was used for the following studies. Its diffraction peaks generally were not sharp, and their intensities were relatively low, which suggested that its crystallinity was not high. This low crystallinity observation could be attributed to the low temperature of 360 ℃ used in the molten salt etching process, which was significantly lower than the normal rutile phase transition temperature from ~600 to ~700 ℃ [12,34,35].

TEM analysis was used to examine the morphology and microstructure of the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample. Figure 1(b) shows its TEM observation, which clearly demonstrated that it consisted of aggregations of nanoparticles with irregular shapes and sizes of dozens of nanometers. Figure S2 in the ESM shows its SEM observation, which further confirmed its nanoparticle morphology with irregular shapes and sizes. Figure 1(c) shows its HRTEM image, in which lattice facets could be well recognized. A pair of lattice facets were clearly observed with the d*-spacing values of ~0.223 and ~0.218 nm, respectively, and the separation angle of ~55°, which matched with (200) and (111) facets of the rutile TiO2 phase. This observation agreed with its XRD analysis result, further confirming that rutile TiO2 was created by the low-temperature molten salt etching of MgTiO3.

Due to this specific low-temperature molten salt etching process, the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample may possess distorted lattices during the reorganization of TiO6 octahedra into the tetragonal rutile TiO2 phase, compared with the standard rutile TiO2 phase obtained through the high-temperature calcination process. To verify it, Rietveld refinement was performed on its XRD diffraction data using the General Structural Analysis System (GSAS-II) software to obtain detailed crystallographic data, and Fig. 1(d) shows the result. The refinement result was converged with reasonable *R*wp (5.73%) and *χ*2 (2.90) factors, which suggested that it was convincible. Its detailed crystallographic data from Rietveld refinement was shown in Table 1, compared with that of the standard rutile TiO2 (JCPDS No. 99-0090). It could be found that the lattice constants of the ER-TiO2 sample had deviations from the standard values. Its lattice constants *a* and *b* differed slightly while that of the standard rutile TiO2 are equal, and its unit cell volume *V* (Å3) was ~9.6‰ larger than that of the standard rutile TiO2. Thus, a structure distortion did occur in these ER-TiO2 nanoparticles during the low-temperature molten salt etching process.

### 3.2 Chemical composition of ER-TiO<sub>2</sub> nanoparticle sample

The chemical composition of the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample was investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Figure 2(a) shows its XPS survey spectrum, which clearly demonstrates the existence of Ti and O elements from their strong XPS peak signals. The clear C 1s peak observed could be attributed to the widespread presence of the C element in the environment, while a very weak peak of N 1s could also be observed due to the use of NH4Cl in the low-temperature molten salt etching process. Figure 2(b) shows the high-resolution XPS scans over Ti 2p peaks of the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample, in which two peaks centered at ~458.4 and ~464.2 eV could be observed, corresponding to the 2p1/3 and 2p3/2 peaks of Ti4+, respectively [36]. Figure 2(c) shows its high-resolution XPS scans over O 1s peaks, which could be best fitted by the combination of three peaks centered at ~529.6, ~531.1, and ~532.3 eV, corresponding to lattice oxygen (OL), oxygen vacancies (OV), and surface oxygen (OC), respectively [29]. Figure 2(d) shows the high-resolution XPS scans over N 1s peaks, which could be best fitted by the combination of two peaks centered at ~398.5 and ~399.7 eV, respectively. Thus, no replacement of O atoms by N atoms in the TiO2 crystal lattice to form Ti–N bonds happened in the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample because the N 1s peak associated with Ti–N bonding generally occurred at ~396 to ~397 eV [12,30,37–39]. N 1s peaks near 400 eV could be attributed to molecularly adsorbed N-containing compounds from ammonium chloride decomposition products [30], which happened easily in the-low temperature molten NH4Cl etching process.

### 3.3 Optical properties and band structure of ER-TiO<sub>2</sub> nanoparticle sample

The diffuse reflectance measurement was used to investigate optical properties of the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample, and its optical absorption spectrum was estimated by Kubelka–Munk function (*F*(*R*) = (1 − *R*)2/(2*R)* where *R* is the diffuse reflection coefficient) [40]. Figure 3(a) shows the optical absorbance spectrum of the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample, compared with that of a commercially available rutile TiO2 nanoparticle sample (CR-TiO2). Both samples had similar strong light absorption in the UV region, while the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample showed a clear absorption tail into the visible region. This visible light absorption tail of the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample could be attributed to its surface-adsorbed N-containing compounds, which had been reported to generate visible light absorption and activity in TiO2 [30]. Thus, the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample could be expected to be activated by visible light illumination, which could largely enhance its solar energy usage.

*
Fig. 3(a) Optical absorbance spectra of commercially available rutile TiO2 nanoparticle sample and ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample (the inset image shows their Tauc plots). (b) Mott–Schottky curves. (c) XPS valence band spectrum. (d) Schematic energy band structure diagram of ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample.

The inset image in Fig. 3(a) shows their Tauc plots (F*(*R*) × *hv*)2 vs. *hv* [24,40] constructed from their optical absorption data. Their band gap (*E*g) values could be determined at ~3.14 and ~3.18 eV for ER-TiO2 and CR-TiO2 samples, respectively, which was consistent with the fact that surface adsorbed N-containing compounds on the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample could only arouse visible light absorption tail but could not change its intrinsic band gap structure [30,41,42]. Compared to bulk rutile TiO2, both ER-TiO2 and CR-TiO2 samples had wider band gaps, which may be attributed to the quantum size effect from their nano-size feature [43].

To explore its energy band structure, Mott–Schottky (M–S) curve and XPS valence band spectrum of the ER-TiO2 sample were further explored. Figure 3(b) shows its M–S plots measured at 2, 3, and 5 kHz with the Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The positive slopes of its M–S plots indicated that the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample also possessed the n-type semiconductor nature of rutile TiO2 [44], and its flat-band potential could be determined at ~−1.02 V vs. the Ag/AgCl reference electrode under a neutral electrolyte at pH of ~7. Thus, its flat-band potential vs. the normal hydrogen electrode (NHE) could be determined at ~−0.82 V. The conduction band minimum (CBM) of an n-type semiconductor is generally smaller than its flat-band potential by 0.1–0.3 V [45]. Thus, CBM of the ER-TiO2 sample could be calculated at ~2.02–2.22 V vs. NHE. Figure 3(c) shows its XPS valence band spectrum, which demonstrated that its valence band maximum (VBM) was ~2.24 V. Its measured VBM value from the XPS valence band spectrum was generally in agreement with that calculated by its band gap value and CBM value determined from its M–S plots, which suggested that the determination of its CBM was correct. Thus, its energy band structure diagram could be constructed as shown in Fig. 3(d), which demonstrates that it has a proper energy band structure for various photocatalytic applications.

### 3.4 Piezoelectric response of ER-TiO<sub>2</sub> nanoparticle sample

The ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample had a distorted lattice structure from its low-temperature molten salt etching synthesis process, which could subsequently endow it with specific properties that normal rutile TiO2 synthesized through the high-temperature calcination process could not have. The piezoelectric response force the microscopy (PFM) technique was used to explore if the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample could have piezoelectric responses from its distorted lattice structure. Figure 4(a) shows the PFM phase hysteresis loops and amplitude hysteresis loops of the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample. Well-defined piezoresponse phase reversal hysteresis loops and the characteristic amplitude–voltage butterfly loops were observed on the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample, which clearly demonstrated its piezoelectric characteristics. Thus, the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample possessed an unusual and interesting piezoelectric response, which had not been found in TiO2 previously.

*
Fig. 4(a) PFM phase hysteresis loops and amplitude hysteresis loops of ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample. (b) d*33 values of ER-TiO2 sample and various polar photocatalysts reported in Refs. [24,30,46–48].

The maximum effective piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33) could be calculated from the slope of the smoothest segment of the displacement–voltage curve, which is generally used to quantify the sample’s piezoelectricity. From its displacement–voltage curve shown in Fig. 4(a), the *d*33 value of the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample could be calculated at ~41.6 pm/V. Figure 4(b) compares *d*33 values between the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample and various polar photocatalysts reported in the literature, including Bi2WO6 [24], BaTiO3 [30,46], and ZnO [47,48]. It could be found that the ER-TiO2 nanoparticle sample had a comparable or even higher *d*33 value than these well-explored polar photocatalysts, which suggested that it had the potential to be excited by external periodical stress to generate extra reactive species and enhance photogenerated charge carrier separation to boost its catalytic performance through the synergic photopiezocatalysis process.

### 3.5 Boosted photopiezocatalytic degradation of tetracycline hydrochloride by ER-TiO<sub>2</sub> nanoparticle sample

TC–HCl was chosen as the model organic pollutant in this study to demonstrate the superior photopiezocatalytic degradation performance of the ER-TiO2 sample. Figure 5(a) shows the photocatalytic TC–HCl degradation performance of the ER-TiO2 sample under visible light illumination, compared with that of the commercially available rutile CR-TiO2 sample, and that under visible light illumination without the photocatalyst presence. After 1 h under visible light illumination without the photocatalyst presence, TC–HCl showed a very slight degradation with a high residual percentage of ~96.9%. Thus, visible light illumination itself did not have an obvious degradation effect on TC–HCl. For the photocatalytic degradation experiment, the photocatalyst sample was added into the TC–HCl solution and stirred in dark magnetically for 0.5 h to reach the adsorption/desorption equilibrium before the visible light illumination was turned on. It was found that both of them could effectively degrade TC–HCl under the visible light illumination. After 1 h treatment, the residual percentage of TC–HCl was ~26.5% for the CR-TiO2 sample and ~20.7% for the ER-TiO2 sample, respectively, which demonstrated that the ER-TiO2 sample had a comparable and even better photocatalytic performance compared to the CR-TiO2 sample although it was synthesized under a much lower temperature and had relatively low crystallinity.

Due to its specific piezoelectric response, the photopiezocatalysis strategy was applied for the first time for the TiO2 photocatalyst to enhance the degradation performance of the ER-TiO2 sample. Figure 5(b) shows the TC–HCl degradation experiment under various conditions by the ER-TiO2 sample. Without the ER-TiO2 sample presence, TC–HCl showed a slight degradation under the simultaneous visible light illumination and ultrasonication treatment. After 1 h treatment, its residual percentage was still ~90.9%. After 1 h photocatalytic treatment, its residual percentage was ~20.7%, while its residual percentage dropped to ~13.5% after 1 h piezocatalytic treatment. As expected, the photopiezocatalytic treatment showed a further enhancement effect on the TC–HCl degradation by the ER-TiO2 sample. After 1 h treatment under both visible light illumination and ultrasonication with the presence of the ER-TiO2 sample, the residual percentage of TC–HCl largely dropped to ~6.9%, which demonstrated clearly the superior TC–HCl degradation capability of the ER-TiO2 sample when the photopiezocatalysis strategy was applied for TiO2 photocatalyst.

These TC–HCl degradation curves under different treatments could be best fitted by the first-order model (Eq. (2)):

(2) ln⁡(C/C0)=−kt

where *C* and *C*0 are the residual and initial concentrations of TC-HCl, respectively, *t* is the treatment time, and *k* is the reaction rate constant. The TC–HCl degradation efficiencies under different treatment conditions could be compared quantitively by their *k* values. The TC–HCl degradation rate constant (*k*) of the CR-TiO2 sample was determined at ~0.0213 min−1 for the photocatalytic reaction, while that of the ER-TiO2 sample was determined at ~0.0264, ~0.0352, and ~0.0452 min−1 for photocatalytic, piezocatalytic, and photopiezocatalytic reactions, respectively. The ER-TiO2 sample’s photocatalytic TC–HCl degradation efficiency was ~24% higher than that of its commercially available rutile nano-TiO2 counterpart, while its piezophotocatalytic TC–HCl degradation efficiency was even ~71% higher than its photocatalytic TC–HCl degradation efficiency. This result clearly demonstrated that the synergistic photopiezocatalysis effect boosted the performance of this specifically synthesized TiO2 photocatalyst by the molten salt etching process through the creation of lattice distortion and subsequent piezoelectric response in it. Figure S3 in the ESM shows the total organic carbon (TOC) measurement results of the TC–HCl solution treated by the ER-TiO2 sample under the photopiezocatalytic treatment for a series of time. It demonstrated that the TOC amount in the treated TC-HCl continuously decreased with the treatment time increase, which suggested that TC–HCl could be mineralized by the ER-TiO2 sample through the photopiezocatalytic treatment.

The ER-TiO2 sample was stable during its storage under normal laboratory conditions and water treatment practice. Figure S4 in the ESM compares XRD patterns of the ER-TiO2 sample just obtained and after being stored in the laboratory for 1 month. It clearly demonstrated that the two XRD patterns were identical. Thus, the structure of the ER-TiO2 sample did not change under normal storage conditions. Figure S5 in the ESM compares XRD patterns of the ER-TiO2 sample before and after the photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation experiment, which clearly demonstrated that the two XRD patterns were also identical. To explore its structure information in detail, Rietveld refinement was also performed on its XRD diffraction data after the photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation experiment, and the detailed crystallographic data from Rietveld refinement is shown in Table S3 in the ESM. It was found that its detailed crystallographic data before and after the photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation experiment was also very close. For example, its lattice constants *a* and *b* also differed slightly after the photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation experiment, and its unit cell volume was very close (only ~0.48‰ larger) to that before the photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation experiment. These results demonstrated that the ER-TiO2 sample had good structure stability, and its distorted lattice structure could be well reserved during the photopiezocatalysis process.

### 3.6 Main active species functioned in photopiezocatalysis process of ER-TiO<sub>2</sub> nanoparticle sample

The quenching experiments with the presence of a series of active species scavengers in the reaction system were conducted to explore the main active species that functioned in the photopiezocatalysis process of the ER-TiO2 sample [24,49,50]. Figure 5(c) shows photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation curves of the ER-TiO2 sample with the separate presence of TBA (1 mM), BQ (0.5 mM), NaBrO3 (1 mM), and KI (1 mM) in the reaction system, compared with that without any active species scavenger. It could be found that the addition of NaBrO3 in the reaction solution did not have an obvious effect on the photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation behavior, while the addition of BQ, KI, and TBA clearly hindered the photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation behavior. Thus, superoxide radicals (· O2−), holes (h+), and hydroxyl radicals (·OH) were the main active species that functioned during the photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation by the ER-TiO2 sample, consistent with its energy band structure. Figure S6 in the ESM shows the photocatalytic TC–HCl degradation curves of the ER-TiO2 sample with the separate presence of TBA (1 mM), BQ (0.5 mM), NaBrO3 (1 mM), and KI (1 mM) in the reaction system, respectively, compared with that without any active species scavenger. It could be found that the addition of BQ totally inhibited the photocatalytic TC–HCl degradation, which suggested that · O2− played the most important role in it. Both h+ and ·OH also contributed to the photocatalytic TC–HCl degradation by the ER-TiO2 sample as that in the photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation. However, e− was also found to contribute to the photocatalytic TC–HCl degradation by the ER-TiO2 sample, which was different from that in the photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation. Thus, these observations suggested that the piezo-activation of the ER-TiO2 sample may also accelerate the reaction between e− and O2 to generate ·O2− during the photopiezocatalysis process.

### 3.7 Cyclic stability and performance in real water of ER-TiO<sub>2</sub> nanoparticle sample during photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation

To investigate the photopiezocatalytic performance stability of the ER-TiO2 sample, the cyclic photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation experiment was conducted. Figure 5(d) shows the photopiezocatalytic degradation percentages of TC–HCl for four consecutive runs by the ER-TiO2 sample, which demonstrated that the similar TC–HCl degradation performance was observed, and the degradation percentages of TC–HCl were generally ~90% after 1 h treatment in these four consecutive runs. Figure 5(e) shows the photopiezocatalytic TC–HCl degradation performances by the ER-TiO2 sample in DI water and tap water, which clearly demonstrated that its photopiezocatalytic degradation performance on TC–HCl was only slightly affected in tap water, compared with that in DI water, although various cations, anions, and residual organics exist in tap water. Thus, the ER-TiO2 sample demonstrated good performance stability during the photocatalytic reaction and could work well in real water matrix, both of which were beneficial for its potential applications in water treatment practices.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, rutile TiO2 nanoparticles were created by etching [MgO6] octahedrons away from a perovskite structure material MgTiO3 in the molten salt of NH4Cl under a relatively low temperature of ~360 ℃. This specific synthesis process induced a lattice distortion into the rutile TiO2 structure, which subsequently endowed it with an unusual piezoelectric response not found in TiO2 photocatalysts previously. It was found that the TC–HCl degradation efficiency of the ER-TiO2 sample was largely enhanced by 71% through the synergistic photopiezocatalysis effect, which demonstrated that the photopiezocatalysis strategy could be utilized successfully to enhance the photocatalytic performance of TiO2 photocatalyst. Furthermore, the ER-TiO2 sample had good stability and could work well in real water matrix, beneficial for its potential applications in water treatment practices. Thus, the photopiezocatalysis strategy was firstly proposed and verified for TiO2 photocatalyst by this work, which opens a new approach for the photocatalytic performance enhancement in TiO2 through the creation of lattice distortion to induce piezoelectricity. It could also be readily applied to various photocatalysts that the photopiezocatalysis strategy has not been applied to. In general, structure distortions could be induced by proper synthesis processes to endow materials with novel functionalities that they do not usually possess, which could create novel applications in various technical areas.
