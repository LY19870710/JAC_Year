## 1 Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) transition-metal carbides and nitrides (MXenes) [1] have evolved as competitive materials and fillers for developing composites and hybrids for sensors [2], catalysts [3,4], supercapacitors [5], and electromagnetic interference shields [6]. MXenes are typically synthesized by selectively etching the main group element A in the corresponding MAX phase (M for transition metal, X for carbon or nitrogen [7]) in aqueous acid solution, rendering MXenes terminated with a mixture of F, O, and OH functional groups, and these functional groups are usually represented as T*x* [8]. Although the active functional groups create the premise for the surface functional modification of MXenes [9], they are also conducive to easy oxidation in the air or aqueous media. Meanwhile, the study on the high-temperature stability of Ti3C2T*x* MXene shows that Ti3C2T*x* undergoes a phase transition when the temperature exceeds 800 ℃, completely transforming into a lamellated non-stoichiometric TiC*y* phase at temperatures above 1500 ℃ [10]. Besides, MXenes possess excellent mechanical properties [11], which is due to the fact that the effective Young’s modulus of monolayer Ti3C2T*x* is 0.33±0.03 TPa [12]. The outstanding characteristics, as well as the transition metal carbide/nitride cores make MXenes ones of the most promising reinforcing 2D nanomaterials for ultra-high-temperature applications [13,14].

Meanwhile, when used to strengthen and toughen metal [15,16] or ceramic [14,17] matrices, MXenes undergo oxidation or *in-situ* phase transition, which still limits mechanical properties of related composites for high-temperature applications. In this regard, there is even a certain stereotypical perception of the high-temperature application of MXenes, that is, the lamellar carbide reinforcements that are transformed by *in-situ* phases of MXenes at high temperatures, rather than MXenes themselves. In fact, the propensity for easy oxidation and high-temperature phase transition of MXenes is because of the high activity of a superficial transition metal atomic layer [10]. In the parent MAX phase, the metal bond between the transition metal atom and the A atomic layer leads to M*n*+1X*n* stabilization. Therefore, materials such as Ta4AlC3 MAX can be stable at higher temperatures without experiencing the phase transition [18]. In a word, if the transition metal atomic layer on the surface of MXenes can be stabilized, it can be structurally stable at high temperatures. Considering that the parent phase Ti3AlC2 of Ti3C2T*x* can be regarded as the Ti–C layer separated by the Al atomic layer along the  direction of TiC [19], there is a structural similarity between Ti3C2T*x* and TiC, which may facilitate the formation of a strong interface, thereby stabilizing the Ti-atom layer on the Ti3C2T*x* surface.

To exploit the specific correlation between two different dimensions, namely 2D Ti3C2T*x* and three-dimensional (3D) TiC, we attempted to use 2D Ti3C2T*x* cross-scale strengthening and toughening 3D TiC via spark plasma sintering (SPS) at 1900 ℃ under a high pressure (50 MPa). It was shown that the Ti3C2T*x* phase was encapsulated into the TiC ceramic matrix, which prevented the phase transition of Ti3C2T*x* due to the confinement of the superficial Ti atomic layer through the strong interfacial interaction between Ti3C2T*x* and TiC. This finding has changed the perception of the ultra-high-temperature applications of MXenes, and while effectively improving the low fracture toughness of TiC, it is also expected to open new directions for ultra-high-temperature applications of 2D materials.

### 2.1 Materials

Commercial Ti powder (99.5 wt% purity, −45 μm; Jinzhou Institute of Metal Material, China), Al powder (99.7 wt% purity, −29 μm; China Northeast Light Alloy Co., China), and TiC powder (99 wt%, 2–4 μm; Aladdin Industrial Co., China) were used as raw materials. Lithium fluoride (LiF; 99 wt% purity), hydrochloric acid (HCl; AR), and absolute ethanol were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Limited Corporation, China. Comprehensive details concerning the materials and the preparation of Ti3AlC2 are provided in earlier papers [20−23]. Deionized water with resistivity of > 18 MΩ·cm was prepared using a TS-RO-10L/H ultrapure water system (TAOSHI Water Equipment Engineering Co., Ltd., China).

### 2.2 Synthesis of MXene

Multilayer Ti3C2T*x* (M-Ti3C2T*x*) flakes were prepared by mixing Ti3AlC2, LiF, and HCl (12 M) in a ratio of 1 g : 1 g : 10 mL at 35 ℃ for 24 h. The composites were repeatedly washed with deionized water until pH was greater than 6. During each washing process, M-Ti3C2T*x* composites were separated from waste liquid at a centrifugal speed of 3500 r/min for 5 min. The cleaned M-Ti3C2T*x* materials were placed in an appropriate amount of deionized water and ultrasonically stripped for 2 h in an argon atmosphere. The mixed suspension was centrifuged to obtain few-layer and single-layer Ti3C2T*x* (S-Ti3C2T*x*) suspension colloidal solution. The statistical analysis (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) showed that the thickness and size distribution of S-Ti3C2T*x* are approximately 1.5 nm and nearly 2 μm, respectively. The S-Ti3C2T*x* suspension was prefrozen at –80 ℃ for 24 h and then vacuum freeze-dried by SCIENTZ-10N lyophilizer (Ningbo SCIENTZ Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) at –50 ℃ for 3 d.

### 2.3 Synthesis of TiC−MXene composite

A high-shear rotor-stator mixer was used to uniformly mix the dry Ti3C2T*x* and TiC powder. Such a mixer can produce a high-shear force on the liquid materials to achieve an evenly mixed powder without destroying the MXene structure. High-shear mixing has been widely used for emulsification homogenization [24,25]. Dehydrated S-Ti3C2T*x* product was then high-shear mixed with TiC powder in absolute ethanol using an emulsifying machine (JRJ300-SH, Shanghai Huxi Industry Co., Ltd., China) at a rotating speed of 6000 r/min for 2 h. After high-shear mixing, the mixture was transferred to a rotary evaporator (RE-52, Shanghai LICHEN-BX Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., China). The rotary evaporator was connected to a vacuum pump in a negative pressure state (–0.09 MPa). The mixture was heated under a negative pressure at 50 ℃ to obtain dry powder. The mixed powder was consolidated via an SPS system (SPS-20T-10, Shanghai Chenhua Science Technology Co., Ltd., China) at 1900 ℃ under a uniaxial pressure of 50 MPa in vacuum of 5 Pa for 10 min. During SPS, the temperature was increased to 700 ℃ within 3 min and held for 1 min; after that, the heating rate of 100 ℃/min was maintained until a temperature of 1900 ℃ was achieved. Depending on the amount of added Ti3C2T*x*, the bulk TiC–MXene specimens (40 mm in diameter and ~3 mm in thickness) with Ti3C2T*x* mass fractions of 0%, 1%, 2%, 5%, and 10% were prepared.

### 2.4 Characterization

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Bruker D8 advance, Germany) equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source operating at 50 kV and 150 mA was used to determine crystalline phases. The apparent density of the sintered samples was measured in distilled water using Archimedes method, and the theoretical density was calculated according to the rule of mixtures. After mechanical testing, a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JEOL JSM-7600F, Japan) operating at 5 kV was employed to confirm the presence of gold coatings on the fracture surfaces of specimens. Moreover, an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS; Oxford INCA X-Act, UK) was utilized for the distribution analysis of Ti, C, O, F, and Cl elements. A focused-ion-beam SEM instrument (FIB-SEM; Helios G4 PFIB HXe, USA) was used to separate lamellar substances in TiC–MXene composites. In the SEM image, at least 30 parallel lines were drawn, and the grains penetrated by each line were counted. The average number of grains was calculated, and the average grain size was determined.

Three-point bending tests were conducted on the samples with dimensions of 3 mm × 4 mm × 36 mm to determine flexural strength. The fracture toughness was measured by conforming to the single-edge notched beam (SENB) method under ambient conditions. A span length of 30 mm and a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min were kept in the flexural strength tests. For the fracture toughness measurements, the size of specimens was 2 mm × 4 mm × 22 mm, and a notch with a depth of 2.0 mm and a width of 0.2 mm was cut out in the center part of a test specimen. A span length of 16 mm and a crosshead speed of 0.05 mm/min were set for the toughness tests. The value of *K*IC was calculated as Eq. (1) [26]:

(1) KIC=3FLa2bh2[1.93−3.07(ah)+14.53(ah)2−25.07(ah)3+25.8(ah)4]

where *K*IC is the fracture toughness, *F* is the fracture load, *L* is the span distance, *b* is the specimen width, *h* is the specimen thickness, and *a* is the specimen incision depth. The critical energy release rate (*G*IC) was calculated as Eq. (2) [27]:

(2) GIC=KIC2(1−νm2E)

where *E* is the elastic modulus of the TiC–MXene composites, and  νm denotes Poisson’s ratio of TiC-based ceramics (in this study, the relevant value of 0.19 is used) [27]. Both flexural strength and fracture toughness data were obtained by averaging over 5 trials. Vickers hardness was determined via Vickers diamond indentation method under an indentation load in the range of 0.98−9.8 N; the hardness values were calculated from the average of 10 indentation results.

The chemical states of TiC and TiC–MXene composites were studied using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; ESCALAB 250XI, Thermo Fisher, USA) equipped with a 400 µm monochromated Al Kα X-ray beam source to irradiate the sample surface at a base pressure of 1×10−8 mbar. An Ar ion beam was operated at 3.8 kV and 150 µA for 1 h to sputter the sample. The samples were analyzed at a takeoff angle of 45° between the sample surface and the path to the analyzer. The Ti 2p, C 1s, O 1s, F 1s, and Cl 2p spectra with high resolution were collected at a pass energy of 50 eV with a step size of 0.05 eV and fitted in the XPSPEAK Version 4.1 software. Before quantification and peak fitting, a Shirley function was employed for background subtraction. For 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 components, the peak intensity ratios were kept at 2 : 1. Microstructures of the composites were examined using a transmission electron microscope (TEM; HT-7700, Hitachi, Japan). Prior to the TEM measurements, composites were shaped to the samples with a thickness of 30 μm and a diameter of 3 mm. Ion milling was conducted using a Gatan 691 Precision Ion Polishing System to prepare a thin zone in the middle of each sample. An atomic force microscope (AFM; Multimode 8, Bruker Dimension Icon, Germany) was applied to analyze the average thickness and size of MXene nanosheets.

### 2.5 Computational details of interface

The Cambridge sequential total energy package (CASTEP) [28], underlying the density functional theory (DFT), was employed in this paper. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE) functional was used as the exchange-correlation functional. The plane-wave cutoff energy in this work was 350 eV for all bulk crystals, surfaces, and interfaces. Sampling of the irreducible edge within Brillouin zone was carried out based on a regular Monkhorst–Pack grid of 9 × 9 × 9 *k*-points for the 3D bulk crystal and that of 9 × 9 × 1 *k*-points for the 2D surfaces and interfaces. The convergence tolerances of energy, the maximum force, and the maximum displacement were set as 1×10−4 eV, 0.005 eV/Å, and 0.005 Å, respectively. A vacuum was applied at 20 Å above the surfaces to avoid unexpected interactions with the periodic cell structure. The adsorption energy (*E*ad) between TiC and Ti3C2T*x* was calculated as Eq. (3) [29,30]:

(3) Ead=ETiC/Ti3C2Tx−(ETiC+ETi3C2Tx)

where * ETiC/Ti3C2Tx,  ETiC, and  ETi3C2Tx are the total energy of TiC–Ti3C2Tx* system, isolate TiC, and Ti3C2T*x*, respectively.

### 3.1 Phase composition and microstructure of TiC–MXene composites

A simple scheme of the preparation of Ti3C2T*x* MXene reinforced TiC composites was implemented in this work as follows: a high-shear mixing method [24,25] was first used to mix freeze-dried Ti3C2T*x* powder and TiC powder in absolute ethanol; the dispersed liquid was then removed, and TiC–MXene bulk composites were finally produced by SPS. A photograph of a typical bulk composite sample is shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. Figure 1(a) displays the XRD profiles of the freeze-dried MXene powder, high-shear mixed TiC with 10 wt% Ti3C2T*x* powder, and TiC–10 wt% MXene bulk composites. The typical (002) diffraction peak of Ti3C2T*x* MXene appears at around 2*θ* = 6.36°. After the high-shear mixing with TiC powder, this peak shifts to the lower angles (2*θ* = 5.88°). This distinct downshift is caused by ethanol molecules that are adsorbed on the surface of Ti3C2T*x* [31]. Meanwhile, no characteristic peak of MXene is observed in the sintered sample. This can be due to the fact that once the 2D nanomaterials are uniformly dispersed in the matrix, the reduction of the characteristic 2D surface can lead to the reduction or even disappearance of the characteristic peaks [20,32]. At the same time, the peaks of the sintered sample are identified as TiC, which is consistent with the TiC peak before sintering.

The inset images depict the morphologies at a higher resolution, revealing the intergranular fracture as the dominant mode in the ceramics. As seen from the SEM micrographs of pure TiC, the grains became coarser after sintering (Fig. 1(b)). Moreover, there were abundant unclosed intergranular pores which indicated that the sintering temperature or dwelling time was insufficient to eliminate these pores and to obtain fully dense TiC materials. At the amount of added MXene of 5 wt%, many lamellae substances can be observed between the grains (Fig. 1(c)).

To accurately identify the lamellar substances in the TiC–MXene composites, the tungsten needle probe in the FIB-SEM was used to assist in the separation and sampling. Namely, the freshly fractured surface of the TiC–5 wt% MXene composite was exposed to the FIB-SEM analysis, and the lamella covering the matrix was repeatedly poked with a tungsten needle, as shown in Fig. 1(d), until being separated from the matrix. Since the composite bulk was not treated with a gold spray before the FIB-SEM measurement to reduce contamination, the imaging effect in Fig. 1(d) was poor. Once a certain amount of lamellar substances was treated, the composite bulk was taken out from the FIB-SEM setup, and the operating surface of the tungsten needle was carefully placed in anhydrous ethanol for ultrasonic treatment, allowing the stripped lamellar substances to be dispersed in anhydrous ethanol. The exfoliated lamellar substances were collected with a copper mesh microgrid and further characterized by TEM, as shown in Fig. 1(e). The lamellar substances exhibit some flexibility and appear as an overlapping of several monolayer sheets. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) results within the frame in Fig. 1(e) are illustrated in Fig. 1(f). The crystal plane diffraction spots exhibit a standard regular hexagonal arrangement, identified as Ti3C2T*x* without phase transition to TiC*y* or oxidation to TiO2 after calibration [33]. So far, it can be confirmed that the MXenes in the TiC–MXene composite have undergone an incomplete phase transition, and there are still lamellae that remain as Ti3C2T*x* phase.

### 3.2 Influence of interface on ultra-high-temperature stability of MXene in TiC–MXene composites

It has been confirmed that Ti3C2T*x* still exists in the lamellar reinforcement within TiC–MXene composites, which is different from the results on the Ti3C2T*x* phase transition reported in Ref. [34]. To further explore the reason for this difference, the addition of Ti3C2T*x* was increased to 50 wt%, and the TiC–50 wt% MXene composite was prepared by SPS at 1900 ℃ under an uniaxial pressure of 50 MPa and the pressureless conditions, respectively, and the XRD patterns of these two kinds of composite bulks are depicted in Fig. 2(a). Compared with the XRD pattern of the TiC–10 wt% MXene composite in Fig. 1(a), a TiC*y* phase appears alongside the TiC phase. Because of the increased addition of MXene, faint diffraction peaks can also be observed at 17.6°, 26.6°, and 35.7°, which can be indexed as the (004), (006), and (008) planes of Ti3C2T*x* [34], respectively, indicating the presence of MXene. However, the strongest peak of MXene at ~6° is not visible, which can be attributed to the reduction of characteristic 2D surfaces caused by the uniform dispersion of 2D nanomaterials in the matrix, as discussed earlier. Another possibility is that TiC*y* preferentially forms along the (002) plane of Ti3C2T*x* due to the close atomic packing. The magnified image clearly shows the separation of characteristic peaks between TiC and TiC*y*. However, in the unpressurized sample, only characteristic peaks corresponding to the (111) and (200) planes of TiC*y* are observed at 2*θ* angles of 36.0° and 41.8°, respectively, while there are no peaks typical of TiC. The possible causes for these results remain unclear. It is speculated that it may be related to the resolution of the instrument or the excessive generation of TiC*y* phases on certain crystal planes of the pressureless sample, leading to the masking of the relevant TiC diffraction peaks. The diffraction peak of TiO2 indicates that MXene is partially oxidized, which is consistent with the XPS data in Fig. S3 in the ESM. The weak diffraction peak of C indicates that a small amount of carbon remains after the phase transition of MXene. The sample sintered under the non-pressure conditions demonstrates only a single characteristic peak of TiC*y*. The phases of samples sintered under different pressures are obviously different. Under the non-pressure conditions, MXene is completely converted to TiC*y*, which agrees with the phase transition of MXene after sintering at 1500 ℃, observed by Wyatt *et al*. [34].

The SEM images of cross sections of the TiC–50 wt% MXene composite sintered at 50 MPa and in the absence of pressure are shown in Figs. 2(b) and 2(d), respectively. In the central part of Fig. 2(b), lamellar materials can be observed between TiC grains. After further magnification (Fig. 2(c)), the morphology is consistent with that of the TiC–MXene composite in Fig. 1(c). The morphology of the materials in the left-hand side of Fig. 2(b) is not observed in the fracture of the TiC–10 wt% MXene composite, but resembles the morphology of TiCy* transformed from MXenes [34]. In Fig. 2(d), the MXene structure has undergone a significant phase transition during sintering under non-pressure conditions. The EDS results in Fig. 2(e) also indicate that the morphological change of MXene is due to a phase transition rather than the oxidation. In conclusion, by analogy with the reported results [34], Ti3C2T*x* undergoes a phase transition at high temperatures. However, when Ti3C2T*x* is in the TiC environment, and both closely fit each other under a high pressure, the phase transition of MXenes is suppressed. This situation may be related to the strong interface bonding between Ti3C2T*x* and TiC, which confines the surface Ti atomic layer of Ti3C2T*x* to make it as stable as the titanium carbide core inside Ti3C2T*x*. Because of the limited diffusion ability of atoms within TiC and Ti3C2T*x* crystals, interfacial bonding can only be formed through a close matching of the two materials under a high-pressure environment.

To gain more insight into the nature of the interfacial bonding between Ti3C2T*x* and TiC, a theoretical simulation was carried out to investigate the interfacial electronic structure of the composite. According to previous reports [35−37], the TiC(111) surface is polar, and the TiC(111) crystal plane at the Ti-terminal interface has higher adsorption energy and lower interfacial energy compared to the C-terminal one [38]. Combining XPS (Fig. S3 in the ESM) and EDS results (Fig. S4 in the ESM) for the TiC–MXene composite, as well as the atomic structure models of the TiC(111) plane with a Ti-terminal interface, it is established that Ti3C2O2 has the O functional groups and Ti3C2OF possesses the F/O functional groups, as shown in Fig. S7 in the ESM. Impressively, the mismatch between the 2D Ti3C2T*x*(0001) and TiC(111) surfaces is quite small (only about 0.4%), which is because the 2D Ti3C2T*x* structure has been produced from the bulk layered Ti3AlC2 ceramic where the Al atom layers separate the Ti–C layers along the  direction. The calculation results demonstrate that the interfacial adsorption energy for the Ti3C2O2/TiC(111) interface is −1.85 eV, which is obviously larger than that for the Ti3C2OF/TiC(111) one (−0.46 eV). According to Figs. 2(f) and 2(h), the bond lengths and bond angles in the unit cells both indicate that the binding force of the O functional group is greater than that of the F functional group when MXene is bound to TiC. The differential charge densities for the Ti3C2O2/TiC(111) and Ti3C2OF/TiC(111) heterointerfaces are shown in Figs. 2(g) and 2(i), respectively, revealing the relevant information regarding electron transfer. As seen in Figs. 2(g) and 2(i), the charge accumulation around O atoms is more pronounced than that around F atoms, and ionic bonds form because of the charge offset at the interface. In addition, a slice of the differential charge density along the Ti−O−Ti or Ti−F−Ti bonds for the two heterostructures is shown in Fig. S8 in the ESM, revealing the bonding nature at MXene/TiC interfaces. The hybridization of each electron orbital can be determined from the partial density of state (PDOS) data, as shown in Fig. S9 in the ESM. The electronic densities for both kinds of interfaces are clearly observed near the Fermi level, which indicates that these interfaces possess significant metallic properties. According to the electronic structure analysis of the heterointerfaces, both the O and F functional groups on the surface of Ti3C2T*x* can form a strong chemical bond with a TiC(111) surface, where the bonding strength between O and TiA/TiB is stronger than that between F and TiA/TiB. This interfacial bonding stabilizes the surface Ti atomic layer of Ti3C2T*x*, thereby avoiding the high-temperature phase transition of MXene and maintaining stability at 1900 ℃.

### 3.3 Mechanical properties of TiC–MXene composites

The flexural strength data on the pure TiC and TiC–MXene composites with different filler loadings are shown in Fig. 3(a). It is clear that the added Ti3C2T*x* phase has increased the flexural strength of the composites compared to that of pure TiC. With an increase in Ti3C2T*x* content, the properties first increased and then decreased. The flexural strength of the TiC–MXene composites reached the maximum at the Ti3C2T*x* content of 5 wt% (603.07 MPa), increasing by 15.6% compared to that of pure TiC (521.64 MPa). The elastic modulus and other properties are summarized in Table S1 in the ESM. The reinforcement effect of the uniformly dispersed Ti3C2T*x* increased performance of the composites until the Ti3C2T*x* dopant content reached a certain level. In general, the optimization of the interface bonding between the reinforcement and matrix accelerates the load transfer from the matrix to the reinforcement, consequently enhancing the overall strengthening effect. For the TiC–MXene composites, the first-principles calculations based on DFT have unveiled a robust chemical interface bonding between Ti3C2T*x* and TiC, which serves as the principal factor behind the increased mechanical properties of the composite. Besides, the incorporation of MXene results in a reduction in grain size within the matrix, as illustrated in Fig. S6 in the ESM, and the grain size data are meticulously documented in Table S1 in the ESM. This grain refinement contributes to a notable enhancement in the strength of the materials. The fracture toughness of TiC–MXene composites with different filler loadings was evaluated using the SENB method, and the results are shown in Fig. 3(b). The fracture toughness value is found to positively correlate with the amount of added MXene. In particular, the TiC–10wt% MXene has the largest fracture toughness value (6.56 MPa·m1/2), which is 55.8% higher than that of pure TiC (4.21 MPa·m1/2). It is noteworthy that the grain refinement alongside the blocking and deflection of MXene lamellae has occurred during crack propagation; therefore, the energy consumption upon the MXene pull-out increased, and the fracture toughness of TiC–MXene composites was improved. The presence of MXene increases the critical energy release rate of the composites, which is required for crack propagation in a TiC matrix. The *G*IC values of TiC–MXene composites are also displayed in Fig. 3(b). Crack propagation through the TiC matrix is severely hindered, which leads to the dissipation of the higher fracture energy in the composites. Moreover, adding the 10 wt% MXene content to the TiC matrix made the *G*IC value increase by ~140%. As shown in Table 1, the relative increments of the properties of the TiC–MXene composites studied herein are also comparable to those of TiC reinforced by graphene [39−43] or carbon nanotubes [27].

By comparing the flexural strength (Fig. 3(a)) and fracture toughness (Fig. 3(b)) values, it is found that the same MXene content can result in inconsistent improvement in the two properties, which may be caused using different testing methods. The flexural test data depend on the properties of the relatively large sample. Specifically, regardless of the material nature, a single defect in the sample (e.g., large agglomerate) can cause failure. In contrast, the SEBN fracture test was carried out along the pre-crack, preventing the results from being easily affected by a single defect. Therefore, when the amount of MXene exceeded 5 wt%, the flexural strength decreased because of aggregation and other reasons, but the fracture toughness of the material continued to increase. It is worth mentioning that the uniform dispersion of MXene is critical to the systems’ performance. This is because a single large defect or agglomerate can reduce the toughness of the surrounding matrix. Therefore, the inconsistencies between flexure strength and fracture toughness improvements are a common phenomenon [44,45].

### 3.4 Derivation and calculation of cross-scale mechanical properties of TiC enhanced by MXene

Through the construction of a stress transfer mechanics model of the TiC–MXene composite, the principle of cross-scale strengthening and toughening is further revealed. The interface stress transfer between MXene and TiC-matrix determines the effectiveness of the reinforcement phase. The relatively weak van der Waals forces between the MXene lamellae reduce the efficiency of load transfer from the matrix to the inner lamellae of the few-layer MXene. Therefore, the number of layers in few-layer MXene has a significant effect on the interfacial stress of the reinforcement. The relational model between the number of layers (* n) and the thickness of the reinforcement ( h) is established according to Fig. 4(a) as Eqs. (4) and (5):

(4) h=n×hs+(n−1)×hi

(5) hi=d−hs

where the thickness of monolayer MXene ( hs) is 0.894 nm [46], and  hi is the distance between the MXene monolayers. Here,  d is the spacing between the diffraction planes, calculated by Bragg’s law as Eq. (6):

(6) d=Nλ2sinθ

where  N is an integer (in this work, N* = 1), * λ is the X-ray wavelength (Cu Kα radiation,  λ = 0.154 nm), and  θ is the incident angle.

According to the well-known Cox shear lag model modified by Hsueh [47], the interfacial stress transfer between the few-layer MXene and TiC-matrix was described according to a model shown in Fig. 4(b). To simplify the calculation, it was assumed as Eq. (7):

(7) llm=hhm=ab=tu

where a few-layer MXene with a length of  l = 2 t and a thickness of  h = 2 a is embedded at the center of a TiC-matrix element with a length of  lm = 2 u and a thickness of  hm = 2 b. The value of  b can be calculated as Eq. (8):

(8) φf=(ab)(tu)2=ρρfωf

where  ρ is the density of the TiC–MXene composite,  ρf is the density of MXene, and  φf and  ωf are the volume fraction and the weight fraction of added MXene in the composite, respectively. The interfacial shear stress ( τ) at a TiC–MXene interface can be calculated as Eq. (9) [47]:

(9) τ=aφψσ0(exp(φy)−exp(−φy)exp(φt)+exp(−φt))

where  φ and  ψ can be estimated as Eqs. (10) and (11), respectively:

(10) φ=1b−a3[aEp+(b−a)Em]2a(1+νm)Ep

(11) ψ=bEpaEp+(b−a)Em

where  Ep is the elastic modulus of MXene,  Em is the elastic modulus of TiC,  νm is the Poisson’s ratio of TiC,  σ0 is the tensile fracture stress of TiC, and  y is the distance from the center of MXene to its end. According to XPS (Fig. S3 in the ESM) and EDS (Fig. S4 in the ESM) data,  Ep is taken as the theoretical value of Ti3C2O2. In the computations,  ρf = 5.2 g·cm−3 [48],  Ep= 466 GPa [49],  νm = 0.19 [27],  Em = 405.26 GPa, and  σ0 = 258 MPa [50]. According to the average size of MXene measured via AFM (Fig. S1 in the ESM),  l ≈ 2 μm. The values of  ρ (Fig. S10 and Table S1 in the ESM) were obtained experimentally, and  θ = 4.54° [51]. At  y=t,  τ reaches the maximum.

The SENB fracture (Fig. S6 in the ESM) observation shows that the MXene pull-out is an important toughening mechanism of the TiC–MXene composites. The energy consumed during the crack propagation within the TiC–MXene composites can be used to evaluate the effectiveness of the few-layer MXene. The pull-out energy of a few-layer MXene ( Gpullout) can be calculated as Eq. (12) [52]:

(12) Gpullout=φf(Δl)2τ3h

where  Δl is the pull-out length of the few-layer MXene. According to the difference of G*IC between the TiC and TiC–MXene composites with various MXene contents, the corresponding critical energy release rate ( ΔG) can be obtained. The contribution of the few-layer MXene pull-out to the increase in the critical energy release rate can be estimated by the ratio of the computed  Gpullout and  ΔG values. As shown in Fig. 4(c), 3 and 19 layers were taken as the boundary numbers of the few-layer MXene in each group of composites, and the region image of the functional relationship between the  Gpullout/ΔG ratio and  Δl can be obtained. The area where the four component images overlap corresponds to the effective range of the pull-out length and the enhancement efficiency of the few-layer MXene. In turn, the shadowed part indicates that the contribution of the MXene pull-out to the improved critical energy release rate is over 100%, which is meaningless. As depicted in Fig. S6 in the ESM, the length of the few-layer MXene pull-out is estimated to be about 100–400 nm within the effective pull-out length range ( Δl) is 250 nm, the  Gpullout/ΔG ratio is about 90%; thus, it can be considered that the pull-out of the MXene layer is the main reason for the toughening of the TiC–MXene composites.

It is evident from Fig. 4(d) that when  Δl is 250 nm, the computational energy consumed by the MXene pull-out decreases with the increase of the number of MXene layers, which means that the van der Waals forces between the layers reduce the load transfer efficiency. Considering that the pull-out of MXene is the main reason for toughening, the higher the proportion of  Gpullout in  ΔG, the more likely it is for the corresponding number of layers of MXene to be the average number of layers. In the 1–5 wt% MXene composites, the average layer number in the few-layer MXene is 7. For TiC–10 wt% MXene composites,  Gpullout is in the range of  ΔG until MXene exceeds more than 10 layers, indicating that MXene phases have agglomerated to increase the number of layers. To sum up, the calculations demonstrate that it is more advantageous to utilize a reduced number of MXene layers to enhance the strength and toughness of the ceramic matrix, rather than employing multiple layers of MXene.

## 4 Conclusions

The specific correlation between the materials of two different dimensions was employed in this study by taking 2D Ti3C2T*x* and 3D TiC systems to produce TiC–MXene composites through SPS. This innovative approach enabled to achieve cross-scale strengthening and toughening of 3D TiC by adding 2D Ti3C2T*x*. The layered materials separated within the TiC–MXene composite was identified as MXene without undergoing a phase transition. Further experimental exploration revealed that the absence of phase transition in MXene at 1900 ℃ was attributed to its robust interface binding with TiC. According to the first principles calculations, O and F functional groups on the surface of Ti3C2T*x* can form a strong interfacial binding with a TiC(111) plane, stabilizing the superficial Ti atomic layer on the Ti3C2T*x* phase. This prevents the high-temperature phase transition of MXene and maintains its stability at 1900 ℃. Mechanical performance testing and microstructure morphology analysis revealed that MXene improved the bending strength of the TiC matrix by 15.6% through grain refinement and crack deflection. Additionally, mechanical modeling calculations showed that MXene could have enhanced the fracture toughness of the TiC matrix by 49.9% through the layer pull-out mechanism. These findings not only complete the existing knowledge regarding high-temperature oxidation or phase transition of MXenes but also expand the range of ultra-high-temperature applications for MXenes. Relying on the large variety of carbides in the MXene family, a new path for designing and developing similar composites, such as V2CT*x*-reinforced VC, Nb2CT*x* or Nb4C3T*x*-reinforced NbC, Hf3C2T*x*-reinforced HfC, Zr3C2T*x*-reinforced ZrC, and Ta4C3T*x*-reinforced TaC, can thus be provided.
