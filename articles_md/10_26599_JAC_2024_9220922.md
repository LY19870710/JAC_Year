## 1 Introduction

The growing market demand for wearable electronic devices, spanning both civil and military applications, has spurred substantial interest in advancing self-powering technology. Thermoelectric (TE) technology, which facilitates direct conversion between heat and electricity, has emerged as a promising energy alternative [1,2]. Particularly noteworthy is its capacity to efficiently convert skin heat into electricity, attracting considerable attention in the domain of wearable electronics [3,4]. The TE performance of the material can be evaluated by the TE figure of merit [5]: *zT = S**2**σT/κ*, where *S* is the Seebeck coefficient, *σ* is the electrical conductivity, *T* is the absolute temperature, *κ* is the thermal conductivity, and the power factor (PF), as a comprehensive indicator of electrical properties, is defined by *S**2**σ* [6]. To achieve higher *zT*, it is thus imperative to enhance the values of *S* and *σ* while concurrently reducing *κ*. To date, the typical high-temperature and mid-temperature TE materials are SiGe alloys and filled CoSb3 [7−10], while the most widely used room-temperature TE materials are Bi2Te3, Sb2Te3, Bi2Se3, and their alloys [11−13]. Due to their simpler composition and lower band gap, binary Sb2Te3 and Bi2Te3 are often selected as p-type and n-type TE legs, respectively, for TE conversion [14−16].

Many studies have focused on the optimization of the TE performance of Sb2Te3 (Bi2Te3) films [17]; however, reports on their moisture permeability are lacking. As shown in Fig. 1(a), the quintuple atomic layers are arranged in a-Te1–Sb(Bi)–Te2–Sb(Bi)–Te1- manner in the cell structure of Sb2Te3 (Bi2Te3). The Sb(Bi) and Te atoms are combined by covalent/ionic bonds, while the –Te1–Te1– bonds between the quintuple layers are van der Waals forces. However, the dense inorganic structure of these materials undoubtedly results in ultralow moisture permeability. In real-world scenarios, insufficient moisture permeability in a film may subject individuals to prolonged warm and humid conditions, creating an environment conducive to bacterial proliferation and potentially leading to skin lesions. The fundamental role of healthy human skin involves regulating the equilibrium of heat and moisture [18,19]. When perspiration fails to be promptly and comprehensively expelled from the skin, an accumulation of moisture occurs on the skin surface and beneath the covering, eliciting discomfort characterized by sensations of heat and humidity [20]. The variation in material moisture permeability significantly impacts the tactile experience of human skin. Employing breathable electronic materials is imperative for wearable electronics to conduct prolonged health assessments, a critical component in the realms of disease prevention, detection, and treatment. Therefore, the rapid development of a Bi2Te3 (Sb2Te3) film characterized by outstanding moisture permeability is imperative for its practical utilization within the domain of wearable electronics.

The design of materials featuring commendable moisture permeability necessitates concurrent consideration of their electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding performance. In recent years, there has been growing concern regarding pollution induced by electromagnetic waves [21]. The presence of electromagnetic waves not only has detrimental effects on electronic systems but also poses a discernible threat to human health [22,23]. Carbon fibers (CFs), produced through stabilization and carbonization processes utilizing polyacrylonitrile (PAN) as a precursor [24,25], have garnered considerable interest. This heightened attention is attributed to their notable attributes, including commendable moisture permeability, exceptional electrical conductivity, and proficient EMI shielding performance [26−29]. Nevertheless, the TE performance of CFs is hindered by their notably low S*, which is measured at 9.4 μV∙K−1. Recognizing the exceptional TE performance, especially high *S*, exhibited by Sb2Te3 (Bi2Te3), the prospect of coating CFs with Sb2Te3 and Bi2Te3 has become a promising avenue. This approach is anticipated to yield a composite film endowed with improved TE properties, augmented moisture permeability, and enhanced EMI shielding performance.

Coating CFs with Sb2Te3 and Bi2Te3 demands a meticulous choice of coating method. Various methods for coating TE materials have been explored to date, including magnetron sputtering (MS) [30−32], thermal evaporation (EVP) [33−35], pulsed laser deposition (PLD) [36−38], chemical vapor deposition (CVD) [39], molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) [40], and others. Wei *et al*. [41] synthesized Sb2Te3/Te films on polyimide (PI) using direct current MS, radio frequency MS, and directional thermal diffusion. Despite the promising outcomes, the cost associated with employing these three methods is notably high. Teresi *et al*. [42] prepared Sb2Te3 thin films using MS, and although the TE properties are commendable, the thickness is only 15 nm. Such thinness is insufficient to yield high power output. The constraint of limited film thickness is also evident in alternative methods, including PLD, CVD, and MBE. Electrodeposition (ED) has emerged as a cost-effective and straightforward method for TE coating, offering the advantage of achieving substantial coating thickness [43]. The chemical raw materials utilized in ED are typically oxides or salts, which are more economically viable. Additionally, ED can be conducted directly in ambient air, eliminating the need for high vacuum or elevated temperatures [44]. It can also be seamlessly integrated with complementary metal‒oxide‒semiconductor (CMOS) technology, facilitating the fabrication of high-power-density TE generators or coolers. Furthermore, the ED process is governed by the electric field, which confines electrochemical reactions to conductive regions and enables selective deposition in specific patterned areas [45]. Therefore, it is feasible to electrodeposit TE materials onto conductive CFs to obtain thick films.

In this study, Sb2Te3 and Bi2Te3 were electrodeposited onto CFs with the aim of enhancing the TE properties, increasing the moisture permeability, and improving the EMI shielding performance. ED, a wet process, offers the advantage of electrolyte penetration into the interstices among CFs. Consequently, ED can be conducted not only on the surface of CFs but also within their pores. The influence of the deposition potential on the microstructure and performance of the composite films was systematically investigated. The comprehensive analysis covers the preparation methodology, microstructures, and various performance aspects, including the TE conversion efficiency, moisture permeability, EMI shielding effectiveness, and sensing capabilities.

### 2.1 Materials synthesis and TE generator fabrication

Sb2Te3 aqueous electrolytes were prepared by dissolving antimony oxide (Sb2O3, Aladdin, 99.99%), tellurium oxide (TeO2, Aladdin, 99.99%), L-tartaric acid (C4H6O6, Meryer, > 99%), and nitric acid (HNO3, Sinopharm, 65.0%–68.0%) in deionized water. The final electrolyte contained 3.2 mM  HTeO2+, 1.2 mM SbO+, and 1 M HNO3. The aqueous electrolyte Bi2Te3 was prepared by dissolving tellurium oxide (TeO2, Aladdin, 99.99%), bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (Bi(NO3)3·5H2O, Aladdin, > 99%), and nitric acid (HNO3, Sinopharm, 65.0%–68.0%) in deionized water. The final electrolyte contained 8 mM Bi3+, 10 mM HTeO2+, and 1 M HNO3. The reduction rate is predominantly dependent on the ion mobility, which is inversely proportional to the ion diameter. Considering that the radius of Te ions is smaller than that of Bi ions, Te ions are presumed to exhibit faster mobility than Bi ions within the electrolyte. Consequently, the designated concentration ratio diverges from the stoichiometric ratio. CF (0.8 cm × 2 cm) converted by carbonization of PAN [46] was sequentially rinsed with ethanol and deionized water and used as the working electrode. A platinum sheet (2.5 cm × 2.5 cm) was used as the counter electrode (CE), and saturated KCl was used as a reference electrode (RE). ED of Sb2Te3 and Bi2Te3 was carried out by pulse ED under stirring using an electrochemical workstation (CHI660E, Shanghai Chenhua Instruments). The CF/Sb2Te3 and CF/Bi2Te3 films were washed with ethanol and deionized water to remove the residual reagent on the surface and then dried at 50 °C for 1 h. The four film legs with a size of 8 mm × 20 mm × 0.205 mm with the best TE performance were selected and pasted on the gauze as the TE legs, which were electrically connected using silver paste (Ag, 3073, Shenzhen Sinwe New Materials). Two Ag wires are drawn from the TE legs at both ends of the thermoelectric generator (TEG) as electrodes. Finally, TEG is packaged with a layer of gauze.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The composite films were characterized by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; SmartLab, Rigaku) with Cu Kα radiation. The microstructure was observed by a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Pharos G1, Phenom-World) and an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS). The composition and chemical bonding of the elements were analyzed by an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Escalab 250Xi, Thermo Scientific). The electrical conductivity (*σ*), mobility (*μ*), and carrier concentration (*n*) were measured by a Hall effect measurement system (CH-100, CH-Magnetoelectricity Technology) under a 400 mT magnetic field. The Seebeck coefficient (*S*) was determined for PTM-3 (Jiayitong Technology, Joule Yacht). A vector network analyzer (VNA; Aligent) is used to evaluate the electromagnetic shielding performance in the frequency range of the X-band (8.2–12.4 GHz).

### 3.1 Synthesis, phases, and microstructures

As shown in Fig. 1(b), CF utilized as a WE is composed of numerous individual fibers. It has a thickness of approximately 185 μm, a low density of approximately 0.44 g∙cm−3, and an impressively high porosity reaching up to 78%, which is beneficial for achieving a composite film with moisture permeability after pulsed ED of Sb2Te3 and Bi2Te3 by a conventional three-electrode system. As shown in Figs. 1(c) and 1(d), the optimal deposition parameters were determined by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) before ED at a scanning speed of 10 mV∙s−1. ### 3.2 Thermoelectric performance

As shown in Fig. 4(a), *n* of the pristine CF is measured to be 2.6×1020 cm−3. Upon ED of Sb2Te3, there is an initial sharp decrease in *n*. This initial decrease can be attributed to the intrinsically lower electron concentration of Sb2Te3 (5.8×1018 cm−3) [43], resulting in an overall decrease in *n* due to a weighted effect. Subsequently, a gradual decrease in *n* is observed, which correlates with the negative shift in the deposition potential. The primary factor underlying this phenomenon is the greater negative deposition potential of Sb than that of Te. The negative shift in potential serves to facilitate the deposition of Sb, fostering enhanced bonding with Te to yield Sb2Te3. This results in a stoichiometric ratio closer to 2 : 3, consequently reducing the vacancy concentration. As a direct consequence, there is a subsequent decrease in *n*. At a deposition potential of −0.18 V, *n* for CF/Sb2Te3 is measured to be 3.1×1019 cm−3, representing an eightfold reduction. PFs achieved in the films electrodeposited using the pulse method in this study surpass those obtained through potentiostatic ED, as reported in prior work [45]. The advantage of pulse electrodeposition over potentiostatic ED lies in the application of a constant potential for a specific duration, effectively compensating for the loss of metal ions from the electrode surface. As shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM, during the deposition (ON) phase, the concentration of metal ions decreases gradually on the electrode surface. Conversely, in the non-depositional (OFF) phase, metal ions are replenished within the diffusion layer. This cyclic process facilitates the formation of a uniform and densely packed TE layer, thereby enhancing the power factor of the composite film. By varying the deposition charge, composite films of different thicknesses were prepared. As shown in Fig. S5(a)–S5(c) in the ESM. As the deposition charge increases, the film thickness increases due to the thickening of the shell. *S* increases with film thickness due to the mixing rule and reaches a plateau with further deposition. *σ* initially increases significantly with thickness, as the formation of a cross-linked core−shell structure promotes carrier migration. Drawing upon the TE properties of the film, its potential application extends to testing temperature sensing capabilities through a self-powering mode. This renders it suitable for various applications, including touch sensing. As illustrated in Fig. S6(a) in the ESM, the output voltage (∆*V*) demonstrated a robust linear correlation with the temperature differential (∆*T*) across the two ends of the film leg. The calculation of ∆*V* can be performed utilizing Eq. (10):

(10) ΔV=ΔTS=ΔT∑i=1nSi

The touch sensor, composed of multiple legs, exhibits a noteworthy response when touched with an increasing number of fingers, with each finger corresponding to one leg. This results in an augmentation of the effective *S* in Eq. (10), consequently leading to an amplified ∆*V*. The determination of the effective *S* is contingent upon both the number of TE legs being contacted and their intrinsic *S**i*. The touch sensing functionality is thus achieved by modulating the effective *S*, thereby manifesting discernible signals in the form of ∆*V*.

Figure 5(d) shows a schematic diagram of the self-powered touching sensor device being touched by varying numbers of fingers. Leveraging its precise temperature recognition capability, we engineered a self-powered touch sensor module. This module has the capacity to directly convert the voltage signal into specific language outputs. In Figs. 5(e) and 5(f), alterations in the touch hand index lead to varying output voltages. Notably, when a finger makes contact, there is a substantial increase in the voltage signal of the sensor, denoted as the letter "D". As illustrated in Fig. S6(b) in the ESM, the temperature sensor exhibits a remarkably rapid response, requiring only approximately 0.7 s. Building upon this foundation, the touches with two, three, and four fingers are designated as the letters "G", "L", and "O", respectively. By exploiting these symbols, creative arrangements can be made. For instance, touching the sensor successively with two fingers, four fingers, three fingers, and finally one finger corresponds to the word "gold". As depicted in Fig. S7 in the ESM, various combinations allow the formation of words such as "go", "old", and "good". The cycle stability of the touch sensor was evaluated over a period of 300 s. As depicted in Fig. S8(b) in the ESM, the wave fluctuations observed are within acceptable limits, indicating that the stability of the sensor is satisfactory. This self-powered touch sensor design holds promise for assisting individuals with language barriers in the future.

Modulating ∆*T* in Eq. (10) while maintaining a constant effective *S* results in a corresponding variation in ∆*V.* Based on this, as illustrated in Fig. 5(g) and Fig. S8(c) in the ESM, we devise a TE respiration sensor. Positioned inside the mask, one side of the sensor serves as the hot end, coming into contact with warm exhaled air from the human body. The other end, situated outside the mask, functions as the cold side and is exposed to ambient air. Throughout the breathing process, the warm exhaled air acts as a heat source, generating a temperature difference between the two sides and consequently producing a voltage signal. The respiratory rate (RR) is a critical metric for assessing an individual's metabolic status. During rest, the body typically sustains a consistent RR. However, as metabolic demands increase, so does RR, reflecting the body's need for increased oxygen intake and carbon dioxide removal. This relationship underscores the role of RR in evaluating cardiopulmonary function. Elevated RRs often indicate abnormal cardiopulmonary function or physiological stress, while reduced RRs may indicate cardiovascular disorders or other underlying health concerns. Thus, RR monitoring provides valuable insights into an individual's overall health and well-being. As depicted in Fig. 5(h), the use of respiratory sensors enables the monitoring of changes in RR across various activities, such as standing, squatting, and sitting down. Different activity states directly impact cardiovascular and lung conditions, consequently influencing RR and resulting in distinct output voltage frequencies [69]. In the standing position, the RR is lower, approximately 25.3 breaths per minute (bpm), corresponding to a period of *T* = 2.37 s. Transitioning to motion, RR increases to 27.0 bpm (*T* = 2.22 s). Upon concluding the exercise and adopting a seated position, RR decreases once again to 25.9 bpm (*T* = 2.31 s), falling between the values observed in the standing and exercise states. Different exercise intensities result in varied RRs, and this variation can be attributed to the vertical pressure gradient between the heart and the head in different postures [69]. Conclusively, a stability test of the voltage output from the respiratory sensor is conducted during 300 s of normal breathing. As illustrated in Fig. S8(d) in the ESM, both the RR and voltage amplitude consistently maintain stable values throughout the test duration. This steadfast behavior indicates the excellent stability of the respiratory sensor.

### 3.5 Moisture permeability

To ensure the comfort of the material when it is in contact with human skin, it is imperative for the material to possess a certain moisture permeability. This quality guarantees that sweat can efficiently pass through the composite membrane. In cases where the moisture permeability of the material is inadequate, there is a risk that the material may block the sweat gland ducts, potentially leading to skin inflammation or irritation. Therefore, optimizing moisture permeability is crucial for maintaining skin health and comfort. The moisture permeability of wearable textiles is assessed through water vapor permeability (WVP) [70]. Traditionally, the ED method for preparing Bi2Te3 and Sb2Te3 involves the use of conductive materials such as gold-plated Si wafers or stainless steel as substrates. However, these materials lack self-supporting properties, necessitating the use of materials such as PI or epoxy resin to detach Bi2Te3 and Sb2Te3. Unfortunately, PI and epoxy resin have minimal pores, resulting in materials with low moisture permeability. This limitation underscores the importance of exploring alternative methods or materials to enhance the moisture permeability of Bi2Te3- and Sb2Te3-based materials for wearable textile applications.

Notably, following ED of Sb2Te3 (Bi2Te3) onto CFs, a uniform wrapping of Sb2Te3 (Bi2Te3) around CFs occurs, forming a cross-linked core–shell structure. The morphology of the CF skeleton remains clearly visible, and a significant number of voids persist. This structural arrangement ensures that when the composite film is in contact with human skin, it maintains good moisture permeability. As illustrated in Fig. 6(a), to assess the stability of moisture permeability, the evaporation of water vapor is monitored over different time periods. The results reveal notable changes in CF/Sb2Te3 and CF/Bi2Te3, while PI exhibits almost no moisture permeability. This finding underscores the effectiveness of the designed composite films in providing a stable and moisture-permeable interface when in contact with the skin. As shown in Fig. 6(b), WVP of the pure CF was 3289 g∙m−2∙d−1. After ED, WVP is only marginally reduced by approximately 5%. Specifically, the WVP values for CF/Sb2Te3 and CF/Bi2Te3 were 3116 and 3190 g∙m−2∙d−1, respectively. This marginal reduction in WVP after ED of the TE materials is attributed to the increase in the diameter of CFs during the ED process. Consequently, there is a slight decrease in the void sizes around CFs. According to Fig. S5(d) in the ESM, as the deposition charge increases, the diameter of the individual fibers increases, and the size of the surrounding pores decreases, leading to a gradual decrease in moisture permeability.

### 3.6 Electromagnetic shielding performance

In this work, the electromagnetic shielding performance of CF/Sb2Te3 is investigated using the waveguide method. Figure 7(a) compares the total shielding effectiveness (SET) between CF and CF/Sb2Te3 films across the X-band. SET is the sum of the reflection (SER), absorption (SEA), and internal multiple reflection loss (SEM) of the shielding material. When SET surpasses 15 dB, SEM can be disregarded, and SET can be calculated by SET = SER + SEA. SET for the pure CF film consistently exceeds 70 dB across the entire frequency spectrum tested. The incorporation of Sb2Te3 and Bi2Te3 notably enhances EMI SE of the films. Specifically, the SET value for CF/Sb2Te3 (−0.18 V) peaks at 82 dB, with values consistently exceeding 78 dB throughout the entire X-band. Notably, CF/Bi2Te3 achieves the highest SET value at 0 V, reaching an impressive 93 dB and maintaining SET values consistently above 88 dB across the entire X-band. In Fig. 7(b), the average values of SER, SEA, and SET for each sample are presented. Given the dependency of EMI SE on sample thickness, a comparative analysis is conducted with reported data from other studies [71–80]. Figure 7(c) illustrates that EMI SEs of CF/Sb2Te3 and CF/Bi2Te3 surpass those of Sb2Te3 nanosheets and other materials, underscoring the superior shielding performance of the composite films. Figure S5(e) in the ESM illustrates that with increasing thickness, more Sb2Te3 (Bi2Te3) grains form an effective conductive network, thereby enhancing the reflection and absorption capacity of electromagnetic waves and improving EMI SE. However, excessively thick coatings can reduce the internal multireflection of electromagnetic waves, thus limiting further decreases in EMI SE. The radar chart of the TE properties, moisture permeability, and electromagnetic shielding efficiency (Fig. S5(f) in the ESM) indicates that the film with a thickness of 205 μm exhibits the best overall performance.

To ascertain the primary shielding mechanism, we conducted a comparison based on the SEA/SET ratio. As illustrated in Fig. 7(d), for the deposition of Sb2Te3 (Bi2Te3) onto CF, the SEA/SET ratio exhibited no marked change compared to that of pure CF. However, the absolute SEA increased (Fig. 7(b)). In Fig. 7(e), alterations in skin depth relative to that of the pure CF are depicted for various samples. Due to the superior conductivity of CF/Bi2Te3 over CF/Sb2Te3, the skin depth (*δ*) is smaller. Figure 7(f) presents the power coefficient (*A*), reflectance (*R*), and transmittance (*T*) for diverse samples within the 8.2–12.4 GHz range. Notably, CF/Bi2Te3 has a reflection coefficient as high as 0.986, an exceptionally low absorption coefficient of 0.014, and a nearly negligible transmission coefficient. These findings indicate an exceptionally low transmittance of electron beams through the material, underscoring its commendable shielding performance. In Fig. 7(g), we utilized an electromagnetic wave detector to visually assess the electromagnetic shielding efficacy of the composite film. The proximity of the Bluetooth headset to the electromagnetic wave detector resulted in a robust signal. Conversely, when the film was positioned adjacent to the detector, an immediate loss of signal occurred. This observation signifies the outstanding electromagnetic wave shielding functionality of the film.

Figure 7(h) provides a detailed illustration of the electron beam transmission path in the CF/Sb2Te3 films. Due to the impedance mismatch between free space and the prepared sample, some incident electrons in the electromagnetic wave on the surface of the film are immediately reflected. This phenomenon is primarily a result of synergistic effects among mechanisms, including transmission loss, multiple reflections, and polarization between the CF column and the Sb2Te3 (Bi2Te3) layer. First, the conductive CF serves as a structural skeleton, facilitating electron migration and ensuring efficient electron transport. As the electromagnetic wave is transmitted to the absorber, electrons can migrate along the CF column or jump to the adjacent Sb2Te3 (Bi2Te3) crystal, traversing the defect/interface between the CF and Sb2Te3 (Bi2Te3) crystal. Both CFs and Sb2Te3 (Bi2Te3) can generate an induced current in an alternating electromagnetic field, resulting in a certain dielectric loss and a significant attenuation of the incident electron beam energy. Second, the cross-linked core–shell structure contributes to the formation of a 3D conductive network, enabling multiple reflections and significantly prolonging the propagation path of electromagnetic waves. Finally, as the electromagnetic wave passes through CF, it interacts with the pores within CF, leading to the absorption of the energy carried by the electromagnetic wave. The existence of distinct phases of CF, Sb2Te3, and Bi2Te3 introduces variations in the electrical conductivity and dielectric properties. Additionally, the induced Maxwell–Wagner–Sillers (MWS) interface polarization enhances the absorption performance of the electron beam [81]. This complex interplay of material phases and interface polarization contributes to the overall efficiency of energy absorption in the films.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, we fabricated a cross-linked core–shell structure through the electrodeposition of Sb2Te3 (Bi2Te3) onto CFs, with the TE materials forming a tightly wrapped shell around the core (individual fibers). The establishment of this cross-linked core–shell architecture has yielded significant improvements in the TE performance compared to that of pure carbon fibers. Specifically, the carrier mobility is enhanced, accompanied by a reduction in carrier concentration. This structural modification also led to a substantial increase in the Seebeck coefficient, resulting in a remarkable 300-fold increase in the power factor. Furthermore, the composite film exhibits exceptional electromagnetic interference shielding efficiency and moisture permeability, contributing to its suitability for human health protection. The versatility of this material enables its application in various scenarios, such as serving as a touch sensor to aid individuals with language barriers or functioning as a respiratory sensor for health monitoring purposes. This work also provides an avenue for the fabrication of thick TE films by coating carbon fibers with inorganic TE materials, including but not limited to CoSb3, PbTe, and SiGe, for medium- to high-temperature TE conversion.
