## 1 Introduction

Electromagnetic wave (EMW)-absorbing materials have attracted much attention recently due to the growing threat that electromagnetic pollution poses to human health, the normal operation of electronic equipment, and the transfer of confidential information [1−3]. Therefore, the development of thin, lightweight EMW-absorbing materials with broad absorption bandwidths, strong absorption, and structure‒function integration has emerged as a prominent and active research area [4,5].

SiC-based absorbing materials are potential candidates for EMW absorption due to their tunable permittivity [6], high temperature resistance [7], and superior chemical stability [8]. Current studies on SiC-based EMW absorption materials include SiCnw/C foam [9], porous SiC–Si3N4 [10], and SiCf/Si3N4–SiOC–Si3N4 [11]. These studies have demonstrated the potential for developing EMW-absorbing materials with excellent properties. However, manipulating the EMW absorption properties of materials only at the microscopic scale has limitations, such as complex preparation processes and difficulties in regulating broadband strong absorption, which restrict their future applicability. It is common to tailor the impedance matching and electromagnetic parameters by regulating the macrostructure of composites for structural EMW-absorbing materials, which has emerged as the major research trend for modern stealth materials in recent years [12,13]. However, it is challenging to fabricate complex structures using traditional processes [14−16]. The development of 3D printing technology has made it possible to prepare arbitrarily complicated structures and provides a new perspective for EMW-absorbing metamaterials. In particular, DLP 3D printing has been widely used in the fabrication of EMW-absorbing materials due to its comparable advantages, including high dimensional precision and the flexibility of macro-microstructure design [2,17−20]. Mei *et al*. [21] designed and fabricated a porous Al2O3/SiCw composite with oblique honeycomb structures based on 3D printing technology, and the minimum reflection loss was −63.65 dB at 9.8 GHz with a thickness of 3.5 mm. Feng *et al*. [22] prepared SiOC components by 3D printing with an optimized log-pile structure. The reflection loss was approximately −8 dB in the X band. Yao *et al*. [23] developed SiOC-based EMW-absorbing materials with a gyroid structure using 3D printing. These materials had an effective absorbing bandwidth of 6.11 GHz in the X-Ku band, with a minimum reflection loss of −58.05 dB.

Unfortunately, the abovementioned EMW absorption materials suffer from poor mechanical properties for practical applications. Generally, the high porosity deteriorates the mechanical properties, which hinders their application. Mullite–SiC composites have attracted a lot of interest not only for their superior impedance matching and dielectric characteristics due to the good synergistic relationship between mullite and SiC but also for their superior mechanical properties, high melting point, oxidation resistance, and good chemical compatibility [24−26]. Current studies on EMW-absorbing materials include SiCf/mullite [27], SiCN/mullite [28], SiCf/mullite–SiO2 [29], and porous mullite–Al2O3–SiC [30]. The introduction of the mullite phase is expected to effectively enhance the mechanical and EMW absorption properties of SiC-based materials. However, there are no reports on the design of mullite-SiC metastructures.

The EMW properties of materials and macro-structures are two important influences on EMW devices. In this work, a novel mullite anti-gyroid/SiC gyroid metastructure was designed and prepared to improve its EMW absorption and mechanical properties (Fig. 1) by systemically combining the principle properties of materials and macro-structural design. Porous SiC composites with gyroid structures are fabricated through DLP 3D printing and sintering. Subsequently, the mullite anti-gyroid/SiC gyroid composites with an anti-gyroid/gyroid metastructure are obtained by the PIP method, demonstrating a significant increase in the mechanical properties compared to those of porous mullite/SiC composites. The dielectric properties of the samples are realized by the gyroid structure design, and the mullite phase content changes with structural units, which improves the dielectric properties and impedance matching characteristics of the materials.

### 2.1 Design parameters of TPMS architectures

As a complex surface, the TPMS architecture cannot be modeled directly. The gyroid structure, a type of TPMS structure that exhibits more remarkable mechanical properties than others, was first proposed by Luzzati in 1967 [31−33]. To enable the design and parameterization of the gyroid structure, the structure can be described by Eq. (1) [34−36]:

(1) cos⁡(2πax)sin⁡(2πay)+cos⁡(2πay)sin⁡(2πaz)+cos⁡(2πaz)sin⁡(2πax)=c

where *a* is the unit cell length; *c* controls the three-dimensional expansion of the surface; *x*, *y*, and *z* are the independent variables of the model along the *x*, *y*, and *z* axes, respectively.

The design principle is based on the relationship between the structural unit size and the quarter-wavelength limit, and the material exhibits the best EMW-absorbing properties when the structural unit size is an odd multiple of the wavelength [37,38]. The gyroid structure models based on the electromagnetic parameters of SiC and applications in the X-band were created using the n-topology and magics software, with three samples of different parameters, as shown in Figs. 2(a)–2(c).

### 2.2 Preparation of SiC-based photosensitive slurry

To ensure printability and dimensional precision in the DLP, the rheological behavior and curing capability of the photosensitive slurry are considered to be the fundamental properties [39]. First, the photosensitive resin was obtained by mixing 1,6-hexanediol diacrylate (HDDA, molecular weight: 226, purity: 90%) and aliphatic polyurethane acrylates (code: 2121, functionality = 2) in a ratio of 3 : 7 (vol%). The slurry was obtained by mixing 88.83 g of silicon carbide powder (SiC, median particle size (*D*50) ≈ 30 μm), 33.13 g of silicon dioxide powder (SiO2, *D*50 ≈ 3 μm), 11.90 g of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA, *D*50 ≈ 10 μm), 3.99 g of aluminum oxide (Al2O3, *D*50 ≈ 1.2 μm), and 1.12 g of phenylbis (2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphine oxide (BAPO, purity: 97%) into 50 mL of solvent photosensitive resin. Finally, a homogeneous slurry is obtained by vacuum mixing and defoaming treatment.

### 2.3 Preparation of mosaic mullite–mullite/SiC structure

The green body was obtained by digital light processing (DLP; wavelength = 405 nm) 3D printing, where the layer thickness of the model was set to 50 μm and the exposure power was 40 mJ/cm2. After that, the green body was debonded at 900 °C for 0.5 h in a vacuum atmosphere, and after phenolic infiltration, it was sintered in a tube furnace under an Ar atmosphere for 4 h to obtain porous mullite/SiC. The detailed process flow has been described in our previous study [40,41]. Then, the samples were infiltrated in a mixture of Al-sol (Al2O3 = 22±1 wt%) and Si-sol (SiO2 = 30±1 wt%) solutions (at a mass ratio of 3.82 : 1). After infiltration, the samples were dried at 150 °C for 3 h, heated to 1000 °C and held for 2 h in Ar. After 10 precursor infiltration and pyrolysis (PIP) cycles, the final sample was obtained. Figure 3 shows a schematic of the preparation route for the mullite–mullite/SiC composites.

### 2.4 Characterizations

The phase composition of the composites was analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany). The microstructure and micromorphology were characterized by a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Themis Z, FEI, USA) and a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU8220, Hitachi, Japan) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The density (*ρ*0) and porosity of the composites were measured using Archimedes’ method, and the pore size distribution was examined with the mercury intrusion method (Poremaster 60, Anton Paar, USA). The flexural properties were evaluated using a universal material testing machine (Instron-5566, Instron, UK) with a loading speed of 0.5 mm/min, and the specimen size was 3 mm × 4 mm × 36 mm. The compressive strength of the structures was characterized using a universal testing machine (Instron-5566, Instron, UK) at room temperature with a crosshead speed of 0.1 mm/min, and the specimen size was 15.0 mm × 15.0 mm × 17.0 mm. The electromagnetic parameters of the samples with dimensions of 22.86 mm × 10.16 mm × (3.00–4.00) mm rectangular bars with different thicknesses in the X-band (8.2–12.4 GHz) were tested by a vector network analyzer (VNA; N5234B, Agilent, USA).

### 3.1 Structure and morphology analysis

At high temperatures, the main reactions of SiO2, C, and Al2O3 in the system are Reactions (2)–(6) [42]:

(2) SiO2(s)+C(s)=SiO(g)+CO(g)

(3) SiO(g)+2C(s)=SiC(s)+CO(g)

(4) SiO(g)+3CO(g)=SiC(s)+2CO2(g)

(5) 2SiO2(s)+3Al2O3(s)=3Al2O3⋅2SiO2(s)

(6) 3Al2O3⋅2SiO2(s)+4SiO2(s)+18C(s)=3A12O3(s)+6SiC(s)+12CO(g)

In this case, Reactions (2)–(4) are carbothermal reduction reactions; Reaction (5) is the Mullite phase generation process, and Reaction (6) is the Mullite phase decomposition process.

Based on the above reaction mechanisms, Fig. 4(a) shows the XRD patterns of the samples sintered at various temperatures. As shown in Fig. 4(a), diffraction peaks of SiO2 and Al2O3 were apparent at 21.95°, 25.59°, and 28.50° at a sintering temperature of 1400 °C, while the microstructure results were similar to those shown in Fig. 4(d), indicating that the reaction did not occur at this temperature. As the temperature increased to 1500 °C, the mullite diffraction peaks at 26.02° and 26.31° were observed, while the SiO2 and Al2O3 peaks disappeared. This suggests that the reaction is conducted more adequately at this temperature, and the samples are primarily crystalline SiC and mullite, as shown in Fig. 4(e). When the sintering temperature increased to 1600 °C, the diffraction peak of Al2O3 appeared at 25.59°, indicating that increasing the temperature promoted the decomposition of the mullite phase and the formation of the alumina phase [30], as shown in Fig. 4(f). The densities, porosities, and bending strengths of S1400–S1600 are shown in Figs. 4(b) and 4(c). It is obvious that as the sintering temperature increased, the density of the samples decreased from 1.55 to 1.19 g/cm3, and the porosity increased from 33.49% to 62.56%. This phenomenon arises from the reaction behavior at different temperatures. At 1400 °C, the reaction does not occur, resulting in low material porosity. However, as the temperature increases to 1500 °C, Reactions (2)–(5) occur, leading to an increase in porosity. Notably, as the reaction temperature rises to 1600 °C, the higher reaction temperature increases the partial pressure of SiO gas, causing an overflow of SiO, CO, and other gases within the system, which in turn, in the course of the reaction, results in the formation of more pores [43]. Due to its low porosity, S1400 has a flexural strength of 16.61±0.51 MPa. At 1500 °C, the density decreased to 1.43 g/cm3, and the porosity increased significantly to 53.70%. Due to the generation of the mullite phase, the flexural strength decreased slightly to 13.28±1.15 MPa. Furthermore, when the reaction temperature increased to 1600 °C, the density decreased to 1.19 g/cm3, and the corresponding porosity increased to 62.56%. This suggests that a higher reaction temperature increases the SiO gas partial pressure, which in turn causes more pores to form during carbothermal reduction. Additionally, the mullite phase decomposition caused by the higher reaction temperature significantly reduced the flexural strength to 2.75±0.46 MPa. Therefore, S1500 exhibited the best comprehensive properties.

From the XRD patterns in Fig. 5(a), it is evident that the samples G1–G3 all showed clear mullite and SiC peaks after 10 PIP cycles. After normalization, the relative intensity of the mullite peak is the highest in G3, indicating that there was more mullite in the sample. TEM was carried out to characterize the microstructures of the mullite/SiC composites, as shown in Figs. 5(b) and 5(c). The SiC-mullite heterostructure is shown in Fig. 5(c), where lattice spacings of 0.25 nm and 0.34 nm corresponded to SiC and mullite, respectively. In addition, Fig. 5(c) shows that the interfacial bonding between mullite and SiC is tight, and there is no transition layer, which is favorable for the migration and accumulation of electrons at the heterogeneous interface under the action of an electromagnetic field to form an interfacial dipole, thus dissipating electromagnetic waves [44]. The microscopic morphology of the mullite anti-gyroid/SiC gyroid ceramic metastructure is shown in Figs. 5(e)–5(g). As shown in Fig. 5(e), the structure of the sample exhibits a size of approximately 2.5 mm, indicating the effectiveness of the method in achieving such metastructures. Figures 5(f) and 5(g) show enlarged images of Fig. 5(e), illustrating the morphology of the samples after the PIP process. ### 3.3 EMW absorption performance

In general, the electromagnetic absorption properties of a material can be evaluated by reflection loss (RL) and effective absorption bandwidth. Based on transmission line theory and metal-back-plate theory, RL can be calculated by Eqs. (7) and (8) [25,50]:

(7) RL(dB)=20log|Zin−Z0Zin+Z0|

(8) Zin=Z0μrεrtanh[j(2πfdc)μrεr]

where *Z*in, *Z*0, *μ*r, *ε*r, *f*, *d*, and *c* refer to the input impedance, impedance of the free space, relative complex permeability, relative complex permittivity, frequency, thickness, and velocity of light, respectively. Generally, when RL is less than −10 dB, more than 90% of the electromagnetic wave can be effectively absorbed, and the corresponding frequency range is called the effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) [51].

The RL plots of the various samples are shown in Figs. 7(a)–7(h). Figures 7(a) and 7(b) show that for S1500, the RLmin value is −16 dB at a thickness of 2.10 mm. The EMW absorption performance of the samples varies with alterations in metastructural design. For G1, the absorption properties are not enhanced over those of S1500 because of the tiny structure, which limits the multiple reflections of the mullite transmissive phase induced by the PIP process, resulting in RLmin = −11 dB at a thickness of 1.6 mm. RLmin for G2 is −27 dB at a thickness of 2.4 mm, and the EAB is 3.2 GHz at a thickness of 2.0 mm. For G3, RLmin is −54 dB at a thickness of 1.90 mm, and the EAB is 3.2 GHz at a thickness of 2.2 mm. These results suggest that the mullite mosaic in the gyroid structure can enhance the EMW absorption characteristics of the samples, and the reflection loss increases with increasing unit size in the gyroid structure. In conclusion, structural mullite/SiC composites directly affect their EMW absorption capacity by changing the macroscopic structure of the composites. Overall, G3 exhibits the best EMW absorption characteristics.

The electromagnetic parameters and the EMW absorption performance of the materials exhibit a significant correlation. The waveguide method was employed to investigate the dielectric characteristics to further analyze the electromagnetic absorption mechanism. The real part (ε*′) and imaginary part (*ε*″) of the complex permittivity and the dielectric loss tangent (tan*δ*) for various samples are displayed in Figs. 8(a)–8(c). SiC and mullite are nonmagnetic materials, and the real part (*μ*′) and imaginary part (*μ*″) of the permeability are 1 and 0, respectively. S1500 exhibited a relatively low *ε*′ due to its high porosity. In contrast, the average *ε*′ values of G1–G3 are 17, 14, and 12, respectively. The relatively high content of SiC in S1500 results in an increase in dielectric loss, leading to a comparatively high imaginary part of the dielectric constant and, consequently, a larger dielectric loss tangent. The variation observed in G1–G3 is associated with the design of the gyroid structural unit, with the imaginary parts of the dielectric constant and dielectric loss tangent measuring 10, 5, and 4 and 0.58, 0.38, and 0.36, respectively. where *V**i* and *ε**i* represent the volume and dielectric constant of the *i* phase (*i* denotes SiC, mullite, SiO2, Al2O3, and pores), respectively. Consequently, the dielectric constant of composites is intricately linked to the content of each phase. In the case of the G1 structure, an increase in the SiC content resulted in an increase in the dielectric loss. With an increase in the structural unit size, the mullite phase also increases, resulting in a noticeable improvement in the impedance matching characteristics. Simultaneously, there is a decrease in the complex dielectric constant.

To further explain the dielectric loss mechanism of the material, Cole‒Cole curves for the composites can be drawn according to Eq. (10) [56,57]:

(10) (ε′−εs+ε∞2)2+(ε″)2=(εs−ε∞2)2

where *ε*s and *ε*∞ refer to the static permittivity and the relative permittivity, respectively. When the plot of *ε*′ vs. *ε*″ is a semicircle, it indicates that there is polarization in the EMW absorption process.

For dielectric materials, the real and imaginary parts of the dielectric constant can be expressed according to Debye theory (Eqs. (11) and (12)) [58]:

(11) ϵ′=ε∞+εs−ϵ∞1+ω2τ2

(12) ε″=εs−ε∞1+ω2τ2ωτ+σωε0

The value of *ε*′ depends on the polarization loss, while the value of *ε*″ depends on the polarization loss and the conductivity loss. Equations (10)–(12) reveal that each semicircle within the Cole‒Cole curve represents the relaxation polarization loss, indicating that EMW dissipation occurs due to relaxation polarization loss during this phase. Conversely, the straight-line segment corresponds to the conduction loss process. The transition of the Cole–Cole curve from a straight line segment to a semicircle signifies the onset of a relaxation polarization process [44,59].

The Cole‒Cole curve indicates the presence of a Cole‒Cole semicircle for S1500, indicating polarization during the EMW absorption process that is related to stacking defects in the SiC. Furthermore, it is noticeable that the curve extends toward the upper right like a “tail”, which indicates that the conductivity loss formed by the three-dimensional network of SiCnw plays an important role in EMW attenuation [55,60]. In contrast, the Cole‒Cole curves in G1‒G3 exhibit increasing semicircles. This phenomenon is attributed to the introduction of more mullite phases, which, in turn, introduce additional lattice defects at the mullite–SiC interface. This process enhances dipole polarization and interfacial polarization, which results in increased absorption of electromagnetic waves.

In addition, the impedance matching and attenuation constant *α* are two important parameters to measure the EMW absorption performance of composites, in which *α* is given by Eq. (13) [61, 62]:

(13) α=(2πfc)⋅(μ″ε″−μ′ε′)+(μ′ε″+μ″ε′)2+(μ″ε″−μ′ε′)2

Generally, a higher *α* indicates a better ability to attenuate electromagnetic waves. This is attributed to the crucial role impedance matching plays in reflection loss. When there is a lack of impedance matching, the incident electromagnetic wave tends to reflect rather than be absorbed at the surface of the material. The EMW absorption mechanism of the mullite anti-gyroid/SiC gyroid composites is shown in Fig. 10(b). First, as a kind of EMW transparent material, mullite plays a vital role in reducing the reflection of electromagnetic waves on the surface of the material. This attribute is conducive to achieving favorable impedance-matching properties for the material [72−74]. Second, the extensive specific surface area of the gyroid structure facilitates multilevel reflection of electromagnetic waves on the surface of the material, which promotes the absorption of electromagnetic wave energy through multiple scattering paths [23,38,48,75]. Moreover, on the microscopic scale, effective electromagnetic wave attenuation is achieved through interfacial polarization induced by the three-dimensional interface and dipole polarization formed by the reaction-formed mullite phase [28,76]. Multiple loss mechanisms contribute to the efficient attenuation of electromagnetic waves.

## 4 Conclusions

In conclusion, in this work, mullite anti-gyroid/SiC gyroid metastructure composites were manufactured by DLP 3D printing combined with the PIP method. Compared with those of the porous mullite/SiC composites, the flexural strength and EMW absorption properties of the composites improved simultaneously. The EMW absorption properties of structural composites can be tailored by adjusting the gyroid structural parameters. The PIP process effectively enhanced both the EMW absorption properties and the mechanical characteristics of the composites. Mullite anti-gyroid/SiC gyroid metastructural composites had an effective absorption bandwidth of 3.2 GHz and a minimum reflection loss of −54 dB in the X band. The flexural and compressive strengths were improved by 377%–586% and 459%–1358%, respectively, compared with those of the porous mullite/SiC composites. This work demonstrated that 3D printing technology can be used to successfully prepare specially designed complex EMW-absorbing metastructures and devices with tunable EMW absorption properties, which has great potential for application in the field of preparing high-performance EMW absorption metamaterials and devices, especially in the aerospace field, such as fighter intakes, fuselage edges, and tips.
