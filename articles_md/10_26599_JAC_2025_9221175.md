## 1 Introduction

Lead-free dielectric capacitors operate via an electrostatic energy storage mechanism and have inherent advantages such as high power density and ultrafast charge and discharge rates [1−3]. These attributes give them broad application prospects in pulse power systems, new energy conversion technologies, and hybrid electric vehicles [4,5]. Furthermore, their superior structural stability and long operational lifespan confer broad potential for use under extreme conditions, such as high temperatures, intense electric fields, and wide frequency ranges [6−8]. At present, the recoverable energy density (*W*rec) and energy storage efficiency (*η*) of lead-free dielectric materials are significantly lower than those of lead-based dielectric materials. This disparity not only limits their development and practical application but also hampers the miniaturization and integration of advanced power devices [9,10]. As environmental and health concerns increasingly restrict the use of lead-based materials, improving *W*rec and *η* of lead-free dielectrics has become a critical prerequisite and foundational step toward the realization of next-generation high-performance energy storage systems.

Strontium titanate (SrTiO3) is a prototypical lead-free paraelectric material that shows considerable promise as an environmentally friendly energy storage dielectric owing to its distinctive linear-like polarization behavior, low polarization loss, and well-established fabrication processes [11]. However, achieving excellent energy storage performance remains challenging because of the relatively low breakdown strength and polarization [12−14]. In general, the energy storage capacity of a dielectric material can be quantified via the polarization–electric field (*P*–*E*) hysteresis loop [15,16], expressed as Eqs. (1) and (2):

(1) Wrec=∫PrPmE⋅dP

(2) η=WrecWrec+Wloss×100%

where *E* is the electric field, *P*r is the residual polarization, *P*m is the maximum polarization, and *W*loss is the energy loss. Notably, *W*rec of the energy storage dielectric is closely correlated with the area enclosed by the *P‒E* loop, which is proportional to the integral of the electric field (*E*) and polarization (*P*) (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). To increase the recoverable energy density and efficiency, increasing *E*b and *P*m, as well as expanding the polarization difference (Δ*P* = *P*m − *P*r), is critical.

Researchers have focused on optimizing the dielectric performance through various strategies: refining the grain size [17], engineering core‒shell structures [18], reducing the film thickness[19], modifying the fabrication and sintering conditions [20] to increase *E*b, and alternatively, utilizing defect control [21], high-entropy engineering [22], and domain engineering [23] to improve the polarization properties. However, in single dielectric materials, a negative correlation often exists between *E**b* and polarization [24], presenting a significant obstacle to increasing the energy storage density. Recently, multilayer composite architectures have emerged as promising solutions for decoupling these conflicting parameters [25], especially in dielectric thin-film capacitors [26]. By exploiting differences in intrinsic dielectric properties and regulating interface mechanisms, the advantages of each constituent layer can be optimally harnessed [27]. For example, Ma *et al.* [28] integrated SrTiO3 linear layers into (Pb,La)(Zr,Ti)O3 relaxor ferroelectric matrices, where the combined effect of interface-induced electric fields and La3+ doping synergistically increased the breakdown strength and polarization performance, achieving an energy storage density of 67.1 J/cm3 under a high electric field of 7.45 MV/cm. Similarly, Zhang *et al*. [29] fabricated multilayer films with alternating high-polarization 0.7Na0.5Bi0.5TiO3–0.3SrTiO3 and high-breakdown 0.6SrTiO3–0.4Na0.5Bi0.5TiO3 layers, adjusting the number of periodic layers and balancing the polarization with *E*b via voltage sharing, culminating in an energy density of 60 J/cm3 at a breakdown electric field of 2612 kV/cm.

Although the energy storage properties of multilayer dielectric films have been widely reported, fundamental aspects such as size dependence, the spatial distribution characteristics of functional layers, and correlations with electrical properties still lack mechanistic studies. In this work, we propose a novel design strategy for an insulator‒paraelectric heterojunction by incorporating amorphous Al2O3 (AO) insulating layers into SrTi0.99Mn0.01O3 (STM) thin films. Through systematic variation in the number and spatial arrangement of insulating layers, the influence of microstructure on energy storage performance has been studied. This work revealed that the AO/STM/AO top-bottom configuration can utilize the synergistic effect of the electrode interface potential barrier and the built-in electric field of the heterojunction to construct a full-path charge transport barrier, which significantly enhances the breakdown strength while concurrently reducing conductive losses. The optimized composite film demonstrated a remarkable breakdown strength of 6467 kV/cm, an ultrahigh energy storage density of 73.33 J/cm3, and an energy storage efficiency of 80.17%. These results outperform currently reported strontium titanate-based energy storage materials.

### 2.1 Preparation

Figure 1(a) illustrates the detailed procedure for fabricating STM/AO multilayer composite films. For the STM precursor solution, the raw material proportions were first calculated on the basis of the molar ratio of Ti4+ : Mn2+ = 99 : 1. Strontium acetate, manganese acetate tetrahydrate, acetic acid, acetylacetone, and ethylene glycol methyl ether were initially mixed. Tetrabutyl titanate was then added dropwise to the mixture, followed by magnetic stirring for 30 min until complete dissolution. After stirring at room temperature for 4 h and aging in the dark for 24 h, a 0.25 mol/L STM precursor solution was obtained. For the AO precursor, a solvent mixture of ethylene glycol methyl ether and acetic acid (3 : 1 vol/vol) was prepared and stirred at 60 °C for 30 min. Aluminum isopropoxide was then added, and the mixture was stirred at the same temperature for an additional 24 h until a transparent solution formed. After a subsequent 24 h dark aging period, a 0.02 mol/L AO precursor solution was acquired. Both aged precursor solutions were uniformly deposited onto Pt(111)/Ti/SiO2/Si substrates via a two-step spin-coating process (2000 r/min for 10 s, followed by 4000 r/min for 20 s). After each coating, the substrates were pyrolyzed at 400 °C for 3 min to eliminate organic residues. This process was repeated to construct the multilayer structure. Finally, all the films were annealed at 625 °C for 180 s in a rapid thermal annealing furnace to complete the fabrication process. To evaluate the electrical performance, a platinum (Pt) circular top electrode (area: 0.126 mm2; sputtering power: 10 W; deposition time: 10 min) was fabricated on the composite film surface via direct current (DC) magnetron sputtering. To enable connection of both the top and bottom electrodes to the test circuit, the film was locally etched with an HF–HNO3–H2O solution (volume ratio of 1 : 1 : 4). A polypropylene fiber swab dipped in the etchant was used to carefully remove material from one corner of the film, thereby exposing the bottom electrode.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(b) presents a structural schematic of the composite films with different AO layer configurations. The multilayer composite films were assembled in the order of STM/AO/STM, with spin-coated layer numbers of 3/1/3, 3/2/3, and 3/3/3, denoted as STM3AO1STM3(S3AS3), STM3AO2STM3(S3A2S3), and STM3AO3STM3(S3A3S3), respectively. Figure 1(c) presents the XRD pattern of the AO/STM composite film. The characteristic diffraction peaks of the STM dielectric material appear at (200), (100), and (211). During preparation, aluminum oxide can exist in multiple phases, with γ-Al2O3, θ-Al2O3, and α-Al2O3 exhibiting characteristic peaks at γ(200), θ(600), and α(012), respectively. Except for the diffraction peak of the Pt electrode, no sharp diffraction peaks from the STM or AO phases are observed, indicating that both components are in an amorphous or weakly crystalline state. This absence of diffraction peaks is attributed to the annealing temperature of 625 °C being lower than the crystallization temperatures of both materials [30,31]. Figure 1(d) shows cross-sectional SEM and surface AFM images of the AO/STM composite film. The results show that as the number of AO insulating layers increases from one to three, the thicknesses of STM, S3A2S3, S3A2S3, and S3A3S3 are 358.4, 378.6, 395.4, and 422 nm, respectively, suggesting that each AO insulating layer has a thickness of approximately 21.2 nm. The AFM images revealed that the root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of the composite film ranged from 0.287 to 0.601 nm, with the S3A2S3 sample exhibiting the lowest roughness.

Figure 2(a) shows images of the STM and S3A2S3 composite films. After the introduction of the AO layers, the surface of the composite film remained flat. Figure 2(b) presents the dielectric constant and tan*δ* of the AO/STM thin films. The results show that after adding the AO insulating layer, *ε*r of the composite films is lower than that of the pure STM film in the low-frequency range (≤ 103 Hz) and further decreases with increasing number of AO layers. This trend likely results from the inherently low dielectric constant of AO, which diminishes the overall polarization capability of the composite. Figure 2(c) and Fig. S3 in the ESM display the *P‒E* hysteresis loops and Δ*P* values of the composite films. The AO/STM film clearly exhibited linear polarization behavior, with the S3A2S3 sample achieving a maximum Δ*P* of 21.64 μC/cm2, representing an increase of 5.88 μC/cm2 over the STM film.

To investigate the variability in the breakdown strength of AO/STM composite thin films, a two-parameter Weibull statistical analysis was employed to evaluate the dielectric breakdown test results, as depicted in Eq. (3) [32,33]:

(3) P(E)=1−exp(EEb)β

where *P*(*E*) is the cumulative failure probability, *E*b is the breakdown strength, and *β* is a shape parameter related to the data distribution. A higher *β* value indicates a narrower data distribution, reflecting increased reliability and enhanced stability of the sample. As shown in Figure 2d, with an increasing number of AO layers, *E*b of the composite film first tends to increase but then decreases. Specifically, the S3A2S3 composite film attains a maximum *E*b of 6070 kV/cm, representing a 25.8% increase over that of the STM (4825 kV/cm). This improvement derives from two primary effects: the voltage-dividing effect [34] of the high-insulating AO film layer and the formation of a built-in electric field (*E*in) at the heterojunction (Fig. 2(g)) [26].

According to the principle of voltage division, the electric field strength experienced by each dielectric layer within a multilayer composite film is dictated by its dielectric constant [35]. When substantial differences exist between the dielectric constants of adjacent dielectric layers, a highly nonuniform electric field distribution results [36]. For instance, when *ε*1 * ≪ ε*2, a significant disparity in the electric field strength arises between layers, expressed as *E*1 * ≫ E*2. Given that *ε*r of AO is lower than that of STM dielectrics, the electric field within the S3A2S3 composite film becomes concentrated primarily within the highly insulating AO layer. In addition, the introduction of the AO insulating layer induces the generation of *E*in, which partially counteracts the external electric field. The built-in electric field primarily arises from the significant bandgap difference between strontium titanate and aluminum oxide layers. According to the relevant literature, the bandgap (*E*g) of amorphous AO is 7 eV, and its Fermi level (*E*F) is 3.6 eV [37], whereas *E*g of STM is 3.2 eV, with *E*F of 1.6 eV [38], indicating a substantial band structure difference between the AO and STM thin films. The disparity in Fermi levels drives charge carriers to migrate from the higher-energy AO layer to the lower-energy STM layer until the interfacial electric field reaches a new equilibrium. If the layer with a higher Fermi level contains a sufficient carrier concentration, the Fermi levels of both layers become equal at equilibrium. At this stage, the built-in electric field develops at the interface, oriented from the AO layer to the STM layer (Fig. 2(h)). The presence of this built-in electric field restricts further electron migration, thereby enhancing the breakdown field strength.

Figure 2(e) shows the leakage current densities of the AO/STM composite films under an external electric field of 2100 kV/cm. The measured leakage current densities for the STM, S3AS3, S3A2S3, and S3A3S3 samples are 3.39×10−4, 2.38×10−5, 1.51×10−5, and 2.01×10−5 A/cm2, respectively. Notably, all the composite dielectrics containing AO layers exhibited lower leakage currents than did the pure STM film, with S3A2S3 demonstrating the lowest current density. The trend of the leakage current over time is illustrated in Fig. S4 in the ESM. Under the influence of a sustained electric field, the leakage current initially fluctuates before gradually stabilizing over time, with S3A2S3 maintaining the lowest value throughout. This observation correlates with the trend observed in the breakdown strength results, which can be attributed to the inhibitory effect of the built-in electric field at the AO/STM interface on charge migration. The energy storage performance of the multilayer AO/STM films was evaluated via a ferroelectric analyzer. As shown in Fig. 2(f), the energy storage density of the multilayer films monotonically increases with increasing applied electric field strength, whereas the energy storage efficiency monotonically decreases. Specifically, under a critical electric field of 6070 kV/cm, the S3A2S3 film achieves an energy storage density of 56.61 J/cm3, which is 1.55 times greater than that of the STM film (36.62 J/cm3). However, the energy storage efficiency decreases significantly to 47.88%, likely due to the enhanced polarization hysteresis induced by the high electric field.

The analysis above indicates that composite films featuring two layers of AO insulating layers achieve excellent energy storage performance. However, this configuration markedly reduces the energy storage efficiency. To address this limitation while maintaining the two-layer AO structure, the polarization behavior was optimized through the regulation of the spatial distribution of a single AO layer, aiming to synergistically enhance both the energy storage density and efficiency. As illustrated in Figs. 3(a1) and 3(b1), based on a six-layer STM dielectric combined with a two-layer AO insulating structure, two additional spatial configurations were introduced: AO/STM/AO (AS6A) and AO/STM/AO/STM (AS3AS3). The corresponding cross-sectional SEM images and surface AFM images (Figs. 3(a2)–3(a3) and 3(b2)–3(b3)) demonstrate that both configurations exhibit dense, smooth, and crack-free morphologies. The thicknesses of the AS6A and AS3A3 composite films were 393.8 and 396.2 nm, respectively. The surface roughness RMS values for the AS3AS3 and AS6A films are 0.369 and 0.274 nm, respectively, suggesting that the AO layer has the smoothest surface. This is attributable to the high melting point and thermal stability of the AO layer, which effectively suppresses uneven contraction or expansion during high-temperature annealing. Figure 3(c) presents the XRD patterns of AO/STM multilayer films with varying AO layer positions. The characteristic peaks of both components remain weak, indicating that the modification of layer position does not alter the predominant amorphous structure of the composite films.

The cross-sectional morphology and crystal structure of the AS6A multilayer inorganic energy-storage thin film were further characterized via transmission electron microscopy (TEM), as shown in Fig. 3(d) and Fig. S3 in the ESM. To meet the requirements for high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) analysis, the number of spin-coated layers for the AO layers on the top and bottom was increased from one to four while maintaining a constant thickness for the intermediate STM layer. The prepared composite film has a clear layered structure with a total thickness of approximately 514 nm, the thickness of the middle STM layer is approximately 358 nm, and the thickness of the surface AO layer is approximately 77–79 nm. Owing to the four-layer AO coating, the average AO layer thickness is approximately 19.5 nm, which is consistent with previous estimations. Figure 3(d1) displays a high-resolution TEM image at the AO/STM interface, revealing a semicrystalline STM layer with lattice stripe spacings of 0.390 nm in the crystalline regions, which coexist with the amorphous areas. In contrast, no lattice stripes were observed in the AO film, indicating a more complete amorphous structure. The boundary between AO and STM is sharply defined at the atomic scale, exhibiting an abrupt transition without evidence of interlayer diffusion or compositional segregation. The HAADF image in Fig. S5 in the ESM corroborates this, showing amorphous diffraction rings in the STM layer. Elemental analysis via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Figs. 3(d2)–3(d7)) confirmed that aluminum (Al) primarily resides in the AO layers, that strontium (Sr) is enriched in the STM layer, and that oxygen (O) is uniformly distributed throughout the film. The elemental composition of each layer aligns with the designed multilayer structure, with no observable elemental interdiffusion at the interfaces.

Figure 4(a) displays the dielectric performance of various structural AO/STM thin films. All the composite thin films exhibited dielectric constants lower than that of the pristine STM film (93.37@1 kHz). Specifically, the S3A2S3 and AS3AS3 films present dielectric constants of 79.08 and 72.73, respectively, at 1 kHz. The dielectric loss remains consistently low (ranging from 0.023–0.053). Notably, a significant change in the insulating layer position markedly reduces the dielectric constant while exerting only a negligible effect on dielectric loss. This behavior is attributed to the change in the layer position amplifying the barrier effect formed by the wide bandgap of AO, which impedes charge migration and polarization in response to the electric field [39].

Figure 4(b) and Fig. S6 in the ESM demonstrate that the AS6A film has the narrowest *P‒E* hysteresis loop among the AO/STM films. *P*m (30.82 μC/cm2) of the AS6A film is comparable to that of the other AO/STM configurations; however, it features the lowest *P*r (3.99 μC/cm2), resulting in the highest Δ*P* (26.59 μC/cm2). A comprehensive comparison indicates that the AO insulating layer distributed on both the top and bottom surfaces yields the optimal energy storage potential. Moreover, comparison of the *P–E* hysteresis loops revealed that the AS6A composite film exhibited the slimmest loop, indicating enhanced reversible polarization and improved field adaptability. This is attributed to the AO layers at both surfaces, which mitigate the local field concentration and optimize the internal electric field by utilizing the built-in field advantage in heterojunctions. Consequently, the dielectric loss and residual polarization are significantly reduced, thereby improving the energy storage efficiency.

Figure 4(c) and Fig. S7 in the ESM present the Weibull distribution fitting results for *E*b of the multilayer films. The AS6A film exhibited the highest breakdown strength, reaching 6467 kV/cm, representing a substantial 34% increase over the initial STM film. This enhancement is attributed primarily to the carrier barrier strategy introduced by the inorganic functional layer, which effectively suppresses charge injection and space charge accumulation. When the low-dielectric-constant AO layer is employed as the top and bottom layers, Gauss’s law indicates that the external electric field predominantly concentrates across these surface layers [40]. Additionally, as shown in Ref. [37], breakdown failure typically initiates at the surface layer with the lowest breakdown strength and propagates inward owing to the intensification of distorted electric fields until reaching the opposite electrodes. Therefore, embedding an insulating AO layer at the top and bottom interfaces effectively reinforces this “weak” surface layer, thereby substantially improving *E*b of the composite film.

Figure 4(d) displays the leakage current density for different AO/STM composite structures. At an applied electric field of 2100 kV/cm, the AS6A and AS3AS3 films exhibit leakage current densities of 1.2×10−5 and 8.2×10−5 A/cm2, respectively. The AS6A film demonstrated the lowest leakage current density among all the composite films while maintaining stability after a certain operating period (Fig. S8 in the ESM). This reduced leakage current density minimizes conductive losses and enhances energy storage efficiency [41]. To elucidate the physical mechanism underlying the insulation enhancement, the leakage current data were fitted via the Schottky conduction model (Eq. (4)) [42,43]:

(4) ln(JT2)=(e3/(4πϵr)kT)E+lnA−ΦkT

where *Ф* represents the barrier height, *T* represents the temperature, *ε*r represents the dielectric constant, *q* represents the elementary charge, and *k* represents the Boltzmann constant. The absolute value of the intercept of the fitted curve corresponds to the barrier height [44]. As shown in Fig. 4(e), the fitted curve for the AS6A composite thin film yields the largest intercept, indicating that it has the highest potential barrier height. This significant improvement is attributed to the wide bandgap characteristics of the aluminum oxide surface insulating layer, which increases the potential barrier height at the interface between the metal electrode and the aluminum oxide layer relative to the interface between the metal electrode and the STM layer. Figure S9 in the ESM shows the band diagrams at the interfaces between the metal electrode and the AO and STM layers. The electron injection barrier energy (*φ*e) at the Pt electrode/AO interface is 3.6 eV, which is greater than that at the Pt electrode/STM interface. A higher potential barrier not only effectively suppresses Schottky emission and charge injection at the electrode (Fig. 4(g)) but also effectively restricts carrier migration (Figs. 4(h)–4(i)), ultimately restricting charge transport throughout the entire path.

The increases in Δ*P* and *E*b significantly improved the energy storage performance of the multilayer composite material. As depicted in Fig. 4(f), the AS6A film achieves a maximum energy storage density of 71.33 J/cm3, which is 1.95 times greater than that of the STM film. Moreover, *η* remains at 80.17%. This demonstrates that through the construction of interfacial built-in electric fields and the regulation of carrier migration pathways via layered structural control, effective decoupling of polarization and *E*b has been realized. Compared with recently reported high-performance ferroelectric multilayer composite films [45−48], the present work, although it leaves room for further improvement in energy-storage performance, demonstrates notable advantages within the SrTiO3-based paraelectric dielectric system. To evaluate the novelty and effectiveness of this approach, the research outcomes are compared with recent representative SrTiO3-based dielectrics (data sourced from Ref. [28,30,49−57]), as shown in Fig. 5(a). The results reveal that this material exhibits leading performance in terms of *E*b, *W*rec, and *η*. Figure 5(b) quantifies six key performance parameters (*W*rec, *η*, *E*b, *ε*r, Δ*P*, and *J*) in radar charts. Compared with SrTiO3 and STM, this AS6A composite material achieves comprehensive improvements in insulation and energy storage capabilities.

In addition to high energy storage density and efficiency, the long-term operational stability of dielectric materials is vital. Therefore, charge‒discharge cycling tests were performed on the optimized AS6A multilayer composite film under various temperatures, frequencies, and electric field reversal conditions (Figs. 5(c)–5(e)). The AS6A film maintains stable performance over 107 cycles, with minimal fluctuations in stored energy (Δ*W*rec rec < 9.2% and Δ*η* < 10.12%. Collectively, these results confirm that the AS6A film has outstanding overall stability under demanding operational conditions.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, we achieved synergistic optimization of the SrTiO3-based composite dielectric *W*rec and *η* by controlling the number and spatial distribution of the AO insulating layers. When the number of AO insulating layers was two, the breakdown strength of the S3A2S3 composite film increased to 6070 kV/cm, and the energy storage density reached 56.61 J/cm3. However, the polarization hysteresis effect induced by the high electric field reduced the energy storage efficiency to 47.88%. Further adjustment of the spatial position of the Al2O3 layer revealed that distributing AO on the upper and lower surfaces of the STM to form a top-bottom insulating structure effectively suppressed charge injection at the electrode‒dielectric interface. This configuration decreased the leakage current density to 1.2×10−5 A/cm2 and reduced the residual polarization to 3.99 μC/cm2. Under the combined effects of the built-in electric field within the heterojunction and the suppression of charge injection, the breakdown strength further increased to 6467 kV/cm, with the energy storage density and efficiency reaching 71.33 J/cm3 and 80.17%, respectively. Additionally, the film demonstrated excellent stability across various temperatures, frequencies, and electrical fatigue conditions.
