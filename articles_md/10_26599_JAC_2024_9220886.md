## 1 Introduction

Aluminum and magnesium base alloy castings with complex cavity structures are widely used in the aerospace and intelligent manufacturing industries. In the casting process, ceramic cores are used for casting complex structures. Traditional ceramic cores are manufactured by injection molding and need to be removed with chemical solvents after casting. These processes make casting expensive and inefficient. Therefore, developing cores with excellent performance and cleaning efficiency is necessary. Water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores are an ideal solution for manufacturing complex hollow castings [1−4]. Compared with traditional casting cores [5,6], water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores reduce harmful decomposition products during the core cleaning process. After casting, the residual cores can be quickly removed with water. In addition, compared with those of salt cores [7−9], the ceramic powder of water-soluble cores has good chemical stability and reduces the shrinkage of the core. Therefore, water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores are widely used in precision casting, especially for aluminum and magnesium alloy castings with complex internal pipes. Currently, the casting methods for water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores mainly include molten casting, pressing sintering, and bonding [10−16]. However, these methods have several disadvantages. For example, the corrosion of the core formed by molten casting is more serious. This method has high requirements for raw materials and equipment. Pressing sintering has high mold requirements, and this method has difficulty manufacturing cores with complex structures. The bonding method has a large gas output. Casting is prone to generating defects, and the overall process is relatively cumbersome. The demand for customized and lightweight parts has increased in high-end manufacturing sectors such as aerospace and new energy vehicles. In addition, some cores with complex internal cavities are challenging to mold by conventional methods. Therefore, there is an urgent need for new methods to achieve rapid fabrication of complex salt-based ceramic cores.

Three-dimensional (3D) printing (additive manufacturing) technology has the advantages of no mold, high design freedom, and fast forming. This technology has been rapidly developed in manufacturing science [17−26]. Recent studies related to the additive manufacturing of water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores are shown in Table 1 [27−31]. Gong *et* *al*. [27] used material extrusion (ME) to fabricate high-strength, low-cost, and water-soluble salt cores. The main materials were Na2SO4 and NaCl powder, and the reinforcing materials were bauxite powder. This research shows that the performance of salt cores is significantly affected by bauxite powder contents and sintering parameters. The salt cores have high strength and good morphology when the bauxite powder content is 20 wt%. Chen *et* *al*. [28] used K2SO4 and KCl to prepare complex high-strength water-soluble salt cores by layered extrusion forming. The microstructure of the KCl–K2SO4 composite salt core was explored in detail. The alumina powder refines the grain size of KCl and results in a dense microstructure. Yang *et* *al*. [29] prepared ceramic-based cores using layer extrusion forming. The ceramic core parts have Ca2SiO4 and CaO phases. Ca2SiO4 improves the solubility, but its collapsibility is relatively low. The sintered parts with a C/S ratio of 2.45 were quickly dissolved in warm water. Zhao *et* *al*. [30] manufactured complex ceramic cores by binder jetting. The nanometer ceramic suspension was used as the binder for the ceramic cores. The surface of the CaO particle was coated with nanozirconia particles. With increasing binder saturation, the linear shrinkage of the sintered body decreases, but the hydration resistance and bending strength increase. Zhao *et* *al*. [31] investigated the effect of the addition method and the amount of zirconia nanoparticles on CaO/CaZrO3 ceramic cores. The application of zirconia nanosuspensions made the green body denser. After sintering at 1500 °C, a thicker and denser CaZrO3 layer formed on the surface of the ZrO2 and CaO particles, which improved the performance of the sintered CaO/CaZrO3 ceramic cores. However, the cores manufactured by layered extrusion forming and binder jetting undergo large deformations, and their bending strength is relatively low. In addition, calcium oxide reacts with water to produce corrosive substances and heat. Therefore, exploring a new method for accessing water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores is necessary.

In this paper, water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores were prepared for the first time by vat photopolymerization. Compared to other 3D printing methods, vat photopolymerization has the advantages of fast fabrication and high surface accuracy [32−37]. As shown in Fig. 1(a), the suspension consisted of dispersant, plasticizer, and photosensitive resin. Sodium chloride and alumina ceramics were used as raw materials. Sodium chloride, which is highly soluble in water and can be sintered at lower temperatures, is the main material for the cores. Alumina particles are chemically stable and improve the water solubility of the core [1,28]. Figures 1(b) and 1(c) show the working principle of vat photopolymerization and model slicing. The printing suspension is cured layer by layer until the green body is finished manufacturing. By slicing the model, the sectional view of each layer is obtained as the printed pattern, and the ultraviolet (UV) projector projects the printed pattern. As shown in Fig. 1(d), complex structural parts are successfully manufactured through vat photopolymerization. The manufacturing process is shown in Fig. 1(e). Compared with traditional casting cores, water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores do not produce harmful decomposition products during the core cleaning process and can be reused by grinding and drying. This technology improves the level and efficiency of manufacturing complex alloy parts and has vital research significance and application value for improving equipment reliability.

### 2.1 Materials and preparation

The photocurable printing suspension mainly comprised a powder mixture and photosensitive resin. According to the previous work of the research group [33], the photosensitive resin was prepared. The photoinitiator (Irgacure 819, BASF) content was 0.5 wt% of photosensitive resin. A powder mixture of alumina (*d*50 = 0.52 μm; North Hengli Technology Co., Ltd.) and sodium chloride (*d*50 = 8.64 μm; North Hengli Technology Co., Ltd.) was added to the photosensitive resin, and the weight ratio of sodium chloride to alumina was 10 : 1. The particle size distribution of the powder mixture (*d*50 = 7.67 μm) is shown in Fig. 2(a). The dispersants (BYK-111, BYK) and plasticizers (PEG-200, Aladdin) were added to the suspension. The mixture was stirred in a homogenizer (ZYMC-580HV, Shenzhen Zhongyi Technology Co., Ltd.) at a speed of 1200 r/min. The solid phase content of the suspensions was 62.6 vol%.

The resolution of the digital micromirror device (DMD) oscillator in a 3D printer (Cerastation 160, Jiangsu Quickdemos Intelligent Manufacturing Hightech Co., Ltd.) is 62.5 μm. The green bodies were manufactured by setting different printing thicknesses (50, 75, and 100 μm) and different energy doses (58–102 mJ/cm2). As shown in Fig. 2(b), a spline model (36 mm × 4 mm × 3 mm) was designed for mechanical property testing. The heating rate and holding time were selected based on the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA; STA 449F5, Netzsch). Finally, the effects of the holding time (3, 5, and 7 h) and sintering temperature (750, 780, and 810 °C) on the final properties and microstructure of the sintered parts were investigated.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The accuracy of the balance (ME104E, Mettler Toledo) was 0.1 mg. The apparent porosity of the sintered part [27,29] was calculated based on Archimedes’ principle. The specific calculation equation is shown in Eq. (1):

(1) Pa=(mb−ma)/(mb−mc)×100%

where *P*a (%) is the apparent porosity; *m*a (g) is the weight of the dry sintered part; *m*b (g) is the weight of the wet sintered part; *m*c (g) is the buoyant weight of the sintered part.

The weight and surface area of the dry sintered parts were tested. The water was heated to different temperatures and kept warm. After the sintered parts were completely dissolved, the dissolution time was recorded. The dissolution rate [4,27] was calculated by Eq. (2):

(2) K=ma/(t×S)

where *K* (g/(m2·s)) is the dissolution rate of the water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores; *m*a (g) is the weight of the dry sintered part; *t* (s) is the complete dissolution time; *S* (m2) is the surface area of the dry sintered part.

The sintered parts were divided into three categories for storage (air, sealed bag, and plastic wrap), and the moisture rate [14,28] was calculated by Eq. (3):

(3) φ=(md−ma)/ma×100%

where *φ* (%) is the moisture rate; *m*a (g) is the weight of the dry sintered part; *m*d (g) is the weight of the sintered part after storage for a period of time.

The line shrinkage [27,35] of the spline is calculated by Eq. (4):

(4) Ls=(la−lb)/la×100%

where *L*s (%) is the line shrinkage; *l*a (mm) is the side length of the model; *l*b (mm) is the side length of the sintered part.

A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Sigma 300, Zeiss) was used to observe the surface morphology.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern was measured by a diffractometer (Empyrean, Malvern Panalytical).

A universal mechanical testing machine (Instron 5965, Instron) was used to measure the bending strength. The span was 30 mm, and the loading rate was 0.5 mm/min. The bending test was repeated five times, and the average value was taken as the test result.

The roughness of the top surface and the sidewall under different printing parameters were measured using a high-precision portable surface roughness meter (SF 200 Basic, Beijing Time-Top Technology Co., Ltd.).

The viscosity was measured at room temperature by a rotational rheometer (DV2T, Brookfield).

The curing depth of the suspension was measured as follows: The UV projector of the 3D printer cured the suspension by varying the energy dose. The projection pattern was square (10 mm). The curing depth was measured three times by a micrometer (Digital Micrometer, SYNTEK), and the average value was used as the final measurement.

Point cloud measurements were used to test the accuracy of the sintered parts. The point cloud data were obtained using a scanner (Marvelscan, ZG Technology Co., Ltd.) and compared to the original model.

### 3.1 Printing accuracy and defect control of green bodies

The accuracy of 3D printing is essential. To manufacture water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores with complex structures, the manufacturing dimensions must be consistent with the design. The dimensional deviation affects the stability and performance of the whole structure. In vat photopolymerization, deformation caused by the accumulation of inner curing stress is the main reason for dimensional deviations. Photopolymerization is a process in which liquid is converted into solid. The monomer molecules are transformed into chemical covalent bonds at the molecular level. The reduction in molecular spacing is macroscopically manifested as volume shrinkage. During the layer-by-layer curing process, inner stress accumulation causes the curing warping deformation of the green bodies. A schematic of the curing warping deformation process is shown in Fig. 3(a). To suppress the curing deformation, plasticizers were added to the suspension to release the internal stress and reduce the warp deformation after curing. As shown in Fig. 3(b), the plasticizer increases the intermolecular distance and reduces the concentration of the photosensitive resin monomer. When the suspension is cured, the number of photosensitive resin monomers that can participate in the reaction decreases. Therefore, the warping deformation of the suspension is reduced. Figure 3(c) shows that the elastic modulus of the green body decreases with increasing plasticizer content. The inner stress generated during the solidification of the suspension is dissipated and released by the green body with a low elastic modulus. To further investigate the effect of plasticizers on warpage, suspensions with different plasticizer contents were prepared, and the energy dose was set to 92 mJ/cm2 (printed thickness = 75 μm). The warping of the green body was observed by optical microscopy. Figure 3(d) shows that the warping deformation is effectively controlled by adding the plasticizer. When the amount of plasticizer added exceeds 20 wt%, the warping problem of the green body is eliminated.

### 3.2 Postprocessing of water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores

The green bodies were slowly heated to approximately 600 °C in air or a protective atmosphere to remove the photosensitive resin. During the degreasing process, the resin decomposed violently. If the decomposition products do not have enough time to be expelled from the green body, cracks appear between the interlayer of the sintered parts. As the size of the green body increases, the decomposition product transmission is limited. Under these conditions, sintered parts are more prone to defects such as surface bubbling and interlayer cracks. Therefore, it is necessary to explore the degreasing process to realize defect formation control. The thermal decomposition behaviors of the green bodies in different degreasing atmospheres were compared. Figures 5(a) and 5(b) show the derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) analysis results. The air atmosphere has two decomposition peaks, and the decomposition rate of the green bodies is fast. Under a nitrogen atmosphere, the green bodies have three decomposition peaks. The decomposition products and heat are released slowly. According to the thermogravimetric analysis results, degreasing systems under different atmospheres are shown in Figs. 5(c) and 5(d). After nitrogen degreasing, the green bodies were heated to 600 °C in air and held for 2 h to remove residual carbon. As shown in Fig. 5(e), the green bodies degreased with nitrogen have no obvious cracks compared to the green bodies degreased with air. Therefore, a nitrogen atmosphere is chosen as the degreasing atmosphere for green bodies.

Sintering is the process of powder densification. After the degreasing process, the photosensitive resin of the green body is removed, and the powder accumulation in the degreased parts is not dense. The sintering process combines these powder particles by increasing the temperature to form dense sintered parts. To improve the strength of sintered parts, it is necessary to set up an appropriate sintering system. The sintering temperature is the critical parameter in the sintering process. The sintered parts and SEM images at different temperatures are shown in Fig. 6(a). The parts sintered at 750 °C are not fully sintered and have pores. Therefore, Fig. 6(b) shows that the porosity of the parts sintered at 750 °C is the highest. The XRD patterns of the sintered parts at different temperatures are shown in Fig. 6(c). When the sintering temperature was 810 °C, sodium chloride began to melt, which led to a decrease in the intensity of the sodium chloride crystalline peak. Therefore, the intensity of the alumina crystalline peak increases at 810 °C. In addition, as shown in Fig. 6(d), during the sintering process, the particles gradually melted and formed a dense sintered part. However, when the sintering temperature increased to 810 °C, the main sodium chloride particles transformed into liquid. Due to gravity and internal stress, the sintered parts are seriously deformed. The mechanical properties of the sintered parts were tested. As shown in Fig. 6(e), the sintered parts have a high bending strength at 780 °C. In summary, 780 °C was selected as the final sintering temperature for the water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores. The shrinkage of the sintered parts at 780 °C is shown in Fig. 6(f). When manufacturing large cores, the model is scaled according to shrinkage.

### 3.3 Properties and storage methods of water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores

To improve the printing efficiency and surface quality of water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores, the suspension needs to have a low viscosity during the printing process. A suspension with high viscosity and low fluidity is unevenly coated as the scraper moves. Compared with the designed sliced layer, the cured printed layer has dimensional deviations, creating a rough surface. The effect of a high-viscosity suspension on rough surface formation is shown in Fig. 8(a). The surface roughness of sintered parts directly affects the core cleaning process after casting. The addition of dispersants effectively prevents the agglomeration of powder particles and reduces the viscosity of the suspension. Suspensions of water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores with different dispersant contents (0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 wt%) were prepared, and the viscosity of the suspensions is shown in Fig. 8(b). The suspension has an obvious shear-thinning phenomenon. When the dispersant content is 0.5 wt%, powder adsorption does not reach saturation, which causes agglomeration of the suspension. When the dispersant content increases to 1.0 wt%, the viscosity of the suspension decreases. In addition, the green bodies with different dispersant contents were manufactured. After degreasing and sintering, the surface roughness of the sintered part was tested. First, the surface of the sintered part was observed through an optical microscope, and the relevant results are shown in Fig. 8(c). Due to poor suspension fluidity, some areas are not evenly filled during the scraper laying process. The cured area has pores or bumps that form a rough surface after curing. According to the surface roughness test results in Fig. 8(d), an appropriate amount of dispersant can prevent particles from agglomerating and help the suspension to be evenly distributed during the scraper laying process. After additive manufacturing and postprocessing, the sintered part has a smooth surface. In summary, the amount of dispersant added should be kept above 1.0 wt% to meet the roughness requirements of water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores. The viscosity of the suspension changes little when more dispersants are added. Considering the results of the actual printing and viscosity tests, the dispersant concentration selected is 1.0 wt%.

Water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores are not suitable for prolonged exposure to air. Due to the hygrophilicity of inorganic salts, water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores are susceptible to moisture in the air, which can lead to low strength. At the same time, during the casting process, steam is generated at high temperatures from the damp cores. Therefore, choosing a suitable storage method to avoid contact between the water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores and the moisture in the air is necessary. The influence of different storage methods on the water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores was explored, and the moisture rate under different storage methods was calculated. Figures 10(c) and 10(d) show the test results and storage methods. The sintered parts were placed in plastic wrap and sealed in bags with good moisture resistance. Therefore, the quality and performance of water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores can be effectively maintained through sealed storage. Finally, the core of the body-centered cubic structure was poured. To facilitate the observation of the internal structure, transparent wax was selected as the pouring materials. As shown in Fig. 10(e), a part with complex internal pipes can be obtained after dissolution and cleaning.

## 4 Conclusions

In this paper, the composition of the suspension, printing parameters, and postprocessing technology are optimized. The water-soluble salt-based ceramic cores are manufactured without obvious cracks. The specific conclusions are as follows.

(1) The suspension has an obvious shear-thinning phenomenon. The addition of plasticizers can reduce the warping deformation, and the elastic modulus of the green body decreases with increasing plasticizer content. The warping deformation is eliminated when the amount of plasticizer added exceeds 20 wt%. However, too much plasticizer causes surface defects and reduces the strength of the green body.

(2) The green bodies degreased with nitrogen and the residual carbon removed with air have no obvious cracks compared to the green bodies degreased with air. The degreased parts are sintered at 780 °C for 5 h, and the mechanical properties of the sintered parts are high. A water-soluble salt-based ceramic core with a complex structure was successfully fabricated.

(3) Sintered parts have a smoother surface because of the reduced viscosity and decreased thickness of the printed layer. The thickness of the printed layer was 75 μm, and the dispersant content was 1.0 wt% according to the roughness and mechanical properties tests. The dissolution rate can be improved by increasing the dissolution temperature. Considering the safety and efficiency, the dissolution temperature was set to 60 °C. In addition, the moisture resistance is improved through sealed storage.
