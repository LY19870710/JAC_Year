## 1 Introduction

Aluminum oxynitride (AlON) spinel, a transparent ceramic with excellent transparency and outstanding mechanical properties to rival sapphire, has been regarded as an ideal transparent armor [1−3]. The damage/failure of ceramic armors during impact is a function of the temporal and spatial interaction of the stresses at the macro-, micro-, and nano-structural scales [4−7]. This includes determining the mechanisms involved in elastic and plastic deformation, damage nucleation and evolution, and failure at the macro- and nano-scale. Importantly, many experimental and theoretical studies have noted that the ballistic performance of ceramics is directly related to plastic deformation mechanisms [8,9]. Hence, ceramic plasticity plays an important role in the impact performance of brittle materials [10−12]. The internal damage evolution in the AlON transparent ceramic was visualized via edge-on impact (EOI) tests, which revealed that the damage velocity of the AlON transparent ceramic does not increase significantly with the impact velocity, which may be related to the role of plastic deformation mechanisms operating in the AlON [13]. Postmortem analysis of the fragment from a confined EOI test revealed that in addition to intergranular fracture, there was a significant intragranular microcleavage pattern [14]. However, whether cleavage is preceded by microdeformational twinning is still unknown. Conventional recovery experiments frequently yield controversial conclusions because of the unknown and uncontrollable loading experience of individual fragments produced by dynamic tests [15].

Recently, computational models have suggested that the mechanical response of AlON transparent ceramics under dynamic loading involves plastic deformation that may result from dislocation gliding, twinning, or stress-induced solid-state phase transitions (amorphization) [16]. However, these plastic deformation mechanisms have not been sufficiently identified by experimental analysis, except for dislocation gliding [17]. In addition, as a polycrystalline material, the intrinsic plastic behavior of AlON single crystal is fundamental and necessary for describing the plastic response of polycrystalline AlON transparent ceramics well [18,19]. However, until now, the dynamic plastic behavior of AlON single crystals has not yet been studied through experimental measurements. Because large single crystals of AlON are not yet available via standard processing routes [16,20], they have not been mechanically tested.

Nanoindentation can perform indentation and scratch tests at small scales [21−23], obtaining the mechanical behavior of materials at the microscopic level, including hardness [24,25], elastic constants [26], and fracture toughness [27−29], while also offering the ability to simulate dynamic loading conditions through nanoimpact tests. To extract the dynamic plastic behavior of an AlON single crystal, a nanoimpact test can be used [30]. The nanoimpact test is a technique for high-strain-rate mechanical characterization of bulk materials and coating systems with nanoscale depth resolution [31,32]. For example, Somekawa and Schuh [33] used a nanoimpact test to study the dynamic hardness of fine-grained magnesium alloys at high strain rates (* 150s−1). The results indicate that the dynamic hardness aligns well with existing quasistatic data within the measurement and extrapolation uncertainty ranges, confirming that the deformation mechanism at this strain rate is dislocation glide. In addition, nanoimpact testing, which can produce self-confining stresses and high contact pressures underneath the indenter, can be employed to investigate the plastic deformation mechanism of brittle ceramics. Recently, increasing attention has been given to nanoimpact, motivated by the need to understand how materials deform under high strain rates and at a small scale. This technique was evaluated on several kinds of materials (such as epoxy resin, fused silica, polycrystalline tungsten, coarse-grain pure copper, ultrafine-grain copper, and aluminum alloy) to determine the dynamic hardness values by Ruede-Ruiz et al*. [34]. On the basis of a nanoimpact dataset, Wang *et al*. [35] proposed that the strength coefficient and strain hardening of the hydride phase decreased with increasing temperature. The strain rate exponent of the hydride precipitates remains almost constant as the temperature increases. In Qin’s work [32], nanoimpacts with different accelerating forces and distances were carried out on a single-crystal of Al(110) to study the dynamic hardness and its indentation size effect (ISE). The dynamic ISEs were more pronounced than those in the quasistatic indentation tests and were attributed to the localized strain caused by the high strain rate and its rapid change during contact. Recently, the dynamic deformation behavior of epoxy resin was studied by nanoimpact, which revealed a bilinear strain rate sensitivity with a transition from 0.03 to 0.05 at a strain rate of approximately  10−1s−1 [36].

As mentioned above, the intrinsic mechanical behavior of AlON single crystals, particularly their dynamic mechanical behavior, is essential for understanding the damage and failure mechanisms of AlON transparent ceramics under dynamic loading and for establishing reliable numerical models. However, obtaining AlON single crystals is currently not feasible. Therefore, the primary objective of this work is to use nanoimpact techniques to extract the dynamic mechanical properties of AlON single crystals from individual grains of AlON ceramics and to elucidate their deformation mechanisms under dynamic loading. The AlON transparent ceramic used in this work has a typical grain size of ~100 μm, which is feasible for extracting the dynamic plastic responses of AlON single crystals from individual grains. The dynamic hardness and plastic deformation behavior of AlON single crystals with different crystallographic orientations were investigated via nanoimpact. Postmortem microstructure characterization was performed to identify the dynamic deformation mechanisms (dislocation gliding, twinning, and amorphization).

### 2.1 Materials

AlON transparent ceramic samples were fabricated using single-phase and highly crystallized AlON powders as the starting material, which were synthesized via carbothermal reduction and nitridation methods. Then, the AlON powders were uniaxially pressed into a 20 mm diameter pellet to prepare the green body. The sintering procedure was performed at 1880 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C/min, and the samples were incubated for 8 h in a vertical graphite furnace filled with flowing high-purity nitrogen under 1.0×105 Pa. The details of the fabrication procedure can be found in our previous works [37,38]. The phase compositions of the prepared samples were confirmed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker Co.) with Cu Kα radiation (wavelength: 1.54184 Å).

### 2.2 Nanoimpact and static indentation

Before testing, the AlON ceramics were first ground with 240, 600, 800, 1200, and 2500 grit diamond sandpaper and then polished sequentially with 1 and 0.05 μm diamond polishing paste. After grinding and polishing, no visible scratches were observed under an optical microscope. High-resolution electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) images were collected on polished samples with areas of 2 mm × 2 mm using a step size of 10 μm. The crystal orientations were determined on the basis of the EBSD map measured via the AZtecCrystal software. To identify the orientations of the grains, the sample after EBSD measurement was etched in phosphoric acid at 300 °C for ~3 min. Then, the individual grains were observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JEM4700, JEOL).

On the basis of the SEM and EBSD results, nanoimpact tests were carried out on three grains with different crystal orientations: (010), (101), and (111). The nanoimpact system (NanoTest Vantage, Micro Materials Ltd.) equipped with a Berkovich indenter is a pendulum-based, force-actuated, displacement-sensing device with the ability to perform energy-controlled nanoimpact. A schematic illustration of this system is shown in Fig. 1. The force was actuated via a magnet electromagnetic coil couple. Two capacitor plates in line with the indenter tip measured the displacement. The pendulum rotated around a frictionless pivot consisting of a fixed beam attached to the frame and a moveable beam connected to the pendulum. To achieve impact indentation in this system, a material with low magnetic hysteresis at the bottom of the pendulum was attracted by a solenoid installed on the bottom plate of the instrument. The pendulum was brought into contact with the solenoid by switching on the solenoid voltage. Simultaneously, a variable current was applied to the coil in the upper part of the pendulum, causing the coil to be held by a permanent magnet. The sample stage was then adjusted so that the distance between the material surface and the tip equaled a predefined impulse distance. Owing to the constraint of the limit stop, the pendulum was fixed at both the top and bottom, resulting in a certain degree of elastic deformation. The solenoid voltage was then switched off (the electromagnetic attraction holding the bottom part of the pendulum was released), and the indenter tip swung toward the sample surface, driven by the predefined acceleration force (the greater the current in the coil above the pendulum, the greater the acceleration force). Under the given acceleration force, the pendulum accelerated toward the surface until contact occurred, at which point the velocity of the indenter reached its peak. The acceleration force was a value that can be set on the instrument before impact and reflects the degree of attraction between the permanent magnet and the coil above the pendulum. In this work, the nanoimpact experiments were performed using acceleration forces of 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 mN with the impact height between the indenter tip and the sample surface fixed at 20 μm.

### 2.3 Microstructural characteristics

After nanoimpact, the surface morphologies of the residual indentations were observed via SEM. To assess the microstructure beneath the residual indentation, SEM and focused ion beam (FIB) analyses were carried out. First, the FIB trenches were sputtered on the residual indentations using 30 kV Ga+ ions with a current of 92 nA. These trenches were subsequently polished using a current of 9.3 nA. Sequential imaging of the milled cross-sections was carried out via SEM at a tilt angle of 52°.

For microstructural investigation after nanoimpact, electron-transparent cross-section lamella samples were meticulously prepared via FIB. Lamella (approximately 10 and 2 μm in length and width, respectively) were cut from the center of the indentation using a Ga ion beam, lifted, and soldered to a Cu grid using Pt. A Ga+ ion beam was applied for rough thinning of the lamella to a thickness of approximately 80 nm on each side. Further thinning and cleaning were performed with a 5 kV Ga+ ion beam, removing approximately 2 nm from each side. Tapering was compensated by a 1.3° tilt to achieve a nearly vertical lamella with uniform thickness. Immediately after sample preparation, images were taken via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; F200, JEOL). Analysis of the slip band was facilitated by electron diffraction. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns were taken from an area 10 μm in diameter, selected by the selected area aperture.

### 3.2 Nanoimpact and dynamic plasticity

Typical output data from the nanoimpact experiments are shown in Fig. 3. The upper panel of Fig. 3 shows the indentation depth, *h*, as a function of time, *t*, during impact. The corresponding velocity–time (*v–t*) curve is plotted, as shown in the lower portion of Fig. 3. The point of initial contact is identified by the onset of indenter deceleration and is assigned as *h* = 0; the velocity at initial contact is denoted * vin. For the final calculation of the dynamic hardness ( HD), the other two analysis parameters, the outgoing velocity ( vout) and residual indentation depth ( hres) at the point where the indenter detaches from the sample on the first rebound, must be determined. On the basis of the  h– t and  v– t curves, the residual indentation depth can be detected on the second impact; then, the outgoing velocity corresponding to  hres can also be calculated by  vout=hres×dh/dt, as shown in Fig. 3.

For a self-similar expanding plastic zone made by the Berkovich indenter, the instantaneous indentation strain rate ( ε˙) is typically determined via Eq. (1) [40]:

(1) ε˙=h˙h

where  h˙ is the indenter displacement rate. Based on this equation, the instantaneous strain rate can be calculated, as shown in Figs. 4(a)–4(c). In our nanoimpact tests, the instantaneous strain rates on the different oriented planes continuously decrease from ~104 s−1 to zero when the indenter rests. In addition, the instantaneous strain rate slightly increases with increasing load. This is different from quasistatic indentation: here, the load is controlled during the whole process, and constant strain rates can be obtained during the whole measurement. However, in impact indentation, the load can be adjusted only at the beginning of the test. Once the indenter leaves the electromagnet, neither the load, displacement nor strain rate can be controlled. Therefore, the strain rate changes continuously during one impact indentation as a function of depth. On the basis of the suggestion of Somewaka and Schuh [33], a representative strain rate ( ε˙r) determined by the maximum velocity divided by the maximum indentation depth can be used. After calculation, the representative strain rates for all the tested samples are equal to ~ 102s−1, as shown in Fig. 4(d).

### 3.3 Dynamic hardness

Generally, the dynamic hardness (* HD) is defined as the loss in kinetic energy (plastic work) divided by the volume of the residual impression, which is not as well defined as the load and area used in static hardness. Considering the nanoimpact process, the kinetic energy loss between the start and end of the contract period can be related to  vin and  vout, respectively. This leads to Eq. (2), which is used to calculate  HD from a single nanoimpact [41]:

(2) HD=12meff(vin2−vout2)Vre

where  meff is the effective mass of the pendulum, which can be determined to be 2352 g by calibration following the approach detailed in Constantinides et al*. [42].  Vre is the volume of residual indentation after nanoimpact, which can be expressed as Eq. (3) for the Berkovich indenter [43].

(3) Vre=13APhr

### 3.4 Morphology and microstructure of residual impressions

Typical impact craters formed at 50 mN on AlON crystals with orientations close to (010), (101), and (111) are shown in Figs. 7(a1)–7(c1), where radial cracks can be observed initiating at the corners of the impressions. When the crystallographic orientation was changed irrespective of the indenter, the surface features and fracture patterns revealed the same crack morphology, as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. Thus, in the case of single-crystal AlON, the type of fracture feature or crack system is independent of the crystal orientation. A closer observation of these impressions, as shown in Figs. 7(a2)–7(c2), reveals many slip traces along certain directions. The slip traces should all be along the intersections of the {111} planes and the surface planes of each crystal, which are characteristic of the selected crystal orientations and play an important role in the mechanics of anisotropy. The crystallographic configurations in the surface planes are also illustrated by the insets at the top left of Figs. 7(a1)–7(c1), with the dashed lines marking the intersections of the {111} slip planes with the surface planes. The patterns of the slip traces suggest that the {111} planes are the active slip planes and that dislocation sliding is one of the dynamic deformation mechanisms for single-crystal AlON at room temperature. The Berkovich indenter is not conical, and its surface is not isotropic (meaning that the intersection line between the slip plane and the sample surface will change when the sample rotates in the plane where the sample surface is located [31]).

### 4.1 Deformation mechanism

To explore the underlying dynamic deformation mechanism of the AlON single crystals with crystallographic orientations of (010), (101), and (111), TEM foils were cut from residual impressions. The bright-field TEM (BF-TEM) micrographs (Figs. 8(a1)–8(a3)) obtained from the indented regions illustrate the typical characteristics resulting from plastic deformation. In all these indented AlON crystals, dislocations, slip bands, and microcracks (lateral cracks) can be found. Additionally, the dislocation lines in the region beneath the indentation are closely packed together, indicating significant plastic deformation. High-density dislocations can be identified by the dense and tangled network of lines within the microstructure. No evidence can be found for the presence of deformation twins, although more than one residual indentation was investigated via TEM analysis (as shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM). Guo *et al*. [17] investigated the deformation behavior of AlON ceramics under static indentation. Their findings revealed a high density of dissociated  dislocations, with no evidence of twinning. In our work, the absence of twinning in the indented regions under nanoimpact aligns with observations from static nanoindentation. The observation of microcracks (both in Fig. 8(a) and Fig. S5 in the ESM), particularly lateral cracks, is indicative of the brittle nature of the AlON ceramics. The slip bands confirmed in Fig. S5 in the ESM suggest that even under high strain rates, the material accommodates some of the applied stress through crystallographic slip, contributing to plastic deformation. Additionally, Fig. 8(a) shows that the distance between adjacent slip planes gradually increases from the near surface to the interior of the material around the indentation. The thickness of the slip band (defined by the adjacent slip planes visible in the TEM images) ranges from approximately 100 to 400 nm. This observation is consistent with the presence of a strong strain gradient beneath the indenter [48].

To investigate more details about the microstructural changes within the indented crystals, enlarged images were taken from the different deformed regions. The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images (Figs. 8(b1)–8(b3)) taken from the white-framed regions in Figs. 8(a1)–8(a3) show the presence of nanosized subgrains with an average size of ~50 nm. Notably, significant misorientation among slip bands can be characterized by the existence of satellite spots and splitting spots in the SAED patterns (exhibited in the the insets in Fig. 8).

When the deformation of a material is dominated by dislocation motion, a higher strain rate results in stronger viscous drag on the dislocations, leading to increased material strength [49]. However, in this work, the static hardness is greater than the dynamic hardness for the AlON crystals, as shown in Fig. 6, which is different from the strain rate hardening response of the deformation of the crystalline material by dislocation slip. Recently, amorphization has become an important plastic deformation mechanism for ceramics, which can help to release the imparted strain energy and is responsible for strain rate softening. For example, local amorphization during dynamic indentation has been shown to reduce the dynamic hardness of B4C ceramics compared with their static hardness [50]. Therefore, we can infer that the occurrence of strain rate softening responses in the AlON single crystals can be attributed to the amorphization mechanism. Furthermore, these TEM analyses reveal that nanoscale amorphous regions coexist with nanocrystals, which is similar to the features observed in gadolinium zirconate ceramics [51]. This suggests that amorphization in the AlON single crystals is the ultimate stage of plasticity and is related to an accumulation of crystal defects, which follow a sequence of microstructural evolution: dislocation slip, nanocrystallization, and then amorphization. Therefore, the dynamic plasticity of the AlON single crystal may be controlled by a combination of mechanisms, including conventional dislocation motion and amorphization.

Some previous works have suggested that AlON transparent ceramics exhibit a twinning mechanism when subjected to impact events [14,52,53], which seemingly disagrees with our results. There are two main reasons for this discrepancy. First, postmortem characterization of recovered fragments is a popular method for characterizing deformation/failure mechanisms during impact tests, which frequently leads to controversial conclusions because of the unknown and uncontrollable loading experience of individual ceramic debris produced by impact events. Furthermore, the phenomenon of the formation of growth twinning in the AlON transparent ceramic has been confirmed in the sintering process [54,55]. Thus, it is plausible that the deformation twinning observed in the published works originates from the pristine sample owing to the unknown absence of twins prior to testing, as no EBSD or TEM analyses were conducted on the pristine samples in these studies. Second, twinning in crystals serves as an alternative mechanism for achieving plastic deformation. Unlike dislocation slip, which involves movement in multiples of the Burgers vector along specific planes, twinning results from homogeneous shear deformation, in which the crystal changes shape at fractions of the atomic displacement vector in all planes. Consequently, deformation twinning is particularly prevalent in crystals with low crystallographic symmetry, including hexagonal close-packed (HCP) and body-centered cubic (BCC) materials. In crystals with higher symmetry, such as FCC materials, deformation twinning is frequently captured under loading conditions of low temperatures or extreme strain rates. Another reason for the absence of deformation twins in our results could be that the strain rates in our nanoimpact experiments were not high enough to induce the formation of twins.

### 4.2 Effect of crystallographic orientation on dynamic hardness

As mentioned before, significant orientation dependence of the dynamic hardness can be observed in the AlON single crystal. Many static indentation experiments have proposed that the orientation dependence of static hardness can be described on the basis of the activation of multiple slip system families [31,56,57]. As revealed by the electronic microscopy analysis after impact, multiple slip planes appeared to be active in the indented AlON single crystals, which show simple slip traces, although the stress distribution underneath the indenter is very complex. This suggests that the crystallographic orientation dependence of the dynamic hardness can also be explained by the Schmid law. According to this law, dislocation slip tends to occur along the slip systems that possess the highest Schmid factors.

Unlike in uniaxial loading conditions, indentation stress fields are far more complex than uniaxial fields [58]. Hence, many simplified models have been developed for calculating the Schmid factors during indentation. Among these models, the easy-slip model has been demonstrated to be in good agreement with the orientation dependence of hardness, in which the plastic deformation around the indenter during indentation is restricted to the close neighboring region of the tip [57,59]. In addition, the plastic stress is assumed to be homogeneous and has only one component of the stress tensor, which is compressive and acts perpendicular to the indenter surface. Only a slight difference between the results of the friction contact model and the frictionless contact model has been demonstrated [60]. Specifically, as shown in Fig. 9(a), the plastic stress field around the Berkovich indenter is appropriately modeled by a conical tip with a cone semiangle * γ= 65°. The elementary forces ( dF) are assumed to act perpendicular to the indenter surface. The stress field inside the material is considered equivalent to thw uniaxial compression with the axis perpendicular to the elementary area ( dA), and it is assumed to be homogenous around the indenter ( dF/dA= constant). In addition, only a single dislocation slip is considered to characterize the initial plasticity. The dislocations are assumed to be equally distributed, and slips are initiated only on those slip planes in those slip directions where the local shear stresses reach the critical resolved shear stress, which is constant for the material.

### 4.3 Indentation size effect

Like the static hardness, as shown in Fig. 6, the dynamic hardness significantly decreases with increasing depth. This reveals a strong ISE. ISE in crystalline materials results from the existence of a strain gradient underneath the indenter tip, which is accommodated by the creation of geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) [60−66]. On the basis of the Taylor mechanism, the N‒G model developed by Nix and Gao [67] can capture the ISE in crystalline materials well via Eq. (10):

(10) H2=H02+k1h

where * H is the measured hardness;  H0 is the bulk hardness, which is independent of depth;  k is a material-dependent coefficient that can be used to characterize the degree of ISE. As shown in Fig. 10, both the depth-dependent dynamic and static hardnesses can be well captured by Eq. (10), with good R*-square values (* R2\gt  0.9). For the dynamic hardness, the fitting parameters ( k) of the (111), (101)-, and (010)-oriented crystals are 691.27, 686.58, and 667.52  (GPa)2∙μm, respectively, which are significantly greater than those (31.37, 28.94, and 28.19  (GPa)2∙μm) of the static hardness. This finding indicates that the depth-dependent dynamic hardness can still be described as a geometric effect related to the GND density. This is a logical argument because the dynamic plastic deformation of AlON single crystals under nanoimpact is controlled mainly by dislocation motion on the basis of the above microstructural analysis. In addition, the calculated depth-independent dynamic bulk hardness values are 13.05, 12.92, and 12.84 GPa for the (111)-, (101)-, and (010)-oriented AlON crystals, respectively, which are lower than the static bulk hardness values (18.14, 18.02, and 17.89 GPa). The lower depth-independent dynamic bulk hardness suggests that amorphization also plays an important role during nanoimpacting. As a result, the dynamic hardness of single AlON crystals simultaneously exhibits indentation size and strain rate effects.

Recently, Liang and Pharr [68] suggested that the indentation size and strain rate effects in some materials can be decoupled in a simple way, as shown in Eq. (11):

(11) H(ε˙,h)=Hf(ε˙)+[H0−Hf(ε˙)]1 +h∗h

where H* (ε˙,h) is the hardness at a given indentation strain rate ( ε˙) and depth ( h);  Hf(ε˙) is the hardness contributed only by the rate-dependent friction stress;  [H0−Hf(ε˙)] and  h* are the size- and rate-independent constants that follow from the N–G model, respectively. As mentioned before, amorphization also plays an important role in the dynamic plasticity of AlON single crystals and results in strain rate softening. However, as shown in Fig. 10, the depth-dependent dynamic hardness can be well described by the N‒G model, which indicates that the size dependence arises from the geometric effects related to the GND density. Therefore, we can simply suppose that the softening effect from amorphization contributes to only item  H0. As a result, ISEs (values for  Hf(ε˙),  H0, and  h* listed in the inset of Fig. 11) predicted by Eq. (11) agree well with the experimental data (Fig. 10). Notably, for the static hardness,  Hf(ε˙) is set to 0 without the rate effect.

On the basis of the dynamic hardness data, the calculated size and rate-independent hardness values ( [H0−Hf(ε˙)]) are 10.81, 9.89, and 9.73 GPa for the (111)-, (101)-, and (010)-oriented AlON crystals, respectively, which are lower than the static bulk hardness values (18.14, 18.02, and 17.89 GPa). This is in accordance with our hypothesis, as the softening effect from amorphization is rate independent and contributes to only  H0. In addition, our results also demonstrated that the ISE of the dynamic hardness in AlON single crystals can be decoupled into the rate-independent size effect item,  [H0−Hf(ε˙)]1 +h/h∗, related to the GND density and the size-independent rate hardening item,  Hf(ε˙), contributed to the rate-dependent lattice friction, although the dynamic plasticity of the AlON single crystal is controlled by a combination of mechanisms, including conventional dislocation motion and amorphization.

## 5 Conclusions

Systematic investigations of the dynamic mechanical response, along with microstructural characterization via SEM and TEM, were conducted on single crystals of AlON with different crystallographic orientations ((010), (101), and (111)) at room temperature. The dynamic plastic behavior and deformation mechanism can be described as follows:

(1) During the dynamic deformation of AlON single crystals, plasticity is controlled by a combination of mechanisms, including dislocation motion and amorphization. Significantly, the localized amorphization induced by mechanical deformation causes mechanical softening (a lower dynamic hardness).

(2) Crystallographic orientation affects the dynamic hardness in a similar manner to the static hardness, with (010) being the lowest and (111) the strongest. This correlates with expectations from considerations using Schmid law.

(3) Both the dynamic and static hardness values represent typical ISEs, which can be effectively described via the strain gradient theory associated with the geometrically necessary dislocations. Moreover, the ISE of the dynamic hardness of AlON single crystals can be decoupled into the rate-independent size effect,  [H0−Hf(ε˙)]1 +h/h∗,which is related to the GND density, and the size-independent rate hardening item,  Hf(ε˙), which contributes to rate-dependent lattice friction.
