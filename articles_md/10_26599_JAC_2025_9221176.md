## 1 Introduction

Tantalum carbide (TaC), an ultrahigh-temperature ceramic, is an important material in the field of aerospace thermal protection because of its extremely high melting point (3880 °C), high-temperature mechanical stability, corrosion resistance, oxidation resistance, and ablation resistance [1−3]. High-quality TaC powders are essential for the fabrication of high-performance TaC ceramics. The conventional synthesis method for TaC powders involves the carbothermal reduction of Ta2O5 powders. Shimada *et al*. [4] reacted activated carbon powders with Ta2O5, successfully synthesizing TaC powders at 1225 °C by enclosing a graphite crucible to retain CO. However, this approach necessitated an excess carbon source (C/Ta2O5 ≥ 8), which compromises product purity. Tang [5] reduced the carbon and oxygen contents in TaC powders through secondary vacuum carbonization. Nevertheless, the excessively high temperature used during carbothermal reduction (> 1700 °C) resulted in larger particle sizes (1.2–1.3 μm) for the synthesized TaC powders. To increase the efficiency of the carbothermal reduction process, Eick *et al*. [6] utilized liquid asphalt as a substitute for graphite, achieving a lower reaction temperature of 1300 °C because of the improved wetting of Ta2O5 powders by asphalt. However, asphalt contains impurities such as Al and Fe, necessitating acid washing for their removal. Du *et al*. [7] and Li *et al*. [8] synthesized amorphous carbon nitride (a-C3N4) and graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) as carbon sources, respectively, enabling low-temperature TaC powder synthesis. Nonetheless, the complex synthesis processes associated with C3N4 and the introduction of nitrogen impurities limit their practical applicability.

To solve the issues of high reaction temperature, excessive grain size, and low purity in solid-phase carbothermal reduction, the precursor-derived method has been applied for TaC powder preparation [9]. This method involves synthesizing a metal-organic polymer containing the target elements in a solution system, followed by cross-linking and pyrolysis to obtain ceramic powders [10]. When TaC powders are prepared via the precursor-derived method, the TaC precursor structure can be precisely designed, significantly reducing the elemental diffusion distance. This approach effectively reduces the pyrolysis temperature of TaC powders, mitigates problems related to excessive grain growth, and enhances the purity of the resulting powders.

The current synthesis method for TaC precursors primarily involves the hydrolysis and condensation of tantalum alkoxide complexes. In the process of synthesizing tantalum alkoxide complex precursors, TaCl5 is mainly used as the metal source, which reacts with alcohol through a substitution reaction to yield tantalum alkoxide. Owing to the vigorous hydrolysis of tantalum alkoxide, ligands such as acetylacetone are employed to react with it, forming complexes that facilitate controlled hydrolysis and condensation. Additionally, to ensure sufficient carbothermal reduction in subsequent reactions, a carbon source such as phenolic resin is often added to the precursor solution [11]. This method not only allows for the preparation of single-phase TaC using tantalum alkoxide complexes [12,13] but also enables the formation of Ta(Hf)C solid solutions by introducing hafnium alkoxide complexes [14−16] or TaC–SiC powders by incorporating silicon-containing polymers [17,18]. The disadvantages of the hydrolysis–condensation method for accessing tantalum alkoxide are as follows: First, the cross-linking reaction relies on the hydroxyl groups produced from controllable hydrolysis. Typically, each tantalum alkoxide monomer can only produce two cross-linking sites, resulting in a relatively low degree of precursor cross-linking. Second, the tantalum source and carbon source are mixed into a precursor solution through mechanical stirring without achieving the desired elemental mixing within the precursor molecules. Furthermore, most researchers have utilized synchronous thermal analysis to delineate the temperature ranges of various pyrolysis stages of the precursors. However, few studies have explored the mechanism of carbothermal reduction [13,15,17].

In response to the aforementioned issues, this study employed 1-penten-3-ol, an unsaturated alcohol, as a substitute for saturated alcohols in the synthesis of the tantalum source. The selection of 1-penten-3-ol as the unsaturated alcohol is primarily based on three aspects. First, in terms of safety, compared with short-chain enols such as allyl alcohol, the 5-carbon 1-penten-3-ol has a higher boiling point, lower volatility, and lower toxicity [19]. Second, regarding cost-effectiveness, 1-penten-3-ol is lower in cost than medium-chain-length unsaturated alcohols such as 3-buten-1-ol, 4-penten-1-ol, or 4-penten-2-ol. Third, in terms of reaction compatibility, its terminal double bond with low steric hindrance and middle hydroxyl group enables stable Ta–O bonding together, facilitating efficient coordination with tantalum, which ensures the structural integrity of the monomer. Additionally, chloroprene was introduced to modify the linear phenolic resin. By incorporating C=C bonds into both the tantalum source and the carbon source, the number of crosslinking sites within the precursor increased. A novel TaC precursor, PTC-pentene (polytantalcarbane synthesized from 1-penten-3-ol), was synthesized through a free radical copolymerization reaction. This approach successfully facilitated the bonding of tantalum and carbon sources within the molecular structure of the precursor. Furthermore, via thermal analysis combined with reaction kinetics analysis, the carbothermal reduction reaction mechanism involved in the preparation of TaC powders via the precursor-derived method was elucidated. As a result, high-purity nanoscale TaC powders have been produced. Currently, TaC is typically combined with pyrolytic carbon (PyC), SiC, HfC, and ZrC to form a matrix of ultrahigh-temperature ceramic matrix composites (UHTCMCs) [20−25]. When evaluating their ablation performance, the analyzed ablation mechanisms are generally attributed to the combined effects of multiple phases. Few studies have focused on the ablation behavior of single-phase TaC ceramics. In this study, after the synthesis of TaC powders, TaC ceramics were prepared via spark plasma sintering (SPS). The ablation mechanisms of TaC ceramics were analyzed following oxyacetylene ablation tests for various durations. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for the application of TaC in aerospace thermal protection systems.

### 2.1 Materials

Commercially available tantalum pentachloride powder (TaCl5) with an average particle size of 500 nm (purity 99.99%, Shanghai Shuitian Materials Technology Co., Ltd., China) and 1-penten-3-ol (purity > 97%, Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) were used to synthesize the tantalum source monomer. Linear phenolic resin (purity BR, Shanghai Maiklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China), chloropropene (purity 98%, Shanghai Maiklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China), n-butanol (purity AR, Shanghai Maiklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China), and KOH (purity AR, China National Pharmaceutical Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) were used to synthesize allyl novolac resin containing C=C bonds (AN) as the carbon source. Lauroyl peroxide (LPO; purity 98%, Shanghai Maiklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) was the initiator for the free radical copolymerization reaction. N2 and Ar (purity of 99.999%, Changsha Mande Gas Co., Ltd., China) served as protective atmospheres during the precursor synthesis and pyrolysis processes.

### 2.2 Synthesis and pyrolysis of PTC-pentene precursor

Linear phenolic resin and n-butanol were added to a three-neck flask. The mixture was heated to 70 °C under continuous stirring for 2 h to ensure complete dissolution of the resin. Subsequently, KOH was introduced as a catalyst. The temperature was then raised to 80 °C and maintained for 1 h, after which the system was cooled to 40 °C. Chloropropene was added dropwise to the flask. Upon complete addition, the temperature was increased again to 80 °C, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 6 h until significant precipitation was observed. The product was filtered under suction to obtain a clear solution, which was then washed three times with distilled water. Finally, the majority of the solvent was removed via vacuum distillation to afford a red solution of allyl novolac resin, designated AN.

Owing to the high susceptibility of TaCl5 to hydrolysis in moist air, the synthesis of PTC-pentene was carried out under a nitrogen atmosphere via oxygen-free and anhydrous Schlenk techniques. At room temperature, 1-penten-3-ol was slowly introduced into a three-neck flask, followed by the gradual addition of TaCl5 powder. After reacting for 1 h under continuous stirring, AN resin was added to the mixture. Stirring was continued for another 0.5 h to ensure homogeneous mixing of the tantalum alkoxide monomer and the AN resin. Then, 5 wt% lauroyl peroxide was added as an initiator, and the reaction was conducted at 80 °C for 1 h, resulting in a cross-linked solid product denoted as the PTC-pentene precursor. The carbon source addition ratio was expressed in terms of the *m*(AN) : *m*(TaCl5) value. For example, when *m*(AN) : *m*(TaCl5) = 0.5, the resulting precursor was named PTC-pentene-0.5. The obtained precursor was further heat-treated at 200 °C for 1 h to promote additional cross-linking and remove any residual solvent.

The cross-linked PTC-pentene was ground into a fine powder and transferred to a pyrolysis furnace. Under an argon flow of 2 L·min−1, the temperature was increased to 800 °C at a rate of 5 °C·min−1 and held for 1 h to facilitate inorganic transformation and crystallization of the precursor. The temperature was subsequently raised again at 5  °C·min−1 to a target temperature between 1000 and 1500 °C and maintained for 2 h to allow carbothermal reduction to proceed. Finally, the sample was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature under an argon atmosphere.

### 2.3 Preparation and ablation of TaC ceramics

The TaC powders derived from the pyrolysis of PTC-pentene precursors were loaded into a graphite mold and sintered via SPS under an Ar atmosphere. The powders were first cold-pressed at 50 MPa and then heated to 1800 °C at a rate of 100 °C·min−1 while the pressure was simultaneously increased to 100 MPa. After both the temperature and pressure were maintained for 15 min to complete the sintering process, the pressure was released, and the samples were furnace-cooled to obtain the final TaC ceramics. The TaC ceramics were subsequently subjected to oxyacetylene ablation testing. The parameters for the ablation process are summarized in Table 1. The TaC ceramics were subjected to ablation for 10, 60, and 120 s and designated TaC-10, TaC-60, and TaC-120, respectively.

### 2.4 Characterizations

The functional groups contained in the precursor were characterized by a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FT-IR; Avatar 360, Nicolet, USA), with a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1. A nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer (1H NMR; Avance NEO 500M, Bruker, Switzerland) was used to determine the molecular structure of the precursor. The precursor was dissolved with deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-D6) as the solvent, and the chemical shift range was 0–7 ppm. A gel permeation chromatograph (GPC; PL-GPC50, Agilent, USA) with tetrahydrofuran (THF) as the mobile phase was used to measure the molecular weight distribution of the precursor at 1 mL·min−1 and 40 °C. The pyrolysis process of the precursor was investigated by a simultaneous thermogravimetric analyzer and differential scanning calorimeter (TG–DSC; Themys H2, Setaram, France) in an Ar atmosphere (20 mL·min−1) and heating to 1500 °C at 10 °C·min−1. The phase composition of the pyrolysis products of the precursor was determined via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; SmartLab SE, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA) over a 2*θ* range of 10°–90° and a scanning speed of 20°·min−1. Chemical states were examined via an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; AXIS SUPRA+, Shimadzu, Japan) with Al Kα radiation (15 kV). The total carbon content and free carbon content of the ceramic powders were measured via a carbon/sulfur analyzer (EMIA-320V2, HORIBA, Japan). The oxygen content was measured via an oxygen/nitrogen analyzer (EMGR-820, HORIBA, Japan). The microscopic morphology of the samples was characterized via a 3–10 kV scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU8100, Hitachi Corporation, Japan) and a 200 kV transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2 F30, FEI, USA). After the powders were ultrasonically dispersed with ethanol, the particle size distribution was statistically analyzed via a nanoparticle size analyzer (Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern, UK).

### 3.1 Characterization of PTC-pentene precursor

To investigate the structural differences between the modified allyl novolac (AN) resin and phenol-formaldehyde (PF) resin, FT-IR characterization was performed on both resins, with the infrared spectra shown in Fig. 1(a). The phenolic O–H stretching vibration at 3351 cm−1 and C–O–H stretching at 1149 cm−1 showed markedly reduced intensities in AN resin, indicating the consumption of phenolic hydroxyl groups through reactions with allyl chloride. The increased peak intensities at 2933 cm−1 (asymmetric CH2 stretching) and 2873 cm−1 (symmetric CH2 stretching) in the AN resin suggested increased methylene content, likely originating from allyl chloride [26]. The successful incorporation of allyl groups was confirmed by characteristic vibrations at 3017 cm−1 (alkenyl C–H stretching), 1645 cm−1 (C=C stretching), 997 cm−1 (alkenyl CH2 twisting), and 929 cm−1 (alkenyl CH2 out-of-plane wagging) [27]. Owing to its polar protic nature, n-butanol induced significant solvation effects. These effects preferentially guided the allylation of allyl chloride to the ortho- and para-carbon positions of the phenoxide anions rather than the oxygen atom. As a result, the formation of the Ph–CH2–CH=CH2 structural motif was favored [28]. The preservation of aromatic ring vibrations at 1596, 1510, 1478, and 1456 cm−1 in both PF and AN demonstrated that the aromatic skeleton remained intact after modification. Critical evidence for Williamson ether synthesis was observed through asymmetric (1240 cm−1) and symmetric (1069 cm−1) aryl ether stretching vibrations. The reaction proceeded through the following steps. First, KOH deprotonated the phenolic –OH group to generate a phenolate anion. This phenolate species then acts as a nucleophile, attacking the electrophilic carbon atom adjacent to the chlorine in allyl chloride via an SN2 mechanism [29]. The nucleophilic substitution resulted in the formation of a new Ph–O–C aryl ether bond while simultaneously expelling the chloride ion as a leaving group. The relative intensities of para-substituted (816 cm−1) versus ortho-substituted (755 cm−1) aromatic C–H stretching vibrations indicated predominant ortho-substitution patterns in both resins [26]. In summary, a comparative analysis of the FT-IR spectra of PF and AN demonstrated that allyl chloride underwent a nucleophilic substitution reaction with phenolic hydroxyl groups, resulting in the successful incorporation of allyl into the phenolic resin.

The incorporation of allyl groups introduced C=C bonds into the phenolic resin, serving as effective crosslinking sites that underwent additional polymerization during the curing process. This transformed the linear phenolic resin into a three-dimensional network structure, thereby enhancing its thermal stability. TG analysis was performed on both PF and AN under an Ar atmosphere, and the resulting thermograms are presented in Fig. 1(b). The PF resin exhibited a 20.4% mass loss from room temperature to 250 °C, which was attributed to the polycondensation of hydroxyl and methylol groups with the concomitant release of H2O, followed by a minor 1.5% loss between 250 and 350 °C [30]. From 350 to 850 °C, a significant weight loss of 22.7% occurred as the ether bonds connecting the phenyl rings and methylene groups broke, leading to carbonization of the phenolic resin. The stage from 850 to 1000 °C marked the completion of carbonization, with an insignificant weight loss of only 0.5%. At a temperature of 1000 °C, the residual carbon content of the PF resin was 54.9%. In contrast, the AN resin demonstrated superior thermal stability, with only 0.7% mass loss below 360 °C due to the absence of polycondensation reactions resulting from prior hydroxyl etherification. The primary decomposition stage occurred between 350 and 800 °C (39.2% mass loss) through the cleavage of ether and methylene bonds, culminating in a higher char yield of 60.1% at 1000 °C. This modified crosslinking mechanism, which transitions from –OH polycondensation to C=C addition polymerization, not only improved the thermal resistance but also reduced the degree of pyrolysis mass loss.

The FT-IR spectrum of PTC-pentene is presented in Fig. 2(a). The O–H stretching vibration at 3357 cm−1 and C–OH stretching at 1119 cm−1 were attributed to unreacted 1-penten-3-ol. The characteristic ester C=O stretching vibration observed at 1729 cm−1 was attributed to cage products formed through side reactions of lauroyloxy radicals generated from the decomposition of lauroyl peroxide. These radicals are spatially confined by the cage effect, which hinders their complete diffusion and consequently leads to the formation of these cage products [31]. The C=C stretching vibration detected at 1640 cm−1 indicated the presence of unsaturated double bonds in the PTC that did not fully participate in the crosslinking reactions. The Ta–O stretching vibration at 612 cm−1 confirmed the formation of tantalum alkoxide monomers [13]. Characteristic vibrations of the benzene ring skeleton were identified at 1619, 1488, and 1458 cm−1. The asymmetric C–O–C stretching vibration of aromatic ethers generated by phenol etherification was detected at 1246 cm−1. The presence of these characteristic peaks verified the successful synthesis of the PTC-pentene precursor through free radical copolymerization between AN resin and tantalum alkoxide monomers.

The PTC-pentene was further characterized by 1H NMR spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 2(b). The resonance peak at 2.5 ppm was assigned to the solvent signal of DMSO. The multiple resonance peaks observed in the range of 0.8–1.6 ppm were assigned to the protons of methylene groups (–CH2–) in saturated carbon chains. These saturated carbon chains were formed through addition reactions of C=C bonds and served as the backbone connecting tantalum alkoxide monomers with AN. Resonance peaks appearing at 3.2–3.5 and 3.6–4.1 ppm were identified as protons on the 3-position carbon of allyl groups attached to benzene rings and methylene bridges between benzene rings, respectively, which confirmed the successful incorporation of AN resin into the PTC molecular structure. The low-intensity resonance peaks at 4.9–5.2 and 5.7–5.9 ppm were attributed to the protons at the C1 and C2 positions of terminal C=C bonds, respectively, demonstrating incomplete polymerization and partial crosslinking of the precursor at 80 °C.

XPS analysis was performed to investigate the chemical states of the elements in PTC-pentene, and the results are presented in Fig. 3. The full spectrum (Fig. 3(a)) confirmed the presence of four elements: C, O, Cl, and Ta. The high-resolution C 1s spectrum (Fig. 3(b)) exhibited two characteristic peaks at 284.8 and 286.2 eV, corresponding to C–C and C–O bonds, respectively [32]. In PTC-pentene, C–O bonds exclusively originated from the Ta–O–C structure in the tantalum alkoxides and ether linkages in the AN resin. The C–Ta peak at 283 eV was not observed, indicating that no direct C–Ta bonding existed in the precursor. The O 1s spectrum (Fig. 3(c)) revealed two components at 530.5 eV (Ta–O) and 532.3 eV (C–O). The weaker intensity of the Ta–O peak relative to the C–O peak resulted from its exclusive presence in tantalum alkoxides, while C–O bonds were distributed in both the alkoxide and ether groups of the AN resin. In the Cl 2p spectrum (Fig. 3(d)), the doublets at 198.7 eV (Cl 2p1/2) and 200.2 eV (Cl 2p3/2) were attributed to residual Ta–Cl bonds. The inability to completely replace Cl was attributed to the stepwise nature of the substitution reaction, coupled with steric hindrance effects present among the enol molecules. As a result, after the formation of tantalum alkoxide salts with 3−4 functional groups, the difficulty in continuing the reaction increased. The Ta 4f spectrum (Fig. 3(e)) shows characteristic doublet peaks at 26.4 eV (Ta 4f5/2) and 28.3 eV (Ta 4f7/2), corresponding to Ta–O bonds. The absence of Ta–C peaks at 23.3 and 25.2 eV [33] provided further evidence that Ta was not directly bonded to C in PTC-pentene.

The molecular weight of PTC-pentene was characterized by GPC, and the results are shown in Fig. 4(a). In the GPC plot, dw*/dlog*M* represented the molecular weight distribution, where *M* was the molecular weight and *w* was the weight fraction of polymer chains at that *M*. The analysis revealed that 67% of the product had a molecular weight less than 1000 g·mol−1, with number-average (*M*n) and weight-average (*M*w) molecular weights of 531 and 1166 g·mol−1, respectively. These results indicated that the free radical copolymerization at 80 °C primarily produced oligomers with a degree of polymerization between 2 and 3. The polydispersity index (PDI) of 2.20 was characteristic of free-radical polymerization, suggesting the occurrence of chain-transfer and chain-termination reactions during the process. At 80 °C, the precursor underwent only partial crosslinking. Further heating to 200 °C for 1 h converted PTC-pentene into an insoluble and infusible solid. The resulting precursor powders after grinding are shown in Fig. 4(b).

In summary, the proposed synthesis mechanism of PTC-pentene is illustrated in Fig. 4(c). Owing to steric hindrance effects, the tantalum alkoxide monomers predominantly underwent tri- or tetra-substitution. The allyl groups in AN resin could be connected either to phenolic –OH groups or directly to the benzene ring skeleton. When the lauroyl peroxide initiator was added and the temperature was increased to 80 °C, the initiator decomposed to generate free radicals, which induced free radical copolymerization between the tantalum alkoxide monomers and AN resin. This process achieves homogeneous mixing of tantalum and carbon sources at the molecular level within the precursor. At this stage, most PTC molecules exist as oligomers, and residual C=C bonds remain in PTC-pentene. Upon further heating to 200 °C, the crosslinking reaction rate increased significantly. The C=C bonds underwent addition polymerization without requiring radical initiation. The residual phenolic –OH groups in the AN resin participated in polycondensation reactions with the release of H2O. Ultimately, an insoluble and infusible TaC precursor was formed.

### 3.2 Pyrolysis mechanism of PTC-pentene precursor

To investigate the structural evolution of PTCs during inorganic transformation, FT-IR analyses were performed on PTC-butene samples pyrolyzed at 80, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 °C, and the results are presented in Fig. 5(a). Comparative analysis of the spectra between 80 and 200 °C revealed a decreased intensity of the C=C stretching vibration at 1640 cm−1, indicating continued addition polymerization of residual C=C bonds during curing, which further increased the degree of crosslinking. When heated to 300 °C, the precursor underwent inorganic transformation. The C=C bond intensity progressively diminished until it nearly disappeared. The 400 °C stage markedly accelerated inorganic transformation, as evidenced by significant intensity reductions in multiple characteristic peaks: 3428 cm−1 (–OH), 2955/2822/2850/1379 cm−1 (–CH3/–CH2–), and 1616/1462 cm−1 (benzene skeleton), accompanied by substantial mass loss from volatile byproducts. By 500 °C, inorganic transformation was essentially complete, with only the prominent Ta–O vibration at 608 cm−1 remaining, indicating the conversion of organic carbon chains into inorganic free carbon. The 600 °C spectrum showed negligible changes from 500 °C, confirming the completion of inorganic transformation [15].

TG–DSC analysis of the 200 °C-cured PTC-pentene sample under an argon atmosphere is shown in Fig. 5(b). In the figure, the TG curve reflected the mass change of the sample during the programmed temperature control process, and the DTG curve was the first derivative of TG with respect to time, characterizing the mass change rate; while the DSC curve was used to measure the enthalpy changes associated with thermal effects. The initial stage from room temperature to 200 °C resulted in a 2.2% mass loss, primarily due to moisture evaporation and the completion of residual crosslinking reactions, accompanied by mild exothermic effects. Between 550 and 930 °C, the amorphous tantalum oxides crystallized into Ta2O5 with negligible mass changes but distinct exothermic peaks. The carbothermal reduction stage occurred between 930 and 1400 °C, during which Ta2O5 was reduced to TaC with concomitant release of CO (Eq. (1)) [13]. The reaction mechanism changed at approximately 1200 °C, leading to the appearance of a mass loss plateau. This stage exhibited a 12.6% mass loss for the PTC, accompanied by strongly endothermic behavior. Upon further heating to 1500 °C, PTC-pentene showed an additional 0.5% mass loss due to the final reduction in residual Ta2O5. The ceramic mass yield of PTC-pentene at 1400 °C reached 58.0%. This result was higher than those reported in previous studies: the ceramic yields of TaC precursors synthesized by Sun et al*. It was demonstrated that PTC-pentene exhibited lower pyrolysis mass loss and a higher degree of cross-linking.

### 3.3 Optimization of pyrolysis process for PTC-pentene precursor

After the carbothermal reduction mechanism of PTC-pentene was analyzed through reaction kinetics, the pyrolysis process of PTC was optimized. The key factors influencing the composition and structure of the pyrolysis products were identified as the carbon source addition ratio and pyrolysis temperature. The influence of the carbon source ratio on the crystalline phase composition of pyrolyzed PTC-pentene precursors was investigated through XRD characterization of products with varying carbon source ratios obtained at 1400 °C for 2 h, as shown in Fig. 7(a). For the PTC-pentene-0 sample, the pyrolysis products contained both Ta2O5 (PDF#89-2843) and TaC (PDF #35-0801) phases. Analysis of the magnified Ta2O5 (001) diffraction plane in Fig. 7(b) demonstrated progressively decreasing peak intensities with increasing AN resin content until no Ta2O5 phase was detected when the *m*(AN) : *m*(TaCl5) ratio reached ≥ 0.6. Therefore, the PTC-pentene-0.6 precursor could be pyrolyzed to obtain TaC powders with no Ta2O5 phase and the lowest free carbon content. Further XRD characterization of PTC-pentene-0.6 at different pyrolysis temperatures (Figs. 7(c) and 7(d)) revealed distinct phase evolution stages. At 800 °C, only weak Ta2O5 peaks appeared, indicating predominantly amorphous material. The 900 °C sample presented increased but broadened Ta2O5 peaks, suggesting the onset of crystallization. The first detectable TaC peaks emerged at 1000 °C, although their intensity remained much weaker than that of Ta2O5, confirming the initial stage of carbothermal reduction. These observations demonstrated that Ta exclusively bonded with oxygen in the PTC structure and that TaC formation required carbothermal reduction rather than direct pyrolysis. From 1100 to 1200 °C, the TaC peak intensities increased significantly, whereas the Ta2O5 peak intensities decreased, corresponding to the first stage of carbothermal reduction. Ta2O5 completely disappeared at 1225 °C, indicating that the beginning of the second stage and holding time could effectively substitute for a further temperature increase. Throughout subsequent heating to 1500 °C, no Ta2O5 phase was detected in the pyrolysis products, which consisted exclusively of the TaC phase.

The pyrolysis process of the PTC-pentene precursor is described in Fig. 10 and progresses through three consecutive stages. Initially, between 200 and 550 °C, inorganic transformation occurred as the precursor backbone underwent cleavage, releasing H2O and small unsaturated hydrocarbons while the organic precursor was converted into an amorphous ceramic containing uncrystallized tantalum oxide embedded in free carbon. During 550–930 °C, the crystallization stage subsequently occurred, where the amorphous tantalum oxide transformed into the crystalline Ta2O5 phase. The final carbothermal reduction stage (930–1400 °C) proceeded through two distinct mechanisms. The initial stage (930–1255 °C) exhibited 1.5-order chemical kinetics, with an average activation energy of 224.948 kJ·mol−1 as free carbon directly reduced Ta2O5. The subsequent stage (1255–1400 °C) was jointly controlled by 3D diffusion and phase-boundary reactions with a relatively high activation energy of 376.214 kJ·mol−1, necessitating carbon diffusion through the formed TaC layers to reach the remaining Ta2O5 interfaces. The PTC-pentene-0.6 precursor yielded the highest-quality TaC particles after pyrolysis at 1400 °C for 2 h. The resulting particles, with an average particle size of 140 nm, were encapsulated by a 2-nm-thick free carbon shell and presented an oxygen content of 0.40 wt%.

### 3.4 Preparation of TaC ceramics

TaC powders, obtained by pyrolyzing PTC-pentene-0.6 at 1400 °C for 2 h, were used as the raw material to prepare dense TaC ceramics via SPS sintering. The sintering schedule was previously described by Liu [40]. A high sintering pressure of 100 MPa significantly enhances TaC densification, and a processing temperature of 1800 °C prevents the excessive grain coarsening observed at higher temperatures. As shown in Fig. 11(a), the SPS process of TaC particles can be divided into four stages. The first stage was the cold-pressing stage, where the temperature was maintained at 300 °C and the pressure was linearly increased to 50 MPa. At this stage, the main occurrence was the rearrangement of TaC particles. The axial pressure forced the particles into closer contact, leading to a reduction in interparticle pores. The second stage was the temperature-increasing and pressure-increasing stage. The sintering temperature was increased from 300 to 1800 °C at a rate of 100 °C·min−1, and the pressure was increased to 100 MPa. The temperature of 1150 °C had not yet reached the sintering temperature of TaC. The increase in relative density was attributed mainly to the increased electrical resistance at the contact areas of the TaC particles, which caused local high temperatures and thus softened the particle surfaces. Moreover, the continuously increasing pressure induced plastic yielding, prompting further rearrangement and sliding of the particles. When the temperature was raised to 1500 °C, the pressure reached 91.7 MPa. Higher temperatures and pressures accelerated the densification process. After rearrangement, the particles came into sufficient contact and began to form sintering necks [41]. As the temperature and pressure were further increased to 1800 °C and 100 MPa, the TaC particles started to sinter. High temperature and pressure provided the driving force for diffusion, and the densification process was jointly controlled by grain boundary diffusion and sliding. The particles underwent plastic deformation to form tetrakaidecahedrons, and cylindrical pores formed at the boundaries, serving as vacancy sources for further sintering [40]. The third stage was the temperature and pressure holding stage, during which diffusion and grain boundary sliding continued. The cylindrical pores between the particles shrank into closed pores and gathered at the vertices of the tetrakaidecahedrons. Moreover, grain growth caused by grain boundary diffusion was also observed. The fourth stage was the cooling and pressure-relief stage. As the temperature and pressure decreased, the sintering driving force gradually weakened.

The sintered TaC ceramics were polished and then subjected to SEM analysis, and the results are presented in Fig. 11(b). All the TaC grains exhibited an irregular polyhedral morphology. A small number of pores were distributed at the grain boundaries, whereas no intragranular pores were detected. The formation of intragranular pores in TaC was related to the oxygen impurity content. During the high-temperature sintering process, Ta2O5 melted into a liquid phase and dissolved TaC. Carbothermal reduction of Ta2O5 with C releases CO, leading to the formation of intragranular pores [42,43]. This indicated that the oxygen impurity content in the TaC powders was low.

To determine the grain size of the TaC ceramics, the grains in Fig. 11(b) were marked, and their diameters were measured. The frequency distribution of the grain sizes is shown in Fig. 11(c). The average grain size was 0.24 μm, and the maximum grain size did not exceed 0.65 μm, which was significantly smaller than the 3.16 μm reported by Liu [40]. Liquid Ta2O5 can wet the grain boundaries of TaC, resulting in intensified migration of TaC grain boundaries and abnormal growth of grains [44,45]. However, the TaC grains generated via PTC-pentene pyrolysis were encapsulated by a thin shell of free carbon, which increased the diffusion distance and reduced the diffusion driving force of TaC, thus inhibiting grain growth.

### 3.5 Ablation mechanisms of TaC ceramics

The ablation performance of TaC determines its effectiveness in thermal components for aerospace applications, making understanding its ablation mechanisms crucial. Oxyacetylene ablation tests were conducted on the TaC ceramics for durations of 10, 60, and 120 s to investigate the ablation mechanisms and morphological evolution of the ceramics during the initial, transitional, and stable stages of ablation.

Figure 12(a) shows the surface temperature profiles of TaC-10, TaC-60, and TaC-120 during ablation. All three samples heated rapidly upon exposure to the oxyacetylene flame, exceeding the melting point of Ta2O5 (1872 °C) within 6 s. TaC-10 reached its peak surface temperature (2090 °C) at the end of ablation. TaC-60 and TaC-120 stabilized at 2354 and 2301 °C, respectively, within 20 s, with fluctuations within 10 °C during prolonged ablation. Thermomechanical erosion occurs when shear forces from high-speed gas flow remove weakly bonded surface material. Thermophysical ablation results from melting, evaporation, or sublimation induced by extreme heat. Thermochemical ablation arises from oxidation reactions that release gases or form low-melting-point compounds. These mechanisms typically act simultaneously, but their dominance may vary at different ablation stages [46,47]. As shown in Fig. 12(b), TaC-10 exhibited negative ablation rates (−0.25 mg·s−1 and −7.81 μm·s−1), indicating mass and thickness gains. This was attributed to dominant thermochemical oxidation, where the lower density of Ta2O5 caused volumetric expansion. In contrast, TaC-60 showed positive rates (4.89 mg·s−1 and 16.47 μm·s−1) because of molten Ta2O5 layer removal by gas flow, exposing fresh TaC to repeated ablation. Figure 12(c) shows an image of the TaC-60 sample immediately after ablation testing, with visible white Ta2O5 droplets expelled to the sample edges. Higher rates (40.06 mg·s−1 and 20.33 μm·s−1) of TaC-120 confirmed intensified mass/thickness loss with prolonged exposure.

The XRD patterns of the ablated surfaces of the TaC-10, TaC-60, and TaC-120 samples are shown in Fig. 14. As shown in Fig. 14(a), the TaC-10 sample surface was completely oxidized with no detectable TaC phase. The pattern contained high-temperature stable tetragonal Ta2O5 (PDF#19-1300) [34], along with low-temperature stable orthorhombic Ta2O5 (PDF#89-2843) and monoclinic Ta2O5 (PDF#70-4775) [48−50]. During ablation, the surface temperature rapidly exceeded 2000 °C, where the oxide layer consisted of melted Ta2O5 and unmelted t-Ta2O5 crystals. After ablation, rapid cooling caused the Ta2O5 melt to solidify and crystallize, with partial transformation of high-temperature t-Ta2O5 to low-temperature phases. However, the predominance of t-Ta2O5 in the pattern demonstrated that this phase transformation, although reversible, proceeded slowly. The t-Ta2O5 exhibited a body-centered tetragonal structure. Owing to the lattice extinction rules, Bragg diffraction and corresponding XRD peaks for the (0 0 l*) crystal planes occurred only when *l* = 4*n* (where *l* was the Miller index and *n* was a positive integer) [51]. However, the relative peak intensities in the experimental XRD pattern significantly differed from those in the standard reference data. The (0 0 8), (0 0 12), (0 0 16), (0 0 20), (0 0 24), and (0 0 28) peaks from the (0 0 1) crystal plane exhibited much higher intensities than the other diffraction peaks. This anisotropy originated from the (0 0 1) crystal plane being the closest-packed surface of t-Ta2O5. During solidification of the Ta2O5 melt, crystal growth preferentially occurred along the most energetically stable orientation. The (0 0 1) crystal plane, with its highest atomic density and lowest energy state, became the dominant growth direction, a phenomenon also reported in the study by Liu *et al*. [52]. As shown in the XRD patterns of the ablated surfaces of TaC-60 and TaC-120 in Figs. 14(b) and 14(c), t-Ta2O5 is similarly identified as the predominant phase, exhibiting preferential growth along the (0 0 1) crystal plane. The o-Ta2O5 peaks showed minimal intensity, while m-Ta2O5 was undetectable, further confirming that most t-Ta2O5 was retained at room temperature due to rapid post-ablation cooling.

After 60 s of ablation, the surface oxide layer of the sample melted. Microcracks formed in the central area due to erosion by high-speed gas flow. The transition area, which was farther from the flame center, developed a relatively dense oxide layer. In the border area, the oxide layer exhibited a dendritic morphology. Ceramic cracks were filled and bonded by Ta2O5 melt. After 120 s, the central area depression deepened. The transition and border areas accumulated significant amounts of crystallized Ta2O5 melt transported from the central region.

## 4 Conclusions

This study synthesized a TaC precursor containing C=C bonds via free radical copolymerization, which increased the number of crosslinking sites of the precursor and achieved molecular-level mixing of Ta and C sources. The carbothermal reduction kinetics of the precursor were analyzed to guide the preparation of TaC powders. The obtained TaC powders were sintered by SPS into TaC ceramics, and their oxyacetylene ablation mechanism was investigated. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) An unsaturated tantalum alkoxide monomer was synthesized as the Ta source through the substitution reaction of TaCl5 with 1-penten-3-ol, while allyl novolac resin was prepared as the C source by introducing allyl chloride into linear phenolic resin. The TaC precursor was synthesized via free radical copolymerization.

(2) The carbothermal reduction process can be divided into two stages on the basis of the reaction kinetics. The first stage (930–1255 °C) was controlled by 1.5-order chemical reactions, whereas the second stage (1255–1400 °C) was jointly governed by three-dimensional diffusion and phase-boundary reactions. The ceramic yield of PTC-pentene at 1400 °C was 58.0%.

(3) The optimal carbon source addition ratio (*m*(AN) : *m*(TaCl5)) for PTC-pentene was 0.6. After 2 h of pyrolysis at 1400 °C, the generated TaC particles with an average particle size of 140 nm and an oxygen content of 0.40 wt% were encapsulated by a 2-nm-thick free carbon shell.

(4) Oxyacetylene ablation tests were conducted on TaC ceramics for 10, 60, and 120 s, with mass ablation rates of −0.25, 4.89, and 40.06 mg·s−1, respectively, and linear ablation rates of −7.81, 16.47, and 20.33 μm·s−1, respectively. During ablation, the TaC ceramic primarily resisted oxygen diffusion through a molten Ta2O5 surface layer, but high-speed gas flow caused erosion of the oxide layer. Simultaneously, thermal stress induced cracks in the TaC ceramic at high temperatures, while the flowing molten Ta2O5 acted to fill and bond these cracks.
