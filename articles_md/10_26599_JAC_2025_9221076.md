## 1 Introduction

Owing to its high Verdet constant, low dielectric loss, and narrow ferromagnetic resonance (FMR) linewidth, yttrium iron garnet (YIG) ferrite is an essential material widely used in microwave devices and modules of wireless communications and radars, including circulators, isolators, and filters. Highly efficient and integrated modern microwave circuit systems urgently call for devices and modules with smaller sizes and lower losses, which places more stringent requirements on the electromagnetic properties of the YIG ferrites used in these devices [1−6]. Generally, the insertion loss of microwave circulators is closely related to the dielectric loss and FMR linewidth of YIG ferrites, and the device size is proportional to the wavelength of the electromagnetic wave transmitted therein. For example, increasing the dielectric constant of traditional YIG ferrites from a typical value of 15–25 can effectively reduce the size of the circulator substrate by approximately 50%, thereby significantly improving device integration and weight reduction [7−9].

While compositing ferrites with high-permittivity dielectrics can increase the dielectric constant, it often leads to increased dielectric loss and a broadened FMR linewidth, limiting its practical application. This highlights the need for alternative approaches to optimize the electromagnetic properties of YIG ferrites. For example, Lin *et al*. [10] synthesized a Ni0.5Ti0.5NbO4–Bi0.4Y2.6Fe5O12 composite via a solid-state reaction route. The magnetodielectric composite possessed a high dielectric constant of 30 but a high dielectric loss tangent of 0.05, which was two orders of magnitude greater than the dielectric loss tangent of pure YIG ferrite.

Optimizing the polycrystalline microstructure of YIG ferrite via preparation processes is an effective method to achieve low loss at microwave frequencies. Sintering with additives with low melting points in an oxygen-rich atmosphere promotes crystallite growth and defect reduction [11−15]. Yang *et al*. [16] prepared Bi–Zn–Sn–In codoped YIG by hot-press sintering at 900 °C. By means of Sn4+ and In4+, the anisotropy linewidth (Δ*H*a = 38.27 Oe) was reduced, and a material with high density and low porosity (Δ*H* = 85 Oe) was achieved. Xiao *et al*. [17] optimized the densification of BiZr-YIG by adjusting the presintering temperature (1000 °C), reducing Δ*H* by 65% (Δ*H* = 92.37 Oe) while maintaining high relative permittivity (*ε'* = 22.50) and magnetization (*M*s = 27.09 emu/g). Oxygen helps eliminate pores induced by oxygen vacancies on the grain surfaces and inhibits the generation of Fe2+ ions in the crystal lattice [18]. This can effectively suppress the FMR linewidth broadening associated with pores and dielectric loss caused by electron hopping between Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions but has limited help in improving the dielectric constant.

Another widely used method for tailoring the microwave electromagnetic performance of YIG ferrite is cation substitution [19]. The multi-ionic substitution of garnet octahedra can reduce the linewidth through anisotropic regulation. Yang *et al*. [20] synthesized pure YIG ferrites codoped with calcium and tin through the solid-phase reaction method. Ca–Sn codoping effectively regulated the FMR linewidth by controlling the anisotropy and densification. Optimized magnetic properties were achieved in Y2.7Ca0.3Fe4.7Sn0.3O12 ferrite, which was demonstrated as 4π*M*s = 2073 Gs, the ferromagnetic resonance linewidth (Δ*H* = 42 Oe), and the Curie temperature (*T*C = 218 °C). Yang *et al*. [21] studied Ca–Zr cosubstituted YIG (Y3−*x*Ca*x*Fe5−*x*Zr*x*O12, *x* = 0.00–0.75) and regulated the FMR linewidth by reducing the anisotropy. The study revealed that moderate substitution (*x* = 0.45) significantly optimized the comprehensive properties: The FMR linewidth decreased to 27.85 Oe, the 4π*M*s increased to 1818 Gs, and the resistivity reached as high as 8.93×106 Ω·m. For example, many studies have demonstrated that the substitution of Bi3+ ions for Y3+ ions in YIG ferrite can significantly increase the dielectric constant and lower the sintering temperature. Li *et al*. [22] studied a new low-temperature sintering technology using Bi substitution for YIG (Y3-*x*Bi*x*Fe5O12, *x* = 0.4–1.2). The results show that the addition of Bi can significantly reduce the sintering temperature for phase formation (below 960 °C), and the crystallinity, morphology, size, and sintering temperature are obviously related to the Bi content. Tsay *et al*. [23] reported that adding 2Ca–V or Bi components to YIG material can significantly reduce the sintering temperature required for material densification. The sintering temperature required to achieve a theoretical density greater than 97% was reduced from 1450 °C (for pure YIG) to 1250 °C (for 0.6 (2Ca–V)-doped YIG) and 1020 °C (for 1.0 Bi-doped YIG). The addition of Bi not only significantly reduces the sintering temperature required to obtain high-density YIG material but also significantly reduces the magnetic loss and improves the microwave properties of the material.

However, Bi3+ ion substitution leads to a pronounced increase in the FMR linewidth to over 100 Oe due to the strong spin‒orbit coupling effect induced by the 6p electrons of Bi3+ ions. Transition metal cations are commonly substituted for Fe3+ ions to tune the magnetic properties of YIG ferrite. For example, the replacement of Fe3+ ions with In3+ [24] and Ga3+ ions [25] reduces the magnetocrystalline anisotropy and suppresses the corresponding FMR linewidth broadening. Moreover, multiple cations can be co-substituted for Y3+ and Fe3+ ions to optimize several microwave electromagnetic properties simultaneously. Xiao *et al*. [26] systematically studied the co-substitution of Ca2+–Ge4+ in BiV-YIG material Bi0.6Y1.4−*x*Ca1+*x*Fe4.5−*x*V0.5Ge*x*O12 (*x* = 0.10–0.50). The study revealed that when *x* = 0.50, the substitution of Fe3+ with Ge4+ ions led to a transformation of the material from ferrimagnetism to paramagnetism. The low saturation magnetization has a negative effect on the FMR linewidth (Δ*H* > 165 Oe). Xu *et al*. [27] synthesized Bi–Ca–Sn co-substituted YIG ferrite, which exhibited increased saturation magnetization and reduced coercivity and FMR linewidth. Chen *et al*. [28] attempted to comprehensively optimize the magnetic and dielectric performance of YIG ferrite through a Bi–Ca–Ge co-substitution strategy. With an appropriate substation amount, the material had an increased dielectric constant of 22, a low dielectric loss tangent of 0.005, and an FMR linewidth of 250 Oe. According to the abovementioned research results, however, it is difficult to achieve a high dielectric constant, low dielectric loss, and narrow FMR linewidth simultaneously because physical trade-offs exist between these properties [29,30]. Therefore, developing low-loss and high-permittivity microwave ferrites remains a significant challenge.

In this work, we aim to develop a Bi–Ca–Zr co-substituted YIG ferrite with optimized electromagnetic properties by systematically investigating the effects of multi-ion substitution on its microstructure and magnetic and dielectric performance. The substitution of Bi3+ ions for Y3+ ions enhances the electronic polarization of Fe3+ ions by unfreezing the orbital angular momentum of 3d electrons. The substitution of Ca2+ and Zr4+ ions for Y3+ and Fe3+ ions suppresses FMR linewidth broadening associated with magnetocrystalline anisotropy. Furthermore, these substitutions also influence densification and grain growth, resulting in a non-uniform microstructure composed of densely compacted crystallites with a bimodal size distribution. The high density contributes to a further increase in the dielectric constant, and the large grains contribute to a further decrease in the FMR linewidth by reducing the number and size of pores. The overall microwave electromagnetic performance of Bi–Ca–Zr co-substituted YIG ferrite is effectively optimized by the synergistic effect of intrinsic and morphological regulation induced by site-controlled multi-ion substitution.

## 2 Material and methods

Yttrium iron garnet ferrite with a chemical formula of Bi*x*Y3−*x*−*y*Ca*y*Zr*y*Fe5−*y*O12 (*x* = 1.2–1.5, *y* = 0.35–0.5) was synthesized via the solid-state reaction route. High-purity powders of Fe2O3, Y2O3, Bi2O3, CaCO3, and ZrO2 were mixed in a ball mill with deionized water for 6 h (240 r/min). The resulting mixture was dried at 80 °C and calcined at 850 °C for 4 h. The calcined powders were then re-milled with deionized water for 6 h. The dried powders were mixed with 12 wt% polyvinyl alcohol solution and pressed into cylindrical green compacts (18 mm in diameter and 2 mm in height) under a pressure of 200 MPa. Finally, the green bodies were sintered in oxygen at 910–930 °C for 30 h. During the sintering process, a degumming step was performed at approximately 450 °C for 1 h, followed by presintering at 850 °C for 1 h. The final sintering temperature was maintained for 30 h under an oxygen atmosphere. Owing to the relatively low melting point of Bi2O3 (825 °C) [31], the sintering temperature was adjusted according to the amount of Bi. Specifically, the samples with *x* = 1.2 and 1.3 were sintered at 930 °C, whereas those with *x* = 1.4 and 1.5 were sintered at 910 °C. The sintered cylindrical samples were pulverized and polished into small spherical samples with diameters of 2.5 and 0.8 mm for the hysteresis loop measurement and FMR measurement, respectively.

The phase composition of the as-sintered samples was identified by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; MAXima_X XRD-7000, Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with Cu Kα radiation over a 2*θ* range from 25° to 75°. The microstructure of the sample cross-sections was examined via a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JCM-7000, JEOL, Japan). The average grain sizes were determined via the interception method performed on the micrographs. The dielectric constant of the cylindrical samples at 1 GHz was measured with an impedance analyzer (E4991B, Keysight, USA). For the measurement of the quality factor (*Q*), we used the Hakki‒Coleman dielectric resonator method with a network analyzer (HP83752A, Agilent, USA). The dielectric loss tangent (tan*δ*) was calculated via the equation tan*δ* = 1/*Q*. The FMR linewidth was measured via a microwave FMR test system (DH811B, Dahua, China). Magnetic hysteresis loops and magnetization curves were obtained via a vibrating sample magnetometer (8604, Lake Shore, USA) with an external magnetic field ranging from −6000 to 6000 Oe for the hysteresis loop and from 0 to 6000 Oe for the magnetization curve. The Curie temperature of each sample was determined via a thermogravimetric analyzer (TA Q50).

### 3.1 Crystal structure and microstructure

The crystal structure of YIG ferrite contains three kinds of crystallographic interstitial sites, i.e., fourfold tetrahedral sites, sixfold octahedral sites, and eightfold dodecahedral sites. As shown in Fig. 1(a), all the Y3+ ions occupy the dodecahedral sites, and the Fe3+ ions occupy the tetrahedral and octahedral sites. For the Bi–Ca–Zr-substituted YIG, the Bi3+ ions replace the Y3+ ions at the dodecahedral sites, whereas the Zr4+ ions substitute for the Fe3+ ions at the octahedral sites. To balance the high valence state of the Zr4+ ions, an equal amount of Ca2+ ions are introduced to replace the Y3+ ions at the dodecahedral sites [32−34]. As shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), the ion occupancy was verified through the XRD refinement results. The larger ionic radii of Bi3+ ions (1.17 Å) and Ca2+ ions (1.12 Å) prevent them from occupying the octahedral and tetrahedral sites, leading to their substitution for Y3+ ions (1.02 Å) on the dodecahedral sites. Additionally, Zr4+ ions (0.72 Å) cannot occupy the tetrahedral sites and instead substitute for Fe3+ ions (0.77 Å) at the octahedral sites.

Figure 1(b) shows the XRD patterns of the Bi–Ca–Zr-substituted YIG samples together with the standard reference Y3Fe5O12 ferrite (PDF#43-0507). All the samples, regardless of the Bi3+ and Zr4+ substitution levels, exhibit a pure garnet phase without detectable impurities. The XRD analysis indicates a general shift of the diffraction peaks toward smaller angles by approximately 0.4° compared with the standard peaks. This shift is attributed mainly to the substitution of Y3+ ions (ionic radius of 1.02 Å) by larger Bi3+ ions (1.17 Å) at the dodecahedral sites [35]. This substitution increases the interplanar distance and the lattice constant, resulting in lattice distortion and the observed peak shifts toward smaller angles.

The lattice constants were determined via the Bragg equation, as illustrated in Fig. 1(c), and the theoretical density of the material was calculated accordingly, as shown in Fig. 1(d). The incorporation of larger Bi3+ ions into the garnet ferrite increased the interplanar distance, resulting in a significant increase in the lattice constant compared with that of pure YIG ferrite, which has a lattice constant of *a* = 12.376 Å. However, the relatively high atomic mass of the Bi3+ ions (208.98 g/mol) counteracted this increase, resulting in a corresponding increase in the theoretical density with increasing Bi3+ content. When the Bi content remained constant, the lattice constant increased with increasing Zr content. Owing to the relatively low atomic mass of Zr4+ ions (91.22 g/mol), this increase in the lattice constant was not sufficiently counteracted, resulting in a decrease in the theoretical density of the samples as the Zr content increased. At *x* = 1.2 and *y* = 0.35, the lattice constant reached a minimum value of 12.539 Å. Additionally, increasing *y* resulted in the theoretical density decreasing from 5.911 to 5.863 g/cm3. At *x* = 1.5 and *y* = 0.35, the theoretical density reached a peak value of 6.120 g/cm3. However, further increases in the Zr substitution level *y* led to a decrease in the theoretical density to 6.060 g/cm3.

The relative density and the lattice distortion are shown in Figs. 1(e) and 1(f), respectively. The lattice distortion can be calculated via Eq. (1) [36]:

(1) Distortion=a−a0a0×100%

where *a* is the measured lattice constant calculated by using the three strong peaks; *a*0 is the theoretical lattice constant obtained after XRD refinement; and this theoretical value is the average value of the refinement results of two XRD tests. The results of the lattice distortion are shown in Fig. 1(f), and the average values of the theoretical lattice constants are shown in Table S6. Owing to the relatively large ionic radius of Bi3+ (1.17 Å), after replacing the Y3+ (1.02 Å) ions, the lattice will undergo distortion, and with increasing Bi content, the degree of lattice distortion will intensify. When Zr4+ replaces the Fe3+ ions, considering the charge conservation, an equal amount of Ca2+ ions will be used to replace Y3+. Since the ionic radius of Ca2+ (1.12 Å) is also relatively large, it will also cause lattice distortion to a certain extent. When the Bi content is 1.5, the degree of lattice distortion reaches a maximum, which increases the probability of the occurrence of defects, pores, etc.

Figures 2(a)–2(d) show SEM images of the microstructures of the Bi–Ca–Zr-substituted YIG material. The microstructure is a bimodal distribution structure composed of large grains and small grains. The larger the large grains are, the lower the probability of defects and pores and the greater the density. The grain size and standard deviation were analyzed via statistical software, and the detailed results are provided in Table S1 in the ESM. Owing to the low melting point of Bi2O3, it forms a liquid phase and facilitates grain growth during the sintering stage [37−40]. When the Bi content increases from 1.2 to 1.3, the average size of the large grains decreases, the degree of densification decreases, and the average relative density decreases. When the Bi content is 1.4, the large grain size reaches the maximum value. At this time, the density is the highest, and the relative density also reaches the maximum value. When the Bi content increases to 1.5, the large grain size decreases to a minimum. At this time, the density is the lowest, and the relative density decreases.

Figures 2(e) and 2(f) illustrate the porosity and area percentage of large grains in different samples, whereas Fig. 2(h) presents the statistical analysis of the initial particle size distribution of the sintered powders as a function of the Bi content. Additional details on the particle size distribution are provided in Fig. S5 in the ESM. At a Bi content of 1.2, a local liquid phase appears. Under prolonged holding in an oxygen atmosphere, the resistance to grain boundary migration decreases, leading to a greater proportion of large grains. Consequently, the average grain size reaches approximately 17.9 μm. When the Bi content increases to 1.3, a slight increase in the initial particle size reduces the particle activity and slows densification. Moreover, the formation of a continuous liquid phase at the grain boundaries enhances boundary pinning, suppressing grain growth. This counteracts the enhanced grain boundary migration induced by long-term holding in an oxygen atmosphere, leading to inhibited large grain growth, a slight increase in small grains due to recrystallization, and a reduction in the proportion of large grains. At a Bi content of 1.4, the initial particle size is the smallest, maximizing the particle activity and promoting large-grain formation. As the Bi content approaches its solid solubility limit, a supersaturated liquid phase layer stabilizes at the grain boundaries. Highly active small particles preferentially drive large grain growth through the continuous liquid phase, resulting in the maximum large grain size (19 μm). However, owing to the limited number of nucleation sites, the proportion of large grains decreases. When the Bi content reaches 1.5, the overall initial particle size is at its maximum, reducing the particle activity and grain boundary mobility. The excessive liquid phase further inhibits boundary migration, promoting new small grain formation through dissolution‒reprecipitation. Consequently, the average small grain size peaks at 2 μm, the proportion of small grains increases, and large grains become difficult to sustain owing to material supply constraints, leading to an overall grain size reduction to 13 µm.

The conventional sintering process of ferrites involves three key stages, as shown in Fig. 2(i). At the initial stage, “necks” form at the particle contact points, with material migration occurring via surface diffusion, grain boundary diffusion, or volume diffusion, gradually rounding the pores. At the intermediate stage, a network of pore channels develops, grains begin to grow, and the densification rate slows. At the final stage, the pores close, the grains grow significantly, and the material approaches its theoretical density.

SEM observations reveal that this sintering process results in a bimodal grain size distribution, where large and small grains coexist. This microstructure arises from the nonuniformity of the initial particle size, the competition between grain boundary migration and diffusion, the prolonged holding time at a constant temperature, and the kinetic effects of liquid-phase sintering regulated by the Bi content [41,42]. During sintering, smaller initial particles exhibit greater surface energy and activity, accelerating “neck shrinkage” and densification in the initial stage. Grain growth is driven by mass transfer mechanisms throughout the process. While densification in ferrites is governed primarily by grain boundary diffusion, grain growth depends on grain boundary migration, which requires a high activation energy. When the grain boundary migration rate exceeds the diffusion rate, further grain growth is promoted [43−45]. Prolonged holding in an oxygen atmosphere generally reduces porosity, lowers resistance to grain boundary migration, accelerates grain growth, and increases the likelihood of large grain formation. Additionally, Bi2O3, with its low melting point, facilitates liquid-phase formation and promotes overall grain growth. However, an excessive liquid phase can lead to grain boundary separation, inducing dissolution-reprecipitation and inhibiting large grain formation.

### 3.2 Magnetic properties

The saturation magnetization (4π*M*s) of the Bi–Ca–Zr-substituted YIG ferrite samples were obtained through measurement of the magnetic hysteresis loop, as shown in Fig. 3(a). In garnet-type ferrites, the magnetic moment of the dodecahedral sites is zero when there are no magnetic ions, whereas the magnetic moments of the octahedral and tetrahedral sites are arranged antiparallel to each other. The net magnetic moment is the difference between the magnetic moments of the octahedral and tetrahedral sites. The main chemical formula in ionic form is {Bi*x*Y3−*x*−*y*Ca*y*}[Zr*y*Fe2−*y*](Fe3)O12 and the net magnetic moment is (1 + *y*)*m*Fe, where *m*Fe represents the magnetic moment of the Fe3+ ion. According to Figs. 1(c) and 1(d), at the same Bi content, the lattice constant is inversely proportional to the Zr content. As the Zr content increases, the relative atomic mass decreases, which leads to a reduction in density and, consequently, a decrease in the number of 4π*M*s. However, as the Zr content increases, the net magnetic moment also increases. These two factors jointly affect 4π*M*s, and the significant decrease in density plays a dominant role. At the same Bi content, as the Zr content increases, the number of 4π*M*s tends to decrease.

In polycrystalline ferrites, the microscopic morphology is closely linked to the magnetic domain structure. Larger grains typically exhibit more uniformly distributed magnetic domains and fewer grain boundaries, reducing the likelihood of impurities and pores. As shown in Table S1 in the ESM, when the Bi content reaches 1.4, the average size of large grains peaks at approximately 19 μm. At this point, the material contains more uniform magnetic domains and fewer grain boundaries, leading to the highest overall number of 4π*M*s. As the Bi content increases beyond 1.4, the size of the large grains gradually decreases, the grain boundary density increases and the pinning effect on the domain walls strengthens, causing a decline in the magnetic properties and an overall downward trend in the number of 4π*M*s. Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. 2(e), porosity is minimized at a Bi content of 1.4, whereas increasing the Bi content results in increased porosity. This inverse relationship between porosity and large grain size further confirms the strong correlation between microstructure and magnetic properties.

Figure 3(b) shows the coercivity (*H*c) of the Bi–Ca–Zr-substituted YIG ferrite samples obtained through the measurement of the magnetic hysteresis loop. According to Herzer’s random anisotropy model [46], the coercivity of soft magnetic material is related to the anisotropy constant (*K*1) and the grain size (*D*) (Eq. (2)):

(2) Hc∝K1D

An increase in the Bi content leads to an increase in the magnetocrystalline anisotropy; that is, |*K*1| increases. In addition, as shown in Fig. 2(f), with increasing Bi content, the proportion of large grains as a whole clearly decreases, resulting in an increase in the proportion of small grains. These two factors together lead to an overall upward trend in coercivity as the Bi content increases.

### 3.3 Dielectric properties

The dielectric constant (*ε'*) and FMR linewidth (Δ*H*) of the Bi–Ca–Zr-substituted YIG ferrites are compared with those of other microwave ferrites reported in Refs. [47–50], as shown in Fig. 4(a). The pentagram symbol denotes the material exhibiting the best performance, as discussed in this paper, whose detailed parameters are listed in Table S2 in the ESM. Material located near the bottom right of the figure demonstrates higher *ε'* values and narrower Δ*H* values, indicating superior overall performance. The Bi–Ca–Zr-substituted YIG ferrite material investigated in this research shows significantly enhanced overall performance compared with those reported in the literature.

Figure S5 in the ESM shows the variation in *ε'* with changes in the Zr content. When the Bi content remains constant, the effect of the Zr content on *ε'* is negligible. Figure 4(b) displays a box plot depicting the changes in *ε'* as the Bi content increases. Since the influence of the Zr content on *ε'* is negligible, the mean *ε'* for this plot was calculated via data from all levels of Zr content. As the Bi content increases, there is a significant increase in *ε'*. The mechanism by which Bi3+ ions influence the *ε'* of ferrites can be elucidated as follows. The 6p electrons of Bi3+ ions exhibit substantial spin‒orbit (S‒L) coupling, facilitating superexchange interactions that “unfreeze” the orbital angular momentum of the 3d electrons in Fe3+ ions. Typically, when the 3d electrons of Fe3+ are in a “frozen” state, their wave functions are spherically symmetric, resulting in a small distance between the negative charge center formed by the electron cloud and the positive charge center at the nucleus. Once the orbital angular momentum of the 3d electrons becomes “unfrozen”, these wave functions of the 3d electrons in Fe3+ ions hybridize with those of the 2p electrons in O2− ions and the 6p electrons in Bi3+ ions, leading to nonspherical electron cloud distributions. This shift increases the distance between the negative and positive charge centers of the nucleus compared with the frozen state. Furthermore, the displacement ranges between the positive (Fe3+) and negative (O2−) ions along the electric field direction also expand, enhancing electronic and ionic polarization, which ultimately increases *ε'* [48]. For Bi‒Ca‒Zr-substituted YIG material, each increase of 0.1 in Bi content corresponds to an approximate increase of 1.3 in *ε'*. Compared with traditional Y3Fe5O12 ferrite, which exibit *ε'* ranging from 13 to 16, the dielectric constant of this high-dielectric garnet material is nearly doubled. According to microwave device design theory, increasing the dielectric constant can significantly reduce the device area, making it ideal for miniaturization and weight reduction. This compactness is crucial for increasing the efficiency and applicability of microwave devices in modern electronics.

Figure 4(c) presents the dielectric loss measured via the Hakki‒Coleman dielectric resonator method, with the resonant frequency determined by the sample size remaining at approximately 8 GHz overall. The variation in dielectric loss is influenced primarily by the porosity and grain size distribution. Pores enhance Maxwell‒Wagner interfacial polarization, increasing interfacial polarization loss. As shown in Fig. 2(e), the overall porosity remains low (below 1%), reaching its minimum at a Bi content of 1.4. At higher Bi contents, the porosity generally increases, with a significant increase at 1.5, leading to increased dielectric loss. Additionally, as depicted in Fig. 2(f), an increase in the Bi content reduces the proportion of large grains while increasing the grain boundary density. This, in turn, increases the likelihood of defects and oxygen vacancies, further contributing to increased dielectric loss.

Figure 4(d) presents the temperature coefficient (*τ*f) of the dielectric constant and dielectric loss for the sample with a Bi content of 1.4. When the Bi content is less than 1.4, increasing the Zr content initially reduces the absolute value of the temperature coefficient, which then increases again when the Zr content reaches 0.5. This behavior is associated with changes in the grain size distribution. As shown in Table S1 in the ESM, at a Bi content of 1.4, the small grain size decreases continuously with increasing Zr content. Smaller grains hinder domain wall motion, as grain boundaries serve as pinning points. This results in a more stable dielectric constant with temperature, thereby reducing the absolute value of the temperature coefficient [51−53]. As illustrated in Fig. 2(f), at a Bi content of 1.4 and a Zr content between 1.2 and 1.4, the proportion of large grains remains relatively stable. However, at a Zr content of 0.5, the proportion of large grains increases significantly, whereas the proportion of small grains is the lowest. This reduces the grain boundary density and the number of domain wall pinning points, leading to a sharper dielectric constant variation with temperature and an increase in the absolute value of the temperature coefficient. The variation in the dielectric loss is influenced primarily by the porosity and grain size distribution. Pores enhance Maxwell‒Wagner interfacial polarization, increasing interfacial polarization loss. As shown in Fig. 2(e), the overall porosity remains low (below 1%), reaching its minimum at a Bi content of 1.4. Beyond this point, the porosity generally increases, with a significant increase at a Bi content of 1.5, leading to increased dielectric loss. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 2(f), increasing the Bi content reduces the proportion of large grains while increasing the grain boundary density. This higher grain boundary density increases the likelihood of defects, such as oxygen vacancies, further contributing to dielectric loss.

### 3.4 Ferromagnetic resonance linewidth

Figure 5(a) shows the variations in Δ*H* at different Bi contents of Bi–Ca–Zr-substituted YIG ferrite. According to the two-magnon scattering theory [54], material with smaller grain sizes exhibits a higher density of grain boundaries, which serve as defect regions that disrupt the alignment of magnetic moments within the grains. These irregular atomic arrangements at the grain boundaries act as additional scattering centers. Consequently, in material with smaller grains, magnon scattering at grain boundaries is more frequent, leading to increased magnetic losses. In contrast, larger grains exhibit more stable magnetic behavior, allowing smoother magnetic domain reversal and thereby reducing magnetic losses. The SEM images of the Bi–Ca–Zr-substituted YIG, as illustrated in Fig. 2(f), reveal a non-uniform microstructure with a mix of large and small grains. As the Bi content increases, the proportion of large grains decreases, whereas smaller grains become more prevalent. This shift increases the density of the grain boundaries, thereby enhancing two-magnon scattering and broadening the Δ*H* associated with the smaller grains. Conversely, the larger grains help minimize two-magnon scattering, contributing less to Δ*H*. The maximum average size of large grains is achieved at a Bi content of *x* = 1.4, where two-magnon scattering in these grains is minimized, contributing the least to Δ*H*. By balancing the contributions of both large and small grains to Δ*H* through two-magnon scattering, the total Δ*H* reaches its lowest point at a Bi content of *x* = 1.4.

The total Δ*H* in polycrystalline ferrite can be described via Eqs. (3)–(5) [26,55−60]:

(4) ΔHp≈1.5⋅(4πMs)p

(5) ΔHa≈2.07K12μ02Ms3

where Δ*H*i, Δ*H*a, Δ*H*p, and Δ*H*res are the linewidths associated with the intrinsic linewidth, the magnetocrystalline anisotropy linewidth, the pore linewidth, and the remaining linewidth, respectively; *p* is the porosity; *μ*0 is the vacuum permeability; *K*1 is the magnetocrystalline anisotropy constant. Since Δ*H*i is material-dependent and typically has a small value, it is often negligible in calculations. The magnitude of |*K*1| can be obtained via the law of approach to saturation, as described in Eqs. (6) and (7):

(6) MH=Ms(1−b0H−b1H2−⋅⋅⋅)+χpH

(7) b1=8105K12μ02Ms2

where *H* is the external magnetic field, *M*H is the magnetization under different external magnetic fields, *M*s is the saturation magnetization, *b*0 and *b*1 are coefficients, and *χ*p is the magnetization susceptibility.

The results of the FMR linewidth separation are shown in Fig. 5(b). As the Zr content increases, Δ*H*a decreases due to a reduction in |*K*1|. However, it contributes a relatively small proportion of the total linewidth. Both the porosity and the size of the large grains show similar trends. At a Bi content of 1.4, the size of the large grains reaches its maximum, corresponding to a lower porosity. Among the components, Δ*H*res constitutes a larger proportion of the total linewidth in all the samples. In this work, process optimization resulted in a dense material with reduced porosity, thereby minimizing the porosity-induced FMR linewidth broadening (Δ*H*p). Additionally, the substitution of Zr4+ ions decreased the magnetocrystalline anisotropy, leading to a reduction in the corresponding FMR linewidth component Δ*H*a. The combined reduction in Δ*H*p and Δ*H*a contributed to achieving a relatively low overall linewidth. The remaining FMR linewidth Δ*H*res primarily originates from inhomogeneity in magnetic moments due to the bimodal grain size distribution, as shown in Figs. 2(a)–2(d). Specifically, Fig. 2(f) indicates that when the Bi content is 1.5, the proportion of small grains reaches its maximum, enhancing double-magnon scattering and increasing the total linewidth. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 5(b), for Bi contents of 1.2, 1.3, and 1.5, Δ*H*res of samples with a Zr content of 0.5 generally reach their highest value. By examining the large grain size parameters in Table S1 in the ESM, we find that within the same Bi content group, samples with a Zr content of 0.5 have the smallest large grain size. This reduction in grain size increases the grain boundary density, enhances magnon scattering, and ultimately leads to higher Δ*H*res. When the Bi content is 1.4, and the Zr content is 0.5, the material achieves a minimum linewidth of 33 Oe.

Figure 5(c) shows the calculated values of the magnetocrystalline anisotropy constant (*K*1). As the degree of Zr substitution increases, |*K*1| tends to decrease. Specifically, for every 0.1 increase in the Zr content, |*K*1| decreases by approximately 244 J·m−3. This decrease is attributed to the replacement of magnetic Fe3+ ions at octahedral sites with nonmagnetic Zr4+ ions, which effectively reduces |*K*1| and consequently lowers Δ*H*a. Conversely, as the Bi substitution level increases, |*K*1| tends to increase. For every 0.1 increase in Bi content, |*K*1| increases by approximately 63 J·m−3. This enhancement is due to the large ionic radius of Bi3+ ions and their strong spin‒orbit coupling, which contributes to the increase in |*K*1|.

Figure 5(d) shows the FMR linewidth contributed by the magnetocrystalline anisotropy (Δ*H*a) of the samples with different Bi and Zr contents. When the Bi3+ content remains constant, an increase in the Zr content results in a decrease in the absolute value of |*K*1|. Although the decline in 4π*M*s is relatively minor, it still contributes to a corresponding decrease in the anisotropic linewidth Δ*H*a, as described by Eq. (5). At a constant Zr4+ content, the strong spin‒orbit coupling associated with Bi3+ ions enhances the overall anisotropy |*K*1| of the magnetic crystal. This increase in |*K*1| results in an expansion of the anisotropic linewidth (Δ*H*a).

### 3.5 Curie temperature

Figure 6(a) shows the test results of the Curie temperature for different Bi/Zr contents. The weight curve and its derivative are obtained through thermogravimetric analysis, and the temperature at which the maximum mass change rate occurs is defined as *T*C [61,62]. The test results of *T*C are shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. The *T*C results for various Bi and Zr contents are summarized in Table S3 in the ESM. An analysis of the *T*C test results revealed that the *T*C in the Bi–Ca–Zr-substituted YIG increases linearly with Bi content and follows a quadratic functional relationship with increasing Zr ion concentration. The correlation between the Bi–Zr content and *T*C was established by fitting it via the Levenberg‒Marquardt algorithm [63]. For the fitting equation between the Curie temperature and Bi/Zr content, the goodness of fit is 0.9953, demonstrating an excellent fit to the experimental data (Eq. (8)).

where *x*Bi is the Bi content, and *y*Zr is the Zr content. As the Bi substitution content increases, the *T*C of the material increases. This phenomenon is attributed to the strong spin‒orbit coupling of the 6p electrons of Bi3+ with O2− ions, which enhances spin‒orbit coupling with Fe3+ and strengthens the superexchange interaction. This enhanced interaction results in a macroscopic increase in *T*C. Specifically, for every 0.1 increase in Bi content, *T*C increases by approximately 2.3 °C. Theoretical analysis of superexchange interactions indicates that the *T*C of ferrite is primarily determined by the superexchange interaction between Fe3+ ions. In garnet ferrites, this interaction occurs between Fe3+ ions located at both octahedral and tetrahedral sites. As the Zr ion concentration decreases, the number of interacting Fe3+ ion pairs at these sites increases, thereby strengthening the superexchange interaction and leading to an increase in *T*C.

Figure 6(b) shows the bond angle (*θ*Fe–O–Fe) calculated from the XRD refinement results and the bond angle (*θ*Fe–O–Fe) between the octahedral Fe³+ and the tetrahedral Fe³+; the detailed data are presented in Table S7 in the ESM. In ferrimagnetic material, the superexchange interaction mainly originates from the antiferromagnetic coupling between Fe3+ ions with different coordinates [64]. For the garnet structure, the superexchange interaction is caused mainly by the interaction between the *a* and *d* sites (that is, the iron ions between the octahedral a site and the tetrahedral d site). The bond angle (*θ*Fe–O–Fe) plays a major role in the magnetic exchange interaction. The closer this bond angle is to 180°, the stronger the exchange interaction. As the content of Bi increases, the strong spin‒orbit coupling of Bi3+ acts on Fe3+ through O2−, causing the bond angle (*θ*Fe–O–Fe) to continuously increase and the superexchange interaction to strengthen. Macroscopically, this is manifested as an increase in the Curie temperature.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, Bi–Ca–Zr-substituted YIG material synthesized via solid-state reactions exhibits a high dielectric constant, low FMR linewidth, and elevated Curie temperature. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the incorporation of Bi3+ ions at the crystallographic dodecahedral sites significantly enhances the electronic polarization of the Fe3+ ions by unfreezing the orbital angular momentum of the 3d electrons, leading to a positive correlation between the dielectric constant and the Bi3+ concentration. Specifically, for every 0.1 increase in the Bi content, the dielectric constant increases by 1.3, reaching a maximum value of 29 at a Bi content of 1.5. Bi-doping has two competing effects in the sintering process. First, Bi2O3, which has a low melting point, causes liquid phase sintering to promote grain growth. The other is that Bi3+ ions with a large ionic radius induce lattice distortion, increasing the interfacial energy and inhibiting grain growth. This leads to a unique nonuniform microstructure composed of densely compacted crystallites with a bimodal size distribution. When the larger grains reach their maximum size, the two-magnon scattering effect is minimized, yielding the measured minimum Δ*H* of 33 Oe. The linewidth separation results indicate that Δ*H*res is the primary contributor to overall linewidth broadening. Furthermore, the introduction of Bi3+ ions at the crystallographic dodecahedral sites enhances the superexchange interaction among Fe ions, leading to a linear increase in *T*C with increasing Bi content, approximately 2.3 °C for each 0.1 increment in the Bi concentration. In contrast, each 0.1 increment in Zr doping results in a decrease in *T*C of approximately 13 °C, primarily because Zr replaces octahedral Fe3+ ions and reduces their concentration. The relationship between *T*C and the concentrations of Bi/Zr was effectively modeled via the Levenberg‒Marquardt algorithm, providing a predictive framework for similar compositions. This high-dielectric garnet ferrite demonstrates significant potential for application in low-loss and miniaturized wireless communication systems, spintronic memory architectures, and novel gyromagnetic metamaterial designs.
