## 1 Introduction

The rapid evolution of science and technology, especially in the realm of microwave communication technology, has led to the advent of the fifth-generation (5G) era. The 5G communication band spans the millimeter wave frequency range of 3–300 GHz, offering a higher transmission rate and enhancing frequency band utilization as well as wider bandwidth compared to third-generation (3G) and fourth-generation (4G) [1,2]. However, the propagation of high-intensity electromagnetic (EM) radiation will inevitably interfere with signals from devices with low-intensity electromagnetic radiation (e.g., bluetooth devices, mobile phones, and wireless accessories), affecting the overall communication quality [3]. Moreover, numerous studies have substantiated the increasing harm of electromagnetic radiation to human health [4,5]. Therefore, it is imperative to develop technologies that reduce or eliminate electromagnetic interference and pollution. Electromagnetic wave (EMW) absorbing materials, or absorbers, play a crucial role in achieving effective absorption and attenuation of EMW energy, which converts EMW into thermal energy and other forms via a dissipation mechanism [6]. Accordingly, the design of suitable electromagnetic absorbing materials is one of the most effective methods for addressing these challenges and advancing 5G communications [7].

In general, incident EMWs can be divided into four parts upon entering absorbers: first reflection, multiple internal reﬂections, absorption, and transmission [8]. Effective absorbers should minimize reflection and transmission while maximizing EMW absorption. The ratio of incident EMW to EMW absorption is related to impedance matching [9], a decisive factor for effective EMW absorption. Optimal impedance matching requires alignment between the EMW absorbing medium and the free space as closely or equally as possible [10]. Despite numerous conceptualized and developed composite absorbers over the past few decades, the most attractive ones are (nano)composites composed of two or more materials with different compositions and structures [11−13], such as metal-based composites [14−17], polymer-based composites [18−20], and carbon-based materials [21−24]. However, certain materials exhibit shortcomings, such as poor corrosion resistance of metal-based composites [25] and low thermal stability of polymer-based composites [26], limiting their practical application. Additionally, the fabrication of multiple composites suffers from a long preparation cycle and a cumbersome process, leading to high energy consumption. Therefore, recent research has emphasized exploring materials that are simple to fabricate while simultaneously achieving optimal impedance matching and strong electromagnetic attenuation capabilities [27].

The MAB phase, analogous to MAX, represents a promising group of alternatives where M stands for a transition metal, A typically denotes Al, and B is boron. The laminated crystal structures of the MAB phase consist of M–B (transition metal boride) layers interspersed with layers of Al [28]. Therefore, the MAB phase possesses both metallic and ceramic properties, offering intriguing attributes such as good electrical, thermal, and mechanical properties, as well as chemical and thermal stability. Since MoAlB was rediscovered by Kota *et al*. [28] in 2016, an increasing number of MAB phase ceramics, including MoAlB [29], WAlB [30], Fe2AlB2 [31], Cr2AlB2 [32], Cr4AlB6 [32], Cr3AlB4 [32], Cr4AlB4 [32], Mn2AlB2 [33], and Ti2InB2 [34], have attracted increasing attention of researchers. Their properties have also been studied by an increasing number of researchers. Among them, MoAlB has the highest Al atom content. To date, explorations of the properties of MoAlB have focused on its mechanical properties and physical properties [35], oxidation resistance and thermal stability [36], high-temperature mechanical properties and thermal shock resistance [37], and high-temperature self-healing behavior [38]. However, only a few studies have investigated its EMW absorbing properties [39−42]. In these limited studies, Zhang *et al*. [41] reported the EMW absorption properties of the ternary layered boride Cr2AlB2. The resulting Cr2AlB2 demonstrated a minimum reflection loss (RL) value of −44.9 dB, along with an effective absorption bandwidth (*E*AB) reaching 4.4 GHz. Its absorbing properties could be maintained at a high temperature of 1000 °C, indicating that MAB phase materials are potential candidates for EMW absorption applications.

The first-principles calculations show the guiding significance for the potential application of MoAlB in more fields. Li *et al*. [43] calculated the structural, electronic, and optical properties of MoAlB using the first-principles calculations and demonstrated its metallic and dynamic stability. Dai *et al*. [44] analyzed the chemical bond and elastic properties of MoAlB from the first principle, indicating that there were many chemical bonds in MoAlB. Zhu *et al*. [45] calculated and studied the structural parameters and elastic and electronic properties of MoAlB and noted its metallicity. Furthermore, Nie *et al*. [46] reported the study of the Fermi, electrical anisotropy, and Fermi liquid properties of MoAlB, indicating that MoAlB can be exfoliated into two-dimensional metal borides. These properties make them potential candidates for applications in several key areas [47], including the field of EMW absorption.

MoAlB, the first synthesized MAB phase material, is characterized by its multilayer microstructure, high conductivity, and robust mechanical properties [48]. However, its application in the field of wave absorption has rarely been reported. In the present work, the first-principles calculations were conducted to prove the promising applications of MoAlB ceramics in the field of EMW absorption. Subsequently, MoAlB samples were synthesized at different temperatures, and their EMW absorbing properties were measured and analyzed in combination with advanced characterization to verify their actual availability. These findings provide a valuable reference for the future design and synthesis of other MAB phases and the exploration of their applications in EMW absorption.

### 2.1 Computational methods

The electronic properties of MoAlB were calculated based on density functional theory (DFT), which was implemented within the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) [49]. The exchange and correlation effects were included within the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) generalized gradient approximation (GGA) [50]. The interaction between the atomic core and valence electrons was described using pseudopotentials based on the projector augmented plane wave (PAW) method [51]. The wavefunctions for the primitive cell of MoAlB were expanded by plane waves up to an energy cutoff of 500 eV. The integration over the Brillouin zone was sampled with a *k*-point grid of 11 × 11 × 11, generated automatically by the Monkhorst–Pack approach [52]. In the calculations, the total energy was calculated with high precision and converges to 10−7 eV/atom, and the structure relaxation will be stopped when the residual forces are less than 10−4 eV/atom. The crystal morphology was simulated using the Bravais–Friedel Donnay–Harker (BFDH) method within Accelrys Materials Studio software (Accelrys Inc., USA).

### 2.2 Synthesis of MoAlB materials

Mo (300 mesh, 99.9%), Al (200 mesh, 99.5%), and B (10 μm, 99%) powders, purchased from Zhejiang Yamei Nano Technology Co., Ltd., China, served as the reactants in this study. The powders of Mo, Al, and B were placed in a zirconia ball mill tank with a molar ratio of 1 : 2 : 1. Agate balls served as the medium in a 15-min dry ball milling process. Excess aluminum was deliberately introduced to counteract its high-temperature evaporation and facilitate the reaction among the three powders, ultimately leading to the formation of MoAlB. After the ball milling process, the blended powder was cold-pressed into a 10 mm diameter circular plate using a cylindrical stainless-steel mold under a 220 MPa load. The synthesis of the MoAlB ceramics involved heat treatment of the reactants at temperatures of 800, 900, 1000, 1100, 1200, and 1300 °C for 2 h at a heating rate of 10 °C/min in a high vacuum environment in a hot-pressing furnace (ZT-25-20Y, Shanghai Chenhua Technology Co., Ltd., China).

### 2.3 Characterization

The phase composition of the obtained samples was analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Smartlab-SE, RIGAKU, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.54178 Å). To ensure the measurement accuracy, data acquisition was performed at a scanning speed of 2 (°)/min with a step size of 0.02°. The microstructure of the resultant specimens was measured using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-IT800, JEOL, Japan) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS; JED-2300, JEOL, Japan). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; Nexsa, Thermo Fisher, USA) was employed to probe the chemical states of the samples.

### 2.4 EMW absorption measurements

The relative complex permittivity of the MoAlB powders, ranging from 2.0 to 18.0 GHz, was gauged using the coaxial method by a vector network analyzer (VNA; R&S ZNA, R&S, Germany). The samples were prepared by mixing MoAlB powders with paraffin at a mass ratio of 8 : 2. These mixtures were then pressed into ring-shaped pellets with an outer diameter of 7.00 mm and an inner diameter of 3.04 mm. The reflection loss (RL) was utilized to assess the EMW absorption characteristics of the samples. In accordance with the transmission line theory and the metal backplane model [53], the RL value of the sample was calculated based on the relative complex permittivity and complex permeability at a given frequency and thickness using Eqs. (1) and (2):

(1) RL=20lg|(Zin−Z0)/(Zin+Z0)|

(2) Zin=Z0μrεrtanh(j2πfdμrεrc)

where * Zin is the normalized input characteristic impedance at the absorber–medium interface;  Z0 is the wave impedance of free space;  μr is the relative permeability;  εr is the relative complex permittivity;  tanh describes the wave propagation characteristics in the thickness direction of the material;  d is the thickness of the absorber;  f is the frequency;  c is 3.0×108 m/s (the velocity of light). For clarity, MoAlB powders synthesized at different temperatures were labeled MoAlB-X*, where *X* represents the synthesis temperature.

### 3.1 Crystal structure and electronic band structure

The crystal structure of MoAlB can be interpreted as the progressive stacking of orthorhombic MoB and body-centered cubic Al, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The unit cell contains two structural units and twelve atoms. Furthermore, B atoms are located above and below the center of the Mo hexagon, forming covalent bonds with Mo atoms. Al atoms weakly bond with Mo and B atoms through both metallic and ionic interactions. Obviously, the crystal structure of MoAlB is composed of alternating layers of MoB and Al along the *c*-axis, with Mo atoms forming a Kagome lattice in the MoB layer and Al atoms forming a triangular lattice in the Al layer, thus resulting in MoAlB acquiring its distinctive layered structure.

*
Fig. 1Formation mechanism of MoAlB crystal.

The study of the electronic band structure is helpful for determining many physical properties of solids. The band structure of MoAlB is shown in Fig. 2(a). The horizontal orange line is the Fermi level. A few valence bands cross this line and overlap with the conduction band, which indicates that MoAlB has metal conductivity. As shown in the simulated total density of states (TDOS) and partial density of states (PDOS) diagrams (Fig. 2(b)), the d-orbital electrons of Mo and the p-orbital electrons of Al are mainly involved in conduction. Moreover, Huang et al*. [54] reported the large three-dimensional (3D) electrical anisotropy of a MoAlB single crystal, and surprisingly strong out-of-plane ( σa/σb=1.43×105,2K) and in-plane ( σa/σc=12.12,2K) electrical anisotropies were observed. The 3D anisotropies of the crystal structure, chemical bonding, electronic structure, and electrical transport of MoAlB were verified. Therefore, the unique layered structure, metallic-like electrical conductivity, and large 3D electrical anisotropy of MoAlB indicate its potential for application as a single-phase material in the field of electromagnetic wave absorption.

### 3.2 Synthesis and microstructure

The phase composition of the synthesized samples was detected by XRD and is shown in Fig. 3. The samples obtained within the temperature range of 800–1300 °C exhibit a consistent phase composition, with the orthorhombic MoAlB compound as the predominant component. In addition, several stable intermetallic compounds (Al12Mo, Al4Mo, Mo5Al22, and Al5Mo) are also observed in the MoAlB system, which is attributed to incomplete reactions and excess aluminum. According to Refs. [55,56], these Al and Mo compounds serve as crucial intermediate phases in the preparation of MoAlB. The Mo–Al–B phase diagram simulated by Potanin *et al*. [55] confirmed the presence of intermetallic compounds (Al12Mo, Al5Mo, Al3Mo, Al8Mo, and AlMo3) as inevitable phases in the MoAlB system. Su *et al*. [56] also noted the formation of intermetallic phases in a MoAlB system prepared by *in situ* spark plasma sintering.

Interestingly, the positions of the diffraction peaks progressively shift with increasing the temperature, which is closely related to the growth and decomposition process of the MoAlB phases. Between 800 and 1100 °C, the MoAlB crystal grows with increasing the temperature, a phenomenon that can be explained by the microscopic morphology of MoAlB. ### 3.3 EMW absorbing performance

The microwave absorption characteristics of a dielectric absorber are intimately linked to its complex permittivity, denoted as (* ε=ε′+jε′′), where the real permittivity ( ε′) signifies the capacity to store electrical energy, while the imaginary permittivity ( ε′′) indicates the potential for energy loss. The EMW absorption properties were evaluated by analyzing the electromagnetic parameters of the MoAlB sample with a VNA.

As shown in Fig. 7(a), the samples prepared at 800–1100 °C demonstrate high and consistent values of  ε′, varying from 10.87 to 21.94 in the 2−18 GHz frequency range, indicating good dielectric storage capacity. As the synthesis temperature increases, the  ε′ of MoAlB synthesized at 1200 and 1300 °C decreases (from 9.09 to 11.87), which is attributed to the decomposition of MoAlB. It is evident that the  ε′ of the sample synthesized at 1000 °C is the largest, indicating that more polarization occurs because of its larger size and smaller surface area than those of the other samples. Regarding the values of  ε′′ (Fig. 7(b)), all the MoAlB samples exhibit a comparable trend of multiple peaks, a characteristic referred to as a nonlinear phenomenon [58,59]. This nonlinear phenomenon is associated with various polarization phenomena, including conductivity and skin effects, electronic spin, charge polarization arising from the polarization center, and interfacial polarization loss occurring between the layers of MoAlB and intermetallic compounds. It is noteworthy that the MoAlB samples synthesized at 900 and 1000 °C show relatively high and stable values among all the samples, indicating their strong dielectric loss ability.

The dielectric loss factor * (tanδε=ε′′/ε′) is vital in assessing the EMW absorption potential of materials, especially in relation to dielectric losses. For the MoAlB samples prepared at 800−1100 °C, both the  tanδε–f* and * ε′′–f* curves exhibit analogous variations. The dielectric loss factor, denoted as * tanδε, varies between 0.17 and 0.72. Significantly,  tanδε remains under 0.5 for frequencies above 4 GHz, as depicted in Fig. 7(c). This finding is significant because a reduced  tanδε value leads to better impedance matching, thereby augmenting the material’s EMW absorption capability [60].

To further investigate the absorption mechanism of the MoAlB samples, the impedance matching (Z* = *Z*in/*Z*0) and attenuation properties (*α*) of the MoAlB samples were studied, as illustrated in Figs. 7(d) and 7(e). Attenuation characteristics refer to the rapid consumption of electromagnetic waves entering the material by various electromagnetic wave loss mechanisms inside the material to avoid the reflection of electromagnetic waves back into the air. The attenuation characteristics of the material can be described by Eq. (3):

(3) α=2fπc(μ′′ε′′−μ′ε′)+(μ′′ε′′−μ′ε′)2+(μ′ε′′+μ′′ε′)2

Enhancing the impedance (*Z* = |*Z*in/*Z*0|) can increase the EMW absorption efficiency [61]. In general, an ideal absorbing material should have a *Z* value close to 1, implying that it allows all electromagnetic waves to penetrate the matrix without being reflected. As shown in Fig. 7(d), the *Z* value of MoAlB synthesized at 900 °C is closest to 1 in the frequency range from 12 to 14 GHz, indicating that it has the best EMW absorption properties. For the attenuation characteristics (Fig. 7(e)), the higher the value of *α* is, the stronger the attenuation ability of the sample. It is worth noting that the *α* values of the MoAlB samples synthesized at different temperatures have a peak at 8–16 GHz, indicating that MoAlB has a strong loss ability to electromagnetic waves in the X and Ku bands. The MoAlB samples synthesized at 900 and 1000 °C have higher *α* values due to their grain size and high content of MoAlB, indicating that they have the best electromagnetic wave absorption performance. With increasing the temperature, the decrease in the MoAlB phase content and the partial decomposition of MoAlB lead to a decrease in the *α* value, and the electromagnetic wave absorption performance also begins to decrease.

The coaxial method was employed to measure and assess the EMW absorption properties of the prepared MoAlB samples. The lower the minimum RL value is, the stronger the absorption capacity of the material. The calculated RL values of the resultant MoAlB samples at different thicknesses and frequencies are shown in Fig. 8. Clearly, the increase in the thickness of the absorbers results in the absorption peak shifting progressively from the high to the low frequency region, a phenomenon accounted for by the quarter wavelength theory [62]. The MoAlB samples prepared at various temperatures exhibit different EMW absorption properties. With increasing the temperature, the electromagnetic wave absorption properties of the MoAlB samples first increase and then decrease. This is primarily evident in the reflection loss and the effective absorption bandwidth. For the minimum reflection loss, with increasing the temperature from 800 to 1300 °C, the minimum RL values are −37.87 dB at 10.0 GHz, −50.33 dB at 12.16 GHz, −37.06 dB at 16.24 GHz, −37.41 dB at 10.72 GHz, −38.57 dB at 16.64 GHz, and −31.20 dB at 16.4 GHz, with thicknesses of 2, 1.7, 1.2, 2, 4.5, and 4.5 mm, respectively. With the same thickness, the effective absorption bandwidths at each temperature are 1.6 GHz (from 9.28 to 10.88 GHz), 2.72 GHz (from 11.04 to 13.76 GHz), 3.12 GHz (from 14.4 to 17.52 GHz), 2.16 GHz (from 9.76 to 11.92 GHz), 1.28 GHz (from 15.92 to 17.2 GHz), and 1.2 GHz (from 15.92 to 17.12 GHz), as shown in Fig. 9. It can be concluded that the change in the microwave absorbing properties is mainly related to the different contents of MoAlB. First, the MoAlB sample synthesized at 900 °C has the highest content of the MoAlB phase and the strongest loss ability to electromagnetic waves, which is reflected in its lowest reflection loss (−50.33 dB). Additionally, the Al–Mo intermetallic compounds play a role in adjusting the impedance. When the synthesis temperature is 1000 °C, the content of the Al–Mo intermetallic compound reaches 9.2 wt%, and the optimal impedance matching leads to the widest absorption bandwidth (3.12 GHz). Second, the MoAlB synthesized at 800 °C is rod-shaped and has a small particle size, which leads to the instability of its dielectric properties, reflecting a narrow effective absorption bandwidth. When the temperature is increased to 900 °C, the crystallization of MoAlB is gradually completed, which is manifested by the higher  ε′ and  ε″ and the optimal  tanδε. Therefore, the minimum RL of MoAlB synthesized at 900 °C is as high as −50.33 dB. With a further increase in temperature, the crystallization of MoAlB tends to be complete, which is reflected in the fact that its real part of the dielectric constant is the highest. A higher  ε′ leads to impedance mismatch, so its minimum reflection loss is −37.06 dB, but its effective absorption bandwidth reaches 3.12 GHz. As the temperature further increases, MoAlB gradually begins to decompose, as discussed in Section 3.1. The decomposition of MoAlB leads to a decrease in its absorbing performance, which is reflected in the bandwidth and thickness of the material used as an absorbing material (up to 4.5 mm compared to 1.2 mm at 1000 °C). This also shows that MoAlB is the main electromagnetic wave absorbing medium, and the AlMo intermetallic compound contributes to the adjustment of impedance matching. Notably, strong absorption at 9.28−17.52 GHz with an ultrathin layer (1.2 mm) can be achieved by adjusting the synthesis temperature. ### 3.4 EMW absorption mechanism

The excellent EMW absorption performance of MoAlB samples is attributed to the layered structure, conductance, and dipole effect. The layered structure of MoAlB can help to adjust the impedance matching of the matrix. Intermetallic compounds such as Al4Mo and Al12Mo are good conductive compounds and can provide rich interfaces, which produce strong interface polarization under an alternating electric field. Additionally, the conductivity of intermetallic compounds is better than that of MoAlB ceramics, which can not only provide dielectric loss but also adjust the impedance matching of the absorber. The presence of Al–Mo intermetallic compounds can also form a local conductive network with MoAlB, providing another method for dielectric loss [84].

In the case of dielectric materials, the Cole‒Cole semicircle is employed to elucidate the mechanisms contributing to the dielectric loss in the EMW absorber [85]. According to the Debye dipole relaxation model, Eq. (4) delineates the correlation between the real part (* ε′) and the imaginary part ( ε′′) of the dielectric constant:

(4) (ε′−εs+ε∞2)2+(ε′′)2=(εs−ε∞2)2

where  εs and  ε∞ denote the static permittivity and relative dielectric permittivity at the high-frequency limit, respectively. The  ε′ vs.  ε′′ curve should form a semicircle, known as the Cole‒Cole semicircle, where each semicircle symbolizes a single Debye dipolar relaxation process.

As depicted in Fig. 11, the semicircles prove the occurrence of the relaxation process in all the samples. The MoAlB-900 °C sample exhibits more Cole‒Cole semicircles, which proves that there are more Debye dipole relaxations and a consequent increase in dielectric loss. Nevertheless, the distortion of the Cole–Cole semicircles suggests the presence of additional polarization mechanisms, including Maxwell–Wagner relaxation, atomic polarization, space charge effect, and interfacial polarization [86,87].

In summary, the superior EMW absorption performance of MoAlB is due to various underlying mechanisms, as illustrated in Fig. 12. Dipole polarization refers to the movement of dipoles in polar or nonpolar molecules in an alternating electric field. According to research on the electronic properties of MoAlB, the Mo‒B bond is a polar covalent bond with a short distance [43], suggesting that there is a difference in electronegativity between Mo and B in the MoAlB crystal. Consequently, the inherent dipole, influenced by the external electromagnetic field and the polarity of the Mo‒B bond, undergoes rearrangement, leading to directional polarization. In addition, the electrons gathering around the Mo‒B bond will lead to an uneven distribution of the electron cloud [88], resulting in the loss of electrical energy. Based on the analysis, MoAlB will undergo dipole polarization and relaxation processes under the effect of an external electromagnetic field, which consumes the energy of the EMW. Additionally, the enhanced EMW absorption performance can be attributed in part to the interfacial polarization of the MoAlB samples. As a layered boride, MoAlB has different conductivities between layers; Mo‒B bonds are strong covalent bonds, and Mo‒Al bonds are weak metallic bonds. The unique layered structure leads to different movement speeds of charge carriers, causing multiple processes of polarization and relaxation and consuming the energy of the EMW. The presence of intermetallic compounds will also cause interfacial polarization. At the same time, the difference in conductivity will also enhance the conduction loss of the sample, thereby further improving the energy loss of the EMW. In other words, MoAlB demonstrates superior EMW absorption due to the combined influence of multiple dielectric loss mechanisms.

## 4 Conclusions

This work integrates theoretical calculations and experimental characterization to investigate the potential application of MoAlB in the field of EMW absorption. MoAlB ceramics with unique layered crystal structures were successfully synthesized at 800−1300 °C under vacuum, and their EMW absorption properties were investigated. The results show that the synthesis temperature of the MoAlB samples plays an important role in tuning the phase composition and crystal size and adjusting the electromagnetic parameters and impedance. Remarkably, the sample prepared at 900 °C achieved an exceptional minimum RL (−50.33 dB) at 12.16 GHz with an ultrathin thickness of 1.7 mm. It is worth noting that strong absorption at 9.28−17.52 GHz with an ultrathin layer (minimum of 1.2 mm) can be achieved by adjusting the synthesis temperature. The EMW absorption characteristics of MoAlB can be attributed to factors such as optimal impedance matching, dielectric loss, and geometric effects including multiple reflections and scattering. Owing to its distinctive crystal structure, amalgamation of properties, and effective absorption mechanisms, MoAlB has emerged as a compelling alternative to conventional microwave absorbing materials.
