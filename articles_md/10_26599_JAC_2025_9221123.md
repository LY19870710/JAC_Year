## 1 Introduction

With the continuous advancement of global nuclear energy development and utilization, the disposal of nuclear waste and the prevention and control of radioactive pollution have become urgent global environmental issues [1]. Among the radionuclides of concern, 137Cs and 90Sr are the main products with high fission yields (6.09% and 5.73%, respectively) of the nuclear fission of 235U [2]. In addition, 137Cs and 90Sr pose significant ecological risks because of their long half-life periods (*t*1/2 = 30.17 and 28.8 years) and high biological toxicity [3], which can easily enter the ecosystem through hydrological systems and atmospheric pathways, subsequently accumulating in the food chain to create persistent threats to human health [4]. 137Cs and 90Sr exist in aqueous environments in the form of highly migratory Cs+ and Sr2+ ions, and the ionic radii (Cs+ and Sr2+: 1.67 and 1.18 Å) and charge densities of 137Cs and 90Sr enable them to compete relatively easily and replace essential biological cations (K+, Ca2+: 1.33 and 0.99 Å), which may lead to DNA damage, cellular dysfunction and even induce cancer [5,6]. Therefore, the development of highly efficient adsorbents to remove 90Sr and 137Cs from nuclear waste before they leak into the environment is essential and urgent. Current remediation strategies for radionuclides include chemical precipitation [7], ion exchange [8], membrane filtration [9,10], organic extraction [11], and adsorption [12]. Among these methods, the adsorption method has become a hot topic in radionuclide treatment research because of its advantages, such as straightforward operational procedures, excellent removal efficiency, and relatively economical cost [13,14]. There are already numerous well-designed and synthesized materials for Sr2+ and Cs+ removal, such as Prussian blue [15], functionalized carbon-based materials [16], metal sulfides [17], and metal organic frameworks (MOFs) [18]. However, these materials have disadvantages such as high synthesis requirements, low mechanical strength, poor radiation resistance, and secondary pollution. In contrast, zeolites, as microporous crystalline aluminosilicates featuring unique three-dimensional (3D) frameworks, have emerged as promising adsorbents for radioactive contamination remediation owing to their excellent ion exchange capacity, simple preparation conditions, and certain radiation resistance [19,20].

The current mainstream zeolite synthesis technologies include hydrothermal [21], solvent-free [22], and solid-phase synthesis [23]. However, the hydrothermal method requires the use of organic templating agents at high temperatures and pressures, which leads to the generation of toxic and strong alkali waste liquid emissions [24]. To overcome the limitations of hydrothermal methods, researchers have attempted to develop novel methods. The solvent-free method offers significant advantages, including rapid crystallization (2–4 h) and lower energy consumption through elimination of liquid-phase solvents, resulting in a 40%–60% reduction in carbon emissions. However, there are limitations in phase purity due to restricted solid‒solid diffusion, which often leads to phase mixing and the need for ultrafine precursors (25]. Solid-phase methods operate at near-quantitative atomic efficiencies (~95%) under ambient conditions, which conforms to the principles of green chemistry. However, owing to the lack of a uniform dispersion effect of the liquid phase medium, it is difficult for the raw materials to achieve ideal uniformity during the mixing process, resulting in an uneven distribution of components during the crystal growth process and poor controllability of the crystal morphology and structure [26]. In addition, the prepared zeolites are mostly powder with low mechanical strength, which easily results in a high pressure drop in dynamic adsorption experiments. This defect severely affects practical engineering applications. The spherical materials exhibit good fluidity and low powder resistance through dynamic adsorption [27,28]. At present, spherical zeolites are mainly formed via the binder pelletizing method, leading to pore blockage and performance degradation. Therefore, developing an *in situ* growth method for the preparation of spherical zeolites is necessary. It is well known that NaX zeolites possess a faujasite (FAU) framework structure defined by supercages of 7.4 Å and interconnected sodalite cages [29]. This unique architecture endows NaX zeolite with a high cation exchange capacity, large specific surface area (SSA), and molecular sieving properties. NaX zeolites are widely used commercially in gas separation [30], CO2 capture [31], dye removal [32], and catalysis [33]. However, their application in radionuclide adsorption remains less explored. Given the above, developing a new and efficient approach to synthesize NaX zeolite microspheres with both high mechanical strength and excellent adsorption performance has become a key challenge in the field of radionuclide pollution remediation.

Geopolymers represent a class of inorganic polymers formed by alkali activation of aluminosilicate precursors [34], featuring a 3D amorphous network and exhibiting high mechanical strength [35], excellent irradiation resistance [36], and an eco-friendly preparation process [37,38]. The unique silica‒aluminum network structure of geopolymers shows high structural similarity to that of zeolite precursors, and this zeolite‒like topology provides a material basis for their *in situ* conversion into zeolites without binders [39,40]. Moreover, the three-dimensional amorphous gel network structure of the geopolymer itself has high mechanical strength [41], which makes it possible to grow high-strength zeolite in the geopolymer matrix during the *in situ* conversion process. Geopolymer technology enables the *in situ* conversion of aluminosilicate precursors into zeolites under mild conditions at normal pressure. Water acts solely as a reaction medium [42], minimizing waste liquid generation and energy consumption. By precisely adjusting the ratios of silicon, aluminum, and alkali sources, the depolymerization‒condensation reaction of geopolymers occurs at low temperatures and low water contents, promoting the rearrangement of the silica‒aluminum network structure and enabling *in situ* growth of zeolites with regular crystalline forms. This approach uniquely combines the advantages of solvent-free and solid-phase synthesis while overcoming the difficulty of controlling the crystal morphology caused by uneven mixing of raw materials, which overcomes the technical limitations of traditional zeolite preparation and provides a new strategy for the green fabrication of high-performance zeolite materials with high mechanical strength and excellent adsorption capacity.

This study aims to overcome the aforementioned limitations by developing a novel and efficient synthesis strategy for NaX zeolite microspheres that simultaneously possess high mechanical strength, excellent radiation resistance, and superior adsorption performance for radionuclides. To achieve this goal, our research proposes geopolymer *in situ* conversion technology under mild conditions (normal pressure, 105 °C) to pioneer the synthesis of robust NaX zeolite microspheres in Guangxi university (GXU-NaXs) directly from precisely controlled aluminosilicate precursors and water glass, eliminating the need for extra binders and avoiding pore blockage. The whole preparation process was simple, easy to operate, environmentally friendly, and easy to scale up. Static batch adsorption studies were also carried out for Cs+ and Sr2+ and ensured exceptional radiation stability, which was critical for nuclear waste remediation applications. The adsorption mechanism of GXU-NaXs on radionuclides was also investigated via scanning electron microscopy (SEM)/energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), and density functional theory (DFT) theoretical calculations. The novelty of this work lies in the innovative application of geopolymer technology for the *in situ* and one-step fabrication of NaX zeolite microspheres in combination with mechanical robustness, radiation tolerance, and adsorption capacity. This approach circumvents the high-temperature/high-pressure requirements, toxic waste generation, and morphological control challenges of hydrothermal synthesis. Furthermore, it inherently provides a pathway to solve the disadvantages of the phase purity limitations of solvent-free methods and the mixing uniformity issues of solid-phase synthesis. This research not only provides new insights and prospects for the industrial-scale removal of radionuclides from nuclear wastewater but also holds great significance for the controllable preparation of high-strength zeolite microsphere adsorbents and further understanding of the adsorption mechanism for Cs+ and Sr2+.

### 2.2 Synthesis of the Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>–2SiO<sub>2</sub> precursor powder

The aluminum sols were first prepared by dissolving aluminum nitrate hydrate in ultrapure water at a molar ratio of *n*(Al(NO3)3·9H2O) : *n*(H2O) of 1 : 35, followed by magnetic stirring at 25 °C for 1 h to form a transparent and homogeneous sol. Next, the silica sol was prepared by thoroughly mixing tetraethyl orthosilicate and anhydrous ethanol at a ratio of *n*(Si(OC2H5)4) : *n*(C2H5OH) = 1 : 5 at 25 °C for 0.5 h, yielding a transparent and uniform silica sol upon completion of the reaction. Moreover, the prepared aluminum sol was slowly poured into the stirred silica sol at a Si/Al ratio of 1.0 and stirred at 750 r·min−1 for 1 h to yield a clear and uniform aluminum‒silicon composite sol. The wet gel was prepared by placing the well-stirred aluminum‒silicon sol in a 70 °C water bath, which was maintained until the gel retained its shape when the beaker was tilted at 45 °C. The gel was then aged at 25 °C for 36 h, followed by drying in a 120 °C oven for 12 h. During the drying process, the dry powder was stirred with a glass rod every 0.5 h until the surface of the powder became pale yellow, subsequently calcined at 800 °C for 2 h, and ball milled for 6 h to obtain the Al2O3‒2SiO2 powder.

### 2.3 Preparation of GXU-NaXs

Figure 1 depicts the synthesis route for GXU-NaXs. A total of 20 g of Al2O3‒2SiO2 powder was weighed in a plastic beaker (Henan Yaohua Glass Co., Ltd., China), to which a defined amount of liquid water glass was added. The solid‒liquid mixture was first manually homogenized with a glass rod, followed by mechanical dispersion at a speed of 2000 r·min−1 for 1 min to ensure uniform consistency. The well-mixed slurry was subsequently slowly poured into room-temperature silicone oil at a dispersion speed of 1000 r·min−1. Driven by the surface tension of the silicone oil, the geopolymer slurry gradually formed spherical particles that solidified quickly during the settling process, and finally, the silicone oil containing GXU-NaXs was put into a 105 °C oven for curing, followed by vacuum filtration, washing, drying, and programmed calcination (500 °C, 3 h). Finally, GXU-NaXs with a size distribution of 75–150 μm were sieved and chosen for subsequent use.

### 2.4 Preparation of the NaX zeolite block

The Al2O3–2SiO2 powder and water glass solution were mixed and stirred at a ratio of *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) = 1.8–3.8, *n*(Na2O)/*n*(Al2O3) = 1.4–1.8, and a curing time of 6–24 h. The mixture was then dispersed at 2000 r·min−1 for 1 min via a high-speed disperser (U400/80-220, Shanghai Weite Motor Co., Ltd., China) and poured into a standard 20 mm × 20 mm × 20 mm cubic mold. To eliminate entrapped air bubbles, the mold was vertically vibrated for 3 min. Finally, the samples were demolded after 12 h of curing in a 105 °C oven. The results of each group of 6 blocks were averaged to obtain the final data.

### 2.5 Wear resistance experiments of GXU-NaXs

A certain mass of the GXU-NaXs was placed in a dynamic shaker at 160 r·min−1, and samples were taken every hour, dried, and weighed to observe whether there was any mass loss to evaluate the feasibility of the subsequent materials in the dynamic adsorption experiment. The average of at least six sets of parallel data was taken.

### 2.6 DFT calculations

The Castep module in Materials Studio software 2017 was used to optimize the structure of GXU-NaXs. The corresponding structure and total energy were then optimized via the Castep module. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was adopted as the exchange-correlation functional. To ensure the accuracy of the simulation, all the calculation settings were adjusted to a high level of precision. To achieve self-consistent field convergence of the total electronic energy, the cutoff energy and the *k*-points were set to 450 eV and 2 × 2 × 2, respectively. The adsorption energy was calculated in detail in the electronic supplementary materials.

### 3.1 Effects of <i>n</i>(SiO<sub>2</sub>)/<i>n</i>(Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>) on GXU-NaXs

The molar ratio of SiO2/Al2O3 (*n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3)) in the geopolymer slurry determines the nucleation and crystal growth of zeolite during the crystallization process [43]. This work regulated the total *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) ratio of the reaction system by varying the amount of nanosilica dissolved in a sodium hydroxide solution, such as water glass, in the synthesis step and investigated its effect on the sphericity, crystalline phases, and compressive strength of the GXU-NaXs (Fig. 2). Figures 2(a)–2(c) show that the microsphere bonding phenomenon increased with increasing *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) ratio because the geopolymer slurry became more viscous with higher molecular weights of *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3), and the prepared NaX zeolite microspheres had poor sphericity and an irregular elliptical shape. The surface morphology of the microspheres began to show a cubic structure when *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) = 1.8, as shown in the inset of Fig. 2(a). With increasing *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) = 2.8, as shown in the inset of Fig. 2(b), the surface morphology exhibited typical X-shaped octahedral structural features. However, excessive *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) leads to the inability to form a better X-shaped structural feature at *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) = 3.8 (the inset of Fig. 2(c)). Moreover, as shown in Fig. 2(d), when *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) = 1.8, the microspheres existed mainly in the crystalline forms of NaA zeolite (PDF#39-0223, Na96Al96Si96O384) and sodalite (PDF#42-0216, Na6[AlSiO4]6·4H2O). NaX zeolite (PDF#38-0237, Na2Al2Si2.5O9) can be formed at *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) = 2.8 and 3.8, respectively. Moreover, sodalite also occurred as a heterogeneous phase at *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) = 2.8 and 3.8, respectively. This was due to the six-membered ring structure of the X-type zeolite occurring at higher *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) ratios [43]. However, when *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) = 3.8, the crystallinity and crystal form growth of the NaX zeolite were poor because insufficient Al–O–Si bonds in the skeleton led to incomplete polycondensations of the gel precursor, reduced crystallinity, and further weakening of the characteristic peaks of the NaX zeolite [44]. Figure S1 in the ESM shows that as *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) increased, the compressive strength also improved from 12.75 to 24.61 MPa. This occurred because the addition of *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) increased the rigidity and density of the network structure [45]. Therefore, considering the sphericity, XRD results, and strength of the GXU-NaXs, *n*(SiO2)/*n*(Al2O3) = 2.8 was chosen as the optimal condition.

### 3.2 Effects of <i>n</i>(Na<sub>2</sub>O)/<i>n</i>(Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>) on GXU-NaXs

The molar ratio of Na2O/Al2O3 (*n*(Na2O)/*n*(Al2O3)) during the geopolymerization reaction has a significant effect on the structure of zeolite frameworks [46]. To adjust the *n*(Na2O)/*n*(Al2O3) ratio of the reaction system, nanosilica was dissolved in different amounts of sodium hydroxide solution as water glass was added, and its effects on the sphericity, crystalline phases, and compressive strength of the GXU-NaXs were investigated (Fig. 3). As shown in Fig. 3(a), when *n*(Na2O)/*n*(Al2O3) = 1.4, the gel slurry was relatively viscous, resulting in poor sphericity of the formed zeolite microspheres. When *n*(Na2O)/*n*(Al2O3) = 1.6, the slurry mobility increased, resulting in the formation of more uniform zeolite microspheres (Fig. 3(b)). Figure 3(c) shows that obvious adhesion occurred in the micropheres at *n*(Na2O)/*n*(Al2O3) = 1.8. In addition, the surface morphology of the microspheres began to exhibit small X-shaped cubic structures, as shown in the insets of Figs. 3(a)–3(c). As *n*(Na2O)/*n*(Al2O3) increased to 1.8, these X-shaped structures became more obvious, but an excessive sodium-to-aluminum ratio resulted in poorer X-shaped structure characteristics and noticeable agglomeration. It is evident from Fig. 3(d) that NaX zeolite (PDF#38-0237, Na2Al2Si2.5O9) can be formed under different *n*(Na2O)/*n*(Al2O3) conditions, but the crystallinity and crystal growth of the NaX zeolites were better at *n*(Na2O)/*n*(Al2O3) = 1.6. The compressive strength of the samples (Fig. S2 in the ESM) shows that with increasing *n*(Na2O)/*n*(Al2O3) ratio, the strength first increased but then decreased. This occurred because the excess alkali produced OH- at *n*(Na2O)/*n*(Al2O3) = 1.8, which consumed part of the aluminum source in the geopolymer reaction, leading to a reduction in the strength of the microspheres [46]. Therefore, combining the sphericity, XRD results, and strength of the GXU-NaXs, it can be seen that *n*(Na2O)/*n*(Al2O3) = 1.6 was the best condition.

### 3.3 Effects of curing time on GXU-NaXs

Figure 4 illustrates the influence of different curing times on GXU-NaXs. When the curing time was 6 h, the zeolite microspheres had good sphericity and dense microsphere surfaces, as shown in Fig. 4(a) and the inset of Fig. 4(a). The zeolite microspheres maintained excellent sphericity, and the surface of the microspheres displayed obvious X-shaped structural features at 12 h (Fig. 4(b) and the inset of Fig. 4(b)), which indicated that the NaX zeolite grew successfully. However, the zeolite microspheres clearly agglomerated as the curing time increased to 24 h (Fig. 4(c) and the inset of Fig. 4(c)). The XRD analysis in Fig. 4(d) revealed that the crystalline phase of the geopolymer microspheres was still amorphous at 6 h, and the crystalline transition had not yet occurred. As the curing time increased to 12 and 24 h, a crystalline phase of NaX (PDF#38-0237, Na2Al2Si2.5O9) with higher crystallinity appeared. However, sodalite (PDF#42-0216, Na6[AlSiO4]6·4H2O) appeared at 12 and 24 h, and the peak intensity of sodalite was greater at 24 h. Meanwhile, the compressive strength of the samples gradually decreased as the curing time increased (Fig. S3 in the ESM). This occurred because as time increased, the amount of amorphous gel decreased, resulting in the formation of more crystalline zeolite [41]. The strength at 24 h (13.25 MPa) was lower than that at 12 h (19.21 MPa). Therefore, combining the sphericity, XRD results, and strength of GXU-NaXs, it can be concluded that the optimal curing time was 12 h.

### 3.4 Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) and Barret–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) analysis

According to the IUPAC classification, the N2 adsorption‒desorption isotherm of GXU-NaXs in Fig. 5(a) is type I [47], which is characteristic of zeolite microporous materials with H4-type hysteresis shapes and narrow adsorption‒desorption hysteresis. The SSA of GXU-NaXs was 568.41 m2·g−1 at a medium to high level of typical NaX zeolite (500–700 m2·g−1), indicating that its microporous structure was well developed, which was consistent with the high SSA characteristic of zeolite type X. The total pore volume (*V*total) of GXU-NaXs was 0.16 cm3·g−1, and approximately 80% (0.13 cm3·g−1) of *V**t*otal was the micropore volume (*V*micro) of NaX zeolite, whereas the contribution of the mesopores (*V*mesop) was approximately 20%. The SSA of GXU-NaXs was contributed mainly by the inner surface of the micropores, and *V*micro was positively correlated with the SSA, which further verified that GXU-NaX had a high SSA of 568.41 m2·g−1. The *V*total was significantly lower than the typical value (0.25–0.35 cm3·g−1). This occurred because different synthesis methods, such as activation and modification, can cause deviations in the SSA and pore volume of zeolite [48−51]. Furthermore, the utilization of zeolites is subject to friction and interparticle impacts, thus affecting their performance. The experimental findings demonstrate that the Vickers hardness of GXU-NaXs is 216.30 and that the wear rate is 0.0035%, which indicates favorable wear resistance and stability in dynamic industrial applications.

#### 3.5.2 Adsorption kinetics

To explore the kinetics of Sr2+ and Cs+ removal onto GXU-NaXs, the experimental data were analyzed via pseudo-first/second-order (PF/SO) and intraparticle diffusion (IPD) models. The corresponding fitting results are presented in Table S2 in the ESM. As shown in Figs. 7(a) and 7(c), the adsorption of Cs+ and Sr2+ by GXU-NaXs increased rapidly with increasing adsorption time within the initial 20 min and reached adsorption equilibrium at 45 and 30 min. This occurred because at the early stage of adsorption, Cs+ and Sr2+ rapidly occupied the active sites on the surface of the GXU-NaXs, resulting in a rapid increase in adsorption. After 45 and 30 min of adsorption, the active sites of GXU-NaXs were gradually occupied by Cs+ and Sr2+ until adsorption equilibrium. The correlation coefficients of the PSO kinetic models for Sr2+ and Cs+ (*R*Sr2 = 0.973 and *R*Cs2 = 0.985) were both better than those of the PFO models (*R*Sr2 = 0.967 and *R*Cs2 = 0.981), suggesting that the removal process predominantly occurred via chemisorption [53]. The IPD model was employed to investigate the adsorption mechanism and rate-controlling step, as shown in Figs. 7(b) and 7(d). The fitting results revealed that the adsorption process of GXU-NaXs on Cs+ and Sr2+ can involve three distinct stages: liquid film diffusion, IPD, and adsorption equilibrium. In addition, the nonzero intercepts of the IPD model fits indicated that IPD was not the sole controlling step in the adsorption process [54].

#### 3.5.3 Isothermal adsorption

Figures 8(a) and 8(c) show that the equilibrium adsorption capacity (*Q*e,exp) of GXU-NaXs increased with increasing equilibrium concentration of Cs+ and Sr2+ and that as the equilibrium concentration increased, the adsorption of GXU-NaXs on Cs+ and Sr2+ tended to reach equilibrium. This occurred because the effective active sites of the zeolite microspheres became saturated and were unable to adsorb an excess of the two ions. To analyze the adsorption performance of GXU-NaXs for Cs+ and Sr2+, the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Dubinin–Radushkevich (D–R) isothermal adsorption models were used to fit the experimental data, and the corresponding findings are provided in Table S3 in the ESM. The *Q*e,exp of GXU-NaXs decreased with increasing temperature, indicating that higher temperatures unfavorably affected the adsorption of Cs+ and Sr2+ by GXU-NaXs. The fitting results at 298, 318, and 338 K revealed a stronger correlation for the Langmuir model (*R*Sr2 = 0.947, 0.964, and 0.954; *R*Cs2 = 0.928, 0.930, and 0.917) than for the Freundlich model (*R*Sr2 = 0.896, 0.922, and 0.929; *R*Cs2 = 0.889, 0.913, and 0.825), with maximum exchange capacities (*Q*m) of 153.60 and 138.30 mg·g−1 for Sr2+ and Cs+ at 298 K, respectively, which indicated that the adsorption process of GXU-NaXs on Cs+ and Sr2+ was mainly monolayer adsorption [55]. As shown in Figs. 8(b) and 8(d), these curves fitted well with D–R model, and the formula was listed in the ESM. In the model, *Q*e represents the adsorption capacity at equilibrium time, and *ε* represents the Polanyi potential energy. Moreover, Figs. 8(b) and 8(d) and Table S3 in the ESM reveal that the binding energies of Sr2+ and Cs+ by GXU-NaXs were lower than 8 kJ·mol−1, which implies that the adsorption process also involved physical adsorption [56].

Fig. 8Adsorption isotherms of GXU-NaXs for Cs+ and Sr2+ fitted with (a, c) Langmuir, Freundlich, and (b, d) D–R models.

To verify the feasibility of the Sr2+ and Cs+ removal process by GXU-NaXs, the thermodynamic parameters were determined via the van’t Hoff equation, and the results are summarized in Table S4 in the ESM. ΔG*0 was −32.78/−95.73, −34.01/−99.89, and −35.24/−104.25 kJ·mol−1 (298, 318, and 338 K) for Sr2+ and Cs+, respectively, suggesting that the Sr2+ and Cs+ adsorption reactions were spontaneous. The values of Δ*H*0 = −14.46/−32.50 kJ·mol−1 confirmed that the adsorption reaction was exothermic, whereas positive values of Δ*S*0 = 61.48/213.02 J·mol−1·K−1 suggested increased disorder at the solid‒solution interface. These results demonstrated that lower temperatures enhanced the adsorption reaction process for Sr2+ and Cs+ removal by GXU-NaXs, which was consistent with the trend observed in Figs. 8(a) and 8(c).

#### 3.5.4 Influence of coexisting ions and real water on Cs<sup>+</sup> and Sr<sup>2+</sup> removal by GXU-NaXs

Many multiple species of coexisting ions are present in industrial wastewater, and these ions may have a certain inhibitory effect on the performance of GXU-NaXs for the adsorption of Sr2+ and Cs+. Therefore, the effects of GXU-NaXs on the adsorption of Sr2+ and Cs+ were investigated under the conditions of coexisting ions such as Na+, K+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ at different molar concentrations (0.01, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 M), and the results are presented in Figs. 9(a) and 9(b). The adsorption capacity of Sr2+ and Cs+ decreased with increasing concentrations of various coexisting ions, primarily due to the increased competition for active sites on the adsorbent surface under high concentrations of coexisting ions. In addition, GXU-NaXs affected the adsorption of Sr2+ in the following order: Ca2+ > Mg2+ > K+ > Na+. The presence of Ca2+ had the greatest effect on Sr2+, which can be attributed to the fact that Ca2+ and Sr2+ belong to the homologous group of the cycle and have similar hydronium ionic radii (Ca, Sr: 4.12 Å) [57]. The GXU-NaXs affected the adsorption removal of Cs+ in the following order: K+ > Na+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+. At 0.5 M, the greater inhibition of Cs+ adsorption by K+ and Na+ was due to the similarity between the hydrated ionic radii of K+ (3.31 Å) and Na+ (3.58 Å) and that of Cs+ (3.29 Å) [58]. Moreover, the affinity of the adsorbent for metal ions was assessed via the distribution coefficient (*K*d). The *K*d values of GXU-NaXs for Cs+ and Sr2+ reached 4586 and 3228 mL·g−1 (> 104 mL·g−1), respectively, as shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM, which indicated that GXU-NaXs had a strong affinity for Cs+ and Sr2+ with a certain degree of selectivity. These findings suggest that GXU-NaXs are promising adsorbents for the remediation of nuclear wastewater. Figsures 9(c) and 9(d) show that the Sr2+ (Cs+) adsorption capacities of GXU-NaXs in seawater, natural surface water, and tap water were 99.98% (98.87%), 94.14% (92.16%), and 95.65% (91.65%), respectively, at a dosage of 2.4 g·L−1. When the adsorbent dosage was increased to 3.2 g·L−1, GXU-NaXs resulted in better removal of > 95% Cs+ in seawater and natural surface water. These findings demonstrate that the adsorption efficiency of GXU-NaXs for Sr2+ and Cs+ is promising for industrial-scale operations.

### 3.6 Adsorption mechanism

The microscopic morphology and composition changes of GXU-NaXs before and after adsorption were studied via SEM/EDS and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 10). Figures 10(a)–10(c) show that the overall structure of the GXU-NaXs did not change significantly before and after adsorption, indicating obvious X-type structural characteristics. Moreover, GXU-NaXs retained structural integrity after adsorption (Figs. 10(b) and 10(c)), indicating that GXU-NaXs had good mechanical properties. Moreover, the element distribution map obtained via TEM revealed that the distribution of Si, Al, and O remained evenly distributed before and after the reaction. However, the content of sodium decreased significantly after adsorption. Moreover, cesium and strontium were detected after adsorption, which proved that the elements were successfully adsorbed onto GXU-NaXs and confirmed that the adsorption mechanism was ion exchange between sodium and cesium/strontium ions.

Figure 11(a) shows the FT-IR spectra before and after the adsorption of Sr2+ and Cs+ by GXU-NaXs. The peaks at 3461 and 1648 cm−1 were caused by the stretching and bending vibrations of hydroxyl groups (–OH) in water molecules [53]. ### 3.7 Radiation resistance of GXU-NaXs

Given the intrinsic characteristics of gamma radiation in nuclear environments, it is crucial to examine the radiation resistance of materials for practical applications. The NaX zeolite blocks and GXU-NaXs cured for 7 and 280 d were placed in a gamma-ray space with a dose of 500 KGy, and the results are presented in Fig. 13. Figures 13(a) and 13(b) show the effects of irradiation on the appearance and microscopic morphology of the NaX zeolite blocks before and after irradiation. Moreover, the surfaces of the blocks cured for 7 and 280 d did not differ from those of the unirradiated blocks after irradiation with 500 KGy. The compressive strengths at 7 and 280 d (19.25 and 20.62 MPa) after irradiation with 500 KGy did not significantly decrease compared with those at 7 and 280 d (19.21 and 21.03 MPa), indicating that the irradiated GXU-NaXs still had relatively high compressive strengths, as shown in Fig. 13(c). The XRD pattern (Fig. 13(d)) confirmed that the characteristic diffraction peak of the NaX zeolite (PDF#38-0237, Na2Al2Si2.5O9) was preserved after 500 KGy, suggesting that irradiation at 500 KGy had no significant effect on the morphology or phase of the GXU-NaXs. The adsorption capacity effects of 500 KGy irradiation on Cs+ and Sr2+ are shown in Fig. 13(e). The irradiated GXU-NaXs still had good adsorption capacities for Cs+ and Sr2+ (132.10 and 148.02 mg·g−1, respectively) compared with those before adsorption (138.30 and 153.60 mg·g−1, respectively). These findings indicate that GXU-NaXs have excellent radiation resistance and are expected to provide a new approach for the removal of wastewater containing radioactive nuclides.

### 3.8 Adsorption and desorption cycles and column experiments

Figure 14(a) shows the desorption rates of GXU-NaXs after adsorbing Sr2+ and Cs+ with different desorption agents. Dissolution occurred when the samples were desorbed in 0.1 mol·L−1 HCl or EDTA-2Na solutions, and there was almost no desorption in water. However, Sr2+ and Cs+ had certain desorption effects on 0.1 mol·L−1 C6H5Na3O7 and NaCl solutions. Different concentrations of NaCl and C6H5Na3O7 solutions were used as desorption agents to conduct a 12 h desorption experiment on the GXU-NaXs. Figures 14(b) and 14(c) show that with increasing NaCl concentration, the desorption rate of Cs+ by GXU-NaXs first tended to increase but then decreased. The desorption rate of Cs+ in a 1 mol·L−1 NaCl solution was 98.26%. Therefore, a 1 mol·L−1 NaCl solution was selected as the desorption agent for the adsorption‒desorption experiments, and it still had a good removal effect of 97.89% after three cycles. Figures 14(d) and 14(e) show that with increasing C6H5Na3O7 concentration, the desorption rate of Sr2+ by GXU-NaXs first tends to increase but then decreases. The desorption rate of Sr2+ in 0.5 mol·L−1 C6H5Na3O7 solution was 56.39%. Therefore, a 0.5 mol·L−1 C6H5Na3O7 solution was selected as the desorption agent for the adsorption‒desorption experiments, and it still had a removal rate of 70% for Sr2+ after three cycles. To simulate continuous treatment for practical application to water bodies, this study analyzed the dynamic adsorption effect of GXU-NaXs on Cs+ and Sr2+ via dynamic column experiments and fitted the results with the Thomas model (Fig. 14(f) and Fig. S4 in the ESM). The dynamic breakthrough curves were all “S” shaped, and the breakthrough times of Cs+ and Sr2+ were 586 and 502 min at a flow rate of 6 mL·min−1 and a column height of 4 cm, reaching complete breakthrough at 1200 and 1700 min for Cs+ and Sr2+, respectively. Therefore, GXU-NaXs exhibit favorable dynamic adsorption performance for Cs+ and Sr2+, which has great application advantages in the removal of radionuclides from nuclear wastewater.

There are many promising methods for sintering zeolite into solid-state species [73]. In this work, our group utilized in situ* thermal curing methods for calcination. This can be explained by the removal of physically and chemically bound water by the zeolite [74]. Between 200 and 400 °C, a mass loss of 7% was observed, indicating dehydration of the zeolite framework [75]. The endothermic peak without mass change was observed at 920 °C and was associated with the initial formation of new phases, which required external energy for the start of the reaction and showed heat absorption, and the exothermic peak at 1100 °C without significant mass loss was due mainly to the radical crystalline transformation that occurred [76]. The thermal stability of the GXU-NaXs was stable when the temperature was increased to 1100 °C.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, GXU-NaXs with a high mechanical strength of 19.21 MPa under mild conditions were prepared via an *in situ* conversion method using geopolymer technology. GXU-NaXs exhibited exceptional adsorption performance for Cs+ and Sr2+, reaching equilibrium at 45/30 min, with maximum saturated adsorption capacities of 138.30 and 153.60 mg·g−1, respectively, following predominantly monolayer adsorption. It was also possible to qualitatively predict that the optimal adsorption site models and adsorption energies for Sr2+ and Cs+ capture were the interior of the cage of GXU-NaXs (*E*ads = −3.52 eV) and the surface of the cage of GXU-NaXs (*E*ads = −2.43 eV), which revealed that Sr2+ was mostly immobilized inside the GXU-NaX microspheres after adsorption and diffusion, whereas Cs+ was mainly adsorbed on the microsphere surface. Dynamic adsorption experiments demonstrated that GXU-NaXs had good dynamic adsorption at 6 mL·min−1. In addition, GXU-NaXs affected the adsorption of Sr2+ and Cs+ in the following order: Ca2+ > Mg2+ > K+ > Na+ and K+ > Na+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+. The Sr2+ and Cs+ removal rates of GXU-NaXs in seawater, natural surface water, and tap water were 99.98 (98.87), 94.14 (92.16), and 95.65 (91.65)%, respectively, at a dosage of 2.4 g·L−1. The GXU-NaXs prepared in this study offer novel experimental and theoretical support for improving the current zeolite synthesis process, realizing the controllable conversion of zeolites, and achieving an in-depth understanding of the adsorption mechanism at the atomic level. Future work should focus on scaling up the synthesis, evaluating long-term stability under continuous radiation and flow conditions, and assessing the economic viability and performance in the pilot-scale treatment of real radioactive effluents to fully realize their engineering potential. Moreover, sintering methods that are currently used and promising worldwide will be used to further study the immobilization of the possible processes of zeolite consolidation into solid-state species in the future.
