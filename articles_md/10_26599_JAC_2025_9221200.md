## 1 Introduction

The rapid development of wireless communication technology and microwave-band electronic devices has brought convenience but also poses a severe threat to human health and the operation of precision electronic equipment due to electromagnetic radiation [1−5]. Electromagnetic wave absorbing materials, which convert electromagnetic energy into thermal or other forms of energy, have become crucial in addressing this issue. Ideal MA materials must possess characteristics such as light weight, thinness, a wide frequency range, and strong absorption [6−8], which are essential for proposing an effective strategy to achieve excellent electromagnetic properties [9−11].

The current electromagnetic loss mechanisms are mainly composed of dielectric loss and magnetic loss [8,12,13]. Although carbon-based materials have the advantages of being lightweight and having high conductivity and dielectric loss, their impedance matching performance is poor [14−18]. Therefore, optimizing impedance matching and enhancing the magnetic‒carbon synergistic effect through magnetic‒carbon composites should focus on different magnetic‒carbon materials, including biologically derived, metal‒organic framework (MOF)-derived, and surface‒modified carbon substrates. Biologically derived magnetic-carbon materials include CoNi/CNT carbon nanotube aerogels derived from algae [19], FeCo alloy-modified carbon aerogels from cellulose [20], Co/C aerogels from chitosan [21], and CoNi/C magnetic biocarbon nanocomposites from papaya seeds [22]. Additionally, MOF-derived magnetic carbon materials include Co@C [23], CoNi/C composites [24], and FeCoZn@C [25]. Furthermore, magnetic particles loaded on carbon nanotubes, such as NiCoC/CNT/RGO reduced graphene oxide aerogels [26], Co@NC [27], graphene/polyimide/CoNC aerogels [28], and high-entropy alloy@graphene nanocapsules [29], exhibit excellent MA properties. In summary, the distribution of magnetic microspheres plays a vital role in regulating dielectric and wave-absorbing properties [30,31]. The presence and state of magnetic materials in carbon substrates are crucial for controlling the electromagnetic properties and wave-absorbing performance of composites, including the size [32], distribution [33], content [34], and type [34,35] of magnetic particles. For example, Liu *et al*. [32] fabricated Co@C nanocapsules with adjustable sizes through one-step carbon reduction, with wide-frequency electromagnetic wave absorption characteristics. Wen *et al*. [33] prepared CoNi@Mo2C composites via *in situ* derivation, and their magnetic particles were evenly distributed on carbon nanotubes, resulting in excellent absorption properties. Zhang *et al*. [34] prepared Co/C composites via the solvothermal method to regulate the cobalt content to achieve wide-band high-efficiency electromagnetic wave absorption. Zhang *et al*. [35] combined four magnetic alloys (FeCo, CoNi, FeNi, and FeCoNi) with carbon nanocoil templates to prepare composites with similar morphologies, which have good low-frequency absorption properties. However, the precise regulation of the spatial distribution of magnetic particles in carbon spheres has rarely been reported for a single system of magnetic‒carbon composite microspheres.

Herein, a two-step spray drying‒pyrolysis method was proposed to construct synergistic magnetic‒dielectric Co@C-CNT microspheres. On the basis of the melamine-induced growth mechanism, the distribution of magnetic Co NPs on carbon microspheres (including internal, surface, and exterior) can be effectively regulated. First, the reduced Co NPs catalyze the graphitization transformation of polyvinylpyrrolidone to form a carbon layer. Second, the nitrogen and carbon sources produced by melamine decomposition not only participate in the formation of graphitized carbon layers but also promote the growth of magnetic cobalt particles, ultimately forming Co@C-CNT microspheres. By introducing melamine, the distribution of magnetic particles in the carbon microspheres is not only adjusted from the interior to the exterior of the carbon microspheres, but the interface properties and conductivity of the microspheres are also optimized, and excellent impedance matching and multiple loss mechanisms are realized. Co@C-CNT-2 has excellent absorption performance, with a reflection loss (RL) of −32.48 dB at a thickness of 2.0 mm and an effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) of 5.2 GHz at an ultrathin thickness of 1.8 mm. This study offers a novel strategy for designing excellent MA materials.

## 2 Experimental

Materials: Cobalt nitrate hexahydrate Co(NO3)2·6H2O, *N*,*N*-dimethylformamide (DMF), anhydrous ethanol, and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP; K30) were purchased from Shanghai Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., all of which were of analytical reagent grade. Milli-Q water, generated by a Milli-Q system (Millipore), was utilized in all the experimental procedures.

Synthesis of the precursor powders: First, 4 mmol Co(NO3)6·6 H2O and 1.5 g PVP-K30 were dissolved in a mixed solution, which included deionized water (10 mL), ethanol (10 mL), and *N*,*N-*dimethylformamide (10 mL), with magnetic stirring for 30 min. Second, the solution was transferred into a spray drying machine with parameters set to an inlet temperature of 180 °C, an outlet temperature of 97 °C, and a solution flow rate into the spray nozzle of 20 revolutions per second. Finally, pink precursor powders were obtained at the same time.

Co@C and Co@C-CNT microspheres: Pink precursor powders were mixed with varying quantities of melamine to achieve mass ratios of 1 : 0, 1 : 0.5, 1 : 1, and 1 : 2. The mixed powders were subsequently annealed under a H2/Ar atmosphere at 600 °C for 3 h, and the H2/Ar gas ratio was 5 : 95. Magnetic carbon-based microspheres, termed Co@C, Co@C-CNT-1, Co@C-CNT-2, and Co@C-CNT-3, were obtained with increasing melamine ratios. A constant heating rate of 5 °C min−1 was used for all the annealing procedures.

Characterizations: The crystal structure of all the samples was characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8-Advance, Bruker), a Raman spectroscope (Renishaw), a vibrating sample magnetometer (MPMS VSM, Quantum Design Co.), a scanning electron microscope (SEM; S4800, Hitachi), and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-2100F, JEOL). The Co@C-CNT microspheres were dispersed in a paraffin matrix at a 60 wt% filler ratio and pressed into coaxial rings with an outer diameter of 7.0 mm and an inner diameter of 3.0 mm. The electromagnetic parameters were measured via a vector network analyzer (N5230C, Agilent) in the 2–18 GHz frequency range. The reflection loss and impedance matching were calculated according to Eqs. (1) and (2):

(1) Z=|Zin/Z0|=μr/εrtanh(j(2πfd/c)εrμr)

(2) RL=20|(log⁡(Zin−Z0)/(Zin+Z0))|

where *Z* represents impedance matching, RL refers to reflection loss, *Z*in and *Z*0 are the input impedance and output impedance, respectively, *ε*r is the complex permittivity constant, *μ*r is the complex permeability, *f* is the test frequency, *d* is the simulated thickness, and *c* is the speed of light in free space.

### 3.1 Preparation and characterization of Co@C-CNT composites

The synthesis route of the Co@C-CNT composites is shown in Fig. 1(a). The microspheres were prepared via spray drying‒pyrolysis. When polyvinylpyrrolidone is used as a carbon source, metal nitrate (cobalt nitrate) is mixed with it to obtain cobalt precursors via spray drying (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). The cobalt precursors were subsequently annealed with different proportions of melamine at 600 °C for 5 h in a H2/Ar atmosphere to produce Co@C (Fig. S2 in the ESM) and Co@C-CNT. Melamine promotes the growth of cobalt particles; melamine acts as a coordinating agent for directing Co NPs to the surface, and it also serves as a source of carbon/nitrogen species, which can enhance graphitization and suppress particle agglomeration. Moreover, cobalt nanoparticles catalyze the formation of a graphitized carbon layer in K30 and finally form Co@C-CNT composites microspheres. X-ray diffraction analysis was performed to investigate the phase composition of the material (Fig. 1(b)). In the XRD pattern, a characteristic diffraction peak is observed at approximately 26°, corresponding to the graphitized carbon (002) crystal plane converted by K30. The three characteristic diffraction peaks at 44.2°, 51.6°, and 76.0° correspond to the (111), (200), and (220) crystal planes of face-centered cubic cobalt (JPCDS No. 15-0806), respectively, confirming the existence of cobalt nanoparticles. Raman spectroscopy was used to analyze the degree of graphitization of the microspheres (Fig. 1(c)), and all the microspheres presented characteristic peaks at approximately 1350 cm−1 (D-band) and 1580 cm−1 (G-band), with ratios representing the degree of graphitization of the carbon materials [36]. The *I*D*/I*G values of Co@C-CNT-1, Co@C-CNT-2, and Co@C-CNT-3 were 0.97, 0.98, and 1.01, respectively, and the *I*D/*I*G values increased, and the increase in melamine increased nitrogen doping, increased sp3 defects, and decreased sp2 order, resulting in a decrease in the overall graphitization degree. The distribution process of cobalt nanoparticles on the carbon microspheres was studied by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As the amount of melamine increased, the size of the Co@C-CNT composite structure increased from ~5.84 to 7.08 μm and 7.21 μm (Figs. 1(d) and 1(f) and Fig. S3 in the ESM). Moreover, the distribution statistics revealed that the average sizes of the Co@C-CNT-1 and Co@C-CNT-2 cobalt particles were approximately 0.12 and 0.30 μm, respectively (Figs. 1(g) and 1(h)). The average length of the carbon nanotubes (CNTs) grown in Co@C-CNT-3 was 0.55 μm (Fig. 1(i)), indicating that melamine effectively regulates the spatial distribution of magnetic particles on the carbon spheres, which in turn governs the intrinsic electromagnetic properties of the microspheres. Taking Co@C-CNT-2 as an example, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) also revealed the chemical composition and elemental valence state of the Co@C-CNT microspheres. The full XPS spectrum indicates that the microspheres contain Co, C, and N (Fig. 1(j)). The Co 2*p* spectrum contains six characteristic peaks: the Co 2*p*1/2 orbital at 796.0 eV splits into metallic zero-valent Co0 and divalent Co2+ components (Fig. 1(k)) and satellite peaks at 787.4 and 803.3 eV, which are consistent with literature reports [37]. The C 1*s* spectrum can be divided into three characteristic peaks, corresponding to C–C (284.8 eV), C–O (286.2 eV), and O=C (288.2 eV) bonds (Fig. 1(l)) [38]. The peaks in the N 1s spectrum (Fig. 1(m)) can be divided into three parts, corresponding to graphite nitrogen (402.3 eV), pyrroliazine (400.3 eV), and pyridine nitrogen (398.5 eV), where *N* is derived from melamine [36]. XPS spectra of Co@C-CNT-1 and Co@C-CNT-3 are available in the supporting information (Fig. S4 in the ESM). Additionally, high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) STEM and EDS mapping revealed the distributions of Co, N, C, and O (Fig. S5 in the ESM).

The microstructure of Co@C-CNT-2 was further characterized via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figs. 2(a)–2(c)). With increasing amounts of melamine, the carbon nanotubes (CNTs) on the Co@C-CNT microspheres exhibited initial absence, sparse growth and, finally, abundant coverage. Focusing on Co@C-CNT-2 (Fig. 2(d)), magnetic cobalt particles were distributed on the surface of the carbon microspheres and extended carbon nanotubes. The distribution of CNTs within Co@C-CNTs-2 is shown in Fig. 2(e). The intensity profile across the Co/C heterointerface in Co@C-CNT-2 (Fig. 2(f)) and the lattice strain at the heterointerface of Co@C-CNT-2 were obtained via geometric phase analysis (GPA) (Fig. 2(g)), indicating the presence of an interaction force at the cobalt‒carbon heterointerface (Fig. 2(h)). Point defects and dislocations were observed within the cobalt particles (Figs. 2(i) and 2(j)), along with the corresponding strain distribution maps. The results confirm that the geometric strain is predominantly concentrated at the dislocation cores and interfacial regions (Fig. 2(j)).

### 3.2 Electromagnetic waves absorption performance

To investigate the influence of the surface magnetic particle distribution on the electromagnetic wave absorption performance, the reflection loss (RL) intensity and effective absorption bandwidth (EAB, RL ≤ −10 dB) of the obtained Co@C-CNT microspheres were evaluated within the 2–18 GHz frequency range [39−41]. The Co@C-CNT microspheres exhibited favorable electromagnetic wave absorption properties. Co@C demonstrated a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −15.11 dB at a thickness of 4 mm and showed no effective absorption bandwidth (Figs. 3(a) and 3(e)). Co@C-CNT-1 achieved an RLmin of −17.93 dB at a thickness of 2 mm, which exhibited an EAB of 4.62 GHz at a thickness of 1.5 mm (Figs. 3(b) and 3(f)). Co@C-CNT-2 exhibited the best performance: it achieved a significantly increased RLmin of −32.48 dB at a thickness of only 2 mm. In addition, Co@C-CNT-2 measured an adequate absorption bandwidth of 5.2 GHz at a thickness of only 1.8 mm (Figs. 3(c) and 3(g)). Co@C-CNT-3 attained an RLmin of −32.48 dB at a thickness of 2 mm (Fig. 3(d)), and this microsphere achieved a broad EAB of 4.16 GHz at a thickness of 1.6 mm (Fig. 3(h)). Compared with Co@C, Co@C-CNT-1, and Co@C-CNT-3, the Co@C-CNT-2 microspheres exhibited stronger RLmin and a wider EAB, indicating that the melamine-induced magnetic particle distribution has better EAB performance (Fig. 3(i)). Compared with Co@C microspheres, Co@C-CNTs have superior absorption properties (Figs. 3(a)–3(d)), and the associated absorption mechanism can be attributed to dielectric and magnetic synergy. With increasing melamine content, Co@C-CNTs have good impedance matching, dielectric loss, and magnetic loss (Fig. 3(j)) [42−44]. The magnetic spatial distribution not only tailors the magnetic response ability for improved impedance matching but also creates abundant heterogeneous interfaces and nitrogen-doped defects, significantly enhancing interfacial polarization and conduction loss.

The good absorption performance can be attributed to impedance matching and electromagnetic parameter loss. Ideally, impedance matching requires that the Co@C-CNT microsphere input impedance (Z*in) is equal to the impedance of free space (*Z*0). When the incident electromagnetic wave reaches the surface of the absorbing material, it is not reflected into free space, thus entering the absorption system, and is attenuated by various loss mechanisms [45]. The |* Δ| function can be used as an effective index to characterize the impedance matching characteristics (Eqs. (3)–(5)) [36]:

(3) |Δ|=|sinh2(Kfd)−M|

(4) K=4πμ′ε′×sin(δe+δm2)c×cosδe×cosδm

(5) M=4μ′cosδe×ε′cosδm(μ′cosδe−ε′cosδm)2+(tan(δm−δe2))2×(μ′cosδe+ε′cosδm)2

Typically, an impedance matching degree |Δ| = 0.4 is used to evaluate the level of impedance matching, where a broader area with |Δ| Fig. 4, Co@C has a limited area satisfying |Δ| Figs. 4(a)–4(d)). Specifically, for Co@C-CNT-2, the value of |Z*in/*Z*0| approaches 1 at a thickness of 2.0 mm (Fig. 4(e)). This originates from the optimized regulation of electromagnetic parameters achieved by its “internal-to-surface” gradient magnetic distribution network.

The excellent MA performance is attributed to both dielectric loss and magnetic loss. The real parts (ε'*, *μ'*) represent the material’s storage capability, whereas the imaginary parts (*ε''*, *μ''*) represent its loss capability [45−49]. Dielectric loss is associated primarily with the intrinsic electrical conductivity (*σ*), heterogeneous interfaces, and dipole defects of the microsphere. Within the 2–18 GHz range, the *ε'* values of Co@C-CNT-1 decrease from 18.45 to 9.66, those of Co@C-CNT-2 decrease from 16.29 to 8.22, and those of Co@C-CNT-3 decreases from 15.16 to 9.07 (Figs. 5(a)–5(c)). The ε*''* values exhibit a similar decreasing trend: Co@C-CNT-1 from 7.17to 6.32, Co@C-CNT-2 from 6.56 to 4.42, and Co@C-CNT-3 from 5.67 to 3.63. The results demonstrate that the electromagnetic parameters of the Co@C-CNT microspheres are significantly greater than those of Co@C (Fig. S6 in the ESM). With increasing melamine content, the average *ε'* and *ε''* values of the Co@C-CNT-1, Co@C-CNT-2, and Co@C-CNT-3 microspheres generally decrease (Fig. 5(d)). Calculations of the attenuation constant (*α*) (Fig. 5(e)) indicate that the electromagnetic attenuation capability of the Co@C-CNT microspheres is significantly greater than that of Co@C, confirming the superior dielectric loss capability of this system. Furthermore, within the 2–18 GHz range (Figs. 5(g) and 5(h), Fig. S6 in the ESM), both the *μ'* and *μ''* values of the Co@C-CNT and Co@C microspheres gradually decrease as the frequency increases. The real parts of permeability (*μ'*) for Co@C-CNT-1, Co@C-CNT-2, and Co@C-CNT-3 range from 1.13 to 0.95, 1.11 to 0.97, and 1.16 to 0.91, respectively (Fig. 5(g)). To evaluate the dissipation capability, we calculate the dielectric loss tangent (*δ*e, tan*δ*e = *ε*″/*ε*′) value and the magnetic loss tangent (*δ*m, tan*δ*m = *µ*″/*µ*′) value (Fig. S7 in the ESM).

The imaginary parts (μ''*) range from 0.08 to 0.02, 0.06 to 0.001, and 0.11 to 0.001, respectively (Fig. 5(h)). Theoretically, the permeability of magnetic absorbing materials is influenced by multiple factors, including intrinsic saturation magnetization (*M*s), coercivity (*H*c), particle size, and crystalline magnetic anisotropy. Among these factors, a competitive *M*s is beneficial for enhancing permeability, according to Eq. (6) [50]:

(6) μr=MSμ0(akHCMS+bλξ)

where *a* and *b* are material-dependent constants. *λ*, *k*, and *ξ* are the magnetostrictive, proportional, and internal strain coefficients, respectively. Hysteresis loop tests (Fig. 5(f)) revealed that the saturation magnetization strength (*M*s of Co@C-CNT-2 is 14.63 emu/g, which is significantly greater than that of Co@C-CNT-3, which is 0.4 emu·g-1. An excessive melamine content promotes excessive dispersion and encapsulation of cobalt nanoparticles within the carbon matrix, resulting in the small size of the cobalt nanoparticles. Therefore, this leads to the lower saturation magnetization of Co@C-CNT-3. In addition, the coercivity of Co@C-CNTs-2 is 1.86 Oe, indicating that Co@C-CNT microspheres are soft magnetic materials with an enhanced magnetic response [50].

According to Debye theory, the relationship between *ɛ′* and *ɛ′′* can be expressed as shown in Eq. (7), where each Cole-Cole semicircle represents a Debye relaxation process [51,52].

(7) (ε′−εs+ε∞2)2+(ε′′)2=(εs−ε∞2)2

The Cole‒Cole curve demonstrates the existence of polarization mechanisms. Compared with Co@C, Co@C-CNTs exhibit superior dielectric loss (Fig. 5(i) and 5(l)). This increased complexity is closely associated with their intricate heterogeneous interfaces (Co–C interfaces, defects) and the dipoles introduced by nitrogen doping.

To thoroughly evaluate the electromagnetic wave absorption (stealth) capability of materials under practical far-field conditions [53,54], this study conducted a radar cross-section (RCS) simulation analysis (Fig. 6). According to classical electromagnetic scattering theory, the RCS value (*σ*) of a scattering source in a spherical coordinate system is determined by both the azimuth angle (*θ*) and *φ*. Its mathematical expression is given by Eq. (8) [55]:

where S* is the simulated plate area, *λ* is the incident electromagnetic wavelength, and *E*s and *E*i represent the electric field intensities of the scattered and incident electromagnetic waves, respectively. On the basis of the obtained electromagnetic characteristic parameters, simulations were performed using a plane wave as the excitation source on a perfect electric conductor (PEC) plate (150 mm × 150 mm, thickness of 1 mm) to evaluate the stealth performance of the Co@C-CNT composite microsphere absorbing coating. The coating thickness was set to 1.5 mm in the simulation. The three-dimensional RCS patterns (Figs. 6(a)–6(d)) show that the PEC plates coated with all three Co@C-CNT samples exhibit similar radar wave scattering signal characteristics. In this model, the positive *z*-axis direction (*θ* = 90°) is defined as the electromagnetic wave incidence direction, with *θ* representing the detection angle. The color distribution and radiation lobe structure in the patterns reflect the scattering signal intensity, where a lower scattering intensity indicates superior absorption performance. The scattering signals from the PEC plates coated with Co@C-CNTs are significantly lower than those from the bare PEC plate (Figs. 6(a)–6(d)), demonstrating that all samples possess excellent electromagnetic wave absorption capabilities. Within the detection angle range of −90° to 90°, the RCS values of the Co@C-CNT microspheres remain below −20 dB·m2 across the entire angular range, among which Co@C-CNT-2 results in the greatest RCS reduction, with a maximum reduction value of 11.41 dB∙m2 at the 0° detection angle (Fig. 6(e)). At specific angles (0°, 30°, and 60°), the RCS values of Co@C-CNT-2 are consistently superior to those of Co@C-CNT-3 and Co@C-CNT-1 (Fig. 6(f)). These RCS simulation results indicate that the Co@C-CNT microspheres can effectively suppress electromagnetic wave scattering and reflection from the surface of the PEC substrate. These findings are consistent with the excellent absorption performance, suggesting that this composite material holds promising potential for application in stealth coatings.

## 4 Conclusions

This study achieved controllable spatial distribution regulation of Co NPs within carbon microspheres via a melamine-mediated strategy, constructing a composite microsphere with tunable electromagnetic wave absorption performance. The investigation revealed that the magnetic Co NPs catalyze the formation of a graphitic carbon layer, which simultaneously confines the further growth of the Co NPs. Melamine plays a dual role during pyrolysis. On the one hand, its nitrogen-containing groups coordinate with the cobalt precursor, increasing the surface enrichment of the cobalt particles. On the other hand, the carbon‒nitrogen species generated from its decomposition promote graphitization and suppress the agglomeration of Co NPs. This precise regulation ultimately resulted in the formation of a gradient magnetic distribution network. This structure optimizes impedance matching and synergistically enhances multiple loss mechanisms. The optimized Co@C‒CNT-2 composite microspheres demonstrated excellent microwave absorption performance, achieving a minimum reflection loss of −32.48 dB and an EAB of 5.2 GHz at an ultrathin thickness of 1.8 mm. This research offers a novel strategy for designing high-performance, lightweight, thin-layer electromagnetic wave absorbing materials.
