## 1 Introduction

With the increasing demand of textile and printing industries for synthetic organic dyes over the last several decades, the discharge of dye wastewater significantly increased at the same time [1−4]. If the dye wastewater without treatment is discharged, it will generate tremendous threats to both aquatic ecosystems and human health [5−7]. The current methods for treating dye wastewater include physical, chemical, and biological treatment, but they all have certain limitations [8,9]. Physical treatment methods, such as precipitation, filtration, and adsorption, are used to remove these suspended solids and these colored substances from wastewater [10]. However, these adsorbents need to be replaced regularly, and the used adsorbents must be properly managed [11]. Chemical treatment methods involve adding chemical reagents to induce chemical reactions with dye molecules, resulting in the precipitation for decolorization and dye removal [12]. However, this method requires expensive chemicals and will generate the secondary pollution [13]. Biological treatment methods involve using microorganisms to biodegrade dyes and organic pollutants, making it a more environmentally friendly option [8]. However, this process takes a long time and is influenced by factors such as temperature, pH, and other constituents present in wastewater [13]. Therefore, it is necessary to employ a new dye wastewater treatment technology which is efficient, environmentally friendly, non-toxic, and convenient.

The advanced oxidation process as an efficient and environmentally friendly process for decomposing organic dye has attracted considerable attention [14−17], such as the photocatalytic process which uses solar energy, a clean and renewable energy, to induce redox reaction has been widely reported in environmental remediation [18−20]. Photocatalytic dye decomposition represents one such method whereby photo-induced holes (h*+*) and electrons (e−) are reacted with dissolved oxygen (O2) and hydroxide ion (OH−) in aqueous solution to generate superoxide radicals (•O2−) and hydroxyl radicals (•OH), respectively [21−23]. These •O2− and •OH have the capacity to efficiently decompose these organic molecules into more benign substances [24−27]. However, the low utilization of solar energy, selectivity of light wavelength, and inability to function in dark limit the practical applications of photocatalysis [28−30].

In addition to solar energy, the thermal energy generated by the cold–hot alternation can also serve as a clean and renewable source for inducing the generation of positive and negative charges and further realizing redox reactions, which is named pyrocatalysis [31−35]. Temperature alternation can cause changes in the internal polarization intensity of these pyroelectric materials, thereby exhibiting an imbalance between the internal polarization charges and the external compensation charges [36,37]. Then, these positive and negative charges that are induced by the ferroelectric effect move towards the surface of the catalyst, respectively [38,39]. Finally, these charges can further interact with OH− and O2 to produce highly-reactive free radicals that possess oxidizing properties [39]. These radicals are capable of breaking down dye molecules through chemical reactions [40−42].

Many types of materials, such as BaTiO3 [43], NaNbO3 [44], and Ba0.7Sr0.3TiO3 [45], exhibit pyrocatalytic performance for organic dye decomposition. However, the practical application of pyrocatalysis for dye decomposition is constrained by the swift recombination of both positive and negative charges that are produced [46].

Recently, numerous strategies have emerged to enhance the performance of pyrocatalysis. These strategies, as previously documented, include element doping [47,48], constructing heterojunctions [49,50], and loading noble metals [51]. By adopting these approaches, it becomes possible to effectively separate these positive and negative charges generated in the pyrocatalytic process, prevent carrier recombination, and accelerate the migration of the carriers [50−52]. Notably, Chen *et al*. [50] have reported that the BiFeO3/10% g-C3N4 heterojunction can decompose ~94.2% Rhodamine B (RhB) dye after 18 cooling–heating cycles. Xu *et al*. [51] have researched Ba0.7Sr0.3TiO3@1.5% Ag nanoparticles, which exhibit the capacity to decompose around 99% of RhB dye through pyrocatalysis after undergoing 50 consecutives cold–hot cycles. BaTiO3, recognized as ferroelectric materials, has been extensively investigated for its pyrocatalytic performance in both dye decomposition and hydrogen production [45,53]. However, strategies, such as constructing heterojunctions, for enhancing the pyrocatalytic performance of BaTiO3 have rarely been reported.

Pr2O3, as a metal-oxide-semiconductor, has been reported the property to enhance the dielectric constant and spontaneous polarization of barium strontium sodium niobate which is ferroelectric materials [54]. Therefore, fabricating the heterojunction with Pr2O3 is a feasible way to enhance the property of pyroelectric materials.

In this work, the BaTiO3 nanofibers are synthesized via a hydrothermal approach. Pr2O3 is loaded on the surface of BaTiO3 by the impregnation method to obtain the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst. Pr2O3 loading for forming a heterojunction with BaTiO3 can obviously enhance the pyrocatalytic performance. With the increase of Pr2O3 loading content in the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction from 0 to 4 wt%, the pyrocatalytic decomposition ratio of the RhB solution after 42 cold–hot cycles reaches a maximum of 91% at 3 wt%, then decreases at 4 wt%.

### 2.1 Synthesis of BaTiO3 nanofibers and BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction

The chemical reagents utilized in this work are of analytic reagent grade. For the preparation of BaTiO3 nanofibers through hydrothermal synthesis, the following precursors are employed based on the report provided in a previous study: hydrochloric acid (HCl), titanium dioxide (TiO2), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), and barium hydroxide (Ba(OH)2). Specifically, a mixture of 1.25 g TiO2 and 60 mL of 10 M NaOH solution is prepared. The resulting blend is then transferred into a 100 mL Teﬂon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and subjected to a temperature of 180 ℃ for duration of 24 h. Afterward, the resulting powders undergo the immersion of 4 h in 0.2 M HCl solution to obtain the intermediate product hydrogen titanate (H2Ti3O7). Subsequently, H2Ti3O7 is dissolved in aqueous solution containing 0.05 M Ba(OH)2, and the resulting mixture is placed in an autoclave with a volume of 100 mL for a hydrothermal reaction conducted at a temperature of 210 ℃ for duration of 85 min. Finally, the obtained BaTiO3 product is rinsed with the deionized water and subjected to a drying process at 70 ℃ for 12 h.

To synthesize the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst, the impregnation method is employed [55]. Specifically, the synthesized BaTiO3 nanofibers are dispersed into 5 mL absolute ethanol. The mixture is then stirred for duration of 30 min. Through the manipulation of the mass of Pr2O3, a series of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst with mass fractions of 1%, 2%, 3%, and 4% are synthesized. The mixed solution is dried at 80 ℃ for 12 h. Finally, the composites are annealed at 500 ℃ for 2 h to obtain the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst with different contents of Pr2O3.

### 2.2 Material characterization

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Rigaku MiniFlex/600, Japan) is used to determine the crystal structure of the synthesized BaTiO3 catalyst and BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Phenom ProX, the Netherlands) is used to record morphology data for synthesized BaTiO3 and BaTiO3/Pr2O3. The chemical composition of the BaTiO3 nanofibers and BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst is analyzed by an energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDX; Phenom ProX, the Netherlands) and an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB 250Xi, USA), respectively. A spectrophotometer (Hitachi U-3900, Japan) is used to record the absorbance of the RhB solution, which enabled to assess the pyrocatalytic performance of the BaTiO3 and BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction.

### 2.3 Pyrocatalytic dye decomposition

The pyrocatalytic performance of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst is evaluated by determining the degree of the RhB decomposition under the cold–hot alternation under 29–57 ℃ in dark. Specifically, the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst of 50 mg is dispersed in the RhB solution of 50 mL with a concentration of 5 mg/L. The mixture is magnetically stirred for 1 h to achieve absorption–desorption equilibrium. To monitor the progress of the reaction, the mixture of approximately 3 mL is extracted and centrifuged after every 6 cycles of heating–cooling. This centrifugation step played a crucial role in removing the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst. Finally, the concentration of RhB remaining in the solution is determined using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (U-3900, Hitachi, Japan).

The schematic diagram illustrating the pyrocatalytic mechanism of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst is presented in Fig. 3. BaTiO3 nanofibers are ferroelectric materials with the spontaneous polarization property [58]. When the BaTiO3 nanofibers are maintained in a thermodynamic equilibrium with the external environment, the external compensation charges on the surface of BaTiO3 completely screen the internal polarization charges of BaTiO3 so that BaTiO3 is electrically neutral. With the temperature alternation, the thermal equilibrium is broken, and the intensity of polarization will change, leading to an imbalance between the internal polarization charges and the external compensation charges of BaTiO3. Then, the carriers generated by the pyroelectric effect move from the surface of BaTiO3 to Pr2O3 due to the internal electric field formed by the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction. Because of the difference in the band structure between the conduction band (CB) and the valence band (VB) of BaTiO3 and Pr2O3, these negative charges which are pyroelectrically-induced in CB of the BaTiO3 transfer to CB of Pr2O3, while these positive charges transfer from VB of Pr2O3 to VB of BaTiO3. The intimate contact between BaTiO3 and Pr2O3 results in a reduction in the interface energy, thereby accelerating the carrier-transfer process, which increases the separation of these positive and negative carriers. This process improves the pyroelectrically-induced catalytic performance. In RhB dye solution, q− on the surface of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction can react with the dissolved oxygen to generate •O2−. Meanwhile, q+* in the solution can produce •OH by reacting with OH−. Both •OH and •O2− with the strong oxidizing ability can decompose molecules of RhB [59−62].

The absorption spectra of RhB with BaTiO3/Pr2O3 which load different contents of Pr2O3 from 0% to 4% under the same cold–hot cycles are shown in Figs. 4(b)–4(f). Through the pyrocatalytic process of dye decomposition, the intensity of RhB solution’s characteristic absorption peak at approximately 554 nm gradually diminishes as the number of cold–hot cycles increases, indicating the decomposition of RhB molecules. After 42 cold–hot cycles, the characteristic absorption peaks of RhB of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction with different Pr2O3 contents (1, 2, 3, 4 wt%) are lower than that of pure BaTiO3, confirming that the pyrocatalytic performance of BaTiO3/Pr2O3 is improved than that of pure BaTiO3.

The decomposition ratio (D*) can be expressed by Eq. (5) [63]:

(5) D=(1−C/C0)×100%

where *C*0 and *C* represent the initial concentration and the residual concentration of the RhB solution at 0 and *t* cycles, respectively, where *t* is the number of cold–hot cycles. The RhB decomposition ratio of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction with different Pr2O3 contents of 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 wt% can reach 52%, 63%, 75%, 91%, and 84%, respectively, as exhibited in Fig. 5(a). With the increase of the Pr2O3 loading content, the decomposition ratio of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction exhibits the increasing trend and reaches the maximum at 3 wt% Pr2O3 then decreases at 4 wt%, which can be seen in Fig. 5(b). The enhancement of the pyrocatalytic performance after loading Pr2O3 is attributed to the internal electric field of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction, which effectively separates the carriers. When Pr2O3 is over 3.0 wt%, an excess of Pr2O3 may act as a recombination center to capture positive and negative carriers, which results in a slight decrease in the pyrocatalytic performance.

To further investigate the pyroelectrically-induced pyrocatalytic mechanism of BaTiO3/*Pr2O3 under cold–hot alternation, the free radicals and hole trapping experiments are employed to reveal the main active species which act on the pyrocatalytic process. As depicted in Fig. 7(a), the BaTiO3/3% Pr2O3 heterojunction pyrocatalytic RhB decomposition experiments rerepeated with the addition of different scavengers to evaluate their influence on the pyrocatalytic performance. Under cold–hot temperature alternation, the decomposition ratio of the RhB solution is ~91% in the presence of the BaTiO3/3% Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst without adding any scavengers. After adding tert-butyl alcohol (TBA) of 1 mM which demonstrates the property to trap •OH [64], the pyrocatalytic performance of the BaTiO3/3% Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst for decomposing RhB has been slightly suppressed. In contrast, the decomposition ratio of RhB is significantly inhibited after adding 1 mM of both ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA), h+ scavenger, and benzoquinone (BQ), •O2− scavenger [65,66], with decreases of 30% and 58%, respectively. These results indicate that •OH, •O2−, and h+* are the active species, which occur in the pyrocatalytic process of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst for the RhB decomposition.

Furthermore, a recycling test of the catalyst is also applied in this work, which is used to evaluate the reusability and stability of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst. The result of the recycling test for three cycles is demonstrated in Fig. 7(b), and the decomposition ratio of the RhB solution shows a only slight decrease, after 126 cooling–heating cycles, proving the high reusability of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst, which bodes well for the practical application in wastewater purification.

In general, ferroelectric materials exhibit both the pyrocatalytic and piezo-catalytic performance [67]. Besides the pyrocatalytic performance of BaTiO3 which is demonstrated in this work, the excellent piezo-catalytic performance of BaTiO3 also has been demonstrated, which can decompose RhB molecules [68]. The utilization of bi-catalysis synergy proves a promising method to improve the catalytic performance [69,70]. It is anticipated that the catalytic performance of BaTiO3/Pr2O3 can be further enhanced on the basis of the piezo/pyrocatalysis synergy through harvesting the mechanical energy and thermal energy at the same time in future.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, this work shows that the performance of the pyroelectrically-induced pyrocatalysis can be efficiently improved by the heterojunction catalyst. Under a cold–hot cycle of 29–57 ℃, 54% RhB can be decomposed for pristine BaTiO3 nanofibers. However, with the increased loading content of Pr2O3 from 0 to 4 wt%, the pyrocatalytic performance of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst exhibits an increasing trend then follows by a decreasing trend, eventually achieving a maximum at 3 wt%. The enhancement of the pyrocatalytic performance of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 catalyst can be attributed to the internal electric field generated by the heterojunction, which effectively separates the carriers. •OH, •O2−, and h*+* are the main active species in the process of pyrocatalytic dye decomposition. The strong pyrocatalytic performance of the BaTiO3/Pr2O3 heterojunction catalyst makes it hopeful for application in organic dye decomposition by harvesting the thermal energy generated by natural cold–hot alternation in the future.
