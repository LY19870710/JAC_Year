## 1 Introduction

High-entropy ceramics, such as borides, carbides, nitrides, and dual-phase boride/carbides, have attracted considerable interest due to their outstanding mechanical, tribological, and high-temperature properties, including superior hardness, wear resistance, and creep resistance [1−8]. Among these materials, high-entropy carbonitride ceramics (HECNs) stand out as especially promising candidates for high-temperature applications and other extreme environments. Two primary methods have been established for synthesizing HECN ceramics: One involves the use of commercially available transition metal carbides and nitrides as precursors, and the other utilizes pre-synthesized HECN powders. The first method typically employs high-purity precursor powders of carbides, nitrides, and graphite, which are manually mixed and subjected to high-energy ball milling. Tungsten-carbide milling balls and containers, in conjunction with media such as stearic acid, are commonly used. For example, Dippo *et al*. [9] densified the resulting powder mixtures via spark-plasma-sintering (SPS) at temperatures as high as 2200 °C. This process yielded carbonitrides that exhibited a 31% improvement in modulus and a 39% increase in hardness over rule-of-mixtures (ROM) predictions. These enhancements were attributed to the increased configurational entropy and reduced valence-electron concentration, which served as tunable parameters for mechanical property optimization. Wang *et al*. [10] extended this method by using commercially available ZrC, NbC, HfC, and TaC powders together with NbN and HfN to prepare (Zr–Nb–Hf–Ta)C*x*N1−*x* ceramics (*x* = 1.0, 0.9, 0.8, 0.7). The powders, containing 2 wt% stearic acid, were ball milled in tungsten-carbide pots under argon and then spark-plasma-sintered at 2200 °C for 10 min under a maximum pressure of 40 MPa under vacuum, yielding dense ceramics with grain sizes of 5–30 µm.

The second synthesis route involves pre-synthesized HECN powders, which offer finer particle sizes and improved sinterability. These powders, which are commonly fabricated via carbothermal reduction–nitridation, facilitate uniform elemental distribution in the final ceramics. For example, Jing *et al*. [11] produced (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2)(C0.9N0.1) powders with an average particle size of 203 nm and minimal oxygen content (0.2915 wt%). The hot-pressing of these powders at 1600–1900 °C yielded ceramics with a 99.8% relative density, a nanoindentation hardness of 32.4 GPa, and a fracture toughness of 4.87 MPa·m1/2.

Innovative synthesis techniques have also emerged. Zhou *et al*. [12] employed carbothermal reduction–nitridation with graphite or carbon black to synthesize (Ti,Nb,Ta,Mo,W)(C,N) powders. The use of carbon black produced finer powders with minimal free carbon content (0.032 wt%), which significantly enhanced the mechanical properties of the resulting ceramics. Similarly, Suvorova *et al*. [13] used a mechanically induced self-sustaining reaction under high-energy ball milling in a nitrogen atmosphere to produce single-phase (Hf,Ta,Nb,Zr)(C,N) powders. Post-SPS treatment resulted in ceramics with a nanoindentation hardness of 30.1 GPa and a fracture toughness of 4.2 MPa·m1/2. On the other hand, Xia *et al*. [14] combined sol–gel techniques with carbothermal reduction–nitridation to synthesize (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C*x*N1−*x* powders, achieving high compositional uniformity and controllable particle size.

The tribological properties of high-entropy ceramics have also undergone notable advancements. Sun *et al*. [15] evaluated single-phase (Hf0.2Mo0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2Ti0.2)C ceramics via a rotational tribometer with Al2O3 balls, which demonstrated that excellent wear resistance and mechanical stability in the range of 25–600 °C. Similarly, Chen *et al*. [16] examined (VNbTaMoW)C5 carbides and observed consistent wear resistance with reduced friction as the sintering temperature increased. Medved' *et al*. [17] investigated (Mo–Nb–Ta–V–W)C carbides and identified key wear mechanisms such as grain pull-out and fracture under various loads. Finally, Naughton-Duszová *et al*. [18] analyzed dual-phase high-entropy boride/carbide ceramics, revealing oxidation-driven tribochemical reactions at low loads and mechanical wear dominance at high loads.

Compared with high-entropy carbides, borides, or nitrides, high-entropy carbonitrides possess a nonmetallic sublattice containing both C and N. Multiple independent studies have confirmed that this dual-anion configuration magnifies the high-entropy effect, suppresses elemental diffusion during sliding, and improves physicochemical stability, resulting in markedly enhanced wear resistance [19].

Systematic work by Han *et al*. [20] demonstrated that the C/N atomic ratio strongly influenced the tribological behavior: (Ti0.25V0.25Nb0.25Ta0.25)(C1−*x*N*x*) ceramics produced by SPS at 2100 °C (10 min) formed a homogeneous face-centered cubic (fcc) solid solution, and their wear resistance peaked at a C/N ratio of 0.8/0.2.

High-entropy carbonitrides readily bond to diverse substrates via magnetron sputtering, laser cladding, or thermal spraying, yielding dense, adherent coatings on Si, SiO2, WC–Co, Ti–Al, and W–Co alloys [21]. Yi *et al*. [22] deposited (TiZrNbMoTa)C*x*N1−*x* coatings on YG8 cemented-carbide via reactive radio-frequency magnetron sputtering. The dual-anion lattice distortion refined the grain size, increasing the hardness, while a C-rich variant (HEC0.5N0.5) achieved the lowest measured coefficient of friction (COF = 0.391) and wear rate (8.8×10−8 mm3/(N·m) because of the amorphous-carbon tribofilms.

The development of high-entropy ceramics represents a systematic approach to multicomponent material design, extending beyond traditional binary or ternary systems [2−6]. Various synthesis strategies have been explored for high-entropy carbonitrides, including direct powder processing and coating deposition techniques, each offering unique advantages for tailoring microstructure and performance [23−26,9,11]. These approaches leverage configurational entropy effects and lattice distortion to achieve a property enhancement in ceramic systems. Building upon the dual-anion advantages outlined above, bulk high-entropy carbonitrides offer potential for superior tribological performance through controlled microstructural design.

Research in the field of bulk high-entropy carbonitrides has focused on optimizing the processing techniques to enhance the mechanical and thermal properties of these ceramics. However, despite notable advances, comprehensive studies on the tribological performance of HECN ceramics, particularly in fine-grained systems, are lacking.

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the tribological performance of a newly developed, fine-grained high-entropy carbonitride ceramic (W0.2Mo0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)(C0.75N0.25). Using linear reciprocating tribological tests and detailed fractographic characterization, we determine the coefficient of friction, effective wear rate, dominant wear mechanism, and overall wear behavior, thereby clarifying the influence of the dual-anion sublattice and multimetal synergy on sliding performance.

The mixed/milled powder was compacted into tablets and heated in a flow of high-purity N2 (101.325 kPa) to undergo the carbothermal reduction–nitridation reaction at 1700 °C for 1 h in a graphite crucible (LHS-2, Zhongshan Kaixuan Vacuum Science & Technology Co. Ltd., China). The reacted bulk was ground and sieved to obtain target powder (W0.2Mo0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)(C0.75Ns0.25).

The bulk composite was prepared in a spark-plasma-sintering furnace (HPD-10-FL, FCT Systems, Germany). The powder was loaded into a 20 mm graphite die and sintered at 2000 °C; the heating rate was 100 °C/min, the dwell time at 2000 °C was 10 min, and the cooling rate was ~100 °C/min, under a uniaxial pressure of 30 MPa in flowing argon. More details concerning the processing route of the powder can be found in Ref. [12].

The ceramic samples were polished in multiple stages via progressively finer abrasives. They were ground with 15 μm particles and polished with 9, 6, 3, and finally 1 μm diamond suspensions. The density of the sintered ceramics was measured via the Archimedes drainage method in distilled water at room temperature. The phase compositions of the samples were analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; MiniFlex, Rigaku, Japan). The structure and chemistry of the tribolayers formed on worn surfaces were examined by a Raman spectrometer (XploRA, Horiba Jobin Yvon, France) with a 532 nm laser operated at low power to avoid local heating. Microstructural analysis of the composite was carried out using a scanning electron microscope equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM+EDS; Auriga, Zeiss, USA). To obtain a more detailed picture of the tribochemical reactions and the oxidation states of the surface species, the study was complemented by micro-Raman spectroscopy, which can unambiguously distinguish amorphous carbon (a-C) from crystalline metal oxides and carbide phases. In addition, detailed fractography was conducted to study the wear characteristics of the HECN material system. A focused ion beam (FIB) was also used to create cross-sections of the wear tracks, providing insights into subsurface deformation and material removal mechanisms.

The Vickers hardness test (HXD-2000TM/LCD, Shanghai Guangmi Instrument, China) was conducted with an applied load of 9.8 N and a dwell time of 10 s. Fracture toughness was measured via the indentation method with a testing load of 98 N and dwell time of 10 s. Tribological testing was performed on a Bruker UMT reciprocating ball-on-disc tribometer to assess the wear resistance of the HECN ceramic samples. A highly polished SiC ball (*R*a 17,18,27]. These tests were carried out at room temperature with 45%±5% relative humidity under normal loads of 5, 10, and 25 N. The stroke length was 5 mm, the sliding speed was 0.1 m/s, and the total sliding distance was 500 m; each loading condition was repeated three times to ensure reproducibility. Loads of 5, 10, and 25 N were deliberately chosen to span three characteristic contact regimes—mild (~1.9 GPa), transitional (~2.4 GPa), and severe/oxidation-dominated (~3.3 GPa)—thereby enabling a systematic investigation of the progressive evolution of wear mechanisms. Material losses, expressed as the volume of the wear tracks, were measured with a PLu neox confocal optical profilometer (PLu neox SENSOFAR, Spain). The specific wear rates (*W*S) were calculated based on the volume loss (*V*) per sliding distance (*L*) and applied load (*F*N) via Eq. (2):

(2) WS=V/(FN×L)(mm3/(N⋅m))

### 3.1 Powder and sintered material characterization

The XRD pattern of the prepared powders is shown in Fig. 1(a), indicating that the powder is composed of a single cubic phase, corresponding to the (W0.2Mo0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)(C0.75N0.25) phase. The XRD pattern shows characteristic peaks at (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222), corresponding to a cubic (face-centered cubic, “rock-salt”) structure. In the case of (W0.2Mo0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)(C0.75N0.25) powders, this indicates the homogeneous dissolution of all the components into a single-crystal structure without forming secondary phases. The most intense peak is at the (111) plane, which is typical for cubic carbides and nitrides. These results confirm the successful synthesis of a high-entropy carbonitride phase with a uniform structure.

Despite overall homogeneity, detailed SEM analysis revealed the presence of minor chemical inhomogeneities. As shown in Fig. 2, the HECN matrix contains isolated larger grains with brighter contrast. Point EDAX analyses (Fig. 2) confirm that these grains are significantly enriched in W and Mo at the expense of Ta, Ti, and Nb, indicating the formation of a minor fraction of W- and Mo-rich segregated phases. This may be related to the more stable phase structure formed by the interaction of Mo and W with Ti after sintering [28−30]. Although this initial chemical heterogeneity was volumetrically small, it was found to be a critical factor affecting local wear behavior.

The material exhibited HV*1 of 23.45±1.62 GPa and a fracture toughness, as determined by indentation, of 2.73±0.11 MPa·m1/2.

### 3.2 Tribological performance

The friction behavior of the HECN ceramic was evaluated at applied loads of 5, 10, and 25 N. The evolution of the COF as a function of sliding distance is shown in Fig. 4. The COF curves exhibit periodic oscillations typical for reciprocating tribological tests. For loads of 5 and 10 N, the COFs stabilized and oscillated around average values of 0.28 and 0.33, respectively. During the test at 25 N, the initial COF was ~0.3, but gradually increased to a higher steady-state value of ~0.38 after 200–300 m of sliding, indicating a progressive change in the nature of the sliding interface.

Fig. 4Coefficient of friction for the investigated HECN system at 5, 10, and 25 N applied loads.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, tests at 5 and 10 N produced relatively shallow wear tracks (depths of 0.25 and 0.45 μm, widths of 600 and 800 μm, respectively) with very similar and low roughness profiles. However, at 25 N, the wear profile changed significantly, with an average width of 900 μm, an average depth of ~0.8 μm, and depth fluctuations between 0.2 and 1.5 μm in the middle of the track. The characteristic wear profiles are consistent with the COF data. The wear tracks after the 5 and 10 N tests are relatively shallow and smooth. In contrast, the track after the 25 N test is significantly wider and deeper and has a much rougher surface, indicating a more severe wear regime and the formation of a substantial, irregular tribolayer.

The specific wear rates of the investigated HECN system, which were evaluated on the basis of the volume of wear tracks measured after tests at three different loads (5, 10, and 25 N), are presented in Fig. 6. At the applied load of 5 N, the specific wear rate is the lowest, with an extremely low value of 3.23×10−9 mm3/(N·m). At 10 and 25 N, the specific wear rates were calculated as 3.73×10−8 and 5.56×10−8 mm3/(N·m), respectively. These values, especially at 5 N, are remarkably low, highlighting the excellent wear resistance of the material. While the observed trend of increasing wear rate with load aligns with general tribological knowledge for ceramics, the absolute values of the specific wear rates for our HECN system are significantly lower—by one or two orders of magnitude than those reported for many other high-entropy ceramics in the literatures (see examples in Table 3 and Fig. 14, e.g., Refs. [17,27]). This underlines the outstanding wear performance of the investigated carbonitride.

#### 3.3.1 5 N (mild wear regime)

The wear track (Fig. 8) is smooth, with minimal mechanical damage. The EDAX analyses show that large portions of the track retain a chemical composition nearly identical to that of the bulk HECN matrix (compared with Table 2, area ID 5 N-Matrix, in Fig. 2). At isolated points (area ID 5 N-oxide), initial tribo-oxidation (~5.0 at% O) is observed. The key transformation is, however, the structure. Raman spectra (Fig. 9) reveal the immediate formation of broad D and G bands, characteristic of a protective, self-lubricating a-C layer. This layer is responsible for the stable, low COF and exceptionally low wear rate.

#### 3.3.2 10 N (transition regime)

At this intermediate load, the wear track (Fig. 10) shows increased mechanical damage. Initial (W, Mo)-rich inhomogeneities are now exposed and actively involved in the wear process. This is confirmed by EDAX analysis (area ID 10 N-W/Mo in Table 2), which identifies areas within the track with high W (15.1 at%) and Mo (11.5 at%) contents. The Raman spectra from these regions (Fig. 9) display the emergence of sharper peaks corresponding to crystalline metal oxides coexisting with a-C bands. The tribolayer thus evolves into a heterogeneous composite consisting of a lubricating carbon matrix with embedded hard oxide particles, leading to the increased wear. Because oxide formation is still restricted to discrete islands rather than a continuous “glaze”, the average oxygen content at 10 N remains low, whereas at 25 N, the development of a thick, fully oxidized “glaze” layer drives the oxygen level above 60 at%.

#### 3.3.3 25 N (oxidation-dominated regime)

At the highest load, the surface is completely transformed (Fig. 11) and is governed by a dynamic process of formation and destruction of a thick and brittle tribolayer. Large areas of the track are covered by a thick, cracked, and highly oxidized layer. The chemical composition of this layer (area ID 25 N-Glaze in Table 2) is radically different, containing 61.1 at% oxygen and 9.4 at% silicon transferred from the counterbody of the SiC ball. The Raman spectra from these regions (Fig. 9) are dominated by strong oxide peaks. The continuous cracking of this hard, abrasive layer is the primary source of material loss, explaining both the increase in wear rate and COF. Simultaneously, in isolated carbon-rich “pockets” (area ID 25 N-C), the Raman spectra reveal extremely sharp D and G bands, indicating the formation of well-ordered nanocrystalline graphite.

### 3.4 Discussion and comparative analysis

The achieved tribological results position the investigated HECN material among the most promising high-entropy ceramics. For a proper appreciation of these findings, a detailed comparison with the literature is essential. Table 3 and Fig. 14 summarize the key wear characteristics from recent studies. A direct comparison is most meaningful for systems 1–4, which have been tested under very similar conditions.

As shown in Fig. 14, the specific wear rate of our HECN system (system 1) is one to two orders of magnitude lower than those of other high-performance bulk high-entropy carbides [15,17] and boride-carbide composites [18]. For example, at a load of 5 N, the wear rate of our material (3.23×10−9 mm3/(N·m)) is substantially lower than that of (MoNbTaVW)C (3.71×10−8 mm3/(N·m)) [17] and (HfTaZrNbTi)C (2.44×10−8 mm3/(N·m) ) [27].

This outstanding performance can be attributed to several factors. First, it is due to the effective formation of a self-lubricating amorphous carbon layer at low loads. Although carbon-based lubrication has also been reported for HECN coatings [22,31], our bulk material achieves exceptionally low wear rates, indicating a more stable and robust tribolayer. Second, the high density and fine-grained microstructure of our bulk material provide better mechanical integrity than many coating systems, which often exhibit higher wear rates (on the order of 10−7 to 10−6 mm3/(N·m) or more; see Table 3).

The observed evolution of the wear mechanism also provides a more detailed picture than that previously reported for some carbides [11], where a compact tribolayer was not formed. Our HECN material clearly demonstrates the ability to adapt its surface chemistry to tribological conditions. The paradox that the oxide layer at 25 N is associated with increased wear, instead of being protective, is also a significant finding. In contrast to high-temperature tests where lubricating oxides may form, our results testing at room temperature show that the formed “glaze” is hard and brittle, and its continuous fracture becomes the dominant mechanism contributing to wear.

In summary, the HECN ceramic (W0.2Mo0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)(C0.75N0.25) exhibits a combination of low friction and excellent wear resistance. Its performance is rooted in its ability to form a dynamic, adaptive tribolayer that evolves from a self-lubricating amorphous carbon film at low loads to a complex, oxide-dominated layer at higher loads. These findings demonstrate the tremendous potential of this material and contribute to a deeper fundamental understanding of the wear mechanisms of high-entropy carbonitrides.

## 4 Conclusions

The aim of this study was to investigate the wear characteristics of a newly developed (W0.2Mo0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)(C0.75N0.25) high-entropy carbonitride composite under dry sliding conditions in ambient air at room temperature, with applied loads of 5, 10, and 25 N. The main findings are as follows:

(1) Dense (relative density of 97.52%) and fine-grained (average grain size of 2.83 μm) HECN ceramics were successfully synthesized. The HECN system exhibited a predominantly homogeneous microstructure with nano-pores and nano-cracks at the grain boundaries. The sample exhibited good overall chemical homogeneity, with W, Mo, Ta, Nb, and Ti each uniformly present at approximately 7 at%, although minor (W,Mo)-rich phases revealed slight inhomogeneities.

(2) The HECN composite exhibited superior mechanical properties, including a *HV*1 of 23.45 GPa and an indentation fracture toughness of 2.73 MPa·m1/2.

(3) The COFs during the tests at 5 and 10 N applied loads were highly stable, with average values of 0.28 and 0.33, respectively. At 25 N load, the COF increased from an initial value of ~0.3 to a steady-state value of ~0.38, indicating a change in the surface state.

(4) The material exhibited exceptional wear resistance. The specific wear rate at 5 N was extremely low, measuring 3.23×10−9 mm3/(N·m). At 10 and 25 N, the wear rates were higher, with 3.73×10−8 and 5.56×10−8 mm3/(N·m), respectively. These values remain substantially lower than those reported for many other high-performance high-entropy ceramics under comparable conditions.

(5) The dominant wear mechanisms evolve with the increasing load. At 5 N, wear was governed by the formation of a protective, self-lubricating amorphous carbon layer directly on the surface, resulting in minimal material loss. At 10 N, a transitional mechanism involving both an amorphous carbon layer and localized oxidation was observed. At 25 N, wear was governed by the formation and continuous fracture of a hard, abrasive, oxide-rich “glaze”, which explained the greater friction and wear.

(6) These findings highlight that the investigated (W0.2Mo0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)(C0.75N0.25) HECN offers a top combination of tribological properties. Its ability to form adaptive surface layers makes it a highly promising candidate for demanding applications that require superior wear resistance.
