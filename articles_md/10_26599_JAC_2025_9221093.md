## 1 Introduction

Alumina (Al2O3) ceramics are widely recognized for their low cost and exceptional properties, including high melting point, excellent thermal resistance, biocompatibility, chemical stability, and abrasion resistance. These attributes have led to their extensive application in aerospace, defense, machinery, petrochemical, and biomedical industries [1−7]. However, the intrinsically low fracture toughness of Al2O3 ceramics severely limits their broader application by compromising their overall mechanical performance. Accordingly, numerous studies have reported that the incorporation of secondary phases such as Al2O3 [8], TiB2 [9], Si3N4 [10], SiC [11], ZrO2 [12], and TiC [13] in various shapes and sizes, including particles, whiskers, fibers, tubes, and platelets, is beneficial for increasing the fraction toughness [13−19]. Among these materials, SiC whiskers (SiCw) have emerged as one of the most effective toughening agents due to their unique properties, including low defect density, high elastic modulus, excellent tensile strength, and superior thermal stability [20−23]. For instance, Perevislov [20] demonstrated that Al2O3–SiCw composites fabricated via liquid-phase sintering and hot pressing (HP) exhibited fracture toughness values ranging from 4.74 to 5.94 MPa·m1/2, depending on the SiCw content. Similarly, Liu *et al*. [22] reported that the fracture toughness of Al2O3–SiCw composites prepared by spark plasma sintering (SPS) increased with the SiCw content, reaching 5.20 and 6.90 MPa·m1/2 at 10 and 30 wt% SiCw, respectively. Despite these advances, the toughening effect of SiCw in Al2O3 ceramics has plateaued in recent years, with fracture toughness values consistently ranging between 3.10 and 6.90 MPa·m1/2 [22,24,25]. This stagnation can be attributed to a predominant focus on optimizing SiCw incorporation methods and sintering techniques instead of innovating the toughening microstructure itself. Consequently, the toughening mechanisms in existing Al2O3–SiCw composites remain limited to the dispersion of single-phase SiCw within the Al2O3 matrix, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a).

In recent years, researchers have explored the simultaneous incorporation of SiCw and various particles into the Al2O3 matrix to further enhance the fracture toughness of Al2O3 ceramics [25−28]. Zhao et al*. [27] fabricated Al2O3–SiCw–TiC composite ceramics via hot pressing and investigated the influence of TiC content and size on their mechanical properties. Their results revealed that the content and particle size of TiC significantly affected the flexural strength, but they had a negligible effect on the fracture toughness of Al2O3–SiCw composites. The optimal fracture toughness achieved for the Al2O3–SiCw–TiC composites was 6.04±0.32 MPa·m1/2, which meant that only a marginal improvement of approximately 5.9% was achieved over that of conventional Al2O3–SiCw composites. Similarly, Liu *et al*. [28] examined the synergistic toughening effects of SiC whiskers and nanoparticles in Al2O3 composite ceramics. They reported fracture toughness values of 5.0 and 5.6 MPa·m1/2 for Al2O3–SiCw and Al2O3–SiCw–SiC composites, respectively. Although the addition of SiC nanoparticles slightly enhanced the fracture toughness of the Al2O3–SiCw composites, the overall toughening effect remained limited. As illustrated in Fig. 1(b), the toughening structure in these composites typically consists of segregated particles and whiskers dispersed within the Al2O3 matrix. Generally, the toughening efficiency of particles is inferior to that of whiskers, which explains why the simultaneous addition of SiCw and particles has not led to significant improvements in fracture toughness. Consequently, there is a pressing need to develop novel toughening structures to overcome the current limitations in enhancing the fracture toughness of Al2O3 composite ceramics.

Our recent studies [29−31] demonstrated that core–shell structural composite particles exhibited superior toughening effects when compared with single-phase particles, which is ascribed to interfacial heterogeneities of core–shell structures that induce complex crack propagation within the ceramics matrix. Accordingly, an innovative cross-dimensional transformation strategy is proposed in this study by considering a significant shift from zero-dimensional (0D) particles to one-dimensional (1D) whiskers with core–shell structures as toughening phases used in composite ceramics. As schematically shown in Fig. 1(c), the construction of core–shell structural SiC composite whiskers as a toughening phase represents a promising direction for breaking through the current toughening bottleneck in Al2O3–SiCw composite ceramics.

In this work, we introduce a novel concept of utilizing core–shell-structured SiC composite whiskers as toughening phases in Al2O3 composite ceramics. This concept is realized through the fabrication of core–shell SiCw@TiC composite whiskers via a molten salt method, followed by their incorporation into the Al2O3 matrix using SPS. Figure 2 provides a schematic illustration of the preparation process for the target composite ceramics. In this process, the molten salt method is an effective and feasible approach for preparing core–shell structural composite powders. It allows for precise control over the structures of the final materials, with typical advantages such as uniform morphological structures and good dispersibility [32,33]. Currently, the molten salt method has been successfully applied to prepare inorganic ceramic composite powders with core–shell structures, such as TiC-coated C composite powder [34], (TiB2–TiC)-coated B4C composite powder [35], and SiO2-coated SiC composite powder [36]. We systematically investigated the characteristics and synthesis mechanisms of the SiCw@TiC composite whiskers, as well as their microstructure, composition, formation mechanisms, and fracture modes within the Al2O3 matrix after sintering. Additionally, the mechanical properties and toughening mechanisms of Al2O3–SiCw@TiC composite ceramics were thoroughly analyzed.

### 2.1 Materials

Commercially available α-Al2O3 powders with an average particle size of 0.3 μm (purity ≥ 99.99%, Sumitomo Chemical Co., Ltd., Japan), β-SiCw with diameters ranging from 0.1 to 0.6 μm and lengths ranging from 10 to 50 μm (purity ≥ 99%, Qinhuangdao Yinuo High-tech Material Development Co., Ltd., China), and Ti powders with an average particle size of 60 nm (purity ≥ 99.8%, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) were used as raw materials. Additionally, NaCl and KCl powders (AR-grade purity, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) were employed as the molten salt medium. The morphological and structural characteristics of the raw materials are illustrated in Fig. 3.

### 2.2 Preparation of SiC<sub>w</sub>@TiC core–shell structural whiskers

SiCw and Ti powders were mixed at a molar ratio of 1 : 1, while NaCl and KCl powders were mixed at a molar ratio of 1 : 1. These mixtures were combined at a mass ratio of (SiCw+Ti): (NaCl+KCl) = 1 : 6 and further dispersed in ethanol via magnetic stirring for 24 h. The resulting slurry was dried using rotary evaporation and a vacuum oven to obtain a homogeneous powder mixture. The mixture was then transferred into corundum crucibles with lids and calcined in a tube furnace at 1300 °C for 2 h under a flowing argon atmosphere at a heating rate of 3 °C/min. After calcination, the product was sequentially washed with water, hydrofluoric acid, and water (three cycles each) to remove impurities. Finally, the SiCw@TiC core–shell structural whiskers were obtained by drying the purified products in a vacuum oven for 12 h.

### 2.3 Preparation of Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>–SiC<sub>w</sub>@TiC composite ceramics

The as-synthesized SiCw@TiC core–shell whiskers were mixed with commercially available Al2O3 powders at a mass ratio of 1 : 9 in ethanol using magnetic stirring for 24 h. The mixture was dried via rotary evaporation in a vacuum oven for 12 h, followed by sieving through a 200-mesh sieve. The sieved powder was then loaded into a cylindrical graphite die (inner diameter: 20 mm) and sintered in a SPS furnace (HPD 60/0, FCT Systeme GmbH, Germany). The sintering process involved rapid heating to 600 °C within 2 min, followed by further heating to 1500 °C at a rate of 100 °C/min and holding at this temperature for 5 min. A uniaxial pressure of 60 MPa was applied starting at 800 °C and then held constant until the completion of sintering. After sintering, the temperature and pressure were allowed to decrease naturally by turning off the power.

To evaluate the toughening effect of SiCw@TiC core–shell whiskers on Al2O3 ceramics, two control samples, monolithic Al2O3 ceramic, and Al2O3 ceramics toughened by 10 wt% single SiCw, were prepared using identical raw materials and sintering processes.

### 2.4 Characterization

The phase composition of the samples was analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Bruker D8 Advance, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation. Microstructural and elemental distribution analyses were conducted using a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM; JEM-2100F, JEOL, Japan) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS) and a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Gemini300, Zeiss, Germany) with EDS. The residual amounts of K+ and Na+ on the SiCw@TiC composite whiskers were tested by an inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP–MS; ISQ 7610, Thermo Scientific, USA). The density of the SiCw@TiC composite whiskers was measured using the pycnometer method. The actual density of the Al2O3-SiCw@TiC composite was determined by the Archimedes method. The theoretical density of the composite ceramic was calculated using Eq. (1):

(1) ρ=mtotalmAl2O3ρAl2O3+mSiCw@TiCρSiCw@TiC

where *ρ* represents the theoretical density of the composite ceramics; *m*total is the total mass of the composite ceramics; * mAl2O3 and  mSiCw@TiC are the masses of Al2O3 and SiCw@TiC whiskers in the composite ceramics, respectively;  ρAl2O3 and  ρSiCw@TiC are the theoretical density of Al2O3 (4.00 g/cm3) and the measured density of SiCw@TiC whiskers (3.84 g/cm3), respectively. The relative density of the Al2O3–SiCw@TiC composite ceramics was calculated as the ratio of the actual density to the theoretical density. The average value and standard deviation of the relative density were derived from measurements of five samples. The diameters of the whiskers were measured using the linear intercept method on SEM images with the assistance of Nano Measurer software. The statistical data of the whisker diameters are based on 200 individual measurements. Three-point flexural strength was tested by a universal material testing machine (WDW-100, Guanteng, China) using 2 mm × 3 mm × 18 mm specimen bars with a span of 12 mm and a loading rate of 0.5 mm/min. The Vickers hardness and fracture toughness were determined using a hardness tester (HVS-10Z, Yanrun, China) with a load of 98 N, applied for 15 s on the polished surface. The fracture toughness (K*IC) was calculated using Eq. (2) [37]:

(2) KIC=0.203⋅HV⋅a0.5⋅(c/a)−1.5

where HV is the Vickers hardness (MPa); *P* is the indentation load (N); *a* is half of the indentation diagonal length (mm); *K*IC is the fracture toughness (MPa·m1/2); *c* is half of the crack length (mm). The reported values for fracture toughness and Vickers hardness represent averages from 10 and 5 measurements, respectively. COMSOL multiphysics software (COMSOL Co., Ltd., Sweden) was adopted to perform calculations and simulations of the magnitude and distribution of the residual stress in the Al2O3–SiCw@TiC composite ceramics based on the thermal field coupling module.

### 3.1 Characteristics of SiC<sub>w</sub>@TiC core–shell structural whiskers

Figure 4 presents the microstructural and compositional characteristics of the SiCw@TiC core–shell whiskers. The XRD pattern in Fig. 4(a) confirms the presence of characteristic peaks corresponding to SiCw and TiC, indicating that the composite whiskers consist solely of these two phases. In reality, when SiC reacts with Ti, in addition to the formation of the desired TiC product, a series of by-products, including Si, TiSi2, Ti5Si3, and Ti*x*Si*y*C*z* (*x*, *y*, *z* > 0), are inevitably produced [38−40]. Fortunately, these undesired by-products are soluble in hydrofluoric acid. Through hydrofluoric acid pickling treatment, the by-products can be selectively dissolved and removed, while the target product TiC is preserved and remains stably anchored on the surface of SiCw, thereby achieving the purification of the SiCw@TiC core–shell whiskers. Only 0 ppm of K+ and 0.21 ppm of Na+ were detected on the finally obtained SiCw@TiC whiskers. The TEM image in Fig. 4(b) reveals a well-defined core–shell structure with a TiC shell thickness of approximately 85 nm. The corresponding EDS elemental mapping images (Figs. 4(c)–4(f)) demonstrate that the dark core corresponds to SiCw, while the lighter shell is composed of TiC, confirming the successful formation of the core–shell structure. SEM images (Figs. 4(g) and 4(h)) further illustrate the surface morphology of the composite whiskers. Unlike the smooth surface of pure SiCw (Fig. 3(b)), the SiCw@TiC whiskers exhibit a rough surface composed of irregular TiC nanoparticles (10–50 nm in size) with numerous voids. These voids are attributed to the volatilization of Si during the reaction and the removal of by-products during the pickling process [40,41]. Statistical analysis of the whisker diameters (Fig. 4(i)) reveals a median TiC shell thickness of ~86 nm, which is consistent with the TEM observations. These results collectively confirm the successful synthesis of SiCw@TiC core–shell whiskers with a TiC nanoparticle shell of ~85 nm thickness.

### 3.2 Microstructures and mechanical properties of Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>–SiC<sub>w</sub>@TiC composite ceramics

Figure 5 depicts the phase compositions and microstructures of Al2O3–SiCw@TiC composite ceramics. The XRD pattern in Fig. 5(a) shows only peaks corresponding to Al2O3, SiC, and TiC, confirming that the initial compositions of the SiCw@TiC composite whiskers remained within the Al2O3 matrix after sintering. The backscattered electron (BSE) image in Fig. 5(b) reveals a high relative density, with minimal porosity observed on the polished surface. The silver-white regions correspond to TiC; the gray regions encircled by the silver-white regions represent SiC; the gray regions outside the silver-white regions indicate Al2O3. The SiCw@TiC core–shell structural whiskers are uniformly distributed throughout the matrix without significant aggregation. An enlarged view of a typical whisker (Fig. 5(c)) and the corresponding EDS element mapping images (Figs. 5(d)–5(i)) demonstrate that the SiCw core is fully encapsulated by the TiC shell, which is consistent with the pre-sintering structure (Fig. 4). The strong interfacial compatibility between SiCw–TiC and TiC–Al2O3 is evident, which is critical for achieving enhanced mechanical properties. Additionally, isolated TiC particles are observed within the matrix, likely resulting from the partial disruption of the core–shell structure during powder processing (e.g., washing, sieving, and mixing). The detached TiC is independently dispersed in the starting sintering powder, inevitably leading to the formation of independently isolated TiC inside the ceramic matrix after sintering. These findings explain why the core–shell structure of the SiCw@TiC composite whisker is maintained inside the Al2O3 matrix after sintering; thus, the target product of Al2O3 composite ceramics toughened by the SiCw@TiC composite whisker was successfully prepared.

Figure 6 presents the fracture surface morphology and elemental analysis of the composite whiskers. The BSE image in Fig. 6(a) and the corresponding EDS element mapping images in Figs. 6(b)–6(e) reveal that the core with a micron-sized length is SiCw and the isolated layer consists of nano-sized TiC grains. The TiC grain size and shell thickness remain consistent with the pre-sintering values, indicating minimal grain growth during sintering due to the high grain growth temperature of TiC (~1700 °C [42]) compared with the sintering temperature (1500 °C). This observation aligns with the results in Fig. 5, further confirming the stability of the core–shell structure. Figure 6(f) shows a rugged surface with sags and crests. The Al2O3 grains and SiCw are micron-sized, while the TiC grains are nanosized. The red rectangular area is enlarged further in Fig. 6(g) to carefully observe the fracture patterns around the core–shell whisker. The fracture surface exhibits a complex morphology with multi-dimensional fracture modes around the composite whiskers. Intergranular fracture occurs at the interfaces between Al2O3 grains and the TiC shell (marked by blue arrows) and between SiCw and the TiC shell (marked by red arrows). The shell of TiC itself also fractures along the grain boundaries of the TiC nanograins, and the generated cross section links the interfaces of both sides of the shell (marked by green arrows). Whisker’ pull-out phenomena, including the extraction of SiCw from the TiC shell (marked by the black circle) and the complete pull-out of SiCw@TiC whiskers from the matrix (marked by the black ellipse), are also evident in Fig. 6(f). Additionally, transgranular fracture within the SiCw cores (marked by wathet blue arrows) and whisker pull-out phenomena (marked by yellow arrows) are clearly presented in Figs. 6(h)–6(k). These diverse fracture mechanisms contribute to the enhanced fracture toughness of the composite ceramics.

### 3.3 Toughening mechanism of Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>–SiC<sub>w</sub>@TiC composite ceramics

Figure 7 presents BSE images of the polished surface with cracks, illustrating the crack propagation behavior in the Al2O3–SiCw@TiC composite ceramics. The crack propagation mechanisms can be analyzed at two levels. First, when the SiCw@TiC composite whisker is considered a single entity, crack deflection, whisker bridging, and whisker fracture occur, similar to the mechanisms observed in Al2O3 ceramics reinforced with single-phase SiCw [43]. However, when the SiCw@TiC whisker is considered a composite structure, an additional crack propagation path emerges within the whisker itself, as shown in Figs. 7(c) and 7(d). When a crack reaches the external interface of the TiC shell layer, it penetrates the shell composed of nano-sized TiC grains and propagates to the internal interface between the TiC shell and the SiCw core. Subsequently, the crack propagates along the SiCw-TiC interface (marked by the blue arrow). Notably, a secondary crack is induced in the opposite direction along the SiCw–TiC interface (marked by the red arrow). The primary crack then traverses SiCw in a transgranular manner before deflecting again along the SiCw–TiC interface (marked by the green arrow). Finally, the crack exits the TiC shell and re-enters the Al2O3 matrix. The core–shell structure of SiCw@TiC significantly extends the crack propagation path and induces reverse cracking along the SiCw–TiC interface. Additionally, the crack is forced to traverse the TiC shell twice, further dissipating crack propagation energy. This multi-level crack propagation behavior is attributed to the complex stress environment surrounding the SiCw@TiC whiskers, which arises from the unique geometric configuration and multi-dimensional heterogeneities of the composite whiskers. The geometric configuration of the toughening phase, characterized by a closed TiC shell composed of stacked nanograins fully encapsulating the SiC whisker, plays a critical role in stress distribution. Factors such as the curvature of the TiC shell, the stacking density of TiC nanograins, and the orientation of the whiskers influence the local stress field. Furthermore, the multi-dimensional heterogeneities, including differences in phase composition (Al2O3/SiC/TiC), grain size (micron/nano), and thermal expansion coefficient (3.8×10−6/K for SiC [46], 7.2×10−6/K for Al2O3 [47], and 7.4×10−6/K for TiC [48]), significantly affect the stress distribution and contribute to the enhanced toughening effect. In summary, while the toughening mechanisms of single-phase SiCw remain active in the Al2O3–SiCw@TiC system, the core–shell structure introduces a unique energy dissipation mechanism. This dual contribution results in the outstanding fracture toughness observed in the Al2O3–SiCw@TiC composite ceramics.

Given the substantial difference in the thermal expansion coefficients between TiC and SiC, the stress at the interface between the TiC shell and SiCw reaches a remarkable value of 2199.7 MPa. This high stress prompts cracks to propagate along the interface between the TiC shell and SiCw. Simultaneously, under the influence of external stress, a stress as high as 2114.0 MPa is generated within the SiCw whisker, causing the crack to traverse SiCw and resulting in intergranular fracture. Owing to the symmetry of the stress distribution, the crack subsequently continues to propagate along the interface between the TiC shell and SiCw. Finally, it traverses the TiC layer and enters the Al2O3 matrix, which aligns precisely with the crack propagation pattern depicted in Fig. 7. Moreover, influenced by the geometric shape of the whisker, at both ends of the composite whisker, the peak stress of 2259.9 MPa emerges at the interface between the TiC shell and SiCw. This phenomenon elucidates why whisker pull-out predominantly occurs at the ends of the composite whisker (Fig. 6(h)). Since the composite whiskers are oriented differently within the Al2O3 matrix, the directions of the tensile and compressive stresses they experience vary. As a result, fractures emerge partially along the grain boundaries between TiC and SiCw and partially along the interface between Al2O3 and TiC (Fig. 6(g)). The excellent agreement between the results of stress distribution simulation and the fracture mode in the micro-region of the composite whiskers provides compelling evidence for the rationality and reliability of the aforementioned explanation of the toughening mechanism of the SiCw@TiC core–shell whiskers.

## 4 Conclusions

Al2O3 composite ceramics toughened with SiCw@TiC core–shell structural whiskers were successfully fabricated using a combination of molten salt synthesis and SPS. The SiCw@TiC whiskers consist of a SiCw core and a TiC nanoparticle shell with a thickness of ~85 nm. After sintering, the core–shell structure of SiCw@TiC is preserved within the Al2O3 matrix, forming a unique composite toughening phase. The regions surrounding the core–shell whiskers exhibit a complex geometric configuration and multi-dimensional heterogeneities, including variations in phase composition (Al2O3/SiC/TiC), grain size (micron/nano), and thermal expansion coefficients (3.8×10−6–7.4×10−6/K). These features create a highly intricate stress environment, leading to multiple fracture modes during crack propagation. As a result, the SiCw@TiC composite toughening phase not only retains the toughening mechanisms of single-phase SiCw but also introduces additional energy dissipation pathways due to their core–shell structure. The Al2O3–SiCw@TiC composite ceramics demonstrate exceptional mechanical properties, including a relative density of 99.16%±0.48%, Vickers hardness of 21.38±0.93 GPa, flexural strength of 693±49 MPa, and fracture toughness of 7.15±0.47 MPa·m1/2. The concept of utilizing core-shell structured whiskers as toughening phases, along with the underlying toughening mechanisms, provides a novel approach and theoretical foundation for enhancing the fracture toughness of other structural ceramics.
