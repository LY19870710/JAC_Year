## 1 Introduction

Carbon fiber reinforced carbon aerogel (C/CA) composites, composed of carbon fibers with a low degree of crystallization and amorphous glassy carbon matrix of three-dimensional (3D) nanostructured porous particle skeletons, exhibit many impressive characters such as high strength and toughness, low thermal conductivity, and good large-size forming capacity [1−3]. Therefore, the composites are considered to be promising for applications as lightweight thermal insulation materials in thermal protection systems of reusable aerospace vehicles [4]. However, the inherent oxidation sensitivity of carbon skeletons in oxidizing atmospheres of more than 400 ℃ just like other carbon materials, as well as the unique microstructure of overlapped nanoparticles and interconnected nanopores, have made the oxidation become a bottleneck of restricting the application of C/CA in oxidizing environments.

Previous studies found that the oxidation behavior of C/CA is jointly governed by the chemical reaction between carbon and oxygen as well as the diffusion of oxygen within nanopores, resulting in the oxidation rate constants that are twice higher than those of conventional dense C/C composites [5]. Applying an oxidation-resisting coating to isolate oxidizing species is regarded as an effective protective method to prevent the diffusion of oxygen into C/CA, thereby significantly decreasing their oxidation rate constants. However, unlike dense C/C, the unique aerogel microstructure of C/CA makes the preparation of coatings quite difficult. When using a reactive melt infiltrating method, the coating is too thick and very easy to crack due to the strong capillary force of nanopores. Moreover, the carbon fibers in C/CA have no protective pyrolytic carbon layer and thus are extremely easily etched. In addition, preparing a ceramic coating by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) inevitably causes it to crack because of its severe strain localization related to the large difference in elastic modulus between the coating and C/CA. Specifically, we developed a gradient-structure and large-thickness SiC coating on C/CA using a selective ceramization method by mild Si–C gas–solid reaction under the joint assistance of low temperature and a porous diffusion barrier layer. Afterward, a dense SiC coating on C/CA with a siliconized gradient inner layer was obtained by CVD, and the resulting bilayer coating had low residual stress and high interfacial bonding strength [6]. Undoubtedly, the successful implementation of these ceramic coatings benefits from the construction of the gradient structure to inhibit strain localization, thus significantly improving its interfacial bonding strength as well as the ablation resistance properties of C/CA.

Even so, several issues still need attention. First, the intense evaporation of the oxide film derived from the oxidation of SiC makes the gradient coating difficult to meet the increasing demands for longer time and higher temperature thermal protection. Fortunately, it has been proved in the coating components of dense C/C that incorporating ultra-high temperature ceramics (UHTCs), especially borides, such as ZrB2 and HfB2 in SiC can extend the operation temperatures in oxidation environments due to the formation of Zr(Hf)O2 solid supporting skeleton, as well as Zr(Hf)–Si–B–O and borosilicate compound glass [7−11]. Second, although the bilayer structure improves ablation properties, there remains a further concern in structure design from monolayer to bilayer or multilayer due to the synergetic effect of complementation and cooperation of each layer [12−14]. Third, the toughness of ceramic coatings is generally low. Though multilayer structures can partially inhibit the generation of penetrative cracks, it is difficult to suppress crack propagation within a single layer on account of the intrinsic character of ceramics [15,16]. Fourth, the high residual stress during the coating preparation tends to be produced due to the large difference in coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE) between UHTC and C/CA. Specifically, the preparation processes such as pack cementation or gas silicon infiltration generally require multiple heating–cooling from 1600–1800 ℃ to room temperature for sintering of each layer [12,17]. Such layer-by-layer processing strategy inevitably induces intense stress accumulation because the deformations caused by thermal responses of the different layers are difficult to coordinate. As a consequence, C/CA is ready to crack due to its relatively weak ability to resist crack propagation. In general, fabricating a multilayer coating containing UHTC with large thickness, low residual stress, high crack tolerance, and minimal side effects on the C/CA substrate is the key to broadening their applications. So far as we know, relevant research has been almost unreported.

Herein, a SiC@SiO2 nanowire-toughened ZrB2–SiC/SiC bilayer coating on the C/CA substrate was prepared using a one-step low-temperature reaction sintering method by the simultaneous formation of a sintered outer layer with even-distributed nanowires and a siliconized gradient inner layer. By courtesy of the synergic thermal response of the layers and the crack deflection induced by the nanowires, the resulting bilayer coating has a large thickness of 500 μm, a moderate residual compressive stress of 0.08–1.22 GPa, a high interfacial bonding strength of 6.02 MPa, a good fracture toughness of 4.36 MPa·m1/2, and thus excellent cyclic ablation resistance with linear ablation rates of 0.1 μm/s at 1650 ℃ for 1500 s and 5 cycles and 0.4 μm/s at 1850 ℃ for 900 s and 3 cycles under an oxyacetylene test.

### 2.1 Coating fabrication

Bulk C/CA composites with a density of 0.6 g/cm3 manufactured by the Institute of Metal Research (IMR), Chinese Academy of Sciences, China, through a high-pressure-assisted polymerization and ambient pressure drying method [2] were machined into 15 mm × 15 mm × 10 mm pieces (for mechanical tests) and *ϕ*30 mm × 10 mm discs (for ablation tests). These pieces and discs were polished, cleaned, and dried in sequence as the experimental substrates. The slurry consisted of 20–40 wt% graphite (1 μm), 10–20 wt% B4C (1 μm), 5–10 wt% ZrO2 (50 nm), 5 wt% Al2O3 (1 μm) powders was uniformly blended in polyvinyl butyral (PVB) alcohol solution with a solid-to-liquid mass ratio of 1 : 4. Then the slurry was brushed on the substrates and cured at 120 ℃ for 2 h. Finally, these pieces and discs covered with cured slurry were put into a graphite crucible filled with a mixture of Si and SiO2 powders with a mass ratio 1 : 1 and heated to 1450 ℃ with holding time of 0.5 h in a vacuum for reaction and sintering. During this process, the CA matrix on the top of C/CA transformed *in* *situ* into a gradient SiC inner layer by the reaction of CA and gaseous Si diffused inward. The graphite, B4C, ZrO2, and Al2O3 powders in porous brushed layer (PBL) transformed into a ZrB2–SiC outer layer by means of the respective reactions of graphite, B4C, and ZrO2, as well as graphite and gaseous Si. As a result, a bilayer coating can be obtained through once low-temperature heating treatment. The coating preparation process and characterized microstructure are schematically shown in Fig. 1. For comparison, another bilayer coating was fabricated by a layer-by-layer strategy, namely undergoing twice heating–cooling. Specifically, a gradient SiC layer with a thickness of ~400 μm was obtained on the surface of C/CA using mild Si–C gas–solid reaction at 1450 ℃. Then the slurry with the same components was brushed on this gradient layer, followed by vacuum sintering at 1450 ℃ with holding time of 0.5 h to obtain the ZrB2–SiC outer layer.

### 2.2 Residual stress and mechanic property measurements

Microscopic residual stress was measured by Raman spectroscopy according to the TO peak offset of SiC relative to its normal position in an unstressed state. The detailed explanations for the test principle can be found in Refs. [18−20]. The residual stresses at the outer/inner coating and coating/substrate interfaces were tested and analyzed. The other information such as pre-treatment of the sample before testing and the instrumental parameters of the test were consistent with previous work. The tensile interfacial bonding strength between the coating and the substrate was measured by a tensile test according to the ASTM Standard C633-79. The schematic of the test process can be found in previous work [6]. The compressive strengths of the pristine and coated C/CA were measured by a compressive test to access the retention of mechanical properties after coating preparation. The indentation method was used to measure the fracture toughness of the coating, and the indentation schematic was shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary material (ESM). The fracture toughness was calculated as the equation of *K*IC = 0.0319 × *P*/(*a*/*l*a1/2) [21,22], where *K*IC is the fracture toughness (MPa·m1/2), *P* is the applied load (the load is 1 kg), *a* is the diagonal half-length of the indentation, and *l*a is the average length of cracks.

### 2.3 Thermal protection performance measurements

An oxyacetylene ablation test was conducted to evaluate the oxidation and ablation resistance of the coating. During ablation, the flux and pressure of O2 were set to 0.22 m3/h and 0.24 MPa, and those of C2H2 were 0.16 m3/h and 0.95 MPa, respectively. The ablation gun was adjusted to be perpendicular to the sample with a distance of 100 and 90 mm from the sample surface at ablation temperatures of 1850 and 1650 ℃, respectively. The surface temperature in the ablation center was measured by an infrared thermometer. Meanwhile, the back-side temperature of the sample was measured by a thermal couple to assess the thermal insulation property, and the test fixture is shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. The sample was exposed to the flame for 300 s in one ablation cycle and then naturally cooled after the gun was removed. Its mass and thickness were measured before and after the cycle. The process was repeated in the next cycle. The accumulated mass and linear ablation rates were obtained by *R*m = (*m*0 − *m*1)/*t*, and *R*l = (*l*0 − *l*1)/*t*, respectively, where *m*0 and *m*1 are the respective masses of the sample before and after each ablation cycle, *l*0 and *l*1 are the respective center thicknesses of the sample before and after each ablation cycle, and *t* is the corresponding ablation time.

### 2.4 Characterizations

Phase components of the coating were identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD). To verify the phase distribution, electron back-scattered diffraction (EBSD) was performed on a scanning electron microscope (SEM; G4, Thermo Scientific) incorporated an automated orientation acquisition system (HKL Chanel 5, Oxford Instruments), and operated at 15 kV voltage with a scanning step size of 0.1 μm. To obtain better quality of EBSD patterns, the coating in cross-section was polished with SiC paper to 3000 grit followed by diamond paste and colloidal silica solutions in sequence to remove the deformation layer of the surface. Fine microstructures of nanowires and phase interface were characterized using Talos transmission electron microstructure. The ablated central region was examined by a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 700, ZEISS, Germany) with a scanning step of 10 µm in depth. Thermal conductivities of the coated C/CA at room and high temperatures were measured using hot wire and heat flow methods at atmospheric pressure, respectively.

### 3.1 Microstructure and formation mechanism of nanowire-toughened bilayer coating

Figures 2(a)–2(d) show cross-section and surface images of the coating on the C/CA substrate prepared by the one-step low-temperature reaction sintering method. The coating has an obvious bilayer structure with a whole thickness of ~500 μm (Fig. 2(a)). On the top is the sintered ZrB2–SiC–SiO2 outer layer with a thickness of ~100 μm, deduced by the combined results of SEM and XRD. The layer is dense with basically invisible pores (Fig. 2(b)), which is composed of a few crystalline phases of ZrB2, SiC, and SiO2 according to the narrow XRD peaks (Fig. 2(e)). No obvious residual raw materials such as C, ZrO2, and B4C are found, suggesting their complete reaction. Note that there is a diffuse diffraction peak at low diffraction angles corresponding to amorphous SiO2, which is possibly generated during the cooling stage. However, it is difficult to distinguish the crystalline and amorphous SiO2 phases just from their morphologies. Thus, an EBSD pattern was used to distinguish the phases by virtue of their crystal structure information. According to the results in Fig. 2(f), it is clear that the crystal SiO2 and ZrB2 particles with respective dimensions of ~100 nm are well-distributed around the main phase of SiC particles. The amorphous SiO2 glass is mainly concentrated in the white zone where the Kikuchi band is hard to distinguish, whose amount is significantly more than the crystalline SiO2. Except for the granular ceramic products, many nanowires are also found (the insets in Fig. 2(b)), especially around the ZrB2 particles (Fig. 3(d)), which were directly *in-situ* synthesized by the reaction of the raw materials rather than the extra introductions by CVD growth or direct addition in traditional methods. On the bottom of the outer layer, there are no delamination or cracks at the interface; some nanowires are also present, playing a role in connecting the two sides (Fig. 2(c)). Meanwhile, the high contents of Si and O with an atomic ratio of approximately 1 : 2 (Fig. S3 in the ESM) near the interface verify the existence of SiO2, which can bond the two layers together just like glue. Consequently, it can be inferred that the presence of nanowires and SiO2 in the interface promotes good interfacial bonding.

Beneath the dense sintered layer is the gradient SiC layer with a thickness of ~400 μm (Fig. 2(a)), derived from the selective ceramization of the surface layer of C/CA by the mild Si–C gas–solid reaction. The microstructure of the gradient SiC layer is similar to the previous results (Figs. S4(a) and S4(b) in the ESM) [6]. In brief, it has dual gradients in composition and compactness along the thickness direction (Fig. S4(c) in the ESM), where the components gradually evolve from SiC to carbon and meanwhile, the structure also changes from dense to porous structure. The dual gradient structure is conductive to reduce the CTE mismatch between the ceramic coating and the C/CA substrate. Figure 2(d) shows the typical structure in the interface between the gradient layer and the substrate. As expected, some spherical SiC particles with diameters less than 500 nm are embedded in the porous CA matrix without introducing any cracks. These mosaic texture can effectively alleviate the CTE mismatch between carbon and SiC, thereby avoiding the interface cracking.

To further explore the role of nanowires, the high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) observation reveals the nanowires have a typical core–shell structure with diameters of ~50 nm (Fig. 3(a)). The outer shell is amorphous while the core is crystalline with an interplanar spacing of 0.25 nm (Fig. 3(b)). Combined with the element mapping results, which show that the shell is rich in Si and O but poor in C, and the core is rich in Si and C but is poor in O (Fig. 3(c)), as well as the SAED results in Fig. S5 in the ESM, we can thus deduce the formation of SiC/SiO2 core–shell nanowires. These nanowires grow surrounding the ZrB2 particles in a random direction (Fig. 3(d)) and can play a role in inhibiting crack propagation around ZrB2, thereby possibly exerting a remarkable toughening effect. In addition, the enlarged view (Fig. 3(e)) reveals the interfaces between the ZrB2 and SiC particles are crack-free, connected by the amorphous SiO2 glass.

In general, a SiC@SiO2 nanowire-toughened ZrB2–SiC/SiC bilayer coating has been prepared on C/CA through one-step reaction sintering at 1450 ℃. The synthesis and densification process of the outer layer can be explained as follows. A possible growth model is illustrated in Fig. 4. The crystal ZrB2 can be produced through the reaction of B4C/ZrO2/C in the raw materials by carbothermal reduction due to the low Gibbs free energy (ΔG*) of –16.342 kJ/mol at 1450 ℃, shown in Reaction (1) (Fig. 4). However, the crystal SiC is produced through two possible reactions correlated with the infiltrated gaseous Si, as shown in Reactions (2) and (3). The most likely reaction proceeded between gaseous Si and carbon due to low Δ*G* of –254.7 kJ/mol. Another strong possibility is that gaseous Si reacts with CO by-product because of quite lower Δ*G* of –787.7 kJ/mol. Though Reaction (3) with lower Gibbs free energy is easier to proceed, Reaction (2) is considered to be the main way to produce SiC since there is less CO as a reactant for Reaction (3) due to less ZrO2 in PBL and the reaction condition of vacuum. Subsequently, the infiltrated silica from the pack cementation powders can connect the crystal ZrB2 and SiC particles to form an amorphous interface during the cooling stage as Reaction (5). Consequently, the crystal ZrB2 and SiC particles with amorphous SiO2 interfaces can be formed. Considering the certain differences between theoretical calculations and actual reactions, the experiments at 1350 and 1500 ℃ were also performed to reveal the reaction mechanisms. The XRD and SEM results (Fig. S6 in the ESM) show that the dominant SiC and ZrB2, as well as the nanowires, can be generated in this temperature range, further confirming that the discussed reactions in Section 3.1 can be produced.

With the above reactions proceeding, the SiC@SiO2 nanowires in the outer layer are also simultaneously in-situ* generated by the reaction of gaseous Si with CO by-product, as shown in Reaction (3). The evidence of massive nanowires around ZrB2 (Fig. 3(d)), where CO is produced through Reaction (1), strongly supports this viewpoint. At first, the produced SiC starts to crystallize and forms the crystal nucleus when it reaches supersaturation at high temperatures. Subsequently, the newly generated SiC absorbs on the nucleus and continuously grows along the  direction due to the minimum energy to form the long SiC core. During the growth of the SiC core, the solute discharge contributes to the concentration of SiO2 on the surface rather than inside the core. Thus, the amorphous shell is formed by the solidification and deposition of the produced SiO2 in Reaction (3) as well as the infiltrated silica in Reaction (4). Consequently, the growth process of nanowires can be inferred to follow a vapor–solid growth mechanism, as no catalyst was added, and the process was implemented by heating the reactants to form a saturated vapor and then precipitating the product to grow into one-dimensional nanomaterials. In addition, though the foregoing analysis excludes the possibility of SiO2 as a reactant to synthesize SiC, its roles in the coating densification and adherence are nonnegligible (Reaction (5)). The raw SiO2 can diffuse in the sintering stage into the outer layer and even the outer/inner layer interface, and then solidify in the cooling stage to fill the remaining pores, thereby adhering with the outer and inner layers.

As to the inner layer, it is generated by the silicification of the CA matrix to SiC. Gaseous Si can continue to infiltrate through the outer layer and then react with the CA matrix to form the SiC–C gradient layer. Its gradients in composition and compactness are caused by the gradually decreased concentration of Si along the depth. More specific explanations have been provided in the previous study [23]. Overall, the resulting bilayer coating with UHTC-containing components, toughened nanowires, and gradient structure is expected to have good thermal shock resistance and cycle ablation performances.

### 3.2 Residual stress of nanowire-toughened bilayer coating

For a bilayer coating, the outer/inner layer and inner layer/substrate interfaces are generally vulnerable due to their possible high residual stress caused by the changes in composition and structure. Therefore, clarifying the stress state in the interface is extremely important since the stress is closely related to the mechanical and ablative properties. Figure 5(a) shows statistical results of the stress in the outer/inner layer interface based on the transverse optical phonon mode (TO) peak offsets of SiC according to the Raman spectra. For comparison, the stress of the samples prepared through a conventional layer-by-layer strategy was also measured. For the bilayer coating prepared by one-step sintering, most of the stresses are concentrated in the state of compression in the range of 0.08–1.22 GPa. It is certain that such residual compressive stress state is beneficial for retarding crack initiation and propagation and thus improving the interfacial bonding property. As to the residual stress in the inner layer/substrate interface (Fig. 5(b)), most of stresses are in the state of tension in the range of 0.33–0.83 GPa, which is consistent with our previous research [6,7]. It can be explained by larger CTE of the SiC spheres than the CA network. Even so, the low tensile stress is not enough to cause the network to crack. However, for the bilayer coating prepared layer-by-layer, the measured stresses are more discrete, more than half are in the tensile state, and the maximum value reaches 1.53 GPa. The wide fluctuation of stresses can easily lead to the coating cracking under thermal shock due to the inconsistent thermal response of these zones. The stresses in the inner layer/substrate interface are roughly the same as those prepared by the one-step sintering since the two inner layers have similar microstructure.

The reason for the significant difference in stress in the outer/inner layer interface lies in their different thermal deformation behaviors, which are closely related to thermal expansion and elastic modulus. For the coating prepared by one-step sintering, the ZrB2–SiC outer layer is gradually sintered from dispersed powders to dense bulk, and the inner gradient layer is gradually generated from porous carbon to relatively dense SiC–C network. The CTE difference between the outer and inner layers is small at the beginning and gradually increases as the reactions proceed. Resultantly, the accumulated thermal strain caused by the thermal response of different layers is not apparent during the coating preparation. Meanwhile, since the outer and inner layers are produced simultaneously, the low elastic modulus of the inner layer can exert only a relatively weak constraint. As a result, the outer layer can deform more easily with the temperature changes. The two factors jointly contribute to the small residual strain. For the coating prepared layer-by-layer, the deformations of the different layers are difficult to coordinate since the strong constraint is imposed by the SiC–C inner layer with high elastic modulus. Consequently, the bilayer coating by the one-step sintering method has less thermal stress accumulation compared with that by the conventional layer-by-layer.

### 3.3 Mechanical properties of nanowire-toughened bilayer coating

To evaluate the interface bonding strength between the bilayer coating and the C/CA substrate, a tensile test was conducted using the fixture inserted in Fig. 6(a). The load–displacement curves of the pristine and coated C/CA show a similar trend of the rapid increase in load followed by a sudden drop, which is a typical brittle fracture mode. By dividing the maximum load by the contact area, the obtained bonding strength is 6.02 MPa, which is close to the pristine C/CA of 6.26 MPa, suggesting good interfacial adhesion. The fracture morphology (Fig. 6(b)) shows the coated C/CA eventually fractured in the substrate instead of at the outer/inner layer interface, although it is commonly vulnerable in a multi-layer coating. Further observation shows that many pulled-out nanowires in the outer layer and carbon fibers in the substrate are present (Figs. 6(c) and 6(d)). It implies extra energy consumed during the tensile test through interface debonding, crack deflection, and nanowire/fiber pull-out, contributing to the high toughness [24,25]. The robust interface is closely associated with the microstructure characters. One is the glass phase near the interface, which can stick the outer/inner layers together (Fig. S3 in the ESM). The other is nanowires rooting in the outer layer and growing towards the inner layer, which can transfer load as a bridging link.

Fracture toughness is closely related to crack tolerance, which is crucial to the thermal shock and cycle ablation performances of the coating. For the nanowire-toughened ZrB2–SiC/SiC bilayer coating, only a short crack with a length of 27.2 μm is present even though the indentation load has reached 1 kg (Fig. 6(e)). Further observation (Fig. 6(f)) reveals that the crack is tortuous rather than traditionally straight, and even some places are linked together, which is attributed to the crack deflection and bridging caused by the nanowires. The existence of nanowires can effectively restrict crack propagation and make for crack deflection or termination. The small amount and short length of cracks and the zigzag appearance indicate that the nanowire-toughened coating has high resistance to cracking. By measuring the lengths of indentations and cracks, the fracture toughness was calculated to be 4.36 MPa·m1/2. The fracture toughness is comparable to the SiC whisker-strengthened thermal barrier coatings with values of 4.43–5.65 MPa·m1/2 [26] but much higher than the ceramic coating without strengthening phases with values of 0.18–0.89 MPa·m1/2 [27], confirming the effectiveness of nanowire toughening.

### 3.4 Cycle ablation performance of nanowire-toughened bilayer coating

Cyclic ablation resistance of the bilayer coating on C/CA was evaluated by an oxyacetylene torch with 300 s duration for each cycle at 1650 ℃ 5 times and 1850 ℃ 3 times, respectively. The mass and linear ablation rates are plotted in Fig. 7(a). For the first and second cyclic ablation at 1650 ℃, the mass ablation rates increase rapidly from 0.18 to 0.34 mg/s and then moderately increase to 0.40 mg/s in the subsequent 3 cycles. While the linear ablation rates slightly fluctuate in the range of 0.09–0.12 μm/s. The rapid mass loss in the initial stage can be explained by the mechanical denudation of extra remnants, such as weak-bonded reaction powders and nanowire protruding from the coating surface under the impact of gas flow. At this moment, the limited oxidation of the ceramic components on the surface contributes to the insignificant mass gain due to the relatively low ablation temperature. As the ablation temperature increases to 1850 ℃, the mass ablation rates increase rapidly from 0.03 to 0.28 mg/s. Meanwhile, the linear ablation rates change from 0.40 to 0.76 μm/s, more than 4 times higher than the counterparts at 1650 ℃. The slight mass ablation rate in the first ablation cycle can be attributed to the balance between the mass loss by mechanical denudation and the mass gain by intense oxidation. The subsequent increase is related to the rapid evaporation of the formed glass due to the high ablation temperature. The significantly larger linear ablation rates are related to the more serious evaporation of oxide film and strong gas scouring (discussed in Section 3.4).

Figures 7(b) and 7(c) show macroscopic features of the bilayer coating surfaces after the cyclic ablation. For all the tested specimens, the coating remains intact, and no visible cracks are found, even in the ablation centers with higher temperatures and more severe gas scouring (marked by a yellow circle). The area of the ablation center at 1850 ℃ obviously enlarges despite only 3-time cycle ablation implemented compared with that at 1650 ℃. The ablation centers are both covered by some white glass, which has a similar oxide composition of SiO2, ZrO2, and ZrSiO4, detected by an X-ray diffractometer (D/Max-2500PC, Rigaku, Japan) (Fig. 8(i)). To further explore the pore depth in the oxide scale, surface profiles of the center regions were obtained by laser scanning confocal microscopy. The three-dimensional morphology reveals that some small and separated ablation pits with a maximum depth of 200 μm are present after 1500 s (300 s × 5 cycles) long-time ablation at 1650 ℃, while the ablation center appears a big and continuous crater with a maximum depth of 450 μm after 900 s (300 s × 3 cycles) ablation at 1850 ℃. Even so, their ablation depths are less than the coating thickness, suggesting that the coating can protect the C/CA substrate from ablation well. Resultantly, the low ablation rates combined with the unbroken appearance of the substrate indicate the excellent cycle ablation and thermal shock resistance of the bilayer coating on C/CA.

Figure 8(a) shows the cross-sectional morphology of the coated specimen after 5-cycle ablation at 1650 ℃. An oxide scale is covered on the surface with the appearance of some pores due to the long-time evaporation of the glass phase. Even so, the ZrB2–SiC outer and SiC inner layers still tightly bond together, and no transverse cracks are found in each layer or at their interface, suggesting strong interfacial adherence. Further observation in the outer layer indicates that several nanowires near the ablation center survived (Fig. 8(b)). Moreover, the nanowires in the inner/outer interface are also well preserved (Fig. 8(c)). Generally, nanowires are extremely susceptible to ablation and are difficult to observe in the ablated structure [28−30], especially for a long period of ablation. Herein, the preservation of the nanowires may be related to the unique core–shell structure that the SiO2 shell slows down the diffusion of oxygen to the SiC core, making the skeleton not easily damaged. There is no doubt that the survival of the toughening phase can inhibit crack propagation at the interface and contribute to the crack-free microstructure after 5-cycle ablation, thereby proving the good thermal shock of the bilayer coating.

With the increase of ablation temperature to 1850 ℃, the ZrB2–SiC outer layer is severely exhausted, and the inner SiC layer is exposed and oxidized to form an oxide scale with a few pores (Figs. 8(d) and 8(e)). The oxide scale is a composite structure composed of a solid supporting skeleton and glass filler with respective Zr–Si–O atom ratios of 26 : 5 : 69 and 9 : 21 : 70 (Figs. 8(g) and 8(h)). The skeleton is the precipitate from the glass phase with the appearance of typical equiaxial morphology, which may originate from the dissolution of ZrO2 at high temperatures and its precipitation during cooling [31−34]. It has been proved that the composite structure has low oxygen diffusivity and good mechanical denudation resistance [35]. The formation of the pores in the oxide scale is related to the generation, aggregation, and escape of many gaseous by-products. In addition, the magnified view at the interface confirms the formed glass is tightly covered on the C/CA substrate and can still serve as an effective oxygen barrier (Fig. 8(f)).

The main four factors contribute to the significantly improved cyclic ablation resistance of the bilayer coating. The first one is the optimum oxide components. In the initial stage of the cycle ablation, the ZrB2–SiC outer layer is oxidized to form ZrO2, SiO2, ZrSiO4, and borosilicate. Especially, the formation of ZrSiO4 can improve the ablation resistance due to its higher melting temperature, low oxygen permeability, and high thermal stability [36−38]. The resultant multi-phase oxides with solid skeleton and glass filler can effectively resist the scouring of gas flow and inhibit oxygen diffusion to some extent. With the cycle ablation proceeding, however, the evaporation of the glass phase and the intense gas scouring inevitably leave some ablation pits. Nevertheless, the troublesome cracking and delamination of coating, which are generally difficult to avoid, do not occur after the cyclic ablation. This should be closely related to the high toughness of the coating. Specifically, the shell–core nanowires with random orientation and strong survivability at high temperatures can suppress the crack propagation around the ZrB2 particles where the thermal response mismatch is the most severe. At this moment, their existence can act as bridges to connect the ZrB2–SiC outer layer to the SiC inner layer and avoid the delamination or the formation of penetrating cracks [30,39]. In addition, the residual stress is considered as another important factor responsible for this cycle ablation performance. The relatively low stress at the coating/substrate interface contributes to the reliable adherence and ensures that the coating would not be peeled off under the gas impact. Especially, the dominant residual compressive stresses at the inner/outer layer interface exert an intense resistance to crack initiation and propagation [40], which can generate a synergy effect with the nanowires to improve the fracture resistance of the bilayer coating. The good interfacial bonding is regarded as another factor. It enables the bilayer coating to withstand the shear stress of the oxyacetylene flame and the thermal shock during cyclic ablation, thus preventing the spalling of the bilayer coating.

### 3.5 Mechanical and thermal insulation performances of coated C/CA

As mentioned above, the C/CA with a nanowire-toughened bilayer coating can be prepared using the one-step low-temperature reaction sintering method. The retention of the mechanical properties of the C/CA substrate is key to evaluating whether the preparation process is effective. Thus, the compressive strengths of the pristine and coated C/CA are compared, as shown in Fig. 9(a). Their respective values are 74.1±2.5 and 71.9±2.1 MPa, with a slight decrease of less than 3%, indicating that the one-step preparation strategy has a small influence on the robust mechanical strength of the substrate. The slight decrease may be related to the introduction of residual tensile stress in the transition zone and the uncoordinated deformation between the ceramic coating and the carbon substrate due to their large difference in elastic modulus and compactness. Thus, when the coated sample is subjected to an external load, the coating is more likely to debond, resulting in a reduced practical bearing area and consequently leading to a certain decline of strength.

Thermal insulation is the most essential property of C/CA as a thermal insulator, which was evaluated by measuring back-side temperature (*T*b) and front-side temperature (*T*f) during one ablation cyclic. As shown in Fig. 9(b), for the ablation at 1650 ℃, *T*f1 immediately reaches the maximum upon the heating and keeps constant after heating for 25 s. The corresponding *T*b1 reaches the highest value of 811 ℃ at 100 s and then remains stable in the later ablation time. For ablation at 1850 ℃, the curves show a similar trend, with *T*b2 eventually stabilizing at 910 ℃. The coated C/CA with 10 mm thickness achieves the respective temperature drops of 940 and 839 ℃ in the oxidizing atmosphere, indicating the well-preserved thermal insulation performance. It can be attributed to the relatively low thermal conductivities (0.66 W/(m·K) at room temperature by a hot wire method and 1.27 W/(m·K) at 800 ℃ by a heat flow method at atmospheric pressure) because of the porous structure and low crystallinity (Fig. S7 in the ESM). Noteworthy, the *T*b values are lower than our reported values of the bilayer coating prepared by combining CVD at 1100 ℃ and low-temperature reaction at 1400 ℃ [6], confirming that the one-step reaction sintering strategy brings less side-effect to the C/CA substrate. On this basis, it can be easily supposed that if a UHTC-containing bilayer coating is prepared by a traditional layer-by-layer method at higher temperatures of 1600–1800 ℃, the thermal insulation performance of the C/CA substrate will significantly deteriorate due to its crystallization.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, the SiC@SiO2 nanowire-toughened ZrB2–SiC/SiC bilayer coating was prepared on the C/CA substrate using a one-step low-temperature reaction sintering method. The obtained crack-free coating has a sintered ZrB2–SiC outer layer with even-distributed nanowires and a siliconized gradient SiC–C inner layer, with characters of low residual stress, good interfacial bonding, and high toughness. It can be attributed to the *in-situ* generation of shell–core nanowires in the outer layer and at the interface which promotes the deflection or termination of cracks, as well as the gradient structure of the inner layer inducing the low residual stress. Benefited from the multi-phase oxide glass film, high temperature-stable nanowires with good toughness, moderate residual compressive stress, and high interfacial bonding strength, the bilayer coating exhibits excellent cyclic anti-ablation performance. It can withstand the ablation at 1650 ℃ for 1500 s and 5 cycles and 1850 ℃ for 900 s and 3 cycles. Meanwhile, the original good mechanical and thermal insulation properties are well-preserved since the one-step preparation method can minimize the adverse effects of thermal preparation of coatings on the C/CA substrate.
