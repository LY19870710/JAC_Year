## 1 Introduction

Fracture toughness, a critical parameter for assessing a material’s resistance to crack propagation, indicates the ability of a material to withstand crack extension under operating conditions without catastrophic failure [1−5]. Unlike metals [6−8], ceramic material exhibits intrinsic brittleness due to their strongly directional chemical bonds and inability to undergo effective plastic deformation [9−14]. Traditional ceramics [15−22], such as alumina, silicon carbide, and tungsten carbide, are widely utilized in various engineering fields but are limited by their brittleness, with typical fracture toughness values ranging from 2–5 MPa·m1/2. Therefore, developing mechanisms and strategies for the toughening of brittle ceramics has been a crucial and long-standing research focus for material science, with significant efforts directed toward improving the sintering process [23−25], synergistically incorporating fiber or whisker-like architectures [26−28], optimizing microstructures [29,30], and developing composite material [8,31−36]. α-SiAlON composites with a whisker-like microstructure have a high fracture toughness of 6.3 MPa·m1/2, resulting in significant toughening [26]. On the other hand, silicon nitride ceramics, synthesized through dynamic ripening and distinguished by tough interlocking microstructures [31], demonstrate a high fracture toughness of 5.5 MPa·m1/2. An alternative approach to overcome the limitations of brittleness is the introduction of a metal binder into the brittle ceramic system. Nanopolycrystalline WC with 12 wt% Co binder achieves an ultrahigh fracture toughness of 15.3 MPa·m1/2 but is accompanied by a significant reduction in hardness [37]. Consequently, although extrinsic toughening mechanisms have been well developed, intrinsic toughening remains highly important for developing next-generation ceramics with a combination of excellent mechanical properties.

Transformation toughening, as an evident strategy, has been widely used in brittle material [38,39]. The stress at the crack tip induces a phase transformation from a high-density state to a low-density state, which promotes energy absorption and enhances crack extension resistance through accompanying volume expansion, achieving exceptional toughening. Y2O3-stabilized tetragonal ZrO2 polycrystals (Y-TZPs), classic ionically bonded ceramics, have demonstrated impressive toughening through stress-induced martensitic transformation from the tetragonal to monoclinic phase [23,40], thereby rendering them suitable for a wide range of applications in various industries. However, Y-TZPs are susceptible to low-temperature degradation [41,42] and have relatively low hardness (below 12 GPa) [43] and thermal conductivity (below 2.5 W/(m·K)) [44,45]. Therefore, the development of new transformation-toughened material is urgently needed. This toughening mechanism relies on metastable microstructures capable of undergoing stress-induced structural transformation. Crucially, the energy barrier for phase transformation must be lower than the energy driving crack propagation to activate such transformations—a requirement intrinsically linked to the bonding characteristics of ceramics. While covalently bonded ceramics typically exhibit greater strength than ionically bonded ceramics do, they are also severely limited in their ability to be toughened by stress-induced transformation due to their high stability. To address this, reducing the energy barriers of phase transformation through microstructural engineering has become critical to achieving transformation toughening in covalent ceramics. Recent breakthroughs have revealed that dual-phase α/β-Si3N4 ceramics with coherent interfaces exhibit exceptional microcompression plasticity and superior strength [46]. Uniaxial compression induces a β → α phase transformation via bond switching at these interfaces, suggesting their potential as next-generation transformation-toughened ceramics.

Traditionally, toughening in silicon nitride ceramics is achieved by controlling the β phase morphology to a rod-like structure [47−49] but requires a high fraction of β phase Si3N4, resulting in a significant reduction in hardness. Owing to the potential for transformation toughening, coherent α/β-Si3N4 ceramics may have superior toughness; however, this has not been demonstrated. Notably, the current coherent α/β-Si3N4 samples are poorly densified ([46]), which is insufficient for a realistic determination of the fracture toughness. Here, we successfully synthesized dense coherent α/β-Si3N4 ceramics via high-pressure and high-temperature (HPHT) technology and identified transformation toughening in Si3N4 ceramics. As a result, the dense coherent α/β-Si3N4 ceramics have a high fracture toughness of 7.30±0.35 MPa·m1/2, which is more than double that (3.46± 0.10 MPa·m1/2) of the pure β-phase Si3N4 ceramics.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

Silicon nitride (*D*50 = 5.1 µm, purity > 99.9%, Aladdin, China) was selected as the starting material (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). Silicon nitride powder was subjected to vacuum drying at 200 °C for 2 h at a heating rate of 3 °C/min. The powder (50–55 mg) was compacted into cylindrical samples with a diameter of 4 mm and a height of 2.5 mm. To further eliminate adsorbed water from the silicon nitride, the cylindrical samples were heated to 600 °C at a rate of 7 °C/min and held for 1 h.

### 2.2 HPHT sintering

HPHT experiments were conducted in a large-volume cubic press. On the basis of the precalibrated pressure and temperature, graphite and hexagonal boron nitride were employed as the heater and capsule (see detailed assembly in Fig. 1), respectively. During the synthesis, the samples were initially compressed to the target pressure at a rate of 0.067 MPa/s and then heated to different target temperatures at a heating rate of 100–130 °C/s and maintained for a specified duration. Table 1 specifies the heating durations and corresponding temperatures for each experiment. All the samples underwent a controlled slow cooling process (5 min at 1000 °C, followed by 10 min of cooling to room temperature, ~100 °C/min). After heating, the samples were depressurized at the same rate (0.067 MPa/s) and then polished with diamond sandpaper.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The phase contents of the raw powder and sintered bulk samples were examined via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/teX Ultra250 (0/1D), Rigaku Smartlab SE, Japan) with Cu-Kα radiation (*λ* = 0.15406 nm) at 1.2 KW. The XRD patterns were obtained by using an X-ray diffractometer (Cu-Kα, R-AXIS RAPID XRD, Rigaku Co., Ltd., Japan) at 30 kV and 40 mA. The Rietveld refinement method was employed to calculate the β phase content via the generalized structure analysis system (GSAS). The particle size distribution of the raw Si3N4 powders was examined via a laser diffraction particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 2000, Malvern Panalytical, UK). The oxygen and nitrogen contents of the raw Si3N4 powders were measured via a nitrogen-oxygen analyzer (EMGA-830, HORIBA, Japan). Raman spectra of the samples were acquired by using a LabRAM HR Evolution instrument (HORIBA, Japan) with a 532 nm laser excitation source. The indentation morphology was etched at 4.5 kV for 20 min in an argon atmosphere (RES102, Argon Ion Cross-Section Polisher, Leica, Germany) and investigated by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Regulus-8100, Hitachi Ltd., Japan) with an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. The distribution of the β phase was measured via a precession electron diffractometer (PED; DigiSTAR, NanoMEGAS SPRL, Belgium) with precession angles and step sizes of 1° and 3 nm and an exposure time of 20 ms. Nanoscale structural information was obtained via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV and an electron microscope (Double Cs-Corrected Spectra 300, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operated at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV. The samples for PED and TEM analysis were prepared by a focused ion beam (FIB; Helios 5 CX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with ion voltages and currents of 30 kV and 2.5 nA for cutting and 2 kV and 9 pA for thinning. The FIB-prepared samples were subjected to final cleaning at 500 V and 100 pA via a precision ion milling system (Nanomill M1040, Fischione, USA).

The Vickers hardness (*H*V) and fracture toughness (*K*C) were measured via the indentation method with an applied load of 49 N and a dwell time of 10 s. For each sample, the testing region was randomly selected, and at least five indentations were measured to evaluate the standard errors of the measurements. *H*V and *K*C were calculated via Eqs. (1)–(4):

(1) HV=1854.4F/L2

(2) la=ca−1

(3) (KCΦ/(HVa1/2))(HV/(EΦ))2/5=0.035(la)−1/2

(4) KC=0.016(E/HV)1/2(F/c3/2)

where *F* is the applied load (N), *L* is the arithmetic average of the two diagonals of the Vickers indentation (μm),* l *is the Palmqvist crack length (μm), *a* is the average of the half-diagonal in the Vickers indent (μm), *c* is the mean radius of the surface crack (μm), *Φ* is the constraint factor of 3, and *E* is the Young’s modulus (GPa). Equation (3) remains valid when the ratio of the crack length to the indentation half-diagonal (*l*/*a*) falls within the approximate range of 0.25–2.5, and Eq. (4) is specifically applicable for (*c*/*a* ≥ 2) [50,51].

The mass density of the polished samples was determined via Archimedes’ method. The Young’s modulus (*E*) was measured with a Nanoindenter (G200, KLA, USA).

### 3.1 Phase transformation and sample synthesis

The α*-*to-β phase transformation is governed through a dissolution‒reprecipitation process with the formation of α/β coherent interfaces [52,53]. It is challenging to balance the densification of the sintered bulk and the content of the β phase at elevated temperatures. High-pressure sintering is an effective densification method with simultaneous suppression of atomic diffusion to control the phase transformation process. Moreover, in this work, we employed a two-step method of nucleation followed by growth to obtain a submicron β phase, thus achieving a high proportion of coherent interfaces. To identify an appropriate nucleation temperature at an applied pressure of 5 GPa, we first annealed the starting material (93.3 wt% α phase + 6.7 wt% β phase, as shown in Fig. 2) at 1300–1700 °C for a long duration of 1 h, confirming the critical temperature of 1500 °C for the α*-*to-β phase transformation (Fig. 3). Therefore, in the synthesis of dense α/β-Si3N4 ceramics (see methods and Fig. 1 for details), the β phase first nucleates at 1300–1400 °C for 1 h and then grows at a higher temperature of 1800–2000 °C for a few minutes. Figure 4 shows the structural transformation of Si3N4 ceramics under different HPHT conditions. The labels of the samples correspond to the specific synthesis conditions, as given in Table 1. X-ray diffraction patterns (Fig. 4(a)) reveal that the samples recovered by low-temperature nucleation and high-temperature growth are dual-phase α/β-Si3N4 ceramics and that the phase fractions can be easily controlled by tuning the nucleation temperature, growth temperature, and holding time. Figure 4(b) shows the emerging peaks corresponding to the β-phase Si3N4 in the Raman spectrum of the dual-phase α/β-Si3N4 ceramics (SN-046) synthesized at 1400 °C for 1 h and 2000 °C for 2 min, suggesting the formation of the β phase. The characteristic Raman peaks are well aligned with those of the pure β Si3N4 ceramics (SN-024), implying that there is no significant stress within the dual-phase samples. To determine the phase fractions of the dual-phase samples, we refined the XRD patterns via the Rietveld method [54]. The Rietveld refinement results reveal that the SN-046 sample is composed of 78.89 wt% α phase and 21.11 wt% β phase (Fig. 4(c)). We measured the density of the samples, as shown in Table 1. All the dual-phase α/β-Si3N4 ceramics have relative densities above 96%, indicating good densification. Therefore, HPHT synthesis has been demonstrated to be advantageous for both the densification and phase control of dual-phase α/β-Si3N4 ceramics.

### 3.2 Mechanical properties and fracture behavior

Figure 5 shows the mechanical properties and crack propagation behaviors of the dense dual-phase Si3N4 samples compared with those of the pure β Si3N4 sample. The dual-phase Si3N4 samples have a significantly superior combination of hardness and fracture toughness (Fig. 5(a)). Figure S2 in the ESM displays the morphology of the Vickers indentations for all samples, with the corresponding *c*/*a* values labeled in the images. The indentation fracture behaviors of the samples satisfy Palmqvist cracking; therefore, the equation proposed by Lankford was used to evaluate the fracture toughness [55,56]. To ensure accurate input for the fracture toughness calculations, the Young’s modulus was measured via nanoindentation for all the samples, as presented in Fig. 6. The obtained values are consistent with those of a previous study [57]. Notably, owing to the high densification and excellent sinterability of the SN-024 and SN-046 samples, their Young’s moduli reached 317.1±3.2 and 321.9±6.4 GPa, respectively. Owing to the presence of the harder α phase, the dual-phase SN-046 sample exhibited a Vickers hardness of 20.93±0.70 GPa, which is 23% greater than that of the pure β Si3N4 SN-024 sample (16.01±0.10 GPa). In particular, the dual-phase SN-046 sample has a significantly increased fracture toughness of 7.30±0.35 MPa·m1/2, which is up to 110% greater than that of the pure β Si3N4 sample. Figure 5(b) and Table 2 summarize the fracture toughness and Vickers hardness of silicon nitride ceramics and silicon nitride-based structural ceramics [26,31,47,48,57−60], which demonstrates an outstanding combination of hardness and fracture toughness of our synthesized dense dual-phase α/β-Si3N4 ceramics. Notably, the SN-024 sample marginally exceeds the upper bound threshold (*l*/*a* = 2.67 > 2.5). To ensure a consistent comparison of fracture toughness, Eq. (3) is specifically applied to the SN-024 sample, demonstrating the toughening effect. In addition, Eq. (4), which is particularly applicable when *c*/*a* ≥ 2, is used to calculate the precise fracture toughness value for SN-024 (2.03±0.08 MPa·m1/2 at a 5 kg load). Although our results provide direct observations of the increase in fracture toughness, the precise values of fracture toughness need to be determined via standard methods, such as single-edge notched bending (SENB), in future studies on samples with large dimensions.

The current dual-phase silicon nitride ceramics overcome the limitations of conventional toughening mechanisms in silicon nitride-based ceramics, simultaneously achieving enhanced toughness and hardness. Crack propagation behavior was investigated by scanning electron microscopy to analyze the provenance of the increased fracture toughness. Figures 5(c)–5(f) show crack images of the polished surfaces of the SN-046 sample. Typical toughening characteristics such as crack deflection (Fig. 5(c)), crack branching (Fig. 5(d)), and crack termination (Fig. 5(e)) are observed in the crack propagation path, which can extend crack propagation paths and relieve stress concentration at the crack tip. Surprisingly, we found ubiquitous crack bridging in the dual-phase Si3N4 samples, as shown in Fig. 5(f). Note that the crack bridging presented here is strikingly different from the crack bridging through putting out grains or whiskers previously reported in whisker-toughened ceramics [61,62]. The crack bridges in the dual-phase Si3N4 samples exhibit localized plastic deformation features at the submicron scale that provide resistance to cracking, thereby contributing to the improvement in fracture toughness.

### 3.3 Stress-induced β-to-α phase transformation toughening

To reveal the mechanism of localized plastic deformation during cracking, we used PED to examine the microstructure and phase distributions of the dual-phase α/β-Si3N4 ceramics (SN-046) before/after deformation. FIB technology was utilized to prepare the samples from three regions: remote from the crack, at the crack bridges, and at the crack tip (Fig. 7). Figures 8(a) and 8(b) show the morphology and phase distributions of the region remote from the cracks. The β phase content was evaluated to be 23.9 vol%, which is in good agreement with the XRD refinement results (Fig. 4(c)). The inverse pole figure (IPF) map (Fig. 8(c)) reveals that the α-phase grains have random crystallographic orientations. Figure 8(d) shows the FIB sample at crack bridges, exhibiting two regions of submicron-sized (red rectangle) and nanometer-sized (blue rectangle) grains. Compared with the undeformed regions (Fig. 8(a)), the extensive distortion (Fig. 8(e)) and indistinct grain boundaries (Fig. 8(g)) illustrate that grain deformation occurred within both sizes of grains (Figs. 8(e) and 8(g)), indicating the processes of intragranular and intergranular stress dissipation. More importantly, the β phase content is significantly reduced to 8.9 and 13 vol% from 23.9 vol%, with only trace amounts of the β phase remaining in the stress-concentrated region (Figs. 8(f) and 8(h)), suggesting stress-induced β-to-α phase transformation. The dense β-phase nanocrystals and the high proportion of stress-induced phase transformation (Fig. 8(f)) indicate that stress dissipation occurs more extensively within the submicron-sized grains. In the phase distribution shown in Fig. 8(h), the β phase grain boundaries exhibit an irregularly serrated structure with frequent incorporation of α phase nanocrystals (10–20 nm), indicating that stress-induced β-to-α phase transformation occurs at dual-phase interfaces. Triggering successive stress-induced phase transitions becomes more challenging as crack propagation leads to stress attenuation. At the crack tip, the β phase content is 16 vol% (Fig. 8(j)), which is greater than that at crack bridges with persistent grain deformation (Figs. 8(f) and 8(h)). The grain morphology is also clearer and more regular, although we can observe noticeable localized grain deformation at the edge of the large α-phase grains (Fig. 8(i)). Moreover, crack termination clearly occurs in front of the β/α interface, suggesting the critical role of the β/α coherent interface in the stress-induced phase transformation of the dual-phase α/β-Si3N4 ceramic. Therefore, persistent grain deformation, driven by stress-induced phase transformation, fundamentally contributes to improving fracture toughness by forming crack bridges accompanied by localized plastic deformation and directly terminating cracks at crack tips (Fig. 8(k)), effectively hindering crack propagation [63].

### 3.4 Phase transition mechanism

Figure 9 shows the TEM results associated with the stress-induced microstructural changes at the crack bridge and crack tip of the dual-phase α/β-Si3N4 ceramics. At the crack bridge, substantial stress concentrations along both sides of the crack exhibit abundant dislocations accompanied by severe lattice distortions (Figs. 9(a)–9(c)), which is consistent with the results presented in Figs. 8(e) and 8(g). At the crack tip with a relatively low strain region, numerous slip dislocations are observed inside the β phase grain near the dual-phase interface (Figs. 9(d) and 9(e)). On the basis of the high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image (Fig. 9(f)) and the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern (Fig. 9(g) and Fig. S3 in the ESM), we confirmed that the dual-phase interface is coherent along the direction of [100]α//[100]β, and some other coherent relationships of [103]α//[103]β and [102]α//[102]*β* are presented in Fig. 10 and Fig. S4 in the ESM, validating the minimal misorientation between the α phase and the formed β phase in the samples synthesized via the nucleation and growth mechanism with the HPHT method. Figure 9(e) presents a very distinct jagged coherent interface, which is due to stress-induced β*-*to-α phase transformation [46]. The defects near the coherent interface elucidate the process of phase transformation. A significant density of slip dislocations can be observed in the HRTEM images (Figs. 9(h) and 9(i)). The orientation relationship of [201]α//[201]β on the dashed rectangle in Fig. 9(i) was confirmed through FFT analysis (Fig. 9(j) and Fig. S5 in the ESM). Moreover, the double diffraction phenomena suggests additional lattice mismatches and strains in the β phase during phase transformation. The inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT) image further confirms the presence of slip dislocations at coherent interfaces (Fig. 9(k)). Atomic-resolution high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) images reveal a significant concentration of nanoscale slip dislocations and deformation features localized in regions adjacent to the coherent interface (Figs. 9(l)–9(n)). These microstructural observations provide direct evidence that stress accommodation mechanisms operate at the atomic scale near phase boundaries. The theoretical calculations [46] indicate that the β-to-α phase transformation depends on slip along the [* 1¯10] direction, suggesting that an analogous transformation can also occur along the equivalent direction [120] in the hexagonal crystal system (Fig. S6 in the ESM). Given the orthogonal orientation of [120] and [201], the abundant presence of parallel slip dislocations along the [201] direction can be reasonably explained by stress-induced β-*to-α phase transformation. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further confirm thermodynamic feasibility: under coherent interfaces, the energy barrier for β → α transformation (3.57 eV) is lower than that of the reverse α → β process (4.29 eV) [46], which thermodynamically facilitates the stress-driven phase transition under localized stress fields. Notably, the transformation-induced ~0.6% volumetric expansion creates compressive stresses that actively promote crack closure, as evidenced by TEM observations of crack termination at the coherent interface (Fig. 9(e)).

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, this study presents the synthesis of dense dual-phase α/β-Si3N4 ceramics with exceptional hardness and fracture toughness under HPHT conditions. In dual-phase covalently bonded ceramics, we demonstrate stress-induced transformation toughening, which provides new insights into the interface microstructural design for transformation toughening in covalently bonded ceramics. While we have observed the effects of stress-induced transformation toughening and microscopic plastic deformation in dual-phase α/β-Si3N4 ceramics, the high potential energy barrier between the two phases limits the efficacy of this toughening mechanism, thereby hindering further improvements in toughness. To address this limitation, future research may explore strategies to modify the stability of the coherent interface through approaches such as doping, allowing for more significant stress-induced transformation toughening.
