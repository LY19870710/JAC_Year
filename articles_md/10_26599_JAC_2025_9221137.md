## 1 Introduction

High-brightness laser-driven light sources, which are generated by exciting phosphor materials with high-power density blue laser diodes [1−13], hold great promise for applications in long-distance searches [5,6,14−17], high-luminance projection displays [18−25], and long-range night vision systems [26−30]. The luminance of these light sources is primarily influenced by two critical parameters: the maximum luminous flux and illuminated area (i.e., light spot area) [17,21,31−33]. Both factors are contingent on the properties of the phosphor materials used [7,8,34−36]. The maximum luminous flux is determined by the phosphor material’s luminous efficacy and its capacity to withstand the highest blue laser power density without reaching luminance saturation. This critical limit was defined as the luminance saturation threshold [14,21,37−39]. On the other hand, the blue laser light incident on the phosphor material tends to diffuse within the bulk, leading to an expansion of the light spot [40,41]. This increase is generally detrimental to the luminance emitted by the light sources.

Significant research efforts have been dedicated to enhancing the total internal reflection of light within the illuminated area by incorporating a secondary scattering medium with a refractive index, in contrast to phosphor materials, thereby controlling the expansion of light spots [32,33]. For instance, incorporating pores into YAG:Ce phosphor ceramics (PC) significantly reduces the light spot area by nearly 46%. However, this modification also compromises the luminance saturation threshold, leading to a significant decrease in the maximum luminous flux from 1772 to 855 lm. Owing to this trade-off, the maximum luminance achievable is 1710 lm·mm−2 [32]. Another example involves incorporating TiO2 into β-sialon:Eu phosphor films, resulting in an approximately 13% reduction in the light-spot area. This is accompanied by a notable decrease in luminous efficacy, which decreases from 101 to 81 lm·W−1. This modification results in a maximum luminance of 1210 lm·mm−2 [33]. To date, no effective strategy has been proposed to curtail light-spot expansion without compromising the maximum luminous flux.

On the other hand, when phosphor materials are excited within a straightforward transmissive optical configuration, the maximum luminous flux is inherently constrained owing to the low luminous efficacy or suboptimal luminance saturation threshold. Considerable research efforts have focused on optimizing luminous flux to its maximum potential [17,20,21,23]. For example, applying dielectric coatings to YAG:Ce phosphor films significantly boosts the luminous efficacy to 210 lm·W−1 and increases the luminance saturation threshold to 11.2 W·mm−2, yielding a peak luminous flux of 1839 lm [41]. The incorporation of boron nitride (BN) particles into (La,Y)3Si6N11:Ce phosphor films increases the luminance saturation threshold from 7.63 to 12.82 W·mm−2 and increases the maximum luminous flux to 740 lm [42]. Similarly, adding Al2O3 to YAG:Ce PC increases the luminance saturation threshold from 14.0 to 20.1 W·mm−2 and improves the luminous efficacy from 117 to 135 lm·W−1, achieving a maximum luminous flux of 1367 lm [43]. However, these maximum luminous fluxes remain below 2000 lm, and these studies have overlooked the constraint on light spot expansion, limiting the potential output luminance. In essence, there is an urgent need to engineer phosphor materials that both limit light-spot expansion and increase the maximum luminous flux, thereby increasing the luminance of light sources.

In this study, inspired by the architecture of optical fibers, we have engineered core–cladding phosphor ceramics (CCPC) of a YAG:Ce@Al2O3 wafer to confine the expansion of the light spot area and concurrently enhance the maximum luminous flux (Fig. 1). The difference in the refractive indices between YAG:Ce and Al2O3, coupled with the non-luminescent properties of Al2O3, ensures that the light spot is mainly confined to the core region. This allows for precise control of the light spot area by adjusting the core dimensions. In contrast, conventional PC permit the diffusion of both blue lasers and yellow light, resulting in an enlarged light-spot area. Furthermore, the Al2O3 cladding, with its superior thermal conductivity compared to that of YAG:Ce cladding, facilitates more efficient heat dissipation and elevates the luminance saturation threshold. Leveraging these dual advantages, the CCPC can withstand a blue laser power density of 30 W·mm−2, achieving a peak luminous flux of 2100 lm and an ultra-high luminance of 3900 lm·mm−2 in the transmissive optical mode. This research opens a novel avenue for the development of light sources with unprecedented luminance.

### 2.1 Ceramics preparation

The fabrication of the CCPC is delineated in Figs. S1 and S2 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). We meticulously measured the required powders of Y2O3 (99.99%, Baotou Rare Earths Research Institute, China), CeO2 (99.99%, Baotou Rare Earths Research Institute, China), and Al2O3 (99.99%, TAIMEI Chemical Co. Ltd., Japan) according to the stoichiometric formula Y2.994Ce0.006Al5O12 with an additional 20 wt% of Al2O3 incorporated. The core slurry was prepared by homogeneously blending the powders with a pre-mixed solution. This pre-mixed solution comprised 0.5 wt% tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) (99.99%, Aladdin, China), 0.25 wt% ammonium citrate (99.0%, Aladdin, China), 2.5 wt% ammonium acrylate (99.0%, Macklin, China), 0.3 wt% N,N-methylene bisacrylamide (99.0%, Aladdin, China), 1 wt% tetramethylammonium hydroxide aqueous solution (25 wt%, Aladdin, China), and 26 wt% deionized waters. The composition was optimized, as shown in Fig. S1 in the ESM. In contrast, the cladding slurry was prepared by uniformly mixing a pure Al2O3 powder with a pre-mixed solution. The core and cladding slurries were then transferred into separate 250 mL polyurethane ball-milling jars. Al2O3 grinding balls, each with a diameter of 5 mm, were added at a mass ratio of 1 : 13 relative to the powder. Anhydrous ethanol (99.99%, Aladdin, China), which served as the ball-milling medium, was introduced in a quantity equivalent to the mass of the powder mixture. The ball milling process was meticulously controlled at a speed of 340 r·min−1 for 24 h. Next, tetramethylethylenediamine was introduced into the slurry, which was then thoroughly stirred and subjected to vacuum degassing treatment. Ammonium persulfate (99.99%, Aladdin, China) was subsequently incorporated into the cladding slurry prior to gel casting molding. The green bodies of the cladding rings, with an outer diameter of 15 mm and inner diameters of 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 mm, were prepared. The core slurry was then injected into the core region. After curing, the green bodies of the CCPC were obtained, along with monolithic YAG:Ce green bodies, fabricated by gel-casting molding as a comparative benchmark. The green bodies were subsequently sintered at 2023 K for 5 h in a vacuum environment maintained at 10−3 Pa. After sintering, the ceramics underwent an annealing treatment in air at 1723 K for 10 h. Finally, the ceramics were polished to achieve a thickness of 0.5 mm and a diameter of 12 mm for subsequent characterization.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The bulk density of the ceramics was ascertained using Archimedes’ principle, which facilitated the calculation of the relative density. X-ray diffraction patterns were acquired utilizing an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) operated with Cu Kα radiation at a voltage of 40 kV and a current of 40 mA. The diffractometer was set to scan at a rate of 2 (°)∙min−1 with a step increment of 0.02°. Photographs of the ceramics and light source device were taken using a digital camera (EOS 700D, Canon, Japan). The total transmittance spectra of the PC were measured using an ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectrophotometer (UV-3600Plus, Shimadzu, Japan). The microstructure of the CCPC was examined under a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SU70, Hitachi, Japan). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images, and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy mappings were captured with a TEM (F200, JEOL, Japan). Prior to the TEM measurements, the CCPC samples were processed using a dual-beam focused ion beam system (JIB-4700F, JEOL, Japan). The thermal conductivities of the ceramics were measured with a laser internal thermal conductivity analyzer (LFA447, Netzsch, Germany). Temperature-dependent luminescence measurements were conducted using a custom-made system, comprising a 450 nm LED light source, a constant-current DC power supply (RXN-303D, Zhaoxin, China), a temperature control stage (THMS600E, Linkam Scientific Instruments, UK), and a charge-coupled device (CCD) spectrometer (USB2000+, Ocean Optics, USA). The samples were subjected to a temperature gradient from 300 to 575 K in increments of 25 K, at a heating rate of 50 K·min−1. The absorption and quantum efficiencies were determined using a custom sphere-spectroradiometer system, which included an excitation light source (50 mW single-mode blue laser diode with an emission wavelength (*λ*em) of 450 nm) and an integrating sphere (15 cm in diameter), interfaced with a CCD spectrometer (USB2000+, Ocean Optics, USA). The light-spot temperature was monitored with an infrared thermal imager (Tix580, Fluke, USA). The optical performance of the PC under high-power blue laser excitation was evaluated in transmissive optical mode using another sphere-spectroradiometer system. This system consists of a high-power blue laser source (LSR445CP-FC-48W, Lasever, China), an integrating sphere with a 30 cm diameter (Labsphere, USA), and a CCD spectrometer (HR4000, Ocean Optics, USA). The blue laser source was composed of 12 blue laser diodes, each emitting at 450 nm with a power of 4 W. The blue laser irradiation spot was refined to an approximate circular area with a diameter of 0.79 mm using two plano-convex lenses. The power of the blue laser light was quantified with a power meter (LP-3C, Physoe, China). The luminance distribution of the PC in the transmissive configuration was ascertained using an imaging colorimeter (ProMetric IC-PMI16, Radiant, USA). The as-fabricated laser-driven white-light source for industrial endoscopes comprised a blue laser diode, a thin flexible optical fiber bundle (Szlaser Co., Ltd., China), an aspherical lens (19-516, Edmund Optics Inc., USA), a camera (Depstech Co., Ltd., China), and a heat dissipation system. The optical fiber bundle, consisting of hundreds of fibers with a diameter of 30 μm, had an overall diameter of 2 mm, a length of 1000 mm, and a numerical aperture of 0.64.

## 3 Results and discussion

Inspired by the architecture of optical fibers, we designed a CCPC wafer by selecting YAG:Ce, known for its yellow emission, as the core material and non-luminescent Al2O3 as the cladding material. This choice was motivated by the refractive index difference between them (Δ*n* = 0.23), which is crucial for the desired optical properties. The core–cladding structure was realized using the gel-casting method, as depicted in Fig. 2(a) and Figs. S1 and S2 in the ESM. Throughout this process, we were able to freely modulate the core diameters down to 2.0, 1.5, and 1.0 mm, respectively. The fabrication began with the injection and curing of an Al2O3 slurry to create a cladding ring with an outer diameter of 15.0 mm, which was followed by the injection of a YAG:Ce slurry to occupy the core area. Notably, a modest amount of Al2O3 was incorporated into the YAG:Ce slurry. This addition was essential for mitigating the thermal expansion coefficient mismatch between the core and cladding, thereby preventing interface cracking during the high-temperature sintering process. The green bodies were then subjected to sintering at 2023 K for 5 h in a vacuum furnace. In parallel, monolithic YAG:Ce was fabricated to serve as a comparative sample. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the resulting ceramics were designated CCPC-1.0, CCPC-1.5, and CCPC-2.0, corresponding to core diameters of 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 mm, respectively. The monolithic sample was referred to as PC. Upon irradiation with blue laser light, both the CCPC and PC emitted bright white light, with the light emission from the CCPC confined to the core region. The ceramics exhibited high densification, with relative densities ranging from 98.8% to 99.4%, and their phase purity was confirmed by the XRD patterns presented in Fig. S3 in the ESM. The total transmittance spectra in Fig. S4 in the ESM reveal that the PC is nearly transparent, with a transmittance of 75%. In contrast, the core region of the CCPC, owing to the scattering effect of Al2O3, was translucent, with a transmittance of approximately 45%. The Al2O3 cladding region, with a transmittance of approximately 50%, demonstrated no absorption in the visible region, ensuring that it did not interfere with the luminescence process when excited by a blue laser. Taking CCPC-1.0 as a representative example, the interface between the core and cladding regions of the CCPC was meticulously characterized because of its significant influence on the optical and thermal processes during blue laser irradiation. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images depicted in Fig. 2(c) reveal a well-defined interface between the core and cladding of CCPC-1.0. The two regions were tightly bonded and devoid of porosity at the interface, as shown in the inset image. The TEM and HRTEM images presented in Figs. 2(d) and 2(e) provide compelling evidence of a sharp interface with thin and well-crystallized grain boundaries. The HRTEM images in Figs. 2(f) and 2(g) display distinct lattice fringes and well-ordered lattice arrangements. The interplanar spacing of 3.83 Å corresponds to the (012) plane of Al2O3, whereas the spacing of 5.74 Å is associated with the (211) plane of YAG:Ce. The clear grain boundary between YAG:Ce and Al2O3 was further substantiated by the elemental distribution, which showed no observable reactions, as illustrated in Fig. 2(h) and Fig. S5 in the ESM. The dense and clean core–cladding interface provides a solid foundation for excellent confinement of the light spot and efficient dissipation of heat.

The fundamental photoluminescent properties, including the excitation and emission spectra, quantum efficiency, and decay time, were assessed for both the CCPC and PC ceramics (Fig. S6 in the ESM). Then, the ability to confine light spots in the CCPC and PC under blue laser excitation was evaluated. Figure 3(a) illustrates the measurement process, where the light-spot diameters were determined using an imaging colorimeter within a transmissive optical setup. The setup captured the emitted light on the surface of the ceramics when excited by a blue laser at an emission wavelength of 450 nm and a power of 1.72 W, recording the spatial luminance distribution of the light spot. The luminance distribution profiles across the diameter of the ceramics were normalized to determine the light spot diameter, as depicted in Fig. 3(b). The light spot diameter is defined as the distance over which the luminance drops to 1/e2 of its peak value. The measured light spot diameters for the PC, CCPC-2.0, CCPC-1.5, and CCPC-1.0 were 1.92, 1.30, 1.00, and 0.82 mm, respectively, whereas the incident blue laser spot diameter was 0.79 mm. The calculated light spot expansion ratios for these ceramics were 2.38, 1.62, 1.25, and 1.04, respectively, as shown in Fig. 3(c). The CCPC effectively limited the expansion of the light-spot area, with the light-spot diameter decreasing as the core diameter diminished. Consequently, the peak luminance within the light spot increased owing to the concentration of emitted luminance, reaching values of 4.01×106, 5.38×106, 5.48×106, and 5.95×106 cd·mm−2 for PC, CCPC-2.0, CCPC-1.5, and CCPC-1.0, respectively, as indicated in Fig. 3(d). To further elucidate the mechanism behind the containment of light-spot expansion, the light emitted from various sides of the ceramics was measured upon blue-laser excitation at a power of 2.52 W, as depicted in Fig. S7 in the ESM. Utilizing the measured total emission power, along with the forward and side emission powers, we calculated the side emission ratios and forward emission ratios, which are presented in Figs. 3(e) and 3(f). The decrease in the side-emission ratio aligned with the reductions in the light-spot diameter and expansion ratio. Concurrently, the increase in the forward emission ratio corresponded to an increase in the peak luminance. Notably, CCPC-1.0 had a remarkably low side-emission ratio of 4.3% and a high forward-emission ratio of 95.7%. This suggests that both the incident blue laser and the converted yellow emission from YAG:Ce are predominantly confined to the core region, leading to a tightly confined light spot area.

CCPC-1.0 has demonstrated exceptional capability in light-spot confinement, prompting us to further evaluate its performance for high-luminance laser-driven white-light sources, with a PC serving as a comparative benchmark. The Al2O3 cladding in CCPC-1.0 enhances its thermal conductivity to approximately 36 W·m−1·K−1, which is significantly greater than that of PC (14.2 W·m−1·K−1). Notably, CCPC-1.0 maintains a thermal conductivity close to 20 W·m−1·K−1 even at an elevated temperature of 598 K, as shown in Fig. 4(a), indicating its efficient thermal dissipation capability during laser irradiation. Additionally, CCPC-1.0 exhibits remarkable resistance to thermal quenching, retaining approximately 83% of its initial luminescence intensity at temperatures up to 573 K, which is higher than that of PC, which is 71% under the same conditions, as depicted in Fig. 4(b). Furthermore, the quantum efficiency of CCPC-1.0 is 85%, surpassing that of PC (76%), as detailed in Table S1 in the ESM. This is likely due to the scattering effect of the Al2O3 present in the core of CCPC-1.0, which improves the light extraction efficiency [43]. Both ceramics exhibit excellent absorption efficiencies of 90%. When the ceramics are excited by a 6.42 W blue laser, their thermal dissipation capability is evaluated. The temperature distribution curve across the ceramic diameter was measured, as shown in Fig. 4(c). The maximum light-spot temperatures for CCPC-1.0 and PC were recorded at 373.2 and 416.8 K, respectively. The broader temperature distribution curve of CCPC-1.0 indicates more effective heat dissipation, whereas its higher quantum efficiency results in reduced heat generation. The combined effect leads to a lower light-spot temperature for CCPC-1.0 than for PC. Consequently, CCPC-1.0 has a superior luminance saturation threshold of 30 W·mm−2, which is higher than that of PC (20 W·mm−2). Moreover, owing to its superior quantum efficiency, the luminous efficacy of CCPC-1.0 (150 lm·W−1) is greater than that of PC (118 lm·W−1). As a result, CCPC-1.0 achieves a maximum luminous flux of 2100 lm, as shown in Fig. 4(d). The luminous efficacy of CCPC-1.0 gradually decreases with increasing incident blue laser power density, in contrast to the sharp decrease in PC, as plotted in Fig. 4(e). This is attributed to the greater heat generation and inferior thermal resistance of the PC compared with those of CCPC-1.0. With a small light-spot diameter of 0.82 mm, CCPC-1.0 delivers an ultra-high luminance of 3900 lm·mm−2 for the white-light source with chromatic coordinates located at (0.315, 0.335), characterized by a correlated color temperature of 6249 K. In contrast, the PC’s emitted luminance is only 400 lm·mm−2 because of its larger light-spot diameter and lower luminous flux. This results in a 9-fold improvement. To evaluate the luminance of the white light source reported, the maximum luminous flux was divided by the area of the incident laser spot. However, owing to light spot expansion, the area typically exceeds that of the laser spot. Consequently, the actual luminance of the white-light source is considerably lower than the calculated values presented in Table 1. For comparison, both the laser spot area and calculated luminance are illustrated in Fig. 4(f). In contrast, owing to its exceptional ability to confine the light spot, the area of the light spot is congruent with that of the laser spot for CCPC-1.0. As a result, the actual luminance reported in this study markedly surpasses the findings of the most recent research in the domain of laser-driven white-light sources.

To demonstrate this concept, we constructed a laser-driven white-light source for miniaturized industrial endoscopes using a blue laser, reflector, lens, PC, and optical fiber, as depicted in Fig. 5(a). The superior light confinement capability of CCPC-1.0 results in a more focused white light, which couples efficiently with the optical fiber. This setup emits high-brightness white light even with an incident blue laser power as low as 1.6 W. In comparison, the white light generated with the PC is divergent and less efficiently coupled into the optical fiber, resulting in a weak light output, as evident in Fig. 5(b). We assessed the illuminance of the white light emitted from the optical fiber with a 1.6 W incident blue laser power, with the measurement setup detailed in Fig. 5(c). Illuminance was measured along the x*-axis at a fixed angle of *θ* = 90°, varying the distance from the optical fiber outlet from 2 to 10 cm. At 2 cm, the illuminance with CCPC-1.0 was 75,710 lx, approximately 3.76 times greater than that with PC (20,097 lx), as shown in Fig. 5(d). Illuminance decreases with distance, but even at 10 cm, the illuminance from CCPC-1.0 (2556 lx) and PC (1008 lx) meets the endoscopic observation requirements of at least 1000 lx. Additionally, illuminance was measured at angles from *θ* = 0 to 180° at a fixed distance of 4 cm from the optical fiber outlet. As shown in Fig. 5(e), the illuminance distribution follows a parabolic curve pattern, with an angular range of 60°–120°, resulting in a dispersion angle of approximately 60°. The maximum illuminance with CCPC-1.0 reached 14,160 lx, which was significantly greater than that with PC (7001 lx). The high brightness and collimation of white light sources ensure extensive, shadow-free illumination of industrial endoscopes for structural health monitoring. Furthermore, we fabricated CCPC-1.0 and PC-based laser-driven white-light sources in a transmissive optical setup for long-range illumination, as shown in Fig. S8 in the ESM. The chromatic coordinates of the white-light sources, which are based on CCPC-1.0, shift along the blackbody radiation curve as the power of the blue laser increases from 5 to 10 W (as illustrated in Fig. S9 in the ESM). The light confinement ability of CCPC-1.0 is confirmed, as it can produce high-luminance white light illuminating up to 500 m with just a 10 W blue laser excitation. The core–cladding design approach significantly broadens the potential applications requiring high-luminance light sources.

## 4 Conclusions

This study presents a significant strategy to address the inherent trade-off between light spot confinement and luminous flux maximization in light sources through the design of CCPC wafers. The YAG:Ce@Al2O3 CCPC wafer design effectively confines the light spot to an area as small as 0.53 mm2 while achieving an ultra-high luminance of 3900 lm·mm−2. The high thermal conductivity of the Al2O3 cladding in the CCPC enables a luminance saturation of 30 W·mm−2 and a maximum luminous flux of 2100 lm for white light in a straightforward transmissive optical setup. Finally, the potential application of the CCPC in high-luminance light sources has been successfully demonstrated.
