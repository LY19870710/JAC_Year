## 1 Introduction

Transition metal carbides are recognized for their exceptional hardness, wear resistance, and thermal stability, making them ideal for demanding applications such as high-temperature equipment, corrosion-resistant pipelines, and friction components [1−3]. Their ability to withstand extreme conditions, including friction, corrosion, and thermal gradients, is particularly valuable in the chemical and power generation industries [4−10]. However, balancing hardness and toughness remains a major challenge. While hardness ensures resistance to wear and deformation under stress, insufficient toughness can lead to cracking under dynamic or high-temperature conditions, significantly reducing the service life. Addressing this trade-off is pivotal for advancing ceramic materials.

Spinodal decomposition has shown great potential for improving the mechanical properties of ceramics through its unique phase separation mechanism [11−13]. This process drives a homogeneous system to spontaneously separate into distinct phases under specific compositional or thermal conditions, forming nanoscale nodular structures that evolve into alternating nanoscale phases [14]. Such transformations increase both hardness and toughness, providing a promising pathway for designing high-performance materials [15−20]. In our previous study of the (Ti,Zr)C system [21], we investigated how controlled spinodal decomposition influenced mechanical properties and identified compositional trends linked to these improvements. Notably, dislocations observed in spinodal decomposition structures have been shown to increase the hardness and toughness, which is consistent with the Taylor hardening model [22]. In ceramic systems, dislocation multiplication is difficult to introduce, and the deliberate formation of stacking faults generates nucleation sites that facilitate dislocation proliferation and enhance mechanical properties [8,23]. However, the high stacking fault energy of carbide ceramics limits the ability of spinodal decomposition-derived phase interfaces to pin and store dislocations, thereby hindering the full enhancement of hardness and toughness [2,24−30].

To overcome the constraints of high stacking fault energy on dislocation mobility and storage capacity, researchers have proposed various strategies for reducing stacking fault energy. For example, in certain metallic or ceramic systems, doping with small atoms (e.g., Si [31], B [32]) can introduce lattice distortion fields to effectively lower the stacking fault energy. Alternatively, substitutional alloying can modulate stacking fault energy by altering electronic structures and bonding characteristics, thereby modifying slip system responses [33]. As demonstrated by Li *et al.* [34], Cr, V, or W additions reduce the stacking fault energy in TiC, whereas Ta or Zr increases it. However, these methods often rely on complex heat treatment parameters and elemental interactions, complicating the prediction of interfacial structures and energy distributions [35−38]. They may also induce compositional inhomogeneity, secondary phase precipitation, and other defects [39]. Thus, a strategy that significantly reduces the stacking fault energy while offering process simplicity and compositional controllability is critically needed.

To address this gap, we focus on tailoring anion sublattices. Guided by first-principles calculations, we reveal that incorporating N substantially reduces the stacking fault energy. Capitalizing on its excellent miscibility with carbon, this approach minimizes complex interactions and avoids compositional fluctuations or secondary phases. Using the Ti–Zr–C system as a model, we present a novel strategy for lowering the stacking fault energy in spinodal decomposing ceramics. This method exploits N-induced stacking fault energy reduction to liberate dislocations, synergistically coupling dislocation activity with spinodal decomposition to overcome the strength‒toughness trade-off. In this work, (i) under the guidance of first-principles calculations and phase diagram thermodynamics, composite carbide ceramic materials with different N contents are prepared using the SPS method; (ii) the evolution of the spinodal decomposition microstructure under controlled aging temperatures and time is characterized, and the stacking fault–dislocation evolution、mechanism during spinodal decomposition is revealed through high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM); (iii) the hardness and toughness of the samples at different temperatures and aging time are measured, and the structure‒property relationship between the microstructure and properties is established.

### 2.1 Materials preparation

In this study, composite carbide ceramics with varying nitrogen contents were prepared via a one-step sintering method, with three samples produced in various compositions: (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C0.75,N0.25), (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C0.5,N0.5), and (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C0.25,N0.75). The starting materials included titanium carbide (TiC, 300–500 nm, 99%, Macklin Co., Ltd.), zirconium carbide (ZrC, 400 nm–1 μm, 99.9%, Macklin Co., Ltd.), titanium nitride (TiN, 2–10 μm, 99%, Macklin Co., Ltd.), and zirconium nitride (ZrN, ~400 mesh, 99%, Macklin Co., Ltd.). The raw powders of TiC, ZrC, TiN, and ZrN were weighed according to specific proportions and mixed thoroughly. After being weighed, the powders were combined with tungsten carbide milling balls and ethanol as the milling medium in a steel container. Milling was performed at 400 r/min for 20 h to achieve uniform mixing. The resulting powder was then vacuum-dried to completely remove ethanol. The mixed powders were placed in a graphite mold with a diameter of 15 mm and sintered in a spark plasma sintering furnace (SPS-332HF, Fuji Electronics) at 2200 °C under an argon atmosphere, with a heating rate of 100 °C/min and an applied pressure of 30 MPa for 10 min to produce dense, single-phase composite carbide ceramics with varying N contents. After sintering, the samples were furnace-cooled to room temperature. Following sintering, the samples were sectioned into small pieces and subjected to aging treatments in a tube furnace (Keijing GSL-1750X) under an argon atmosphere with a heating rate of 5 °C/min at varying temperatures (1200, 1300, 1400, 1500, and 1600 °C) for durations of 5, 10, or 20 h. After the aging treatment, all the samples were furnace-cooled to room temperature. The mechanical properties and microstructural characteristics of the samples were systematically evaluated across different aging conditions to assess changes in mechanical performance and microstructure.

### 2.2 Materials characterization

Phase identification was carried out using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Rigaku SmartLab) with a scanning rate of 10 (°)/min. Microstructural observations and compositional analyses were performed with a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-7800F, JEOL) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS; Oxford XMax 80). Additional high-resolution microstructural observations were obtained via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; ThermoFisher Talos F200X G2). Vickers hardness (*H*V) and fracture toughness (*K*IC) measurements were conducted at room temperature using a Vickers hardness tester (HV-30A, Laizhou Huayu Zhongxin Testing Instrument Co., Ltd.) with a load of 49 N and dwell time of 15 s. The fracture toughness (*K*IC) was calculated based on Eq. (1) [40]:

(1) KIC=0.016×(EHV)12×Pc32

where  E is the Young’s modulus;  HV is the hardness value;  P is the applied load;  c represents the half-length of cracks formed at the indentation corners. The modulus  E was obtained from first-principles calculations.

### 3.1 Phase diagram calculations

Based on thermodynamic calculations from the CSUTDCC1 database [41], a phase diagram was constructed via the calculation of phase diagrams (CALPHAD) approach [42]. During this process, the thermodynamic parameters of the system were precisely fitted using the Thermo-Calc software [43], resulting in the generation of phase diagrams under various temperature and composition conditions.

### 3.2 <i>Ab initio</i> calculations

The calculations were performed within the spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT) framework, utilizing the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) [44]. The interaction between valence electrons and ionic cores is expanded by the projector augmented wave (PAW) [45], and the exchange-correlation energy is described by the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burk–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional [46]. The partially ordered and disordered models were expressed by the unit cell and special quasirandom structure (SQS) models, respectively. The SQS models were constructed using the gensqs code implemented in the Alloy Theoretic Automation Toolkit (ATAT) package [47−49]. Detailed methods for constructing the SQS models and practical computational examples have been reported in the ATAT user guide and in previous studies [50,51]. The kinetic energy cut-off for the basis set was set to 520 eV. For the first irreducible Brillouin zone (BZ) integration, the Gamma scheme was used with a *k*-point mesh of 3 × 3 × 3 for all the structures. All the atom positions and cell shapes were allowed to relax until the Hellmann–Fynman forces were less than 1.0×10−5 eV/Å.

### 4.1 Stacking fault energy engineering

To study the phenomenon of slip in the material, interplanar slip was examined in three samples with varying nitrogen contents—(Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C0.75,N0.25), (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C0.5,N0.5), and (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C0.25,N0.75). Slip in crystalline solids typically occurs on close-packed planes, where the shear energy required for dislocation motion is minimized, making these planes energetically favorable for plastic deformation. Accordingly, shear-induced stacking faults form along the [111] planes, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a) for the composite carbides [21,52]. The energy (*γ*SFE) required for slip can be evaluated using Eq. (2):

*
Fig. 1First-principles calculations of slip energy for (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C1−x*,N*x*). (a) Schematic diagram of supercell slip. (b) Energy curve calculated via first-principles calculations.

(2) γSFE=1A(Eu−E0)

where *A* represents the area of the stacking fault, while  Eu and  E0 denote the total energy of supercells with and without shear displacement, respectively. The calculated energy profiles (Fig. 1(b)) reveal that the unstable stacking fault energies (USFEs) of (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C0.75,N0.25), (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C0.5,N0.5), and (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C0.25,N0.75) are 920.45, 744.82, and 502.32 mJ/m2, respectively. Each value represents a substantial reduction relative to the USFE of pure (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)C (1092 mJ/m2) and decreases monotonically with increasing N content. The USFE corresponds to the peak of the generalized stacking fault energy (GSFE) curve and represents the maximum energy barrier that must be overcome to initiate slip in a perfect crystal lattice. This trend demonstrates that N incorporation effectively lowers the slip‐energy barrier in the duplex carbide matrix, thereby mitigating the locking effect of stacking faults on dislocation motion. Consequently, during spinodal decomposition, interfacial dislocation pinning is alleviated, facilitating enhanced dislocation glide and multiplication. This liberated dislocation activity is expected to contribute significantly to simultaneous improvements in both hardness and fracture toughness.

### 4.2 Phase diagram

Phase equilibria in the (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C1−*x*,N*x*) system were delineated via CALPHAD-type thermodynamic modeling, yielding the phase diagram shown in Fig. 2. This diagram provides a robust theoretical framework for the precise control of composition and heat-treatment temperature during subsequent aging experiments. The phase diagram clearly shows a miscibility gap [11,12], indicating a thermodynamic driving force for spontaneous spinodal decomposition over a broad compositional range. Notably, the width of this gap diminishes progressively with increasing N content. Consequently, isothermal aging treatments were prescribed at 1200, 1300, 1400, 1500, and 1600 °C (blue symbols in Fig. 2) with various dwell time, enabling a systematic investigation of temperature-dependent microstructural evolution during spinodal decomposition. The equiatomic (Ti0.5, Zr0.5) composition was selected as the base system because it is located near the center of the miscibility gap (Fig. 2), thus providing the maximum thermodynamic driving force for the spinodal decomposition investigated in this study. Furthermore, nitrogen contents of *x* = 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75 were chosen to constitute a systematic research sequence, enabling a clear investigation of the relationship between the nitrogen content and the resulting microstructure and mechanical properties.

### 4.3 Phase composition

The XRD patterns of the synthesized samples are displayed in Fig. 3. The presence of well-defined diffraction peaks indicates a high degree of crystallinity in the prepared materials [53]. XRD analysis confirmed that all the samples exhibited a single-phase structure [54], with no impurity peaks detected. Additionally, with a fixed (Ti, Zr) ratio, increasing the C content shifts the diffraction peaks to lower angles. This phenomenon is due to the larger atomic radius of C than that of N. According to Bragg’s law [55],  nλ=2dsinθ, where  n is the diffraction order;  λ is the X-ray wavelength;  d is the interplanar spacing;  θ is the diffraction angle. The diffraction peak position is directly influenced by  d; thus, a higher C content induces lattice expansion, increasing the lattice parameter  d and thereby decreasing  θ. This trend is observed in the XRD patterns, manifesting as a systematic shift of peaks toward lower angles.

### 4.4 Microstructure evolution

In this study, the microstructural evolution of composite carbonitrides was systematically investigated across various aging temperatures and time to assess the effects of these parameters on phase transformation. The initial microstructure of the as-sintered sample prior to any aging treatment is shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The initial sample is dense and uniform, with no evidence of spinodal decomposition. Furthermore, electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analysis of the as-sintered samples (Fig. S2 in the ESM) confirms that the three different compositions possess comparable initial grain sizes and distributions, which validates the isolation of the nitrogen content as the core experimental variable. As illustrated in Figs. 4−6, the samples exhibited notable differences in morphology and microstructure under different aging conditions. Despite each sample’s unique morphology, they all followed a broadly similar phase evolution trend. Notably, the microstructures presented here are the final states after furnace cooling. However, as microstructural evolution is dominated by atomic diffusion, which is exponentially dependent on temperature, the core features observed are considered representative of the primary high-temperature aging stage. At 1200 °C, after 5 h of aging, fine white precipitates began to form in the lattice, indicating the onset of phase separation. With prolonged aging, the number of these precipitates increased, suggesting continued phase progression. To verify the phase compositions, the material was subjected to detailed characterization. The line scan data, presented in Fig. S3 in the ESM, confirm that the bright white precipitates are the Zr-rich phase, whereas the gray phase is Ti-rich. Concurrently, stripe-like structures emerged along the grain boundaries in some samples, suggesting early microstructural rearrangements. These features became more pronounced with extended aging, reflecting ongoing phase separation and growth. At 1300 °C, the microstructural changes were more significant. Within 5 h, nodular structures with alternating light and dark contrasts appeared along the grain boundaries, potentially indicating distinct phase separation stages with noticeable compositional contrasts. These nodules extended into the grains with aging for up to 10–20 h, suggesting an increased driving force for phase separation throughout the grain structure. These nodular features, which indicate spontaneous decomposition, resemble phenomena common in metals and alloys [13,56,57].

At 1400 °C, the phase transformation accelerated notably, with the nodular structures at the grain boundaries increasing in number and forming elongated patterns. The layered structures became thicker, indicating advanced phase separation and structural reorganization. Over time, these features extended more broadly into the grain matrix, reflecting significant phase boundary evolution. At 1500 °C, the nodular characteristics expanded substantially after only 5 h, suggesting a rapid phase transformation process at high temperatures. Nodular structures grew more pronounced, and the layered regions thickened further into the grains, reflecting a strong driving force for phase separation due to increased diffusivity at elevated temperatures. At 1600 °C, the microstructural evolution reached an advanced stage. Within 5 h, a distinct nodular pattern developed across large areas with complex arrangements, characteristic of spontaneous decomposition and indicating continuous changes within the material. With extended aging, these patterns intensified, and the layered regions thickened, demonstrating significant phase evolution under these conditions. Although all the samples followed similar phase evolution trends, their spinodal decomposition structures differed slightly. As illustrated in Figs. 4−6, owing to the heavier atomic weight of Zr relative to Ti, the dark regions indicate Ti-rich regions, whereas the white regions are Zr-rich.

To elucidate the effect of nitrogen concentration on microstructural evolution, lamellar thickness was systematically measured for samples with varying N contents. Figure 7 shows the evolution of lamellar thickness for the three compositions as a function of aging temperature and duration. The results indicate that lamellae thicken markedly with increasing aging temperature and time and, under identical heat‐treatment conditions, also become progressively thicker with increasing nitrogen content. After aging at 1600 °C for 15 h, the N* = 0.75 sample attained a lamellar thickness of approximately 1700 nm. Under the same conditions, this value is 2.8 and 1.6 times greater than those measured for the *N* = 0.25 and *N* = 0.50 samples, respectively.

To investigate the effect of increasing N content on lamellar thickness during spinodal decomposition, first-principle calculations were employed to quantify the elastic strain energy at the interfaces between the two decomposed phases at various temperatures. As Cahn [58] demonstrated, elastic properties play a critical role in the microstructural evolution of spinodal decomposition, and phase-field simulations by Seol et al.* [59] further revealed that the resultant microstructure was strongly influenced by the elastic anisotropy of a material. Figure 8 displays the elastic strain energy at the phase boundaries as a function of temperature, with each point representing the energy difference across an interface formed by spinodal decomposition. These values correspond to the difference in the elastic modulus between the two phases [60]. The data for these elastic moduli were obtained from previous first-principles calculations and are detailed in Fig. S1 in the ESM. The data reveal that, at a given temperature, the interfacial elastic strain energy increases with increasing N content. An elevated elastic strain energy indicates more severe lattice misfit between the rich and poor phases; when nascent lamellae are too fine, local regions must accommodate disproportionately high strain energy, impeding their growth at early stages. To minimize the total free energy, the system therefore preferentially amplifies composition fluctuations of longer wavelengths, ultimately yielding coarser lamellar structures. These results confirm that increasing the *N* content enhances the interfacial elastic strain energy and promotes an increase in the spinodal lamellar thickness.

### 4.5 Stacking fault–dislocation evolution

Samples with distinct stacking fault energies were successfully synthesized via integrated theoretical design and experimental procedures. To assess their role within the spinodal decomposition system, selected samples were characterized by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) after various aging treatments. For the early-stage sample of (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C0.75,N0.25) aged at 1200 °C for 10 h, TEM was employed to analyze the microstructure. As shown in Fig. 9(a), precipitation occurred at the grain boundaries, while no significant spinodal decomposition structure was observed within the grain interiors, indicating that decomposition initiated at the grain boundaries. This observation is consistent with previous findings. EDS analysis of the precipitated phases revealed the formation of Ti-rich and Zr-rich regions, distinguishable by their contrasting gray and white appearances, respectively. To further clarify the phase composition, EDS results are presented alongside Fig. 9(a), with white regions identified as Zr-rich phases and gray regions as Ti-rich phases. Detailed analysis of the spinodal decomposition region revealed a characteristic stacking fault morphology in the Ti-rich zones (Fig. 9(b)). These observations demonstrate that, in composite carbide ceramics, reducing the stacking fault energy coupled with subsequent aging treatments can effectively induce stacking fault formation. This behavior differs significantly from what we previously reported for (Ti,Zr)C systems [21].

The sample morphology, as depicted in Fig. 10(h), shows a high density of dislocations in both the Ti-rich and Zr-rich regions. Under the driving force of spinodal decomposition, the early stacking fault structures engineered by tuning the stacking fault energy (Fig. 9(b)) progressively deteriorate, with stress concentration zones developing at the fault termini and evolving into dislocation nucleation sites [23]. Subsequently, driven by ongoing compositional fluctuations and internal stress fields, these nucleation sites continuously emit and propagate new dislocations, which ultimately interweave into an interconnected dislocation network within the crystal interior (Fig. 10(h)) [70]. This sequence not only elucidates the evolution mechanism from stacking faults to dislocations but also validates the feasibility of microstructural design by stacking fault energy regulation [71].

For a more detailed investigation of dislocation behavior, a specific dislocation region was selected (Fig. 10(i)), and high-resolution imaging reveals atomic column misalignment indicative of dislocation mismatch. Through IFFT analysis of this region, numerous dislocation pinning effects are observed. According to the Taylor hardening model [72], the presence of dislocations contributes to increased material hardness. Lattice mismatch between the two phases induces an internal stress distribution and notably elastic strain energy [22], which effectively impedes dislocation motion, thereby increasing the macroscopic strength [73]. Additionally, the high coherence of internal stresses restricts dislocation movement without causing stress concentration, enabling dislocations to remain within the material and form dislocation networks. This naturally occurring dislocation network limits slip and grain growth, thereby contributing to improved hardness and toughness [74].

In summary, spinodal decomposition enhances both hardness and toughness by introducing stacking faults and promoting dislocation multiplication. Stacking faults arise from lattice mismatch stresses induced by the periodic phase structure of spinodal decomposition. These faults generate an additional stress field within the material, inhibiting dislocation slip and thereby improving hardness [75]. Furthermore, stacking faults create additional obstacles to dislocation motion (Fig. 9(g)), reducing slip activity and enhancing the resistance of the material to deformation, which improves both the hardness and toughness. Simultaneously, the local stress field generated by spinodal decomposition facilitates dislocation multiplication. At the Ti-rich and Zr-rich phase interfaces, the resulting dislocation network structure can disperse stress, further enhancing the strength and toughness of the material [76]. In conclusion, the microstructural features induced by spinodal decomposition, such as stacking faults and dislocation multiplication, effectively reinforce the mechanical properties of the material, offering a dual mechanism for improving hardness and toughness.

With increasing aging temperature, the KAMave values exhibit a non-monotonic trend—rising initially and then declining—indicating that the dislocation density increases during the early stages of spinodal decomposition but diminishes as the phase-separation reaction approaches completion. This behavior demonstrates that thermal activation can promote the conversion of stacking faults into dislocation sources, thereby generating a substantially higher dislocation density than that observed in our previous studies [24]. Although absolute values of the Burgers vector (B-values) differ among samples, intra-sample comparisons remain qualitatively valid. In the initial decomposition regime, abundant dislocation multiplication occurs; as the decomposition reaction nears equilibrium, the rate of multiplication decreases. According to the Taylor hardening model, elevated dislocation densities contribute directly to enhancements in both hardness and toughness [72]. Thus, by tailoring the stacking-fault energy within a spinodal decomposition framework, one can induce prolific dislocation source formation, achieve markedly increased dislocation densities, and thereby significantly improve the mechanical performance of ceramic materials [77].

In contrast to the conventional grain coarsening typically observed in ceramics at high temperatures, the matrix grain size in this system exhibits a non-monotonic evolution during aging, which has a synergistic effect on the mechanical properties. As shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM, noticeable grain refinement is observed from the initial stage to the peak-aged stage. This finding is consistent with our previous research [24] and is attributed primarily to the high internal stress from spinodal decomposition, which induces the formation of new sub-grain boundaries. This grain refinement further promotes the enhancement of both hardness and toughness via the Hall‒Petch effect. In the over-aged stage, however, a “rebound” coarsening trend is observed. This is because the coarsening of the lamellar structure weakens its pinning effect on the grain boundaries, while the high-temperature environment promotes the recovery and annihilation of dislocations (Fig. 11), together lowering the resistance to grain boundary migration. This grain coarsening, along with the evolution of other microstructural features, is one of the causes for the degradation of the material’s properties.

### 4.6 Performance evaluation

Figure 12 shows the dependence of the hardness and fracture toughness of the fabricated (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C1−*x*N*x*) samples on the aging temperature and time. During the 1200 °C aging treatment, all three compositions—(Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C0.75,N0.25), (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C0.5,N0.5) and (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)(C0.25,N0.75)—exhibit slight hardness increases of approximately 0.3% after 5 h. With prolonged aging, the hardness continues to increase, reaching increases of 13.2%, 19.7%, and 21.5% for the three samples, respectively. Upon increasing the aging temperature to 1300 °C, the hardness continues to increase more markedly. After 20 h, the hardness gains for the three compositions reach 33.7%, 37.0%, and 39.7%, respectively, relative to their initial values. However, at 1400 °C, the hardness evolution follows a “rise-then-fall” pattern: The hardness increases at 5 and 10 h, reaching a maximum at 10 h (48.1%, 52.8%, and 56.6% for the three samples), but it decreases by 20 h. At 1500 °C, a similar trend is observed, with the hardness at 5 h being slightly higher than that at 1400 °C but still below the maximum hardness reached after 10 h at 1400 °C. At 1600 °C, the hardness again peaks at 5 h (with maximum increases of 24.2%, 26.5%, and 22.8%), followed by a decline at longer aging time. Thus, in the 1200–1300 °C range, the hardness steadily increased with time and temperature, whereas in the 1400–1600 °C domain, a pronounced “rise-then-fall” behavior dominated. This finding underscores the critical influence of both the aging temperature and duration on the hardness of a material.

Notably, the hardness and toughness display a degree of synergistic enhancement: at 1200–1300 °C, both properties increase with aging time, whereas at 1400–1600 °C, they both follow the characteristic “rise-then-fall” evolution. This finding indicates that, by selecting appropriate aging temperatures and durations, simultaneous improvements in hardness and fracture toughness can be achieved. Nevertheless, in most materials, these two properties are mutually constrained [78]. From a service-performance perspective, these composite carbides have the potential for progressive mechanical stabilization and enhancement under suitably tailored high-temperature aging treatments.

The “rise-then-fall” evolution of mechanical properties can be understood by considering the interplay between strengthening and coarsening mechanisms. The initial increase in both hardness and toughness is attributed to the formation of a dense spinodal nanostructure. The resulting semi-coherent interfaces act as effective barriers to dislocation motion, while the low SFE environment promotes dislocation multiplication, leading to a synergistic increase in both properties. However, prolonged aging at higher temperatures (≥ 1400 °C) induces an over-aging effect, where the spinodal lamellae coarsen, as quantitatively shown in Fig. 7. This coarsening reduces the density of strengthening interfaces, diminishing their ability to pin dislocations and thus leading to a decline in both hardness and toughness from their peak values.

Furthermore, a comparison of the compositions reveals that the nitrogen content influences the kinetics of this evolution. As indicated by our analysis, a higher nitrogen content increases the interfacial elastic strain energy, which accelerates the lamellar coarsening process. Consequently, samples with higher nitrogen contents (e.g., N* = 0.75) are prone to enter the over-aged state more rapidly, resulting in a narrower optimal processing window. This explains the observed differences in peak performance and provides deeper insight into the design of these materials by balancing the dual roles of nitrogen in reducing SFE and influencing coarsening kinetics.

The simultaneous increase in hardness and toughness is a complex synergistic effect, and the three key microstructural elements—interface effects, dislocation multiplication, and lamellar thickness—all contribute to the resistance of a material to both deformation and fracture. Specifically, the high density of phase interfaces formed by spinodal decomposition provides hardness by impeding dislocation motion and enhances toughness by forcing crack deflection and bifurcation. The high-density dislocation network, induced by the low SFE, not only contributes to hardness via Taylor hardening but is also the fundamental source of toughness by dissipating energy through plastic deformation at the crack tip; indeed, a clear positive correlation between dislocation density and both hardness and toughness is observed (Fig. S4 in the ESM). The lamellar thickness, in turn, is the critical scaling parameter that modulates these mechanisms to achieve optimal balance. An optimal thickness (~200–300 nm) ensures a sufficiently high interface density to effectively pin dislocations and deflect cracks while also providing the necessary space to accommodate dislocation plasticity. This complex synergy, balanced by the lamellar thickness, is directly visualized in our supplementary analysis (Fig. S5 in the ESM). This figure clearly demonstrates that both the hardness and toughness reach a simultaneous peak at this optimal microstructural length scale, confirming that our design strategy successfully balances these multiscale strengthening and toughening mechanisms before degradation occurs due to over-aging.

To intuitively reveal the intrinsic relationship between microstructural evolution and mechanical properties, the material's mechanical properties were correlated with the corresponding lamellar thickness, as shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM. The figure clearly shows that both the hardness and toughness exhibit a non-monotonic relationship with the lamella thickness, where the properties first increase with increasing thickness, reach a peak, and then subsequently decrease. This trend indicates that an optimal lamella thickness range (~200–300 nm) exists where the hardness and toughness of a material can simultaneously reach their peak values. When the thickness is below this range, aging is insufficient, whereas when the thickness is greater, excessive coarsening of the lamellae leads to a decrease in the interface density per unit volume, weakening both the strengthening and toughening effects and thereby causing performance degradation. Therefore, this direct relationship between lamellar thickness and performance provides the most direct evidence for the core strategy of achieving a synergistic increase in hardness and toughness by controlling the microstructural length scale.

To elucidate the unique synergistic effect of carbon and nitrogen in this study, a comparison was made between the (Ti,Zr)(C,N) system and its two end-member systems: the pure carbide (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)C from our previous work [21] and the pure nitride (Ti0.5,Zr0.5)N, which was supplementarily investigated in this study (Sections S1 and S2 and Figs. S7 and S8 in the ESM for details). Although both end-member systems can achieve a certain degree of performance enhancement through spinodal decomposition (the pure nitride system reaches a peak fracture toughness of 3.46 MPa·m1/2), the N-doped carbonitride systems investigated in the main text exhibit superior overall mechanical properties, particularly in terms of fracture toughness (Fig. 12 in the ESM). This comparison strongly confirms that tuning the stacking fault energy by doping nitrogen into the carbide matrix is a more effective strategy for achieving a synergistic increase in hardness and toughness than relying on the mechanisms present in either pure carbide or pure nitride systems alone.

Figure 13 compares the mechanical properties of the materials synthesized in this study with those of conventional ceramics and high-entropy carbide ceramics [79−88]. The results demonstrate that after specific aging treatments, both the hardness and toughness are significantly enhanced. Compared with other high-entropy carbides, this material achieves a substantial increase in toughness while maintaining high hardness. Although its initial mechanical properties are not particularly outstanding, a simple aging treatment under controlled temperature and time conditions enables it to exhibit superior performance within the high-entropy carbide system.

## 5 Conclusions

Transition metal carbides have attracted considerable attention for their outstanding mechanical properties but remain constrained by an intrinsic hardness–toughness trade-off. While spinodal decomposition-mediated phase separation via high-temperature aging has proven effective in enhancing carbide ceramics, their inherently high stacking fault energies limit dislocation pinning at phase boundaries and thus curb further improvements in hardness and toughness. In this study, guided by first-principles calculations, we show that systematic nitrogen doping in (Ti,Zr)C lowers its stacking fault energy and, under precisely controlled aging conditions, induces pronounced intragranular stacking faults that progressively evolve into dislocation sources, significantly promoting dislocation multiplication. Mechanical testing indicates that, at 25% nitrogen and optimized aging parameters, the hardness and fracture toughness increase by approximately 40% and 50%, respectively, effectively overcoming the conventional hardness–toughness limitation; conversely, excessive nitrogen incorporation leads to elastic strain energy accumulation and lamellar coarsening, which attenuate these gains. These findings elucidate the pivotal role of stacking fault energy reduction in directing microstructural evolution during spinodal decomposition and provide a viable compositional and thermal processing strategy for designing next-generation carbide ceramics with superior strength, toughness, and reliability.
