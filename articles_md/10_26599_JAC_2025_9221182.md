## 1 Introduction

Aluminum oxynitride (AlON) is an advanced transparent ceramic material with excellent optical transparency, high strength and hardness, and outstanding thermal stability, making it an ideal candidate for next-generation transparent armor systems and high-performance infrared windows in extreme environments [1−4]. AlON ceramics are commonly fabricated by consolidating AlON powder via various sintering techniques, such as pressureless sintering (also known as conventional sintering, CS) [5−7], hot pressing (HP) [8,9], and spark plasma sintering (SPS) [10−12]. To increase the densification and optical quality of AlON ceramics, hot isostatic pressing (HIP) treatments are widely applied to pressureless and spark plasma presintered samples [13−16]. Among these methods, pressureless sintering, which does not rely on HIP, is the most favored method because of its low cost and practical feasibility. Specifically, pressureless sintering has an additional significant advantage in the fabrication of large-scale and/or complex-shaped components.

In the last two decades, considerable efforts have been devoted to fabricating transparent AlON ceramics by pressureless sintering, including the preparation of pure AlON powder through various routes [17−19], the optimization of particle size and distribution (PSD) [20−22], the selection of sintering additives [23−26], and improvements in forming [5,27−31] and sintering processes [32−34], etc. Although AlON ceramics with high transmittance (≥ 80%) have been successfully prepared via pressureless sintering, holding time of 7–30 h is still generally required to fabricate AlON ceramics with high optical transparency across the visible to infrared range [21,25,26,33]. This undoubtedly increases the energy consumption, cost, and risk in the fabrication of transparent AlON ceramics. Additionally, long holding durations at high temperatures usually result in massive grain growth, leading to the average grain size of AlON reaching 164–189 µm. This may not be conducive to achieving the high mechanical properties expected from AlON products [35−38].

Increasing the holding time at high temperatures typically leads to grain growth in ceramic materials. Thus, reducing the holding time is an effective strategy to inhibit grain growth. Recently, by improving the synthesis method of AlON powder [6,39], optimizing the ball-milling process to reduce the particle size [40], doping appropriate sintering additives to increase the densification ability [41−43], and applying a novel sintering schedule [44], the holding time for pressureless sintering of transparent AlON ceramics has been reduced to 2.5–6 h. Using AlON powder synthesized via improved carbothermal reduction and nitridation (CRN) and direct nitridation (DN) methods [6,39], transparent AlON ceramics with transmittances of 82.5% at 400 nm and 83% at 600 nm were prepared by pressureless sintering for 6 h at 1880 and 1910 °C, respectively. As a result, the grain sizes of the fabricated AlON ceramics were reduced to ~100 µm or slightly larger. Using fine and uniform AlON powder with an average particle size below 300 nm obtained by ball milling at 225 r/min for 45 h, highly transparent AlON ceramics with a transmittance of 84% at 2000 nm and grain sizes of 10–40 µm were successfully prepared through pressureless sintering at 1880 °C for 6 h [40]. Zhang *et al*. [44] prepared AlON ceramics by a low- and high-temperature two-step pressureless sintering method, i.e., holding at 1750 and 1850 °C for 3 h, respectively. The transmittance of the prepared AlON ceramics reached 85.4% at 2000 nm, with a mean grain size of 61.2 μm.

Another way to accelerate the densification process and shorten the holding time in the fabrication of transparent AlON ceramics is to control the microstructure evolution by applying composite sintering additives or optimizing the distribution of additives in the sample. By doping 0.15 wt% Y2O3 and 0.60 wt% MgO [41] or 0.03 wt% La2O3 and 0.04 wt% Y2O3 [42] into AlON powders, AlON ceramics with maximum transmittances of ~82% at 3600 nm and 76%–78% at 400 nm were quickly fabricated via pressureless sintering at 1880 °C for 2.5 h. Compared with microsized lamellar Y2O3, nanosized granular Y2O3 as a sintering additive can effectively promote the densification process of AlON [45]. As a result, highly transparent AlON ceramics with a transmittance of ≥ 80% across wavelengths ranging from 380 to 4150 nm were successfully prepared by holding for 2.5 h. However, because Y can accelerate grain growth by enhancing the mobility of the grain boundaries in the fabrication of AlON [23,26], the average grain size of the fabricated highly transparent AlON still reaches 91.6 μm. Therefore, given the widespread use and beneficial effects of Y2O3 in the fabrication of transparent AlON ceramics [26−29,32], it is necessary to explore new techniques and processes to assist pressureless sintering in resolving the contradiction between restraining grain growth and achieving high density when Y2O3 is doped as a sintering additive.

In 2020, Wang *et al*. [46] developed an ultra-fast high-temperature sintering (UHS) process for preparing ceramic materials by radiative heating under an inert atmosphere. In the UHS technique, the ceramic pellet is placed between two Joule-heating carbon felts, and the desired sintering temperature (up to 3000 °C) can be reached within seconds with an ultra-fast heating rate of 103–104 °C/min. Moreover, the UHS method allows for a controllable and tunable temperature profile, enabling the control of sintering and microstructural evolution, and provides the potential for scalable, roll-to-roll sintering of ceramics. More recently, UHS has become highly attractive for the fabrication of ceramics in a short duration. It has been successfully applied to several systems, including fine-grained Al2O3 [47,48], NaNbO3 [49], and transparent/translucent ceramics, such as MgAl2O4 [50], Al2O3 [51], and ZrO2 [52].

Microstructural coarsening (grain growth, pore coarsening) is a common phenomenon during conventional slow heating, which degrades the sinterability of the body before it reaches the desired sintering temperature. Unlike conventional slow heating, rapid heating in UHS minimizes the time spent in the low-temperature coarsening regime, allowing high temperatures, where densification dominates, to be attained without degrading the sinterability of the green body [47,53−55]. For AlON doped with Y2O3, grain coarsening tends to occur before the densification-dominant stage during pressureless sintering, with fast densification occurring at higher temperatures (≥ 1700 °C), whereas grain coarsening takes place at ≤ 1700 °C [22]. The ultra-fast heating rate of UHS provides a means to inhibit the coarsening of AlON grains at low temperatures, enabling a high relative density to be achieved in a short dwell time. On the other hand, Guo and Todd’s work [53] suggested the presence of nonequilibrium grain boundaries, which have not had time to relax to their lowest energy, the most compact structure, during the short sintering times in the UHS. This phenomenon still pertains to the acceleration of grain boundary diffusion. These findings imply that it is possible to obtain AlON with fine grains, high relative density, and nonequilibrium grain boundaries via UHS. However, to the best of our knowledge, neither UHS nor UHS followed by pressureless sintering/conventional sintering has been considered a viable method for achieving transparent AlON ceramics.

In this work, AlON ceramics with excellent optical transmittance (≥ 80% over a wide wavelength range of 350–4900 nm) were successfully fabricated via UHS followed by CS method, with 2 min of UHS held at 1850 °C followed by 120 min of CS at 1880 °C. To investigate the fast densification mechanism behind the excellent transparency achieved through UHS+CS, UHS and CS were also applied separately to sinter AlON. The relative density, pore size, and grain size of the samples prepared by UHS, UHS+CS, and CS were then analyzed.

### 2.1 Fabrication of AlON ceramics

The homemade single-phase AlON powder synthesized by the carbothermal reduction and nitridation method (described in Ref. [32]) and the nanosized Y2O3 powder with an average particle size of 30.44 nm prepared by calcining Y(CH3COO)3·4H2O (described in Ref. [45]) were used as the starting materials. The AlON powder doped with 0.15 wt% Y2O3 was ball milled in absolute ethyl alcohol via Si3N4 grinding balls. After the balls were removed, the obtained slurry was fully dried at 70 °C and then screened through a 50-mesh nylon sieve. The milled powder was packed into pellets 10 mm in diameter under 50 MPa and then cold isostatically pressed under 120 MPa to prepare green bodies. The green bodies were sintered in N2 via three methods: (1) UHS, heating at 6000 °C/min to 1850 °C for 2 min; (2) UHS+CS, heating at 6000 °C/min to 1850 °C for 2 min, followed by heating at 50 °C/min to 1880 °C for 120 min; and (3) CS, heating at 50 °C/min to 1880 °C for 120 min. For comparison, the green bodies were also heated at 50 °C/min to 1850 °C for 2 min to observe the microstructure of the sintered samples. To investigate the phase assemblage and microstructure of the samples during heating, the green bodies were heated at 6000 °C/min via UHS and 50 °C/min with CS to 1600 °C without a holding time. The prepared AlON ceramics were then ground and mirror polished on both sides to a thickness of 1 mm for optical measurement. The UHS processes were carried out via a Joule ultra-fast heating sintering furnace (UHS-3000, Tianjin Zhonghuan Electric Furnace Co., China), whereas conventional sintering was performed via a common graphite furnace (ZTY-60-23, Shanghai Chenrong Electric Furnace Co., China).

### 2.2 Characterizations

The phase assemblage of the AlON powder and sintered samples was characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/Max-ULtima+, Rigaku, Japan) using Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 0.154 nm). The median particle size of the milled powder was measured via a laser particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern, UK). The morphology of the milled powder and fracture surface of the sintered samples were observed via a scanning electron microscope (FSEM; supra 55, Zeiss, Germany) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray spectrosmeter (EDS). Raman measurements were performed via a Raman spectrometer (LabRam HR Evolution, Horiba, Japan) at wavelengths ranging from 1000 to 2000 cm−1. The microstructure of the polished samples, which were hot etched at 1630 °C for 40 min, was observed via a metallurgical microscope (GX51, Olympus, Japan). The grain size of AlON was statistically calculated as the average diameter passing through the centroid of the grain from the hot-etched image. The bulk density of the fabricated AlON ceramics was measured via Archimedes’ principle, which is presented as the relative density. The in-line transmittance of the AlON ceramics in the wavelength range of 190–2500 nm was recorded with a spectrophotometer (Cary 5000, Varian, USA). The in-line transmittance of the samples in the wavelength range of 2500–6500 nm was measured via a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR; Nicolet iS50, Thermo Scientific, USA). The Vickers hardness (HV) of the transparent AlON ceramics at room temperature was measured under a load of 500 gf using a hardness tester (MH-6, Shanghai Everone Precision Instruments Co., Ltd., China), and the average HV for each sample was obtained from five indentations.

### 3.2 Optical transmittance of AlON ceramics

Figure 2 shows the temperature profile of the UHS process for the fabrication of the AlON ceramics. At an ultra-fast heating rate of 6000 °C/min, the sintering temperature exceeded the set temperature during heating. The highest temperature reached 1873 °C, but the overshoot lasted only 2 s before stabilizing at 1850±6 °C. After holding for 2 min at 1850 °C, the temperature rapidly decreased to 600 °C in approximately 9 s. This means that the UHS process of the AlON ceramics was completed within 3 min. In addition, throughout the fabrication of AlON via UHS, the temperature does not exceed 1880 °C, which is a commonly used pressureless sintering temperature for transparent AlON ceramics [29,32,39,40].

The transparency comparisons among the UHS, UHS+CS, and CS samples indicate that UHS followed by the CS method is an effective and efficient approach for the fast fabrication of highly transparent AlON ceramics. In the UHS+CS method, presintering through UHS is crucial for achieving excellent transparency through the subsequent CS process with a short holding time. By applying presintering with UHS, the transmittance of the fabricated AlON ceramics is significantly greater than that of the CS sample, especially in the visible light range (at 400 nm: 58.82% vs. 80.71%). Moreover, the holding time of 120 min for CS in UHS+CS is markedly shorter than that reported in Refs. [26,31,39,45] for similar optical transparencies (2.5 h in our previous work, 6–30 h in Refs. [26,31,39,45]).

Additionally, the UHS sample appears black (Fig. 4). The Raman spectra of the black UHS samples at wavelengths from 1000 to 2000 cm−1 are shown in Fig. 5. No characteristic peaks of carbon near 1348 and 1580 cm−1 were measured. This suggests that carbon contamination is unlikely to be the cause of the UHS sample appearing black. On the other hand, as discussed by Qi et al*. [56], the color of the AlON sample should become darker in the reducing atmosphere during the subsequent CS if it results from carbon contamination. The high transparency of the UHS+CS sample, which is no longer black, further demonstrates that carbon contamination is not the cause of the black color of the sample.

In fact, many studies [43,44,56,57] have reported that AlON, prepared by pressureless sintering, also has a gray or black color. The very low relative density and very large pores generally cause the sample to appear white, similar to the appearance of the AlON powder. However, when the relative density of a sample increases to a high level but is not high enough to achieve transparency, the sample commonly appears gray or black. In Ref. [56], the gray color of a sample was attributed to defects within the sample rather than to carbon contamination. In the present work, the UHS sample has a relatively high relative density of 97.43%, which suggests reduced diffuse reflection from the surface of the incident light due to fewer and/or smaller pores [58,59]. Therefore, considering the opacity of the UHS sample, its black color appears to be due to the pores (Figs. 6(a) and 6(b)).

For the UHSed sample, the subsequent CS at 1880 °C for 120 min not only results in massive grain growth but also leads to a significant decrease in both the number and size of pores and in the pore size range (Fig. 6, Table 1). Only a very limited number of pores with sizes of 0.11–0.37 μm were observed in the SEM image of the UHS+CS sample. After UHS, the higher relative density of the UHSed sample (97.43%) indicates that the sintering of AlON is in the final stage. During the subsequent CS process, the larger pores in the UHSed sample were gradually eliminated, leading to an increase in the relative density and a decrease in the pore size. Additionally, both the maximum pore size and the range of pore sizes in the UHS+CS sample are significantly smaller than those in the UHS and CS samples. The results of Mie calculations have shown that a minimum of the real in-line transmission (RIT) exists when the pores are almost as large as the wavelength of the incident light, and reducing the pore size, making them much smaller than the wavelength, is an effective way to increase the RIT [61]. Therefore, the smaller pores in the UHS+CS sample, ranging in size from 0.11 to 0.37 μm, are likely the cause of the high transmittance of the UHS+CS sample in the wavelength range of 350–4900 nm.

In contrast, as shown in Fig. 6(d), many large pores (0.45–2.40 μm) are present in the CS sample prepared at 1880 °C for 120 min. Correspondingly, relative densities of 99.71% and 99.30% were measured for the UHS+CS and CS samples, respectively. This finding indicates that the presintering of AlON via UHS contributes to the high relative density of the UHS+CS sample, with a significant decrease in the number, size, and size range of pores through the subsequent CS, which in turn leads to its excellent transparency.

Overall, the pore number and size observed in Fig. 6 are in very good accordance with the measured relative density and transmittance of these samples. Therefore, the relative density, which is influenced by the pore number and size, should be a major factor affecting the transparency of the AlON ceramics prepared in this work. This means that the higher relative density of the UHS+CS sample contributes to its better transparency, whereas the lower relative density of the CS sample results in a lower transmittance. In addition, according to Mie theory, a minimum of the relative in-line transmittance exists when the pores are almost as large as the wavelength of the incident light [61]. As a result, the CS sample shows very low transmittance from the visible to the near-infrared range due to its pore size, which ranges from 450 to 2400 nm.

The advantage of UHS in accelerating the densification of AlON can be further illustrated by comparing the microstructure of the UHS sample with that of another CS sample prepared at 1850 °C for 2 min (Figs. 6(b) and 8). In Fig. 8, a fracture surface characterized by neck growth and many large holes and pores is observed, which is distinctly different from the dense structure with a limited number of small closed spherical pores in the UHS sample (Fig. 6(b)). Moreover, the grain size shown in Fig. 8 is significantly smaller than that of the UHS sample. The microstructure of the CS sample shown in Fig. 8 indicates that the sintering still occurs at an intermediate stage after holding for 2 min via CS, with a relative density of 88.51%, which is significantly lower than that of the UHS sample (97.43%). Consequently, compared with conventional CS, UHS promotes the densification of AlON and improves the grain connection state. This phenomenon is beneficial for further densification and grain growth in the subsequent CS.

The results presented above indicate that UHS plays a crucial role in the fast fabrication of highly transparent AlON ceramics by UHS+CS. After presintering by UHS, AlON with high relative density, numerous closed small pores (190–880 nm), and predominantly small grains (≤ 8 μm) were obtained (Figs. 6(a), 6(b), and 7). In contrast, the CS sample prepared at the same temperature and held for the same duration has a significantly lower relative density, with many large holes and pores (~2 µm) present in the sample (Fig. 8).

To explore the factors contributing to the excellent densification of AlON during UHS, the phase assemblage and microstructure of the UHS and CS samples heated to 1600 °C (without holding) were investigated. As shown in Fig. 11, AlON is still the major phase in the UHS sample, with only a few weak diffraction peaks of α-Al2O3 being detected. In contrast, α-Al2O3 becomes the major phase, and diffraction peaks of AlN are present in the CS sample. This finding indicates that the ultra-fast heating of the UHS successfully inhibited the decomposition of AlON during the fabrication of ceramics. Similarly, as shown in Fig. 12(a), compacted small particles were observed in the UHS sample. However, the abundance of α-Al2O3 in the CS sample and the easy agglomeration and coarsening of α-Al2O3 at > 1200 °C led to significant grain coarsening (Fig. 12(b)). Therefore, the inhibition of AlON decomposition by the ultra-fast heating should be an important factor contributing to its fast densification by preventing microstructural coarsening in the early stage of sintering.

The competition between densification and microstructural coarsening mechanisms is a common explanation for accelerated sintering under ultra-fast heating via UHS [53,54,62]. Recently, Guo and Todd [53] proposed that the acceleration of grain boundary processes with rapid heating can be explained by the presence of nonequilibrium grain boundaries, which have not had time to relax to their lowest energy and most compact structure during short sintering times in UHS. These would be more open and defective than the well-relaxed grain boundaries of conventional sintering, thus decreasing the activation energy for grain boundary diffusion. For the UHS sample prepared in this work, microstructural coarsening was inhibited during the densification process, and nonequilibrium grain boundaries were observed in the sample. Therefore, under the nonequilibrium grain boundaries induced by ultra-fast heating, combined with the high relative density, small grains, and improved grain connection state in the UHS sample (Fig. 7), further densification of AlON was rapidly completed through grain growth during the short holding time of the CS. As a result, AlON ceramics with excellent transparency and high hardness were successfully fabricated rapidly by UHS+CS.

## 4 Conclusions

(1) By ultra-fast high-temperature sintering at 1850 °C for 2 min, followed by conventional sintering at 1880 °C for 120 min, highly transparent AlON ceramics with a transmittance ≥ 80% over a wide wavelength range of 350–4900 nm were successfully fabricated. The obtained AlON ceramics show a maximum transmittance of 84.73% at 3750 nm and a transmittance of 80.71% at 400 nm. Moreover, the AlON ceramics also exhibited a high Vickers hardness of 19.57±0.23 GPa.

(2) In the fabrication of AlON ceramics by UHS+CS, UHS is a crucial step. After presintering by UHS, AlON was characterized by a high relative density, predominantly small grains, and an improved grain connection state was obtained. Therefore, in the subsequent CS, under the influence of nonequilibrium grain boundaries induced by UHS, further densification was rapidly achieved through grain growth during the short holding time. As a result, AlON ceramics with high relative density and small grain size (average grain size of 47.06 μm) were fabricated.

(3) The proposed UHS+CS method is an effective and efficient approach for fabricating high-performance AlON ceramics. Compared with that of the CS sample, the transmittance of the fabricated AlON ceramics with UHS+CS is significantly greater, particularly in the visible light range (at 400 nm: 58.82% vs. 80.71%). Moreover, the holding time of CS during UHS+CS is reduced to 120 min, which is significantly shorter than that reported in the literature for AlON with similar optical transparency.
