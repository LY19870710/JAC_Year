## 1 Introduction

High-entropy diborides (HEBs) with multi-principal transition metal cations in equimolar or near-equimolar fractions sharing the 1a (0,0,0) Wyckoff site in the AlB2-type crystal structure have attracted extensive attention as novel ultra-high temperature ceramics (UHTCs) [1−3]. In 2016, Gild *et al*. [4] first synthesized a series of HEBs and found that HEBs crystallized in a hexagonal structure with alternating rigid 2D boron nets and 2D layers of metal cations. The strong metal−boron and B−B covalent bonds are conducive to enhancing thermodynamic stability of HEBs, which is a distinct advantage of HEBs compared with other high-entropy ceramics [5−9]. In addition, a protective oxide scale containing B2O3 and corresponding metal oxides would form on the surface of HEB bulks during the oxidation process under specific temperatures, inhibiting the inward diffusion of oxygen and consequently upgrading the oxidation resistance of HEBs [10−13]. The above unique comprehensive properties assist HEBs in emerging victorious as candidate materials in extreme environments when compared with other types of high-entropy ceramics.

However, the low fracture toughness of HEBs is one of the main obstacles that seriously limits their applicability in extreme environments. Zhang *et al*. [14] synthesized (Hf0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)B2, (Hf0.2Zr0.2Mo0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)B2, and (Hf0.2Ta0.2Mo0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)B2 ceramics and found that their indentation fracture toughness values (3.64–4.47 MPa·m1/2) were comparable to those of binary diboride ceramics (2.8–4.07 MPa·m1/2). Similarly, the indentation fracture toughness of 2.34 MPa·m1/2 for (Hf0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)B2 was reported by Luo *et al*. [15]. In addition, the moderately low fracture toughness (3.81±0.40 MPa·m1/2) measured by the single-edge notched beam (SENB) method was reported for (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2 ceramics [16]. To improve the sintering ability and mechanical properties of HEBs, SiC was introduced in HEBs. The fracture toughness for the (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2 ceramics could be increased by about 30% after adding 20 vol% SiC particles [17]. Similar improvements in the fracture toughness of (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2–SiC were achieved by Liu *et al*. [16] and Zhang *et al*. [18]. Although SiC can significantly promote the densification and improve the intrinsic strength of HEBs, the flaw tolerance and capacity of energy dissipation of HEBs cannot be effectively improved by the introduction of SiC [19,20]. Therefore, it is imperative to develop strategies for further improving the damage tolerance of HEBs–SiC ceramics. It has come to light that introducing suitable reinforcements can be an effective method for the improvement of fracture toughness and damage tolerance in high-entropy ceramics.

Previously, graphene (G), carbon nanotubes (CNT), and carbon fibers (Cf) were incorporated into high-entropy carbides (HECs) to improve their mechanical properties. For example, Sun *et al*. [21] developed (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Ta0.2W0.2)C–CNT composite via two-step spark plasma sintering. The fracture toughness of the HECs–CNT composite was increased from 4.25 to 5.3 MPa·m1/2 due to crack deflection and crack bridging. Besides, the feasibility of graphene as reinforcement of the (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Ta0.2W0.2)C matrix was demonstrated, and the fracture toughness of the HECs–G composite reached 7.1 MPa·m1/2 [21]. Furthermore, Cai *et al*. [22] prepared carbon fiber reinforced HEC–SiC composite (Cf/(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C–SiC). Abundant toughening mechanisms were observed in the Cf/(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C–SiC composite, such as fiber pull out, fiber bridging, and crack deflection, which significantly elevated the fracture toughness (8.24 MPa·m1/2) of the composite [22]. The foregoing results indicate that carbon fibers may be the most suitable reinforcements for HEBs–SiC ceramics. Unfortunately, to the authors’ best knowledge, no papers on carbon fiber-reinforced HEBs–SiC ceramics have been reported.

To comprehensively improve the fracture toughness and damage tolerance, performs woven from continuous carbon fibers should be used. However, long sample preparation time and complex processes are usually needed for continuous carbon fiber-reinforced ceramic matrix composites [23]. On the other hand, chopped carbon fibers (Cf) are widely used as reinforcements for ceramic matrix composites due to economical and convenient production processes [24−27]. Moreover, the improved damage tolerance has been achieved in chopped carbon fiber-reinforced binary diboride matrix composites [26,27]. Therefore, as a first attempt to improve the fracture toughness and damage tolerance of HEBs, chopped carbon fibers were selected as reinforcements in this work.

Thermal conductivity of the composite is another important performance, determining reliability and durability of the composite [28]. Recent studies have demonstrated HEBs are promising thermal insulation materials due to their lower thermal conductivity in comparison with the conventional binary diborides [29]. A significant effect of additives on thermal properties has been confirmed in ZrB2**-**based composites [30,31]. However, the effects of chopped Cf on the thermal conductivity of HEBs have not been discussed in previous literature.

To investigate feasibility of improving the damage tolerance of HEBs, the (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2 matrix with a uniform element distribution was synthetized via the boro/carbothermal reduction (BCTR) method first. Then, SiC particles and chopped carbon fibers were embedded into the (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2 matrix to prepare the Cf/(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2–SiC composite. The microstructure and mechanical properties of the Cf/(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2–SiC composite were systematically investigated. Subsequently, the thermal conductivity of the Cf/(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2–SiC composite was measured via the hot-wired method. Furthermore, the static oxidation behaviour of the Cf/(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2–SiC composite in air at 1100–1500 ℃ was studied, and the possible oxidation mechanism was proposed based on the phase and microstructure evolution of the Cf/(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2–SiC composite.

### 2.1 Raw materials

Commercially available TiO2, ZrO2, HfO2, Nb2O5, Ta2O5 (*D*50 = 1–3 μm, ≥ 99.5% purity), B4C (*D*50 = 1–3 μm, ≥ 92% purity), and SiC (*D*50 = 500 nm, ≥ 99.9% purity) powders were purchased from Beijing HWRK Chem Co., Ltd., China and used as raw materials. The T300 carbon fibers (diameter = 7 μm, tensile strength = 3.5 GPa, tensile modulus = 230 GPa, Torayca Co., Ltd., Japan) were used as reinforcements to prepare the Cf/(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2–SiC composite.

#### 2.2.1 Preparation of HEB and HEB–SiC ceramics

For the synthesis of (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2 ceramics via boro/carbothermal reduction (BCTR), the molar percentage of raw materials was calculated by Reaction (1) [32,33]:

(1) 2TiO2+2ZrO2+2HfO2+Nb2O5+Ta2O5+527B4C→10(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2+347B2O3(g)+527CO(g)

Approximately 10 wt% excess B4C was added to the raw materials to compensate for the loss of the boron source. The powders were mixed by ball milling at 300 r·min−1 for 6 h in a planetary ball-miller using ZrO2 balls (5–10 mm) and ethanol as milling media. The ball-to-powder ratio was 1 : 1 in weight. After drying with a rotary evaporator at 50 ℃, the homogeneous mixture of the raw materials was calcined at 1600 ℃ for 1 h under vacuum. The synthesized powders were then ground and sieved through a 200-mesh screen. The mean diameter of the obtained powders was found to be approximately 0.4 μm (Fig. 1(a)). Further consolidation of synthesized powders was carried out by spark plasma sintering (SPS) at 2050 ℃ for 15 min with a uniaxial pressure of 60 MPa to synthesize high-entropy (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2 (HEB) ceramics. Figure 1(b) shows the XRD pattern of the as-prepared HEB sample, together with that of ZrB2 (JCPDS 34-0423) and HfB2 (JCPDS 38-1398). It is obvious that the single-phase diboride solid solution was formed, which possessed the same crystal structure as the single binary component phases. However, compared to that of ZrB2 and HfB2 components, the intensity of the (100) reflection peak in the HEB ceramics greatly increased. The diffraction abilities of transition metal atoms can be described by their atomic scattering factor, which is proportional to the corresponding atomic order numbers [34]. The higher diffraction intensity of the (100) plane could be attributed to the substitution of Zr and Hf atoms with higher atomic order numbers, such as Nb and Ta [35]. The elemental mapping diagrams (Fig. 1(c)) indicate that the five metal elements (Ti, Zr, Hf, Nb, and Ta) are uniformly distributed, confirming a random distribution of the transition metal atoms at micrometer scale without segregation. The above results demonstrate the (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2 ceramics are synthesized successfully by this method. Furthermore, the synthesized powders and 20 vol% SiC powders were mixed by a planetary ball miller and then densified by SPS under the same sintering parameters for preparing high-entropy diboride ceramics reinforced by SiC particulates (HEB–SiC). The obtained HEB and HEB–SiC ceramics could be used as reference materials to study the microstructure and properties of the Cf/(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2–SiC composite.

#### 2.2.2 Preparation of Cf/(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2–SiC composite

Firstly, carbon coatings on carbon fibers were prepared by a hydrothermal carbonization process of carbohydrates according to our previous works [36,37]. The carbon coatings can act as a bonding layer between the fibers and the matrix and protect the carbon fibers from interfacial reactions effectively [38−40]. Subsequently, carbon fiber bundles with carbon coatings were cut into ~2 mm in length and mixed with 50 vol% synthesized powders and 20 vol% SiC powders. The mixing and sintering process for the Cf/(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2–SiC composite was identical to that of the HEB–SiC ceramics. The composite sample was denoted as Cf/HEB–SiC. The schematic of the fabrication process of HEB, HEB–SiC, and Cf/HEB–SiC is presented in Fig. 2.

### 2.3 Characterization

Phase compositions of the as-synthesized powders were identified via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Empyrean, PANalytical B.V., the Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5418 Å). The lattice parameters (*a* = 3.0911 Å and *c* = 3.3499 Å) were obtained from the XRD pattern by Rietveld method. The theoretical density of the synthesized HEB sample was calculated to be 8.38 g·cm−3 based on the refined lattice parameters and atomic mass of the corresponding elements. The relative density of the specimens was measured by Archimedes method. The morphologies of the synthesized powders and the as-sintered samples were detected using a TESCAN MIRA LMS scanning electron microscope (SEM) with a voltage of 10 kV. The elemental distributions of the polished surfaces of the as-prepared specimens were analysed using an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDS; Xplore, TESCAN, Czech Republic) with a voltage of 15 kV. The particle size distribution of the synthesized powders was obtained by the Image J software based on SEM images. The interfaces between the fibers and the matrix were characterized using a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai F30, FEI, USA).

The flexural strength of HEB**-**based samples was evaluated by a three-point bending test with a specimen size of 2 mm × 3 mm × 16 mm. The span and crosshead speed were 10 mm and 0.5 mm/min, respectively. Such sample size was determined based on the limited size (*φ*30 mm) of bulks prepared by our SPS equipment. The fracture toughness of the HEB**-**based samples was measured by the SENB method with a specimen size of 2 mm × 4 mm × 22 mm and a notched depth of 2 mm. The span and crosshead speed were 16 mm and 0.05 mm/min, respectively. Elastic modulus was estimated via stress-strain curves of different samples. Microhardness was determined using a Vickers hardness tester (HXD-1000TMC/LCD, Shanghai Taiming Optical Instrument Co., Ltd., China) with a load of 9.8 N and dwell time of 15 s. The crack propagation path of the as-prepared samples was observed after Vickers hardness testing. The thermal conductivity (*k*) of the obtained samples (10 mm × 10 mm × 1.5 mm) was measured using the hot-wired method by a thermal analyzer (TPS 2200, Hot Disk, Sweden) at room temperature. The electrical conductivity of the specimens was tested at the ambient temperature by a voltage–current method.

To evaluate the oxidation resistance of the Cf/HEB–SiC composite, the thermodynamic calculation of oxidation reactions for corresponding binary diborides was conducted using the HSC chemistry 6.0 software. The rectangular samples with a size of 10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm were polished using a diamond paste for experimentally investigating the oxidation behavior of the Cf/HEB–SiC composite. The isothermal oxidation tests of the obtained samples were conducted using an electrical furnace (KSL–1700X, Kejing, China) at 1100–1500 ℃ in air for 10 min. The phase compositions and morphologies of the oxidation layers were analysed by XRD, SEM, and EDS, respectively. Subsequently, the oxidized samples were cross-sectioned along the midline, and then the polished cross-sections were characterized by SEM for further observing the thickness of oxide scales.

### 3.1 Microstructure of HEB, HEB–SiC, and Cf/HEB–SiC composite

Figure 3 shows the microstructural evolution of HEB**-**based samples after the introduction of SiC and Cf. A few pores with sizes of 0.5–1 µm were detected in the HEB ceramics due to their low grain-boundary migration rates and strong covalent bonds (Figs. 3(a) and 3(d)) [41,42]. The SiC particles were uniformly dispersed within a grey HEB matrix, and no agglomeration was detected (Figs. 3(b) and 3(e)). Fewer pores in the HEB–SiC demonstrate a significant improvement of the densification after introducing SiC into HEB. The homogenous and compact microstructure of the Cf/HEB–SiC composite is shown in Figs. 3(c) and 3(f). Chopped carbon fibers were evenly distributed in the Cf/HEB–SiC composite. Fiber pull-out combined with remaining cylindrical holes was observed in the fracture surface of Cf/HEB–SiC. Intriguingly, the carbon fibers retained their intact and smooth surfaces under the protection of carbon coatings. The compact microstructure and well-preserved carbon fibers in the Cf/HEB–SiC composite can ensure the Cf/HEB–SiC composite to exhibit improved fracture toughness and damage tolerance, which will be shown in Section 3.2.

For further investigating the morphologies of the Cf/HEB–SiC composite, the focused ion beam (FIB) technology was applied to fabricate the TEM sample containing the fibers/matrix interface. The high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images with the corresponding selected area electron diffraction pattern (SAED) clearly illustrate the microstructure of the HEB and Cf phases (Fig. 4). Two sets of lattice fringes with interplanar spacings of approximately 0.210 and 0.267 nm can be found in Fig. 4(a), corresponding to the ( 1¯01) and  (010) planes of the HEB phase, respectively. Then, the lattice parameters of the HEB phase were calculated to be a* = 0.308 nm and *c* = 0.332 nm, which were consistent with those obtained by Rietveld refinement based on the XRD spectrum shown in Fig. 1(b) (0.309 and 0.335 nm, respectively). The well-arranged diffraction spots along the major zone axes  [101] in Fig. 4(b) further confirm that the HEB phase has a hexagonal structure. Moreover, the Cf phase can be identified as a polycrystalline structure from its HRTEM image and SAED pattern (Figs. 4(c) and 4(d)).

The main phases, including HEB, SiC, and Cf, are marked in the high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) image (Fig. 5(a)), and the compositional homogeneity of the HEB phase is further demonstrated by scanning transition electron microscopy (STEM) elemental mappings (Figs. 5(b)–5(i)), indicating that the addition of Cf has no side effects on the components of HEB. A strong bonding interface was formed due to the interfacial reactions between the carbon coatings and the residual oxide impurities in the matrix, corresponding to the tight junction displayed in Fig. 5(a). The carbon coatings prepared by the hydrothermal method will suffer a certain volume contraction at high temperatures, thereby forming a nanoscale gap between the carbon fibers and the carbon coatings. This phenomenon can be clearly observed in the Fig. 5(a). In this case, the fiber coatings could act as a “buffer-layer” and conserve the fibers effectively. The well-preserved carbon fibers can facilitate enhancing the damage tolerance of teh Cf/HEB–SiC composite.

### 3.2 Mechanical properties of HEB, HEB–SiC, and Cf/HEB–SiC composite

Table 1 provides a summary of the relative density and mechanical properties of three different samples. The relative density for HEB, HEB–SiC, and Cf/HEB–SiC is 89.7%, 92.1%, and 97.9%, respectively. The addition of SiC and Cf improves the relative density of HEB and inhibits the formation of pores during the preparation process. Benefiting from good uniformity and compactness, the Cf/HEB–SiC composite exhibits relatively high flexural strength (411 MPa), compared to HEB (339 MPa) and HEB–SiC (476 MPa). It could be concluded that chopped Cf could not only improve the relative density but also enhance the mechanical properties of HEB. The Cf/HEB–SiC composite also exhibits higher fracture toughness of 6.15 MPa·m1/2 than HEB (4.20 MPa·m1/2) and HEB–SiC (4.63 MPa·m1/2) ceramics. The toughening mechanism was disclosed by Vickers indentation test, which could visualize the crack propagation paths in the samples [43,44]. A straight indentation crack path with few crack deflections is detected in the HEB sample, demonstrating the poor resistance to cracking of the HEB ceramics (Figs. 6(a) and 6(d)). The crack trajectory relating specifically to the SiC phases within the HEB–SiC sample is intergranular, marked by the red line in Figs. 6(b) and 6(e). However, a significant promotion of the fracture toughness of the HEB ceramics has not been achieved by just the addition of SiC particles. In contrast, the cracks in the Cf/HEB–SiC sample tend to propagate along the interfaces between the fibers and the ceramic matrix (Figs. 6(c) and 6(f)). The longer crack propagation path could absorb more impact energy, which responds to higher fracture toughness of Cf/HEB–SiC compared to HEB and HEB–SiC.

To quantitatively demonstrate the effect of chopped Cf on enhancing the fracture toughness and tolerance damage, the critical crack size (α*cr) and the damage tolerance parameter (*D*t) are used. The critical crack size can describe the flaw tolerance of ceramics, while the damage tolerance parameter reveals the resistance of ceramics to deformation and catastrophic failure [45,46]. Higher values of *α*cr and *D*t represent better damage tolerance of ceramics. The critical crack sizes and the damage tolerance parameters of different samples were calculated from their conventional material parameters as Eqs. (2) and (3) [47,48]:

(2) αcr=KIC2σb2π

(3) Dt=KIC⋅Eσb⋅HV

where *σ*b is the flexure strength, *K*IC is the fracture toughness, *E* is the elastic modulus, and *H*V is the Vickers hardness. The critical crack size increases significantly from 30.1 μm for HEB–SiC ceramics to 71.3 μm for the Cf/HEB–SiC composite, indicating that carbon fibers can significantly improve the resistance to the crack propagation of the composite. The *D*t values of HEB, HEB–SiC, and Cf/HEB–SiC samples are 0.16, 0.10, and 0.29 m1/2, respectively. The higher *D*t value of the Cf/HEB–SiC composite reveals that the damage tolerance has increased effectively after the introduction of the chopped carbon fibers. Surprisingly, HEB–SiC has lower *α*cr and *D*t values than HEB, though the fracture toughness is increased to a certain extent after integrating SiC into HEB. The fracture toughness of brittle materials is closely associated with their intrinsic strength, which depends on the phase compositions and relative density of materials [49]. Thus, the *K*IC values are easily distracted by the different intrinsic strength of materials with disparate components. In this condition, the description of the reliability for HEB and HEB–SiC just by the comparison of the *K*IC values between them seems to be not enough. The *α*cr and *D*t values can be more comprehensive reflections of the reliability of the brittle materials considering the flaw tolerance and the capacity of energy dissipation [45−48]. The introduction of SiC significantly elevates the flexural strength of HEB, resulting in the improvement of its fracture toughness. However, the low *α*cr and *D*t values indicate the poor flaw tolerance and poor capacity of energy dissipation to a local impact of HEB**–**SiC. This result implies that SiC particles mainly play a strengthening role in HEB**–**SiC, rather than a toughening phase to improve damage tolerance of HEB.

To figuratively illustrate the effect of Cf on enhancing the damage tolerance of HEB, the comparisons between the Cf/HEB–SiC composite in this study and other literature are shown in the Ashby plot (Fig. 7). The Cf/HEB–SiC composite exhibits higher values in critical crack size and damage tolerance parameter than those in many previously reported high-entropy diborides and carbides. Therefore, carbon fibers are considered as effective toughening materials to improve the damage tolerance of the HEB ceramics.

### 3.3 Thermal conductivity of Cf/HEB–SiC composite

The thermal conductivity of the UHTCs-based composites is closely related to their phase compositions [30]. In this study, Cf was introduced as the reinforcement to improve mechanical properties of HEB**-**based ceramics. High damage tolerance has been achieved in the Cf/HEB–SiC composite. To further evaluate the effect of Cf on thermal properties of the Cf/HEB–SiC composite, the thermal conductivity was measured and summarized in Table 2 for HEB ceramic, HEB–SiC ceramic, and Cf/HEB–SiC composite. The thermal conductivity (7.8±0.11 W·m−1·K−1) for the HEB ceramic is lower than reported values (12.6–27.8 W·m−1·K−1) of high entropy diborides in the published literature (Table 2) as a result of their lower relative density (89.7%) [29]. However, significantly lower thermal conductivity is obtained in HEB–SiC (4.9±0.09 W·m−1·K−1) and Cf/HEB–SiC (5.0±0.05 W·m−1·K−1), though the SiC and Cf have high intrinsic thermal conductivity [31]. Several reasons can explain the decreased thermal conductivity in the Cf/HEB–SiC composite. Firstly, heavy atoms Nb and Ta in HEB crystal lattice possibly cause anharmonic lattice vibration between reinforcements (SiC and Cf) and HEB phase, increasing the thermal resistance of the Cf/HEB–SiC composite [54]. Moreover, the downward trend for thermal conductivity is corresponding to the decrease of electrical conductivity after adding SiC and Cf into HEB (Table 2). The decreased electrical conductivity has a negative effect on the electron-contributed thermal conductivity in the Cf/HEB–SiC composite [55]. Additionally, the incorporation of carbon fibers can create multiple interfaces (Cf/matrix and Cf/coatings), resulting in the enhancement of phonon scattering [31,56]. On the other hand, the Cf/HEB–SiC composite exhibits lower thermal conductivity (5.0±0.05 W·m−1·K−1) as compared to Csf/ZrB2–SiC (28.8–42.3 W·m−1·K−1), demonstrating its advantage as potential TPS materials (Table 2).

### 3.4 Oxidation resistance of Cf/HEB–SiC composite

HEB**-**based ceramics are considered as structural materials applied in high temperatures and oxygen-containing environments. The synergy between mechanical properties and oxidation resistance is a key concern for the Cf/HEB–SiC composite. The analysis in Section 3.2 suggests that the chopped carbon fibers could effectively improve the fracture toughness and damage tolerance of the HEB**-**based ceramics. Nevertheless, the oxidation behavior of the Cf/HEB–SiC composite has not been investigated systematically at elevated temperatures. Generally, the oxidation resistance of carbon fiber-reinforced ceramic matrix composite is dominated by their matrix because of the rapid oxidation of carbon fibers at high temperatures [57,58]. To evaluate the oxidation resistance of the HEB–SiC matrix, the Gibbs free energy change (Δ*G*) of oxidation reactions of single component diborides is calculated in the temperature range of 0–2000 ℃, and thus corresponding Ellingham diagram is plotted in Fig. 8. The thermodynamic calculations show that ZrB2 and HfB2 can be oxidized easily, while NbB2 and TaB2 are thermodynamically more stable in an oxygen-containing environment. High entropy diboride of (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2 consisting of Nb and Ta components may exhibit a better oxidation resistance compared with conventional ZrB2 and HfB2, which is consistent with the experimental results reported by Gild *et al.* [4] and Wang and Reece [59]. Moreover, SiC was helpful for the formation of a dense oxide scale, inhibiting oxygen diffusion and consequently improving the oxidation resistance of diborides [60]. Therefore, the HEB–SiC matrix in the Cf/HEB–SiC composite has a potential to provide good protection for carbon fibers during the high-temperature oxidation process.

To study the oxidation behaviour and oxidation resistance mechanism of the Cf/HEB–SiC composite, static oxidation experiments were conducted at 1100–1500 ℃ for 10 min. Furthermore, XRD and EDS tests were adopted to characterize phase compositions of the oxidation layer of the Cf/HEB–SiC composite at different temperatures. The surface morphology of HEB–SiC after being oxidized at 1100 ℃ is shown in Fig. 9(a). The oxidation layer is rich in Ti, Zr, and Hf, corresponding to the oxidation products of (ZrHf)O2 and TiO2−x* indexed by the XRD pattern (Fig. 10(a)). This result confirms that the group IVB elements would be oxidized preferentially into corresponding metallic oxides, which is consistent with their lower formation energy on Ellingham diagram (Fig. 8) [67,68]. These thermodynamically favored oxides could evolve into multicomponent phases by further solid solution reactions at high temperatures. As the oxidation temperature increased to 1300 ℃, plate-like regions rich in Ti appear on the oxidized surface (Fig. 9(b)), which are identified as TiO2−*x* and (Zr)HfTiO4 phase by XRD data (Fig. 10(a)). SiC grains were oxidized observably to the SiO2 phase at this temperature, as evidenced by the homogeneous distribution of Si element in the EDS mappings (Fig. 9(b)). As the temperature increased to 1500 ℃, some cracks arose, which can be attributed to the difference in thermal expansion coefficients between various oxidation products (Fig. 9(c)) [69]. The oxides skeleton is validated to be constituted by Ti/Zr/Hf-rich and Nb/Ta-rich phases via the EDS analysis (Fig. 9(c)), in good agreement with the main phase compositions of (Zr)HfTiO4 and (Zr)Hf6Ta2O17 oxides in the XRD pattern (Fig. 10(a)).

The involved oxides with low thermal conductivity could act as a protective layer to resist the oxidation and structure damage of internal carbon fibers. Consequently, the Cf/HEB–SiC composite holds immense potential to be applied as next-generation thermal protection materials, ascribing to its excellent mechanical properties and oxidation resistance.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, SiC particulates and chopped carbon fibers with carbon coatings were incorporated into the HEB matrix successfully to fabricate the Cf/(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2–SiC composite. The relative density, flexural strength, and fracture toughness of the composite are 97.9%, 411±3 MPa, and 6.15±0.11 MPa·m1/2, respectively. Moreover, the damage tolerance parameter and critical crack size increase significantly from 0.10 m1/2 and 30.1 μm for HEB–SiC to 0.29 m1/2 and 71.3 μm for Cf/HEB–SiC. The low thermal conductivity of 5.0±0.05 W·m−1·K−1 was also obtained in the Cf/HEB–SiC composite due to anharmonicity in lattice vibration, decreased electrical conductivity, and generation of multiple interfaces. Moreover, the oxidation resistance of the Cf/HEB–SiC composite has been examined by heating in air at temperatures in the range of 1100–1500 ℃. The group IVB components would be oxidized preferentially and the oxidation products gradually could evolve into some solid solution oxides with the increasing oxidation temperatures. The surface oxidation layer consisting of continuous SiO2 glass phase, (Zr)HfTiO4 oxides, and (Zr)Hf6Ta2O17 oxides could effectively insulate the inward oxygen and thermal, protecting the interior carbon fibers from oxidative damage. The good mechanical properties, low thermal conductivity, and excellent oxidation resistance of the Cf/HEB–SiC composite would inspire more research on carbon fiber-reinforced high-entropy diboride ceramics.
