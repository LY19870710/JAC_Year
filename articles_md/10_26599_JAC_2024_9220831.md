## 1 Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) nanomaterials have gained increasing attention since the discovery of graphene [1−10]. There are a variety of compounds with structures similar to graphite, such as transition metal dichalcogenides (MoS2, WSe2, WS2, etc.) [4−6], hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) [7,8], and MAX/Mxene [9−11]. Due to unusual properties of their ultrathin structure, they have potential applications in photocatalysis, electronics, and energy storage.

As research on 2D nanomaterials deepens, more preparation methods are emerging. Depending on how the materials are formed, they can be broadly classified into two categories. The first category includes bottom–up approaches, in which chemical methods are used to assemble nanomaterials. These methods include chemical vapor deposition (CVD) [12,13], hydrothermal synthesis [14,15], and gas-phase combustion synthesis [16]. Among them, CVD is commonly used to prepare 2D nanomaterials in the laboratory. Somani *et al*. [17] prepared graphene by CVD, and then other researchers used it to synthesize other 2D nanomaterials such as BN and MoS2 [18,19]. However, the selection of raw materials and the design of experimental procedures were complicated, and the yields were limited. Another class of methods is top–down approaches, where weaker forces between material layers (van der Waals forces, etc.) in which 2D monolayers and thin nanocrystals are peeled from larger layered materials [20,21]. The transparent tape exfoliation method was the first successful method to prepare graphene from graphite [1] and has proven to apply to other layered materials such as hBN and MoS2 [22]. Ball-milling exfoliation is commonly used to prepare 2D nanomaterials [23]. BN nanosheets were fabricated on a large scale by low-energy ball milling [24]. However, the usual ball milling treatment is a violent process that produces many defects that can disturb or destroy the crystal structure. Zeng *et al*. [25] exfoliated 2D nanomaterials by a controlled lithium ion intercalation process with ultrasonic treatment. Nevertheless, the aforementioned methods suffer from the same problems of low yield, high destructiveness, and operational complexity. Therefore, a new method is needed to achieve the large-scale production of 2D nanomaterials.

Herein, we propose a facile strategy of three-roll milling (TRM) technology to prepare 2D nanosheets. This strategy uses three rolls and milling medium (phenolic resin) to exfoliate 2D materials, where the shear forces between the rollers of TRM and the bonding forces of the phenolic resin destroyed the van der Waals forces between the layers of 2D materials. Since the phenolic resin wrapped the 2D materials, the stability of the crystal structure of the 2D materials was preserved. Herein, we report that TRM could mechanically exfoliate flake graphite, BN, MoS2, and MAX (Ti3AlC2) particles into nanosheets in mass production. We also demonstrate that the obtained multilayer graphene and MoS2 nanosheets were used as the anode materials of sodium/potassium-ion batteries with excellent electrochemical properties compared with their primary bulk materials. Therefore, this work reveals that the strategy of TRM to prepare 2D nanosheets has advantages of simple operation, high yield, and low cost as well as its scalability and broad application prospects.

### 2.1 Exfoliating schematic illustration

Figure 1 is a schematic diagram of the three-roll milling (TRM) device used to produce 2D nanosheets. It depicts the action of the phenolic resin and the peeling mechanism of the machine. TRM can produce three forces on layered materials: shear, adhesive, and compressive forces. As shown in Fig. 1(b), the shear force misaligns the surface of the materials, while adhesion force exfoliates the misaligned surface material by pulling (Fig. 1(c)). Compressive force reduces the size of the materials and exfoliates thin 2D nanosheets from the edges of 2D materials (Fig. 1(d)). Under the synergistic effect of the three forces, the surface layers of the 2D materials are repeatedly dislocated, pulled, and peeled off, and the 2D materials are continuously thinned during this process, eventually forming single-layer and multi-layer 2D nanosheets. During the thinning process, which is a physical change, the phenolic resin wraps the exfoliated 2D nanosheets, which protects their structures, thereby reducing defects and impurities.

### 2.2 Shear exfoliation model

The binder (phenolic resin) used in the experiment is fluid that provides both shear force and adhesion during the peeling process. The principle of the shear force acting on the surface of the materials is similar to the liquid phase peeling technology studied by Paton *et al*. [26]. By drawing on this model and combining practical analysis, we establish a peeling model to analyze the magnitude of the shear force acting on the 2D materials, as shown in Fig. 2.

*
Fig. 2Schematic of shear exfoliation process.

The study assumes that two small square blocks (L* × *L*) with the same interaction forces between the layers are stacked together in the initial state. They are then placed in flowing fluid with a non-zero shear rate, and the shear stress (* σ) is generated at the top and bottom of the stacked blocks due to the velocity difference. This leads to the delamination of the blocks.

The induced stress is related to the induced force (F*), by *F* = *σL*2. For Newtonian fluid, the applied stress is related to the shear rate by Newton’s law (Eq. (1)):

(1) σ=η⋅γ

Give

(2) F=η⋅γ⋅L2

where * η,  γ, and L* represent the liquid viscosity, the shear rate, and the block size, respectively, and *F* is the shear force. Taking flake graphite as an example, by combining with experimental data (* γ = 176.5 s−1), phenolic resin viscosity of 6000 MPa∙s at 35 ℃, and flake graphite particle size of about 300 µm, the force acting on the materials is about 0.162 N. This is much greater than the interlayer bonding forces of graphite (86.79±8.56 nN) reported by Tang et al*. [27].

### 2.3 2D material preparation and characterization

Natural graphite (> 98.5 wt%, 300 µm) was purchased from Qingdao Jintao Co., Ltd., BN (CG25, 20–30 µm) was purchased from Jiangxi Napu Industrial Co., Ltd. MoS2 (AR, 20–30 µm) and Ti3AlC2 (AR, 15–30 µm) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent. Phenolic resin (PF900A) was purchased from Hebei Zetian Chemical Co., Ltd. A three-roll milling machine (ZYTR-80-E, Shenzhen ZYE Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) was used for exfoliation. Taking flake graphite as an example, natural flake graphite (NG) and phenolic resin (PF) were mixed in a mass ratio of 1 : 50 and then stirred for 0.5 h to make the materials evenly dispersed in phenolic resin. The NG/PF mixture was passed 15 consecutive times through the TRM in gap mode, using the same rotation speed (300 r/min) as the apron roll. Each roll was performed at a fixed speed ratio of 1 : 3 : 9 between *N*1 (feed roller): *N*2 (central roller): *N*3 (apron roller). During the TRM process, the roll-to-roll distance (gap size) was varied, namely, gaps of *N*1/*N*2 = 24 μm and *N*2/*N*3 = 12 μm were used for the 1st to 5th consecutive cycles, 12 and 6 μm for the 6th to 10th consecutive cycles, and then 6 and 3 μm for the 11th to 15th consecutive cycles.

The exfoliated samples were washed with alcohol and treated with ultrasonic vibration and centrifugation for several cycles to remove phenolic resin, and finally freeze-dried to obtain multilayered micro/nano flakes. An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance-A25, Bruker AXS, Cu Kα, *λ* = 0.15406 nm) was used to analyze the diffraction peaks of the samples. A Raman spectroscope (DXR, American Thermo Electron) was used to detect changes in sample defects using 532 and 325 nm lasers as an excitation source. The morphology and microstructure of the exfoliated 2D nanosheets were observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Nova Nano SEM450, FEI) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-210, JEOL). The AFM image and corresponding height profile of exfoliated 2D nanosheets were taken by means of an atomic force microscope (AFM; SPA400, NSK Ltd.).

## 3 Results and discussion

To verify the exfoliation effect of the three-roll milling (TRM), an exfoliation experiment was carried out using natural flake graphite (NG) as raw materials. After multiple exfoliation steps, multilayer graphene (MLG) was obtained. Compared with NG, a high decrease in the peak intensity can be observed after 15 cycles of exfoliation. This should be attributed to that with more exfoliation cycles, the exfoliated products gradually become thinner and smaller under the comprehensive action of three forces shown in Fig. 1. It leads to the occurrence of lattice distortion in exfoliated 2D materials, resulting in the decrease in the peak intensity [28,29] (the evidence seen from the following HRTEM results). Furthermore, the peak position of MLG in the XRD pattern undergoes a left shift (Fig. 3(a)), implying that the interlayer spacing of MLG increases under the action of TRM according to the Bragg equation [30,31]. Raman spectra (Fig. 3(b)) show that the *I*D/*I*G value of MLG is a little bigger than that of NG, indicating that MLG contains a slightly more defects. During exfoliation, raw NG has a pronounced layered structure with a lateral dimension of about 150 µm and a thickness of 15–25 µm (Fig. 3(c)). Herein, 5, 10, and 15 cycles of exfoliation were carried out. Figure S1(a) in Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) shows an SEM image of the product of graphite after exfoliation for 5 cycles. Small amounts of products are MLG, but most are still large graphite flakes with some lifted edges. In the product after exfoliation for 10 cycles, the amount of MLG increases significantly, but there are still some large-grained graphite sheets (Fig. S1(b) in the ESM). Then, after exfoliation 15 cycles, MLG is obtained with a uniform distribution throughout the whole product with 2–3 µm in lateral size and several nanometers to tens of nanometers in thickness (Figs. 3(e) and 3(f)). An AFM image and the corresponding height profile of typical MLG in Figs. 3(g) and 3(h) show a thickness of 5–7 nm. The graphene-like structure of MLG can be clearly observed by TEM (Fig. 3(i)). Under the combined forces, the MLG interlayer spacing corresponding to the (002) crystal plane is 0.39 nm, which is larger than that of the original graphite (Fig. 3(j)). The above results show that TRM exfoliates the micro-sized NG to nano-sized MLG, which has the characteristics of flakes, small particle size, and uniform dispersion. The thickness reaches several nanometers to tens of nanometers.

To further validate the scalability of the three-roll milling method, we investigated other layered materials such as h-BN, MoS2, and Mxene (Ti3AlC2). The results are described below.

Boron nitride is available in six crystalline forms, of which hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) is the most common. h-BN is the white powder with graphite-like layered structure and lattice parameters and is also known as white graphite [32]. After BN was exfoliated, BN nanosheets (BNNS) were obtained. As shown in Fig. 4, the XRD peak position of BNNS shifts to the left, and its intensity is significantly weakened (Fig. 4(a)), indicating that the BN layer spacing is expanded, and the crystalline grains become smaller under the action of the three-roll mill [33]. The position of the characteristic peak in the Raman spectrum of BNNS (1369.58 cm−1) does not change under excitation by the light source of 325 nm, but its intensity decreases (Fig. 4(b)). This indicates that the exfoliated BN gradually becomes smaller as the number of exfoliation treatments increases [34]. To further observe changes in the BN morphology, SEM and TEM were carried out. Original BN has a flake layered structure with a lateral dimension of 20–30 µm, and a thickness of 1–2 µm (Figs. 4(c) and 4(d)). After 15 exfoliation treatments, the overall morphology of the BNNS product changes significantly, their lateral dimension is reduced to several micrometers, and the thickness reaches several nanometers (Figs. 4(e) and 4(f)). An AFM image and the corresponding height profile of typical MLG show a thickness of 4–6 nm (Figs. 4(g) and 4(h)). BNNS become transparent and exhibit a graphene-like structure under TEM (Figs. 4(i) and 4(j)). It shows a lattice spacing of 0.221 nm, which corresponds to the (100) plane of h-BN (0.216 nm in bulk BN), implying that exfoliation expands the lattice spacing (Fig. 4(j)).

A similar process was conducted to exfoliate bulk MoS2 to obtain MoS2 nanosheets (MSNS). The XRD patterns show that the characteristic peaks of bulk MoS2 and MSNS are not significantly shifted, but the peak intensity varies considerably (Fig. 5(a)). However, the peak position of the characteristic peak of MSNS in the Raman spectrum shifts to the left relative to that of bulk MoS2 (Fig. 5(b)), which implies that the MoS2 sheet layers are subjected to tensile forces during the exfoliating process, and the distance between the MoS2 layers becomes larger [35]. The flakes of bulk MoS2 are large and have a distinct lamellar distribution (Fig. 5(c)), while exfoliated MSNS show a multi-layered lamellar structure (Fig. 5(d)). An AFM image and the corresponding height profile of typical MSNS show a thickness of 3–5 nm (Figs. 5(e) and 5(f)). The detailed structure of MSNS is observed by TEM (Figs. 5(g) and 5(h)), which shows the nanosheet structure of MSNS, in which the lattice spacing of the (004) plane reaches 0.317 nm.

MAX phases, a family of nanolaminated ternary carbides/nitrides of the transitional metal mainly with an A-group element, have attracted increasing attentions due to their unique combination of metallic- and ceramic-like properties. By selectively etching A-site atoms of MAX, MXenes with unique 2D structures can be potentially generated [36]. Here, Ti3AlC2 was first etched (Sections S1.2 in the ESM) and then peeled off using TRM. As shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b), the bulk MAX is large and multi-layered, but the interlayer bonds are relatively tight. After etching treatment, the particle size does not change much, but because most of the Al ions between the layers are etched off, the layered structure is more obvious, and the interlayer forces are greatly weakened (Fig. 6(c)). After exfoliation, under the action of interaxial shear force and adhesive force, etched Ti3AlC2 is obviously peeled, the edges are lifted, and the thickness of the exfoliated regions reaches nanometers (Fig. 6(d)). An AFM image and the corresponding height profile of typical exfoliated etched-MAX show a thickness of 4–5 nm (Figs. 6(e) and 6(f)). Further TEM characterization shows that the sheet of peeled etched-MAX is thin and transmittable, and has obvious lattice features (Figs. 6(g) and 6(i)). The above results show that bulk graphite, BN, MoS2, and etched-MAX are successfully exfoliated into 2D nanomaterials by TRM.

The aforementioned large-scale production of multilayer graphene (MLG) and MoS2 nanosheets (MSNS) and their utilization in energy storage prompted us to study how their electrochemical properties changed before/after exfoliation for applications in sodium or potassium ion batteries. As shown in Fig. 7, NG and MLG materials were prepared as battery electrodes and assembled into Na-ion half-cells, respectively, and their performances were tested. NG shows high discharge capacity at low current density of 0.1 A, but the charge capacity is only 82 mAh∙g−1, and the first charge–discharge efficiency is only 28.6%. MLG shows an obvious charge–discharge plateau at 0.6–0.8 V, and the initial Coulombic efficiency reaches 50.6% (Figs. 7(a) and 7(b)). Figure 7(c) shows the discharge cycle curves of NG and MLG at current density of 1 A∙g−1. The initial discharge-specific capacity of NG reaches 208 mAh∙g−1. After 100 charge/discharge cycles, the capacity of NG is only 68 mAh∙g−1, while the initial capacity of MLG is only 137 mAh∙g−1, but still 106 mAh∙g−1 after cycling. According to the analysis, the discharge capacity of the NG electrode is only 85 mAh∙g−1 at 0.1 A∙g−1, which is mainly due to the capacitive contribution of the region I and the intercalated atoms in the regions II and III (Fig. 8(c)). Exfoliation increases the interlayer spacing of MLG, which contributes to the diffusion of the region I and the capacitance of the region III [42,43]. The structural characterization of MLG shows that the layer spacing and sheet thickness of MLG vary greatly after exfoliation by TRM. The larger interlayer spacing promotes the storage and transport of ions and electrons, suppresses the volume expansion of the materials, and reduces intercalation and delamination barriers [44]. The larger specific surface area facilitates better contact and sufficient reactions between the material and electrolyte. Defects and active sites on the nanosheets’ surfaces also contribute to improving their electrochemical properties [45].

As shown in Fig. 9, MoS2 nanosheets (MSNS) exhibit excellent electrochemical performance for energy storage in potassium-ion batteries. The charging and discharging curves of bulk MoS2 are smooth without obvious charging and discharging plateaus, indicating a low capacity, while MSNS shows plateaus between 0.5–0.8 V and 1.5–1.75 V (Figs. 9(a) and 9(b)). In this voltage range, many redox reactions and ion embedding and stripping increase the capacity of MSNS. At a current intensity of 0.1 A∙g−1, the initial discharge specific capacity of bulk MoS2 is 115 mAh∙g−1, and the capacity after 60 cycles is only 46.7 mAh∙g−1. The initial specific capacity of exfoliated MSNS is 319 mAh∙g−1, and it still retains 313.8 mAh∙g−1 after 60 cycles (Fig. 9(c)). Through the rate performance test of MSNS (Fig. 9(d)), MSNS returns to its corresponding capacity under the tests at current intensities of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2 A∙g−1. After activation at different current intensities, the capacity of MSNS at 0.5, 0.2, and 0.1 A∙g−1 current is greatly improved. During the characterization of MSNS, the position of the characteristic peaks in Raman spectra of MSNS shifts to the left, their interlayer spacing increases, and the specific surface area increases due to the thinning of MSNS. These factors contribute to the improvement of the electrochemical properties of MSNS [46]. To the best of our knowledge, the exfoliated MSNS electrode shows high capacity and excellent long-term cycling compared with the MoS2-based electrode materials for KIBs (Table S2 in the ESM).

## 4 Conclusions

We propose a simple and scalable exfoliation method by three-roll milling to prepare multilayer graphene, BN, MoS2, and etched-MAX nanosheets. The prepared 2D nanosheets show relatively complete structures with controllable thickness and can be used for large-scale production. Among them, the unique thin two-dimensional structure produced by exfoliating has a larger specific surface area, which is conducive to ion storage. At the same time, the enlarged layer spacing further promotes the storage and transfer of ions and electrons, inhibits the generation of side reactions such as volume expansion, and finally plays a powerful role in boosting the electrochemical performance of the battery. Therefore, when multilayer graphene (MLG) and MoS2 nanosheets (MSNS) are applied to SIB and KIB battery anode materials, they show better specific capacities compared to the original bulk materials (106 mAh∙g−1 for MLG after 100 cycles at 1.0 A∙g−1, 313.8 mAh∙g−1 for MSNS after 60 cycles at 0.1 A∙g−1). We demonstrate that 2D materials obtained by exfoliating have great potential in the field of energy storage.
