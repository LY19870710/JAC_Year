## 1 Introduction

“Entropy-stabilized oxides” encompass a novel class of multi-component disordered materials, characterized by unique structural features, tailorable chemical compositions, and correspondingly adjustable, exceptional functional properties [1,2]. High-entropy oxide ceramics (HEOCs) are typically composed of no less than five cations or anion sublattices with a single-phase or two-phase solid solution structure [1,3,4]. The remarkable property combinations of these ceramics can be attributed to significant effects such as high-entropy effects [3], severe lattice distortion [4−6], sluggish diffusion effects [7], and cocktail effects [3,7,8]. Consequently, the composition design, synthesis methods, and structural characterization of entropy-stabilized oxides provide vast prospects for improving and customizing the performance of ceramics [9−11].

In high-entropy oxide ceramics, considerable attention has been given to (Y0.2Eu0.2Er0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)3Al5O12 ceramics with a single face-centered cubic (FCC) structure due to its low thermal conductivity and excellent phase stability [12,13]. Consequently, this particular HEOC exhibits considerable potential for industrial applications. Spark plasma sintering [12], reactive flash sintering [14], pressureless sintering [15], and sol−gel methods [16] are the primary preparation methods employed for HEOC components. However, achieving fine microstructures and highly complex component shapes remains a challenge for traditional manufacturing techniques.

Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) or selective laser melting (SLM) is a type of laser additive manufacturing (LAM) technology that enables the direct production of complex, high-precision, and high-performance components [17,18]. The laser‒powder interaction during the LPBF process leads to the formation of a melt pool containing multiple materials, enabling the free movement and spontaneous organization of diverse atomic clusters. Many scholars have adopted LPBF for the direct *in-situ* synthesis of high-entropy alloy (HEA) materials with complex structures at various scales [19]. However, the LPBF of ceramic materials has been relatively underexplored compared with that of metals. Owing to the high melting points, high viscosity, and low thermal conductivity of ceramic materials, the formation of defects such as balling, porosity, and cracking during the LPBF process poses challenges in achieving dense ceramic parts with complex structures [20]. Scholars have employed a variety of methods to eliminate defects, including preheating [18], ultrasonic assistance [18], and process optimization [21]. Slurry-assisted powder spreading enables powder bed densities exceeding 60%, allowing for the utilization of nanoparticles while minimizing organic content to enhance ceramic shape quality [18,21]. In addition, the efficient direct manufacturing of dense ceramics necessitates resolving the contradiction between insufficient material toughness and high stresses induced by rapid alternations between extreme cold and extreme heat conditions [21,22].

Microstructure eutecticization has emerged as a proven and effective technique for enhancing the strength of oxide composites. This approach leads to microstructure refinement [23], dislocation pinning [24], interface strengthening [25], and high-temperature structural stability [26,27]. Moreover, toughness can be improved by micro-nano eutectic structures, which provide a tortuous path for crack propagation during yielding and necking processes. Among various eutectic ceramic materials, Al2O3/Y3Al5O12/ZrO2 has emerged as one of the most promising candidates for long-term use as an ultra-high-temperature structural material in high-temperature oxidizing atmospheres because of its low density, high melting point, high specific strength, and excellent oxidation and creep resistance [28]. Importantly, the garnet structure of Y3Al5O12 is similar to that of RE3Al5O12 designed by entropy engineering paradigm. The thermodynamic properties and mechanical properties of eutectic ceramics can be further improved by introducing Al2O3 and ZrO2 into the HEC high-entropy phase to form ternary eutectic ceramics.

Based on the aforementioned research, the *in-situ* synthesis of nano-HEC via LPBF, as well as weaving with Al2O3 and ZrO2 to form controllable eutectic structures, has potential for the preparation of high-performance HEOC-based composites. Previous studies have reported the preparation of melt-grown Al2O3/(Y0.2Er0.2Yb0.2Ho0.2Lu0.2)3Al5O12 or Al2O3/(Y0.2Er0.2Yb0.2Ho0.2Lu0.2)3Al5O12/ZrO2 eutectic ceramics via directional solidification, exploring their application prospects in structural components of gas turbine engines [29,30]. To date, reports on LPBF research of melt-grown HEOC-reinforced Al2O3/ZrO2 composites are rare. Despite extensive work on optimizing process parameters in LPBF research, the extremely high cooling rates of up to 105–107 K/s remain an unavoidable inherent characteristic [31]. By combining high-entropy and eutectic compositional design with the unique characteristics of LPBF processing to broaden the capability for microstructure control, further enhancing the potential performance of ceramics, a new approach is provided to address the contradiction between insufficient ceramic toughness and the high stresses induced by extreme cooling and high stresses during LPBF. Since high stresses can only be partially alleviated, it is more advantageous to leverage the fast solidification rate of LPBF to form nanosized segregation boundaries, highly dispersed precipitates, and high-density dislocations, enabling the *in-situ* preparation of new materials with a soft-hard heterogeneous microstructure. This approach facilitates the direct fabrication of high-performance, complex-structured HEOC-based composites with integrated LPBF formability.

This article presents a novel direct-forming technique for the production of complex high-entropy ternary eutectic ceramics composed of Al2O3/(Y0.2Eu0.2Er0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)3Al5O12/ZrO2 (HE-AZREAG). The method employs the slurry-based LPBF additive manufacturing process, complemented by a laser-induced *in-situ* synthesis strategy. This approach enables the realization of ultrafine, controllable microstructures, and remarkable mechanical properties from a mixture of oxide powders. The phase composition, microstructure, and mechanical properties of the HE-AZREAG ceramics were meticulously studied. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to unveil the orientation and interfacial relationships of the two phases. The mechanical properties, including fracture toughness, hardness, and elastic modulus, of the HE-AZREAG ceramics were scrutinized and compared with those of Al2O3-based eutectic ceramics such as Al2O3/Y3Al5O12/ZO2. These findings offer valuable insights into the fabrication methods and performance optimization of HE-AZREAG eutectic ceramics.

### 2.1 Preparation of mixed Al2O3–RE2O3–ZrO2 ceramic powder

Commercially available CT3000-SG Al2O3 (Qingdao Almatis Co., Ltd., China), XD-1024-3 ZrO2 (MCC Xindun Co., Ltd., China), Y2O3, Eu2O3, Er2O3, Yb2O3, and Lu2O3 powders (Goldcera New Material Technology Co., China) were selected as starting materials. Figure 1 shows the morphology of the initial powders. To ensure their suitability for laser energy exposure, the particle size of the powders was carefully controlled below 13 μm. Al2O3 and ZrO2 were in the nanoscale range, while the RE2O3 (RE = Y, Eu, Er, Yb, and Lu) powders exhibited a bimodal distribution spanning from 3.96 to 8.37 μm. The multimodal distribution of the powder mixture enhanced the density of powder stacking [32].

In accordance with the ternary phase diagram, a precise mixture of Al2O3, ZrO2, and RE2O3 was prepared, maintaining a molar ratio of 65.8% : 18.6% : 15.6% [28,33]. In this study, five RE2O3 (Y2O3, Eu2O3, Er2O3, Yb2O3, and Lu2O3) powders were mixed in equal molar ratios. The powders were homogenized via wet ball milling, utilizing 50 wt% deionized water as the solvent. The milling process was conducted with a planetary ball mill (QM-3SP4, Nanjing Nanda Instrument Co., Ltd., China) operating at 200 r/min, employing Al2O3 grinding balls [23]. The total duration of the ball milling process was 150 min, including 1 min pauses after every 5 min of ball milling. The mass ratio of grinding balls to powder was set at 2 : 1, with the weight ratio of grinding balls with diameters of 6, 10, and 20 mm being 2 : 5 : 9. The reflectivity of the powders was measured using a Fourier transform infrared spectroscope (FTIR, Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). As shown in Figs. 1(h) and 1(i), after ball milling, the powder particle size decreases, and the particle size *d*50 of the final mixture powder is 0.57 μm.

The slurry-based LPBF process (hereinafter referred to as LPBF) was conducted in an atmospheric environment and involved the following steps:

(1) A sanded and polished Al2O3 substrate was securely attached to the working platform, ensuring horizontal alignment. (2) The slurry obtained through ball milling was applied onto the Al2O3 substrate using a rubber scraper at a forward speed of 20 mm/s. (3) The EIHD, with a power of 1000 W, was activated to induce eddy currents in the carbon platform, generating heat that was efficiently transferred to the slurry through the Al2O3 substrate. (4) The temperature of the slurry surface was monitored using an infrared thermometer. The proportional–integral–derivative (PID) control mechanism was employed to adjust the EIHD power output, maintaining the slurry temperature at 110°C for 30 s to facilitate water evaporation. (5) A contour-free “zigzag” scanning strategy was used to fabricate HE-AZREAG eutectic ceramic samples with dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 4 mm. Based on previous works, the process parameters included a scanning speed of 80 mm/s, a laser power of 100 W, a hatch distance of 100 μm (overlap rate of ~50%), and a layer thickness of 50 μm (descent distance of substrate) [23], which ensured high densities of the formed parts.

### 2.3 Powder and sample characterization

A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Quanta FEG 250, FEI Company, USA) was used to distinguish the surface morphology of initial powders. The powder particle size distribution was measured at 25 °C using a laser particle size analyzer (Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern Instruments, UK) with dynamic light-scattering (DLS) capability. Powders were prepared in a 1.0 mg/mL aqueous solution. The data were analyzed using the Malvern Dispersion Technology Software.

The phase formation and crystal structure of the as-fabricated samples were examined using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Bruker D8, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation operating at 40 kV and 40 mA. The crystalline phases found in LPBF-fabricated ceramics were identified by comparison with the Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS)–International centre for diffraction data (ICDD) for the 2016 database [34]. The lattice constants for the XRD patterns of sample surface were refined using the Rietveld method [13]. Three-dimensional X-ray computed tomography (3D-CT) measurements were carried out using an Yxlon FF35 instrument (Germany) to determine the porosity of samples.

To examine the three-dimensional morphology and surface roughness of the sample, a 3D confocal scanning microscope (VK-X200, Keyence, Japan) was employed. For each sample, 25 data sets were collected, where each data set consists of the sampling length (*d*) and the distance between a point on the sampled profile line and the reference line (*f(x)*). Based on the surface profile and the definition of surface roughness, the sample surface roughness (*R*a) was calculated by Eq. (1).

(1) Ra=1d∫0d|f(x)|dx

where *d* represents the sampling length; *f*(*x*) denotes the distance between a point on the sampled profile line and the reference line.For microstructural analysis, the samples were ground and polished via a metallographic specimen grinder and polisher (MP-2A, Shanghai Metallographic Equipment Company Ltd., China). Initially, the samples were ground with a 1000-grit diamond grinding disc. Subsequently, the samples underwent a series of systematic mechanical polishing stages, involving sequential use of 7, 3.5, and 1 µm diamond suspensions, followed by a 50 nm SiO2 colloidal polishing suspension, to eliminate residual scratches and stress layers. In the final preparation step, the samples were cleansed in an ultrasonic bath of alcohol for 15 min. The microstructure and morphology of the samples were examined using a Quanta field SEM (GeminiSEM 300, Zeiss, Germany) in both the horizontal and vertical printing directions. Phase analysis was carried out using an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDXS) system. The size of the cellular-like shell–core structure, denoted as *d*, was determined using the area method through cross-sectional metallographic observation. In a selected SEM image, contours of more than 500 cells were traced using ImageJ.

TEM thin-film samples of longitudinal cross-sections of HE-AZREAG with a cell-like structure were prepared using a dual-beam focused ion beam (FIB) system (Helios G4 UX, Thermo Fischer, USA) to investigate the interfacial structure and nucleation mechanism. Importantly, a layer of Pt was deposited on the sample surface during the FIB process to increase the electrical conductivity. For the *in situ* preparation and observation of the longitudinal cross-section of HE-AZREAG, a dual-beam xenon ion microscope system (Helios G4 PFIB CXe, Thermo Fischer, USA) was employed, which involved the deposition of metallic tungsten, xenon ion cutting, and electron beam imaging [35]. An FEI Themis *Z* probe-corrected transmission electron microscope (TEM) was employed to characterize the microstructure, high-resolution images, and electron diffraction patterns of thin-film samples. An energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) equipped in FEI Themis *Z* TEM was utilized to investigate the elemental composition and distribution across the sample.

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was used to observe the dislocations in the HE-AZREAG samples, and geometric phase analysis (GPA) was used to confirm the two-dimensional deformation fields in the plane.

### 3.1 Phase composition analysis

Figure 3 shows the XRD patterns of the top surface of the LPBF-fabricated samples (Fig. 3(b)), compared with the patterns of the single powder and ball-milled initial mixed powders (Fig. 3(a)). In the mixed powders, peaks corresponding to Al2O3 (PDF#46-1212), m-ZrO2 (PDF#70-4430), and five different RE2O3 compounds (PDF#41-1105, #34-0392, #08-0050, #41-1106, and #43-1021) were detected. For the LPBF-fabricated samples, all the detected peaks corresponded well to α-Al2O3, c-ZrO2 (PDF#17-3962), and YAG (PDF#33-0040). Figure 3(a) reveals similar peak shapes for various RE2O3 compounds, with slight shifts in peak positions due to the differences in atomic radii of the rare-earth elements (Table 1). The XRD pattern of the ball-milled mixed powders represents a simple geometric stacking of multiple initial single-phase powders, indicating the absence of metallurgical reactions during the mixing process. Notably, the diffraction peaks originating from m-ZrO2 and five single-componential RE2O3 samples were no longer identified in the LPBF-fabricated samples. After laser powder interaction, m-ZrO2 undergoes a phase transition to c-ZrO2 above the melting point. Additionally, RE2O3 chemically reacts with Al2O3, resulting in the formation of single-phase rare-earth alumina garnet (REAG) ceramics.

### 3.2 Micromorphologies of LPBF-fabricated HE-AZREAG samples

Figures 4(a) and 4(b) illustrate the surface quality of the printed samples. Most areas are relatively smooth, as shown in Fig. 4(a), where the roughness (Ra) of this area is measured to be 6.70 μm. There are a few randomly occurring protrusions on the sample surface, as depicted in Fig. 4(b). These protrusions reduce the stability of the molten pool, resulting in increased surface undulations and increasing the average roughness (*R*a) of this area to 13.61 μm. Some transverse and vertical cracks can also be observed on the sample surface. The porosity of the sample is 0.72%, which was determined by CT scanning of the LPBF-ed sample with dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 4 mm (Fig. 4(c)). Figure 4(d) presents the number of pores of different sizes within the entire sample, along with their corresponding cumulative frequency distribution curves. The estimated median pore size (*d*50) is 18.53 μm.

As shown in Fig. 5(c), the microstructure mainly comprises three distinct phases that can be distinguished by their varied geometric shapes and colors. These phases, which act as shells, consist of a deep ebony matrix, a pale gray precipitate, and a radiant white precipitate surrounding the matrix. Together, they form a fine cellular-like shell–core microstructure, which has an average size of 2.73±0.51 μm and a shell thickness of approximately 80–950 nm. Figures 5(d)–5(k) illustrates the uniform distribution of the five rare earth elements in the shell structure, with the distributions of Al and Zr elements being mutually exclusive. In most alloying systems, the combination of different elements results in either short- or long-range ordering, an effect that is further amplified in oxides owing to their ionic covalent nature [47]. Therefore, in combination with the XRD results (Fig. 3(b)), the deep black spherical matrix in the ternary eutectic ceramic is identified asα-Al2O3 phase; while the light gray phase corresponds to HE-REAG, and the bright white phase corresponds to HE-stabilized c-ZrO2. These findings deviate markedly from the frequently observed laminar, rod-like, and Chinese character-like structural characteristics typically found in microstructures of ternary eutectic ceramics produced via directional solidification. It is widely recognized that the phase nucleation competition and the kinetics of solidification restrict the microstructure and morphology, ultimately impacting the properties of the continuous phase [48]. The generation of cellular-like microstructural elements can be ascribed to the existence of micro-vortices within the melt pool [49], which arise from the high-energy density laser beam, transient elevated temperature, and rapid cooling occurring during the LPBF process. The SEM results indicate the absence of original RE2O3 particles (with a particle size distribution of *D*50 = 3.96–8.37 μm), implying that all powders undergo complete melting within the melt pool due to laser irradiation. As a consequence of local convection influencing the material transport within the melt pool, the solute composition deviates from the eutectic ratio near the solidification front. Three phases (α-Al2O3, c-ZrO2, and RE3Al5O12) nucleate and undergo independent crystal growth. Owing to its strong nucleation ability and high stiffness, α-Al2O3 can nucleate and grow more fully during solidification [50,51]. Moreover, c-ZrO2 and RE3Al5O12 undergo ongoing collisions, fragmentation, and reorientation caused by the flow field, ultimately enveloping the Al2O3 matrix and creating a cellular membrane structure [23].

### 3.4 Formation mechanism of amorphous phase in LPBF-fabricated HE-AZREAG samples

The mechanism behind the formation of the shell–core structure in Al2O3–(Y0.2Eu0.2Er0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)3Al5O12–ZrO2 ternary high-entropy eutectic ceramics prepared using LPBF technology is illustrated in Fig. 8. The process of forming the amorphous interlayer structure in the shell–core of Al2O3–(Y0.2Eu0.2Er0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)3Al5O12–ZrO2 can be summarized as follows: nanoscale or submicron-sized multicomponent powders with a uniformly mixed eutectic molar ratio are rapidly heated and fully melted by the focused laser beam controlled by the galvanometer, resulting in the formation of a high-temperature molten pool. The molten pool subsequently undergoes heat and material exchange with both the substrate and powder bed while quenching simultaneously. This process leads to a significant temperature gradient not only between the molten pool and deposition layer but also within the molten pool itself.

*
Fig. 8Schematic diagram of formation mechanism of shell–core structure in Al2O3–(Y0.2Eu0.2Er0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)3Al5O12–ZrO2 ternary high-entropy eutectic ceramics prepared using ISLPBF technology.

The molten pool contains various elements in the form of free ions or atomic groups [53]. Owing to the similarity in solubility and the directional transport effects of localized strong flow fields, these uniformly distributed free ions or atomic groups undergo passive microscale redistribution of solvent–solute interactions within the molten pool, as extensively discussed in previous studies [23]. Additionally, atomic groups with higher coordination numbers tend to form because of their capacity to form more chemical bonds and achieve more stable electronic filling [54]. Consequently, in this experiment, RE4Al2O9 (REAM), REAlO3 (REAP), and REAG exhibit a higher propensity for crystallization. However, for Y, Er, Yb, and Lu elements, the RE–Al–O phase diagram is similar [39−42], where REAG is the stable phase and REAP and REAM are metastable phases. The enthalpies of oxide formation for YAG, YAP, and YAM at 298 K are −116.81, −23.52, and −6.3 kJ/mol, respectively [39], indicating the thermal stability order of YAG > YAP > YAM. Similarly, the thermal stability order can be deduced as REAG > REAP > REAM. Specifically, for the Eu element, the Eu–Al–O phase diagram has EuAlO3 (EAP) and Eu4Al2O9 (EAM) as stable phases, while Eu3Al5O12 (EAG) is a metastable phase [43]. The standard formation enthalpies for EAP and EAM are as Eqs. (6) and (7) [43]:

In summary, the thermodynamic stability relationship is YAG ≈ Er3Al5O12 (ErAG) ≈ Yb3Al5O12 (YbAG) ≈ Lu3Al5O12 (LuAG) ≈ EuAlO3 (EAP) ≈ Eu4Al2O9 (EAM) > REAP > REAM > Eu3Al5O12 (EAG) [39−43,55]. Therefore, other metastable transitional components formed within the molten pool, including REAP and REAM, necessitate both time and space for structural adjustment to transform into REAG.

In this process, four factors act as limitations. First, the cooling rate of the molten pool influences the time required for structural adjustment. Second, volume changes associated with solid-phase recrystallization and phase transitions limit the spatial aspects of structural adjustment. Finally, strong convection within the molten pool along with the uniform distribution of atomic groups restricts the supply of raw materials for crystal growth. Additionally, compared with other rare earth aluminates (REAGs, where RE = Y/Er/Yb/Lu), Eu3+ possesses the largest covalent radius (Table 1, r*C = 198 pm), resulting in slower ion diffusion rates. EAGs exhibit the lowest thermal stability and require more energy and time for structural adjustment from EAP or EAM to EAG. Consequently, during the quenching process of LPBF technology, the amorphous phase with a relatively high concentration of Eu elements is retained as the ordered structures REAP and REAM undergo rearrangement.

Taking all of the above analyses into account, the formation of the amorphous interlayer structure in the Al2O3–RE3Al5O12–ZrO2 shell–core is dependent on four conditions: (1) There needs to be a sufficient heat source to increase the temperature of the high-entropy ceramic powder materials, resulting in the formation of a molten pool where atoms and ions tend towards disorder. (2) Selecting nanoscale or submicron-sized powders with low melting point eutectic proportions that are evenly distributed as feedstocks is crucial for facilitating the diffusion of free atomic groups during the laser‒powder interaction process. (3) The presence of ultra-high temperature gradients drives local convection within the molten pool, allowing for the transportation of atom groups and ions with varying densities, viscosities, and melting points, thereby enabling local compositional rearrangement. (4) An extremely rapid cooling rate is necessary to prevent sufficient diffusion of atomic groups and ions within the molten pool, thereby inhibiting crystallization within the lattice structure. Regrettably, amorphous ceramics exhibit brittleness at room temperature due to the presence of strong ionic and covalent bonds, which restrict typical plasticity mechanisms [28,30]. In high-temperature application environments, these amorphous phases may undergo crystallization, resulting in volume shrinkage, microcrack formation, and the potential for reduced performance [41]. Therefore, future investigations can employ the following approaches to manipulate the fraction of the amorphous phase: (1) enhancing the laser power output; (2) reducing the size of RE2O3 powder particles; (3) modifying the preheating temperature; (4) employing high-temperature heat treatment.

#### 3.5.1 Size effect of nanoindentation

Ceramics with high hardness and Young's modulus increase the crack initiation resistance and reduce the probability of sharp contact damage. The *in situ* microstructural design, guided by laser process control and material composition coupling, directly influences the hardness and Young’s modulus of ceramics. Therefore, considering variations in load (*P*), nanoindentation testing techniques were employed with loads ranging from 0.001 N (0.0001 kg) to 9.8 N (1 kg) to measure the values of nano-hardness (*H*) and Young’s modulus (*E*) of LPBF-fabricated HE-AZREAG ceramics. The results are shown in Figs. 9(a) and 9(b). The mean values of hardness and Young’s modulus exhibited significant fluctuations at loads below 100 mN, stabilizing as the load increases from 100 to 1000 mN. Interestingly, the nano-hardness (*H*) consistently decreases as the load increases. This can be attributed to the presence of surface stress on the sample at shallow depths of indentation (less than 500 nm) when the load is relatively low [56], resulting in higher measured hardness values due to the size effect. The hardness of HE-AZREAG ceramics is intrinsic when the applied load exceeds 1000 mN and the indentation depth surpasses 1000 nm. In general, for homogeneous materials, the Young’s modulus tends to linearly decrease with increasing indentation load, which is in line with the size effect. However, in this work, the microstructure of the sample consists of cellular-like units with an average size of 2.73 μm. When the load is relatively small, the contact size between the indenter and the sample may be much smaller than the size of our cellular-like units. In such cases, factors such as the distribution of microstructural elements and interfaces may influence the Young’s modulus. As shown in Fig. 9(b), an inverse increase in the Young’s modulus is observed as the load increases from 10 to 100 mN. This can be attributed to the increase in contact size between the indenter and sample, ranging from approximately 0.2 to 3 μm, reaching the size scale of the cell-like shell–core microstructure. Previous studies have indicated that residual stress and microcracks in zirconia-toughened alumina (ZTA) likewise influence the Young’s modulus [57,58]. Hence, the interface mismatch and increased intergranular residual stress between different phases within the shell–core microstructure (Fig. 7) contribute to the enhanced intrinsic Young’s modulus of HE-AZREAG ceramics. Furthermore, the measured values of hardness and Young’s modulus exhibit a wide range of distributions and cluster phenomena at loads below 100 mN but become more concentrated as the load increases from 100 to 1000 mN. This is because, at a load of 1 mN, the contact size between the indenter and sample (~0.2 μm) is much smaller than the size of the cellular-like microstructural building ceramics. As a result, three distinct regions of hardness and Young’s modulus values corresponding to the three phases emerge: α-Al2O3 (high value), HE-RE3Al5O12 (intermediate value), and c-ZrO2 (low value).

#### 3.5.3 Toughening mechanism of LPBF-fabricated HE-AZREAG ceramics

The high hardness and Young’s modulus can be attributed to the strong cationic field of RE3+ and Zr4+ ions, along with the high dissociation energy of Al2O3, RE3Al5O12, and ZrO2 (* GAl2O3= 133.8 kJ/cm3,  GY3Al5O12 = 106.7 kJ/cm3, and  GZrO2= 97.5 kJ/cm3), combined with the complex atomic interactions resulting from diverse compositions [70,71]. The introduction of high-field strength cations can increase the coordination number of Al. Du [72], and Wang DY and Wang YH [73] reported that rare earth ions (Y3+ and Eu3+) significantly increased the proportion of highly coordinated Al in aluminates. The atomic packing density of ceramic materials increases with increasing Al coordination number and decreases with decreasing ionic volume of the oxide components. The increase in the atomic packing density can help resist the intrusion of external force, which directly improves the hardness and Young’s modulus of the samples [74]. In addition, the M–M equation [71] states that the theoretical elastic modulus of inorganic non-metallic compositions is directly related to the atomic packing density and dissociation energy of their oxide components. In terms of  Vt and  Gi, the Young’s modulus (Y*) was determined using Eq. (8):

(8) E=2Vt∑iGiXi

where * Xi is the mole percentage (%);  Vt is the total packing density factor;  Gi is the dissociation energy of the oxide. Moreover, a higher dissociation energy of oxides leads to increased bonding strength among ceramic constituent atoms, resulting in improved resistance against intra-crystalline crack propagation.

The enhanced fracture toughness can be attributed not only to the inclusion of the nanoscale high-entropy phase but also to the existence of the microscopic cellular-like shell–core structure.

On a microscopic scale, the cellular-like shell–core structure formed through in-situ* self-assembly is similar to the shell structure in nature. Among them, the Al2O3 matrix is the hard phase, ZrO2 and REAG are the nanoprecipitated particles, and the amorphous phase constitutes the soft layer. This structure plays a pivotal role in enhancing fracture toughness, as demonstrated by Bouville *et al*. [75], Yang *et al*. [76], and Sha *et al*. [77]. They designed a bioinspired ceramic material with a crystalline‒amorphous ceramic, layered structure, and submicrometer layer spacing, which exhibited remarkable strength and toughness for a ceramic material due to the high density of interfaces. During the initial elastic deformation, the applied stress transfers from the Al2O3 matrix to ZrO2, RE3Al5O12, and the amorphous phase, dissipating elastic energy and initiating Griffith brittle fracture at the tip of the indenter [78]. As the stress increases beyond the yield point, the Al2O3 matrix and shell network (composed of ZrO2, RE3Al5O12, and amorphous phase) begin to relax, slide, and incur cracking damage. On one hand, these intricate nanoscale cracks propagate primarily transgranularly within the shell–core structure (which possesses high dislocation energy), indicating strong interfacial bonding between heterogeneous phases. This process consumes a significant amount of crack propagation energy, resulting in an increase in the fracture toughness of HE-AZREAG. On the other hand, high-entropy nanoparticles experience shear stress and are pulled out from the shell, resulting in crack bridging (Fig. 11(b)) among the intact Al2O3 matrix, ZrO2, and RE3Al5O12 nanoparticles, synergistically generating a series of energy dissipation events. With intensified plastic deformation, the extension of nanocracks gradually transitions into void formations, followed by the formation of larger crack deflections. This phenomenon arises from the differences in thermal properties, such as thermal expansion coefficients and Young’s moduli, among the different phases. These differences introduce residual internal stress acting on the crack tip and thus inhibit linear crack propagation. Throughout the process, the plastic deformation produced by the amorphous phase greatly enhances the toughness through mechanisms such as cracking, cavitation, and shear yielding.

Additionally, at the nanoscale, intricate atomic interactions can enhance the fracture toughness through a phenomenon known as cocktail strengthening. To elucidate the toughening mechanism, TEM techniques were employed to observe the indentation cracks. Figure 12(a) illustrates the propagation of cracks into the interior of the matrix on a cross-sectional plane. Figure 12(b) presents a magnified view of a region in Fig. 12(a), revealing a main crack with a width of approximately 115 nm and bifurcation microcracks. After passing through HE-RE3Al5O12 and amorphous phase, the main crack deflects upon entering the Al2O3 matrix. Owing to the concentration of internal stresses determined by external loads, the bifurcation microcracks within ZrO2 propagate in a direction consistent with that of the main crack in the Al2O3 matrix. Figure 12(c) exhibits the deviation and pinning of a microcrack (with a width of approximately 25 nm) after entering the amorphous phase. Figure 12(d) further demonstrates the branching and fusion of microcracks within c-ZrO2. Figure 12(e) shows a magnified distorted lattice fringe pattern from Fig. 12(d). Dislocations are distributed near the path of microcrack, typically appearing in the form of dislocation pairs with equal but opposite Burgers vectors (denoted by solid ellipses). Figure 12(f) shows the geometric phase analysis (GPA) result corresponding to the region in Fig. 12(e), which visually and clearly confirms the strain induced by crack propagation within the nanoparticles of c-ZrO2 phase [79]. Integrating the analyses of Figs. 12(e) and 12(f) reveals that regions with elevated lattice strain levels frequently correspond to higher dislocation densities. The shell structure is composed of high-entropy garnets, fluorites, and amorphous phases. Using RE3Al5O12 as a case, diverse concentrations of rare-earth elements (ranging from 2.9% to 4.25%) lead to increased entropy. The inherent compositional fluctuations facilitate the diversity of coordinated atoms and the multiplicity of structures [5]. As cracks propagate within the lattice, extreme plastic deformation induces high local stresses, leading to the formation of dislocation pairs. The motion of dislocations involves complex atomic and energy landscapes, potentially converting crack propagation energy into stored elastic energy [80]. Furthermore, the structural complexity may increase the resistance to linear crack propagation, causing curved crack growth and ultimately decelerating the linear release of externally applied stress [81]. Notably, this phenomenon is evident in RE3Al5O12 (Fig. 11(d)) and ZrO2 (Fig. 11(g)) through slight deviations and crack bridging.

### 3.6 Case study

During the laser direct forming process of ceramic materials, the presence of transient high-temperature gradients, stress, and the amplified effects of periodic scanning strategies frequently lead to macroscopic brittle fractures and warping deformations, ultimately resulting in print failures. The utilization of the *in-situ* reaction synthesis method during LPBF process enables the formation of Al2O3–RE3Al5O12–ZrO2 ternary eutectic composite materials with a near-net shape, as well as the direct formation of high-entropy (Y*a*Eu*b*Er*c*Yb*d*Lu(1−*a*−*b*−*c*−*d*))3Al5O12 ceramics with arbitrary compositions. Figure 13 illustrates that the formed components exhibit well-defined contours, intricate shapes, and an absence of noticeable deformations, and possess excellent surface quality and interlayer bonding. This can be attributed to the powder system design, which optimizes the stability of the laser‒powder interaction, as well as the nanoscale structure adjustments and microstructure design that synergistically enhance the strength and toughness of the ceramic material. In the future, leveraging laser technologies and optimizing fabrication path planning are envisaged to further improve the distribution of thermal stress fields during the shaping process. By controlling the laser processing parameters and defocusing distances, grain refinement of ternary high-entropy ceramics is expected, leading to enhanced mechanical properties. This would position complex-structured ceramic materials as an ideal choice in various high-end manufacturing industries.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, a high-entropy Al2O3/(Y0.2Eu0.2Er0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)3Al5O12/ZrO2 ternary eutectic is successfully prepared *in situ*, with one-step slurry-based LPBF. The phase formation, microstructure morphologies, phase distribution, crystallographic characteristics, and mechanical properties are systematically investigated. The main findings of the present work could be drawn as follows:

(1) The LPBF-fabricated HE-AZREAG eutectic ceramics contain α-Al2O3, high-entropy c-ZrO2, high-entropy RE3Al5O12 garnet, and high-entropy amorphous phases. The incorporation of rare earth elements (Eu, Er, Yb, and Lu) into the crystal structures of ZrO2 and Y3Al5O12 leads to an increase in the lattice constant, resulting in a decrease in the 2*θ* values of XRD peaks.

(2) A typical microstructure with a shell–core architecture resembling cellular-like features consists of hard phases such as Al2O3, nano-toughening particles of ZrO2 and REAG, and a soft amorphous phase. The selective aggregation of constituents is the combined outcome of solute exclusion and convective transport mechanisms.

(3) Convective disturbances within the melt pool disrupt the parallelism of crystal growth orientations between the shell and core. The possible crystallographic orientation relationships inside the shell are as follows:  [1¯10]ZrO2// [111]RE3Al5O12;  (21¯1¯)RE3Al5O12// (111¯)ZrO2.

(4) During the rapid cooling of LPBF, the amorphous phase is an intermediate metastable product of crystal structure adjustment (REAP, REAM → amorphous phase → REAG). Owing to the slower diffusion rate and higher transition energy threshold of Euelements, more Eu elements are retained in the amorphousphase.

(5) Both the hardness and Young’s modulus demonstrate size effects. In particular, when the indentation approaches the size scale of the shell–core structure, an anomalous increase in Young’s modulus is observed. This increase can be attributed to the synergistic effect of multiple heterogeneous phases present within the unit cell.

(6) Compared with conventional sintering or molten-grown techniques, the LPBF-ed high-entropy Al2O3–(Y0.2Eu0.2Er0.2Yb0.2Lu0.2)3Al5O12–ZrO2 ternary eutectic ceramics demonstrate a synergistic improvement in both hardness and toughness, with a Vickers hardness ( HV) of 18.91±0.72 GPa and a  KIC of 7.83±0.47 MPa·m1/2.

(7) Structural analysis indicates that the remarkable mechanical properties can be attributed to several factors, including the substantial dissociation energy, strong cationic field, and stress transfer among Al2O3, REAG, ZrO2, and the amorphous phase. Additionally, cocktail strengthening and the plastic deformation mechanism of the high-entropy shell contribute to these properties.
