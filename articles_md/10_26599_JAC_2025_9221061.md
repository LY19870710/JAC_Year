## 1 Introduction

Laser lighting, characterized by its high brightness and superior light output, is emerging as a promising technology for various applications [1−5]. In contrast to conventional light-emitting diodes (LEDs), which suffer from efficiency droop, laser diodes (LDs) offer superior performance, making them ideal for demanding tasks such as automotive lighting, displays and projectors [6−10]. However, the integration of phosphor converters for color conversion presents a significant thermal challenge [11−17]. Under intense laser irradiation, phosphor materials experience thermal shock, leading to decreased luminous efficiency and premature saturation [18−23]. Addressing this issue by enhancing the thermal management of phosphor converters is crucial for realizing the full potential of laser lighting technology.

To address the thermal challenges inherent in phosphor converters, researchers have extensively investigated structural modifications as a promising mitigation strategy [24]. Studies by Wang *et al* [25]. and Wen *et al*. [26] have demonstrated the effectiveness of incorporating additional components into phosphor matrices to enhance thermal dissipation. Wang *et al*. [25] observed a significant reduction in the surface temperature of yttrium aluminum garnet (YAG)–Al2O3 composite ceramics under laser excitation, with increasing Al2O3 content leading to a decrease in temperature. Similarly, Wen *et al*. [26] reported a notable cooling effect in LuAG:Ce phosphor-in-glass (PiG) composites when lithium metasilicate crystals were introduced. Furthermore, Liang *et al*. [27] developed a novel phosphor-in-glass film (PiGF) architecture, combining a CASN (CaAlSiN3:Eu2+) phosphor with a sapphire substrate, resulting in a substantial reduction in the operating temperature and improved optical stability. These findings collectively highlight the potential of structural engineering to optimize phosphor converter performance, particularly in high-power applications. However, the introduction of additional components may influence luminous flux and efficiency, necessitating a careful balance between thermal management and optical performance.

Another promising approach to mitigate the thermal challenges associated with phosphor converters under intense laser excitation involves modifying their compositional characteristics. Wang *et al*. [28] conducted a comparative study of Lu2MAl4SiO12:Ce (LMAS:Ce, M = Mg, Ba) phosphor films and demonstrated that the substitution of Ba for Mg significantly reduced the surface temperature under various laser power densities. Specifically, at a power density of 4 W/mm2, the LBaAS:Ce film exhibited a notably lower temperature of 86.8 °C than the LMgAS:Ce film at 143.2 °C. Xu *et al*. [29] further explored the influence of Lu content in Lu*x*Y2.999−*x*Al5O12 phosphor ceramics, and found a direct correlation between increased Lu concentration and decreased laser spot temperature. Wang *et al*. [30] also investigated the impact of phosphor content in β-Sialon PiG films on sapphire substrates, and observed a substantial reduction in surface temperature with decreasing phosphor loading. While these studies clearly indicate that compositional engineering can effectively lower operating temperatures, it is imperative to acknowledge that the inherent limitations of phosphor converters in terms of heat dissipation continue to pose a significant hurdle for applications involving high-power laser excitation. Consequently, the development of phosphor converters with enhanced thermal management capabilities remains a critical area of research.

In this study, we aimed to optimize the thermal management and luminous performance of phosphor converters by simultaneously enhancing the crystallinity and increasing the content of Ce3+ ions in LuAG:Ce films deposited on sapphire substrates. The LuAG:Ce films were fabricated using spray pyrolysis deposition techniques. The crystallinity of the phosphor films was controlled by varying the annealing temperature, while the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio was manipulated through the annealing atmosphere. A comprehensive investigation was conducted to examine the effects of annealing temperature and atmosphere on the thermal and luminescent properties of the films. The results demonstrate that increasing the crystallinity and maintaining a high Ce3+ content are crucial for reducing the operating temperature and enhancing the luminous performance of LuAG:Ce films under high-power laser excitation. These findings provide valuable insights for optimizing the thermal management and luminous performance of phosphor converters used in laser lighting applications.

### 2.1 Synthesis of LuAG:Ce films on sapphire substrates

LuAG:Ce thin films were deposited onto sapphire substrates using a custom-built spray pyrolysis deposition system. The system consisted of a spray nozzle, a peristaltic pump, and an *XY*-axis motion platform. The precursor solution was prepared by dissolving Lu(NO3)3·6H2O, Al(NO3)3·9H2O, and Ce(NO3)3·6H2O in deionized water, resulting in a rare earth ion concentration of 0.1 mol/L and a molar ratio of rare earth ion to Al3+ ion of 3 : 5. The Ce3+ doping concentration was fixed at 1 at%. To enhance film formation, 3 wt% polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) was added to the solution. All chemicals were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Corporation, China. Prior to deposition, sapphire substrates (20 mm diameter, 1 mm thickness; Beijing Xintian Borui Photoelectric Technology Co., Ltd., China) were subjected to ultrasonic cleaning in deionized water, acetone, and ethanol.

The sapphire substrates were heated to 300 °C on a heating stage, with the temperature monitored using a K-type thermocouple. The precursor solution was atomized onto the substrates at a flow rate of 10.5 mL/min using air as the carrier gas (225 kPa). The nozzle–substrate distance was maintained at 140 mm, and the heating stage and spray nozzle were concurrently moved along the *X*- and *Y*-axes to ensure film uniformity. Each layer was deposited over 5 min, followed by annealing at 700 °C for 10 min in a muffle furnace to enhance the film quality. This process was repeated six times to achieve a final film thickness of six layers. Subsequent annealing at varying temperatures and atmospheres produced the desired LuAG:Ce films.

### 2.2 Characterization techniques

The phase composition of the thin films was determined through X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Panalytical PW 3040/60 diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation. Diffraction patterns were collected across a 2*θ* range of 10° to 90° in 0.02° steps with 1 s accumulation time per step. Images of the films were captured using a digital camera (EOS 700D, Canon). The cross-sectional microstructures of the films were characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-7001F, JEOL). Room-temperature photoluminescence (PL) and photoluminescence excitation (PLE) spectra were acquired using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (FLS1000, Edinburgh Instruments). The temperature-dependent emission spectra were measured on a Jasco FP-8600 fluorescence spectrophotometer , equipped with a 150 W xenon lamp as the excitation source, a scanning rate of 500 nm/min, and a 60 mm diameter integrating sphere. The spectral data were analyzed using specialized software to ascertain the color coordinates and correlated color temperature (CCT) of the emitted light. The internal quantum efficiency (IQE) and external quantum efficiency (EQE) were quantitatively determined through a custom-designed integrating sphere apparatus. The luminous flux (LF), luminous efficiency (LE), blue light absorption, and conversion efficiency of the films were evaluated using a sphere-spectroradiometer system (USB4000, Ocean Optics), comprising a 450 nm blue laser source (maximum power 30 W) and an integrating sphere (30 cm in diameter, Labsphere) coupled to a CCD spectrometer. A lens module focused the laser beam to a spot of approximately 0.5 mm2 on the film. An X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; Escalab 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was employed to elucidate the chemical state evolution of the cerium within the films. The surface temperature profiles of LuAG:Ce films were determined through a thermal infrared imaging system (Ti400, Fluke) during exposure to laser irradiation.

### 3.1 Structural, morphological, and optical properties of air-annealed LuAG:Ce films

Amorphous LuAG:Ce thin films were deposited onto sapphire substrates using spray pyrolysis (Fig. 1(a)). This technique offers several advantages over conventional ceramics and single-crystal growth methods, including the elimination of high-temperature vacuum sintering and specialized, expensive equipment. Additionally, spray pyrolysis enables precise control over the film thickness, facilitates large-area deposition, and is compatible with high-volume production. These factors, coupled with their simplicity and efficiency, make spray pyrolysis a promising method for the practical implementation of laser lighting technologies.

LuAG:Ce thin films, annealed across a broad temperature range of 1100–1600 °C, demonstrated a uniform, planar morphology (Fig. 1(b)), indicative of high-quality synthesis due to the absence of macroscopic defects. The optical properties were found to be annealing temperature-dependent, with increasing temperature correlating with decreased transparency and increased yellow coloration. Annealing in air at 1100–1600 °C for 5 h induced a transition from an amorphous state to a single-phase LuAG garnet structure (PDF#73-1638), as confirmed by XRD analysis (Fig. 1(c)). The minimal shift in the diffraction peak positions, consistent with prior research [31], suggests negligible lattice distortion resulting from Ce3+ substitution for Lu3+ ions. The XRD patterns revealed a progressive increase in film crystallinity with increasing annealing temperature, as evidenced by peak sharpening (Fig. 1(d)). This observation was further supported by the decreasing full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the (420) peak with increasing annealing temperature (Fig. 1(e)).

To facilitate a quantitative assessment of the relative crystallinity [32], a specific equation was utilized, as described in Eq. (1):

The relative crystallinity of the LuAG:Ce films significantly increased with increasing annealing temperature, from 75.5% at 1100 °C to 88.3% at 1600 °C (Fig. 1(f)). This observation is consistent with the expectation that elevated annealing temperatures would facilitate the development of a more crystalline microstructure. The results presented here demonstrate the feasibility of fabricating high-crystallinity LuAG:Ce films, which may offer improved luminous properties and warrant further investigation.

Cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy analysis (Fig. 1(g)) confirmed the formation of LuAG:Ce thin film with an average thickness of approximately 22.17 μm. The excellent adhesion to the sapphire substrate is likely attributed to the formation of interfacial Lu/Al compounds, as reported in Ref. [33]. Further SEM analysis (Figs. 1(h)–1(l)) revealed a relatively dense microstructure and a homogeneous distribution of Lu, Al, O, and Ce elements within the film. These structural characteristics are well-known to be conducive to efficient luminescence. Collectively, these results demonstrate the successful fabrication of LuAG:Ce thin films on sapphire substrates.

Figure 2(a) presents the PLE and PL spectra of LuAG:Ce thin films annealed at 1100–1600 °C for 5 h in air. The PLE spectra exhibited two characteristic absorption bands, which is consistent with Ref. [34]. The weaker absorption peak at 340 nm is attributed to 4f → 5d2 transitions, while the dominant peak at 450 nm is assigned to 4f → 5d1 transitions of Ce3+ ions [35]. The PLE spectral profile remained relatively invariant across the investigated annealing temperature range. The PL spectra exhibited a broad emission band centered at approximately 520 nm, corresponding to 5d → 4f transitions of Ce3+ within the LuAG host lattice [36]. The observed asymmetric band profile is consistent with the expected overlap of the 2F7/2 and 2F5/2 sublevels of the 4f configuration [37]. The PL intensity increased with increasing annealing temperature, reaching an optimum at 1500 °C, where the emission intensity was approximately 3.1 times greater than that observed for the film annealed at 1100 °C. XPS analysis (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) revealed minimal variation in the Ce3+ concentration, increasing only from 33.9% (1100 °C) to 35.9% (1500 °C), suggesting that the observed enhancement in luminescence is attributable to improved crystallinity [38]. Deconvolution of the PL spectra into Gaussian components (Fig. 2(b)) resolved two distinct peaks at 500 nm (20,018 cm−1) and 547 nm (18,273 cm−1), which is consistent with the expected energy separation of 1745 cm−1 between the 5d1 → 2F7/2 and 5d1 → 2F5/2 transitions.

A comparative examination of PL and PLE intensities reveals a substantial decrease at an annealing temperature of 1600 °C, in contrast to the LuAG:Ce film annealed at 1500 °C. XPS analysis, presented in Fig. S2 and Table S1 in the ESM, indicates a considerable reduction in the Ce3+ concentration in the LuAG:Ce film annealed at 1600 °C in air, measuring 30.2%, compared with 35.9% in the LuAG:Ce film annealed at 1500 °C. The increased oxidation of Ce3+ in the LuAG:Ce film annealed at 1600 °C in air accounts for the observed reduction in the PL and PLE intensities, as shown in Fig. 2(a) . Figure 2(c) illustrates the IQE and EQE as a function of annealing temperature. Both the IQE and EQE increased with increasing annealing temperature, peaking at 1500 °C, with values of 65.1% and 32.1%, respectively. However, a slight decrease in both IQE and EQE was observed at 1600 °C, coincident with the reduction in the PL and PLE intensities (Fig. 2(a)). The chromaticity coordinates (Fig. 2(d)), derived from the PL spectra, were located within the yellow-green region of the 1931 CIE chromaticity diagram, exhibiting a narrow distribution centered at (0.33, 0.55). A slight decrease in the CCT from 5492 to 5464 K was observed with increasing annealing temperature (1100–1600 °C), indicating a minimal effect of annealing temperature on luminescent color. Collectively, the analysis of the luminescence intensity, chromaticity coordinates, and CCT suggests that the LuAG:Ce film annealed at 1500 °C exhibits optimal photoluminescent properties, demonstrating a favorable balance between spectral emission and color quality.

### 3.2 Laser-driven lighting performance of air-annealed LuAG:Ce films

To investigate the laser-driven lighting performance of LuAG:Ce films under high-power-density blue laser excitation, a custom-built reflective setup was employed (Fig. 3(a)). The setup consisted of a blue laser diode (maximum power 30 W) coupled to a lens system that focused the laser beam (wavelength 450 nm) onto the film surface with a spot size of 0.5 mm2, an integrating sphere (diameter 30 cm) that collected the reflected light, and a USB4000 spectrometer that measured the electroluminescence (EL) spectra.

*
Fig. 3Laser-driven lighting performance in reflective mode. (a) Schematic representation of experimental setup utilizing a 450 nm blue laser to excite LuAG:Ce films. (b) Luminous flux, (c) luminous efficiency, (d) blue light absorption, and (e) conversion efficiency of LuAG:Ce films as a function of laser power density. The LuAG:Ce films were annealed at temperatures ranging from 1100 to 1600 °C for 5 h in air.

The influence of air annealing temperature on the blue laser-driven lighting performance of LuAG:Ce films was investigated. As illustrated in Fig. 3(b), the luminous flux of all the films increased with increasing laser power density but subsequently decreased beyond a threshold due to thermal quenching at elevated temperatures under high-power-density excitation [39]. The annealing temperature significantly affected the luminous flux. The 1100 °C annealed film exhibited the lowest luminous flux (139.8 lm at 16 W/mm2), likely due to its lower crystallinity. Conversely, the film annealed at 1500 °C presented the highest luminous flux (490.8 lm at 13 W/mm2), presumably due to its higher crystallinity. These results suggest that increasing the annealing temperature effectively enhances film crystallinity and, consequently, luminous performance. The luminous efficiency (the ratio of luminous flux to incident laser power) was investigated as a function of the laser power density (Fig. 3(c)), revealing a consistent decrease with increasing power density. The annealing temperature also significantly influenced the luminous efficiency. Notably, increasing the annealing temperature from 1100 to 1500 °C under 13 W/mm2 laser excitation resulted in a remarkable 4.1-fold enhancement in luminous efficiency, increasing from 18.2 to 75.2 lm/W. However, a subsequent reduction to 57.6 lm/W was observed at 1600 °C, which was attributed to further Ce3+ oxidation. These findings highlight the superior luminescence characteristics of the LuAG:Ce film annealed at 1500 °C, which exhibits optimal luminous flux and luminous efficiency.

Figure 3(d) illustrates the correlation between blue light absorption (denoted as η*Ab) and incident laser power density. Specifically, the parameter *η*Ab represents the fraction of absorbed blue laser power relative to the incident power. This quantification relies on Eq. (2):

(2) ηAb=Pin−PrePin=PabPin

where *P*in denotes the incident blue laser power; *P*re represents the remaining blue laser power; *P*ab signifies the absorbed blue laser power. The blue light absorption quantifies the material’s capacity to absorb blue light under varying excitation conditions. Generally, a direct correlation exists between blue light absorption and luminous performance, with higher absorption values indicative of superior luminous properties. The LuAG:Ce films exhibited a consistent decrease in blue light absorption as the incident laser power density increased. Furthermore, the annealing temperature significantly influenced blue light absorption. Films annealed at higher temperatures demonstrated enhanced blue light absorption under identical laser excitation conditions, likely due to improved crystallinity. Remarkably, the film annealed at 1500 °C demonstrated the maximum blue light absorption, reaching 59.9% and 42.2% for laser power densities of 1 and 13 W/mm2, respectively. These findings suggest that elevated annealing temperatures effectively enhance blue light absorption in LuAG:Ce films, potentially leading to improved luminous properties.

The conversion efficiency (*η*CE), quantified as the ratio of converted yellow-green light power to the absorbed blue light power (Fig. 3(e)), can be determined using Eq. (3) [40]:

(3) ηCE=PemPin−Pre=PemPab

where *P*in is the incident blue laser power; *P*re is the remnant blue laser power; *P*ab is the absorbed blue laser power; *P*em is the converted yellow-green light power. Conversion efficiency serves as a metric to evaluate the ability of a material to convert absorbed blue photons into yellow-green photons. A positive relationship is generally observed between the conversion efficiency and luminescence performance, where higher efficiencies are associated with increased yellow-green light emission. Across all the LuAG:Ce thin films studied, a consistent decrease in conversion efficiency was noted as the incident laser power density increased. This decline is potentially linked to thermal quenching mechanisms, which are intensified at higher excitation power densities because of increased local heating. Additionally, the annealing temperature emerged as a significant factor affecting the conversion efficiency. In particular, LuAG:Ce films annealed at elevated temperatures exhibited improved conversion efficiencies, presumably due to improvements in crystallinity. The maximum conversion efficiency was recorded for the films annealed at 1500 °C, reaching 29.4% and 21.2% under laser excitations of 1 and 13 W/mm2, respectively.

The thermal response of LuAG:Ce thin films under high-power laser excitation is crucial for their operational stability and performance. To investigate this, thermal infrared imaging was employed to analyze the temperature profiles during 60 s of exposure to blue laser irradiation at varying power densities. A direct correlation was observed between the incident power density and film temperature (Fig. 4(a)). Specifically, LuAG:Ce films annealed at 1500 °C exhibited a temperature increase from 52.2 to 278.3 °C as the laser power density increased from 4 to 18 W/mm2. In contrast, the films annealed at lower temperatures (1100 and 1200 °C) displayed more pronounced temperature rises, reaching 373.9 and 355.4 °C, respectively, under identical conditions. A clear trend emerged: higher annealing temperatures (1100 to 1500 °C) correlated with lower laser spot temperatures, a finding corroborated by thermographic data (Fig. 4(a)). Figure 4(b) illustrates the temporal evolution of the film temperature as a function of the power density. Below 5 W/mm2, all the films exhibited minimal temperature increases (Fig. 4(c)). The film annealing at 1500 °C was associated with the lowest substrate temperatures. Over the 1–18 W/mm2 power density range, the substrate temperature increased from 29.6 to 206.5 °C. The efficient heat dissipation of the sapphire substrate contributed to the enhanced thermal stability of the LuAG:Ce thin films under high-power laser excitation.

### 3.3 Laser-driven lighting performance of CO-annealed LuAG:Ce films

A comprehensive analysis of the luminescence properties of LuAG:Ce films, as depicted in Fig. 3, reveals that the annealing temperature of 1500 °C is optimal for achieving the desired luminescent characteristics. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that annealing in air can lead to partial oxidation of Ce3+ to Ce4+, resulting in degradation of luminescence properties [41]. To mitigate this oxidation process and maintain the desired Ce3+ oxidation state, a reducing CO atmosphere was employed during the annealing process. Consequently, two sets of LuAG:Ce films were annealed at 1500 °C for 5 h under distinct annealing environments: air and CO. This experimental design was implemented to systematically investigate the impact of the annealing environment on the luminescence properties of the fabricated LuAG:Ce films.

The influence of the annealing atmosphere on the laser-excited luminescence of LuAG:Ce films was investigated. The EL spectra of LuAG:Ce films annealed at 1500 °C in air and CO were acquired under blue laser excitation at varying power densities (Figs. 5(a) and 5(b)). Spectral analysis revealed two distinct emission peaks: a peak at 450 nm, attributed to unabsorbed excitation light, and a broad band between 480 and 650 nm, corresponding to yellow-green emission. The intensity of the yellow-green emission was positively correlated with increasing blue laser power density, saturating at 13 W/mm2 for both annealing conditions. Critically, no significant reduction in yellow-green emission intensity was observed beyond this threshold, indicating robust high-power-density laser irradiation stability without substantial thermal quenching. Furthermore, the CO-annealed LuAG:Ce film exhibited superior luminescence performance across the investigated power densities, demonstrating an approximately 1.5-fold increase in luminescence intensity compared with that of its air-annealed counterpart at 13 W/mm2. Luminous flux, which is consistent with the observed emission intensities, increased with increasing excitation power density and saturated at 13 W/mm2 (Fig. 5(c)), with the CO-annealed film consistently exhibiting greater luminous flux. Specifically, at saturation, the CO-annealed film achieved a luminous flux of 850.3 lm, a 73.3% enhancement over the air-annealed film (490.7 lm). Analysis of luminous efficiency (Fig. 5(d)) revealed a distinct trend. The luminous efficiency of the air-annealed film decreased from 110.8 to 75.2 lm/W with increasing power density (1 to 13 W/mm2). Conversely, the CO-annealed film maintained a relatively stable luminous efficiency, ranging from 190.8 to 130.8 lm/W across the same power density range. At the saturation threshold (13 W/mm2), the CO-annealed film demonstrated a 73.9% higher luminous efficiency. This enhanced performance is consistent with previous reports [42] and suggests that the reducing CO atmosphere facilitates the efficient reduction of Ce4+ to Ce3+, thereby optimizing the luminescence properties of LuAG:Ce films. These findings underscore the importance of controlling the annealing atmosphere for tailoring the optical characteristics of these materials.

XPS was employed to determine the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratios in LuAG:Ce films subjected to annealing in air and CO atmospheres. This reduction was evidenced by decreased PL and PLE intensities (Fig. S4 in the ESM), as well as reductions in luminous flux, luminous efficiency, blue light absorption, and conversion efficiency (Fig. S5 in the ESM). Morphological analysis (Fig. S7 in the ESM) revealed evidence of over-sintering in the film annealing at 1600 °C, potentially explaining the decline in luminescent properties.

The thermal management of light conversion materials is crucial for optimizing device performance. Therefore, the thermal characteristics of LuAG:Ce films subjected to high-power-density laser excitation were investigated following annealing at 1500 °C for 5 h in air and CO atmospheres. Laser spot temperatures were measured via thermal infrared imaging. Across a laser power density range of 2–14 W/mm2, the air-annealed film exhibited an increase in the laser spot temperature from 39.6 to 189.6 °C, while the CO-annealed film temperature increased from 38.2 to 162.5 °C (Fig. 7). Thermographic analysis revealed a consistently lower laser spot temperature for the CO-annealed film across the investigated power densities (Fig. 7(a)). A comparative analysis of the laser spot temperature as a function of power density (Fig. 7(b)) demonstrated negligible temperature differentials below 3 W/mm2, with a progressively increasing divergence above this threshold. At 13 W/mm2, the air-annealed film reached 160.7 °C, while the CO-annealed film reached 140.2 °C, a difference of approximately 20 °C. Critically, no thermal runaway was observed. These results indicate the superior thermal performance of the CO-annealed LuAG:Ce film. This enhancement is attributed to an increased Ce3+ concentration, rather than variations in crystallinity, as demonstrated by comparable crystallinity (Fig. S3 in the ESM). The elevated Ce3+ content promotes yellow-green light emission while concurrently mitigating heat generation. Temporal analysis of the laser spot temperature under 13 W/mm2 irradiation (Fig. 7(c)) revealed a rapid initial temperature increase within the first 30 s, stabilizing at approximately 159.0 and 140.2 °C for the air- and CO-annealed films, respectively, with the air-annealed film exhibiting a more pronounced initial rise. After 1800 s, infrared thermal imaging confirmed these steady-state values, with values of 161.8 and 140.9 °C for the air- and CO-annealed films, respectively, maintaining the ~20 °C differential. Further investigation of the temperature-dependent photoluminescence (Fig. S8 in the ESM) revealed superior thermal stability for the CO-annealed film, retaining 86.6% of its room temperature luminescence intensity at 150 °C, compared with 84.8% for the air-annealed film. Collectively, these findings underscore the excellent thermal stability of both film types under high-power-density irradiation while demonstrating the reduced operating temperature of the CO-annealed LuAG:Ce film.

The significant discrepancies in the thermal behavior of air-annealed and CO-annealed films subjected to high-power-density laser irradiation warrant in-depth investigation. The air-annealed film consistently exhibited a higher surface temperature and a more pronounced initial temperature rise within the first 30 s. To quantitatively analyze these phenomena, heat power is introduced, as defined by Eq. (4), as a suitable metric for characterizing the thermal energy generated under the specified conditions.

(4) Pheat=Pin−Pre−Pem

where P*heat is the heat power; *P*in is the incident blue laser power; *P*re is the remnant blue laser power; *P*em is the converted yellow-green light power. The heat power generated by both the air- and CO-annealed films increased with increasing laser intensity. However, across the range of intensities tested, particularly at higher intensities, the CO-annealed film consistently exhibited a lower heat power (Fig. 7(d)). This reduction is attributable to the higher Ce3+ concentration in the CO-annealed film. The Ce3+ ions promote efficient conversion of blue laser light to yellow-green luminescence, thus minimizing the proportion of absorbed energy converted to heat. Consequently, the CO-annealed film demonstrated a diminished temperature increase under high-power laser irradiation, reflected in its lower heat power. Furthermore, the LuAG:Ce thin films developed herein demonstrate a favorable combination of high luminous flux, high saturation threshold, and low operating temperature (Table S3 in the ESM) compared with previously reported light conversion materials for laser lighting, positioning them as promising candidates for such applications.

Luminous stability is a critical performance parameter for light conversion materials employed in laser lighting, directly impacting device reliability and efficiency. To investigate this property, temperature-dependent luminous flux measurements were performed on LuAG:Ce films annealed at 1500 °C for 5 h under both air and CO atmospheres (Fig. 8(a)). A custom-built integrating sphere system with a heating stage was utilized (inset of Fig. 8(a)). Excitation was provided by a 1 W/mm2 blue laser, with the LuAG:Ce film temperature varying from 25 to 250 °C. EL spectra were acquired after a 3-min stabilization period at each temperature, from which the luminous flux was calculated. Both the air- and CO-annealed films exhibited a decrease in luminous flux with increasing temperature, which is consistent with established thermal quenching mechanisms [44]. However, the CO-annealed film demonstrated superior luminous stability. At 150 °C and 1 W/mm2 excitation, the CO-annealed film exhibited a luminous flux of 82.1 lm, retaining 86.1% of its room-temperature value, whereas the air-annealed film registered only 45.6 lm (82.3% of the initial value). Figure 8(b) compares the luminous stability of the LuAG:Ce films under a higher excitation power density of 13 W/mm2. Following 30 s of irradiation, the luminous flux of the air-annealed film decreased to 388.7 lm and stabilized at 375.6 lm (76.5% of the initial value) after 1800 s. Under identical conditions, the CO-annealed film retained 764.1 lm after 30 s and 740.5 lm (87.1% of the initial value) after 1800 s. These results demonstrate a substantial increase in luminous stability for CO-annealed LuAG:Ce films under high-power-density laser excitation. Post-irradiation analysis (1800 s) revealed uniform yellow coloration without observable cracking, peeling, or surface damage in either film (insets of Fig. 8(b)), indicating excellent laser damage resistance and suitability for high-power laser applications.

The temporal evolution of color coordinates under 13 W/mm2 excitation for 1800 s is presented in Figs. 9(e) and 9(f). The color coordinates of both films demonstrated high stability over this period, further confirming their suitability for high-power-density laser applications. These results underscore the potential of LuAG:Ce films as stable, high-brightness color converters for laser-driven lighting.

## 4 Conclusions

The operational temperatures of LuAG:Ce films on sapphire substrates under high-power laser excitation were significantly reduced by enhancing their crystallinity and increasing the Ce3+ content. This improvement resulted in enhanced thermal stability and luminous performance. Specifically, air-annealed LuAG:Ce films with a crystallinity of 87.4% exhibited a 95.6 °C lower operating temperature than those with a crystallinity of 75.5% under 18 W/mm2 blue laser excitation. Additionally, CO-annealed films with a higher Ce3+ content (46.1%) demonstrated a 20 °C lower laser spot temperature than air-annealed films (35.9%) at a power density of 13 W/mm2. These findings underscore the critical roles of crystallinity and Ce3+ content in determining the thermal and luminous properties of LuAG:Ce films. Furthermore, our results indicate that crystallinity significantly influences the saturation threshold, while the Ce3+ content primarily affects the blue light absorption and conversion efficiency. The synergistic effect of enhanced crystallinity and Ce3+ content has led to superior thermal and luminous stability under prolonged high-power laser excitation, making these materials promising candidates for the development of high-performance color converters used in laser lighting applications.
