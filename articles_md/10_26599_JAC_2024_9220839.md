## 1 Introduction

Switchgears play an indispensable role in various sectors, including industrial manufacturing [1], energy and environment [2], military and national defense [3], and aerospace [4]. Central to their performance and service life are the core components—electrical contacts [5,6]. Among these, Ag-based electrical contacts are widely used in low-voltage switching devices. However, the conventional material, Ag/CdO, is facing limitations due to Cd’s toxicity [7,8]. Alternative reinforcing phases, such as SnO2, ZnO, CuO, C, and Ni, have been explored as substitutes for CdO in Ag-based electrical contacts. Yet, their inherent structural and property shortcomings hinder optimal electrical performance [8−18]. As such, the development of new electrical contact materials is imminent, and the core work has been focused on the exploration of new reinforcing phase materials.

MAX phases, a class of layered ternary compounds that exhibit unique properties combining both metallic and ceramic characteristics, exhibit great potential as reinforcing phase in Ag-based electrical contacts. Defined by the term “MAX”, M corresponds to early transition metals, A denotes group A elements, and X symbolizes either carbon or nitrogen. Their general chemical formula is M*n*+1AX*n*, with *n* represents the number of layers. In recent years, researchers have conducted extensive exploratory research on utilizing MAX phases, such as Ti3AlC2, Ti3SiC2, Ti2AlC, and Ti2SnC, to reinforce Ag-based electrical contacts [19−29]. Compared to the reinforcing phases of oxides and carbides, the MAX phase offers superior electrical and thermal conductivity [29]. This ensures enhanced performance, particularly in maintaining lower contact resistance and minimized temperature increases during the service process. In addition, it is established that the A–Ag interdiffusion behavior of Ag/MAX composite induced by preparing and arc temperature assists in improving the interfacial bonding and mechanical properties [19,30].

Compared to traditional Cd-containing and Cd-free reinforcing phases used in electrical contacts, the MAX phase is distinguished by its unique layered structure and the inherent instability of its A atomic layer. Furthermore, at high temperatures, the MAX phase undergoes a “decomposition–oxidation” process, which manifests in distinct features such as self-healing, diminished arc energy, and a delay in arc destruction [26,31]. These attributes give it unparalleled advantages in its application to Ag-based electrical contacts. However, to date, the Ag/MAX system materials demand substantial research. This encompasses understanding and modulating the interface behavior, achieving a comprehensive balance between mechanical behavior and electrical performance, refining the microstructural design for extended service life, and deeply probing the anti-arc erosion mechanism. In the realm of anti-arc erosion theory, while there has been a concentrated effort on examining surface phase transitions, element distribution, and morphology evolution in Ag/MAX after arc discharge [22,29,32]. However, the underlying interaction mechanism between the MAX phase itself and the arc has yet to be thoroughly elucidated in depth. Drawing on the nanoindentation technique, our team recently undertook a preliminary study of arc-induced damage on Ag/MAX system material during the intermediate stage of arc erosion from the perspective of the macro/micro/nano-mechanical behavior evolution [30]. However, the inherent relationship between mechanical degradation and structural evolution of the MAX phase from the early to the later stages of arc erosion remains unclear.

Herein, we delved into the nano-mechanical properties as well as the microstructural and chemical evolution of Ag/Ti3AlC2 electrical contacts under arc erosion using a combination of nanoindentation and cross-sectional characterizations. Our arc erosion analysis of the Ag/Ti3AlC2 material spanned three distinct stages: early, intermediate, and later stages. Experimentally, we highlighted the non-uniformity of the nano-mechanical properties within the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase using continuous stiffness measurements. Concurrently, our data indicated that the structural decomposition of Ti3AlC2 and its surface oxidation were responsible for the variances observed in the material's nano-mechanical properties across different stages of arc erosion.

### 2.1 Preparation and basic properties of sample

Ti3AlC2 ceramic powders (> 99% purity, ~20 μm in size) prepared in-house, and Ag powders (> 99% purity, ~10 μm in size, XSF Technology, China), were thoroughly mixed with the mass ratio 1:9 by ball milling, and then the mixed powders were dried and pressed into a rectangular green body (size 30 mm × 40 mm) at 120 ℃ under 800 MPa, followed by heat treatment under Ar gas protection (99.999% purity) at 800 ℃ for 2 h. Employing the methodology described in Ref. [33], the density and electrical resistivity of as-prepared samples were measured as 8.80 kg/m3 and 5.46×10−2 μΩ∙m, respectively.

### 2.2 Arc erosion test

The as-prepared bulk sample was cut into disks with 7 mm in diameter and 1.5 mm in thickness. These disks were then polished and welded to copper contact bridge to form electrical contact components. Subsequently, the obtained contact components were installed in a commercial contactor for arc erosion test. For arc erosion test, the incremental arc discharging (1, 10, 100, 1000, and 6200 times) were carried out on the surface of the electrical contacts with the following parameters: AC-3 inductive load, 400 V/100 A current, 50 Hz coil frequency, 600 times/h operation frequency, air atmosphere, and room temperature.

### 2.3 Nanoindentation measurements

The mechanical properties of Ti3AlC2 within the Ag/Ti3AlC2 electrical contacts were evaluated using a standard Berkovich indenter (KLA, iNano, USA) after subjecting the electrical contacts to varying arc discharging times. To safeguard the integrity of the indenter, all indentation points were carefully positioned in the near arc erosion zone of the cross-section in the contacts (Fig. 1(a)).

### 2.4 Morphology and composition characterization

The cross-sectional morphologies of Ag/Ti3AlC2 contacts after incremental arc discharging were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, MIRA3 XMU, Tescan, Czech Republic). Furthermore, energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, AZtecOneX-Max20, OXFORD Instruments, UK) was utilized to analyze the element distribution on the cross sections of these contact samples.

#### 3.1.1 Elasticity and plasticity

The investigation of elasticity and plasticity characteristics at different arc erosion stages offers valuable insights into understanding the resistance of Ti3AlC2 to external damage [34]. In Fig. 2, load–depth indentation curves obtained from constant loading rate indentation tests performed on Ti3AlC2 within the Ag/Ti3AlC2 composites after different arc erosion times are presented. In these tests, a gradual increase in load from 0 to 20 mN at a rate of 1 mN/s was applied, and the resulting maximum indentation depth (*h*m) and springback depth (*h*f) were measured. Here, *h*m refers to how deep the indenter penetrates into the material under the applied load, while *h*f indicates how much the material recovers after the load is removed. Based on the parameters *h*m and *h*f, the plastic depth (*ξ*) and elastic depth (*δ*) were calculated and incorporated into Fig. 2 [30]. Clearly, *ξ* exhibited an upward trend with increasing arc erosion times, suggesting that Ti3AlC2 underwent increasing permanent deformation and lost its ability to recover its original shape as the arc erosion continued. This reflects the progressive mechanical degradation of Ti3AlC2 at different arc erosion stages, consistent with the observed variations in *h*m and *h*f. In addition, we observed a decelerating increase in the parameter *ξ* as arc erosion times increased, suggesting that the degree of plastic deformation reduced, and the material’s ability to resist plastic deformation improved, which is beneficial for its long-time performance in practical applications.

#### 3.1.2 Nano-hardness and nano-modulus

The load–depth curves obtained from constant loading rate indentation measurements, encompassing the loading, holding, and unloading stages, were subjected to data analysis to obtain nano-hardness and nano-modulus using the method proposed by Oliver and Pharr [35]. Based on the data presented in Fig. 3, the nano-hardness and nano-modulus of Ti3AlC2 exhibited a declining trend with increasing arc erosion times. As the arc erosion times increase from 1 to 6200, the nano-hardness decreased from 2.17 to 1.70 GPa, and the nano-modulus decreased from 112.27 to 94.77 GPa. This observation suggests a progressive nano-mechanical degradation of Ti3AlC2, indicating structural changes in the reinforcing phase due to cumulative arc erosion.

#### 3.1.3 Indentation creep

Creep refers to a time-dependent phenomenon in materials where deformation takes place gradually under a constant load or stress. It serves as a crucial indicator of a material’s capacity to withstand plastic deformation when subjected to prolonged loading conditions, making it a crucial parameter in engineering and materials science.

To study the creep behavior of Ti3AlC2 within the Ag base, nanoindentation creep measurements were conducted at room temperature by applying a load of 20 mN for a duration of 120 s. Figures 4(a)–4(e) illustrate the creep curves of Ti3AlC2 within the Ag base after different arc erosion times. Obviously, the creep depth of Ti3AlC2 in the composite exhibited a substantial increase first, and then gradually leveled off as the holding time increased, meaning that Ti3AlC2 experienced rapid deformation during the early stages of loading, which eventually stabilized as the loading continued. Since the most straightforward way to quantify nanoindentation creep is by measuring the “maximum creep depth (*d*max)”, representing the deepest indentation point during the creep phase [36,37], Fig. 4(f) shows the relationship between the maximum creep depth and arc erosion times. Notably, the maximum creep depth of Ti3AlC2 ranged from 15.06 nm after arc erosion of 1 time to 28.58 nm after arc erosion of 6200 times, indicating a degradation in the material’s ability to endure prolonged loading and deformation. These findings are consistent with nano-hardness/modulus and elastic/plastic behavior analysis, suggesting a deterioration in the material’s mechanical properties with increasing arc erosion times. Furthermore, as the arc erosion times increased, the rate of increase in the maximum creep depth gradually decreased, pointing to persistent resistance to deformation. This observation aligns well with our earlier findings on the material’s plastic deformation behavior.

#### 3.1.4 Continuous stiffness measurements

As we know, constant loading rate indentation and indentation creep experiments offer only average insights into the nano-mechanical properties of Ti3AlC2 in Ag/Ti3AlC2 structure. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the indentation process, continuous stiffness measurements were conducted, and the specific parameters for the experimental setup were tabulated in Table 1.

The results in Fig. 5(a) depict the evolution of nano-hardness and nano-modulus of Ti3AlC2 within the Ag base after a single arc erosion event during the indentation process, as obtained from the CSM technique. Notably, both nano-hardness and nano-modulus decreased with increasing indentation depth, indicating the presence of inhomogeneity in the nano-mechanical properties within the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase. Remarkably, the inner region of the reinforcing phase, particularly the area near the center, exhibited superior performance compared to the outer or edge region, suggesting there was a difference in structure or composition between these two regions of Ti3AlC2. And the same trend was observed in other samples subjected to varying arc erosion times of 10, 100, 1000, and 6200 (Figs. 5(b)–5(e)). Moreover, with increasing arc erosion times, both the nano-hardness and nano-modulus of the entire region of the reinforcing phase exhibited a declining trend, indicating cumulative arc erosion progressively damaged the nano-mechanical properties of both the inner and outer regions of Ti3AlC2.

Specifically, in Fig. 5(f), the nano-hardness and nano-modulus evolution of the inner and outer regions of Ti3AlC2 were presented. It was observed that while nano-hardness gradually decreased with increasing arc erosion times, the degree of decrease became smaller and smaller, indicating an improved ability of Ti3AlC2 to resist damage. Similar trend was observed in the nano-modulus evolution of the two regions of Ti3AlC2. This finding corroborates with the results obtained from the previous constant loading rate indentation and creep experiments.

Furthermore, we observed that the degree of mechanical degradation was different between the inner and outer regions of the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase. The hardness of the inner region of Ti3AlC2 decreased from an initial value of 24.12 to 5.90 GPa, representing a reduction of approximately 76% after one time of arc erosion up to 6200 times of arc erosion. In contrast, the hardness of the outer region only decreased by 40%. This finding suggests that arc erosion has differing effects on the mechanical performance of the reinforcing phase in both the inner and outer regions.

### 3.2 Composition characterization of the inner and outer regions of Ti<sub>3</sub>AlC<sub>2</sub>

In order to explore the underlying cause of the difference in nano-mechanical properties between the inner and outer regions of Ti3AlC2 within the Ag/Ti3AlC2 structure, composition characterization was conducted on the near arc erosion zone of the cross-sectional samples after different arc erosion times.

Figure 6 shows the SEM images and the corresponding EDS mapping of the cross-sectional Ag/Ti3AlC2 samples. In the case of sample after arc erosion of 1 time, the interface between the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase and the Ag base was clear and distinct. However, as the arc erosion times increased, it became evident that Al diffused progressively from the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase into the Ag base, while simultaneously, Ag diffused from the base into Ti3AlC2, aligning well with the previous observations [29]. Notably, as depicted in Fig. 6(e), a transition zone formed between Ti3AlC2 and the Ag base when the composite underwent arc erosion of 6200 times. To be more precise, the transition zone was situated within the outer region of the reinforcing phase, Ti3AlC2.

*
Fig. 6SEM images and corresponding EDS mapping images of the cross-sectional Ag/Ti3AlC2 samples after arc erosion of (a) 1, (b) 10, (c) 100, (d) 1000, and (e) 6200 times.

In order to study in detail the transition zone between the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase and the Ag base within Ag/Ti3AlC2 samples under different arc erosion conditions, EDS line scanning experiments were conducted and the results are shown in Fig. 7. Interestingly, a transition zone of approximately 1.07 μm in thickness between Ti3AlC2 and the Ag base was already observable in the original, uneroded sample. This can be attributed to the heat treatment process during sample preparation. The heat treatment process led to slight decomposition of Ti3AlC2, accompanied by elemental diffusion of Ag and Al at the interface, thereby enhancing the interfacial bonding between the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase and the Ag base, as well as improving the overall density of the composite [38]. The composition of the transition zone differed from that of both the reinforcing phase and the base, which should account for the disparity in mechanical properties between the inner and outer regions of Ti3AlC2 (as shown in Fig. 5). Additionally, the average thickness of the transition zone increased as the arc erosion times increased, starting from the initial 1.15 μm after a single arc erosion event and reaching 2.04 μm after arc erosion of 6200 times. The underlying reason for this phenomenon is likely that as the arc erosion times increased, the high temperatures introduced by arc discharging accelerated atomic diffusion within the Ag/Ti3AlC2 and consequently promoted the gradual decomposition of the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase. This decomposition was initiated by the deintercalation of Al, leading to the formation of TiCx* [19]. Simultaneously, Ag diffused into Ti3AlC2, giving rise to the formation of a solid solution referred to as Ag(Al) [19,29,39]. This explanation offered insight into the progressive deterioration in the mechanical properties of Ti3AlC2 as the arc erosion times increased (Fig. 5). Moreover, it explained the different degree of mechanical degradation observed within the inner and outer regions of the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase (Fig. 5(f)). However, even after undergoing arc erosion of 6200 times, the inner region of the reinforcing phase managed to retain its structural integrity and maintained a composition closely resembling that of Ti3AlC2.

#### 3.3.3 Microstructure characterization of Ti<sub>3</sub>AlC<sub>2</sub> in Ag base

To achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the nano-hardness evolution in the Ag/Ti3AlC2 material subjected to arc erosion, SEM was utilized to examine the microstructure of the Ti3AlC2 in Ag base after arc erosion of 1, 10, 100, 1000, and 6200 times. Figure 9 shows the SEM-acquired secondary electron images, showcasing different zones within the cross-sectional samples. After arc erosion of 1 time, only two phases were evident within three zones of the samples: the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase and the Ag base (Figs. 9(a1)–9(a3)). Upon increasing the arc erosion times to 10, dark aggregates emerged in the arc erosion zone, while no discernible changes were observed in the other two zones (Figs. 9(b1)–9(b3)). As the arc erosion times increased to 100, dark aggregates became more prominent in arc erosion zone and near arc erosion zone, and cracks was detected at the interface between Ti3AlC2 and Ag base (Figs. 9(c1)–9(c3)). As the arc discharging times further increased to 1000, more dark aggregates and cracks were observed in both arc erosion zone and near arc erosion zone, no obvious changes of Ti3AlC2 were found in distant zone (Figs. 9(d1)–9(d3)). Remarkably, after undergoing arc erosion of 6200 times, the samples displayed an increased concentration of dark aggregates surrounding Ti3AlC2 in arc erosion zone and near arc erosion zone, while virtually no cracks present in any of the two zones [31,40]. However, a small number of dark aggregates were first observed in distant zone (Figs. 9(e1)–9(e3)), which further indicate that the long-time arc temperature cycle has a gradual impact on the whole Ag/Ti3AlC2 material. According to our previous study, these dark aggregates were oxides composed of Al2O3 and TiO2, resulting from the decomposition and subsequent oxidation of Ti3AlC2 [29]. These oxides appeared to play a pivotal role in filling cracks and enhancing the mechanical properties at the interface between the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase and the Ag base, thereby contributing to the observed incremental increase in the surface nano-hardness of the entire Ag/Ti3AlC2 material as the arc erosion times progressed (Fig. 8, Table 2).

### 3.4 Nano-mechanical degradation mechanism of Ti<sub>3</sub>AlC<sub>2</sub> in Ag base material

The evolution of the structure and properties of the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase is key to in depth understanding the mechanical degradation of Ag/Ti3AlC2 material during arc erosion. According to the results presented above, the relationship between the nano-mechanical properties, microstructure, and composition of the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase was thoroughly discussed. Consequently, the nano-mechanical degradation mechanism of the Ti3AlC2 in Ag base during arc erosion was unveiled, as depicted in Fig. 10.

Before arc erosion, the structure of Ti3AlC2 remained nearly pristine, maintaining an atomic ratio close to the standard (Fig. 10(a)). When the electrical arc made contact with the Ag/Ti3AlC2 material surface, the high temperatures generated by arc discharging began to alter the structure and composition of Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase. This thermal effect initiated at the surface and progressively moved inward. Therefore, the outer region of Ti3AlC2 initially decomposed, enabling the Al atoms to escape and outward diffuse into the Ag base. Concurrently, Ag atoms diffused inward, resulting in the formation of an Ag(Al) solid solution [19,29,39]. This led to a reduction in the mechanical properties of the Ti3AlC2’s outer region. Such a phenomenon accounted for the observed difference in nano-mechanical properties between the inner and outer regions of Ti3AlC2 during the early stages of arc erosion (arc erosion of 1–10 times, Fig. 10(b)). However, the minor structural decomposition and diffusion did not significantly damage the internal structure of Ti3AlC2, allowing the Ag/Ti3AlC2 material to retain its excellent resistance to arc erosion.

During the intermediate stages of arc erosion (100–1000 times, Fig. 10(c)), heat generated by arc discharging increasingly permeated the inner region of the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase. As a consequence of these elevated temperatures, oxidation manifested on the surface of Ti3AlC2 [19,30]. This oxidative process enhanced the nano-mechanical properties at the interface between the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase and the Ag base. Simultaneously, the decomposition of the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase intensified, resulting in slight decomposition in its inner region and a reduction of its nano-mechanical properties. Additionally, cracks formed on the surface of Ti3AlC2.

During the later stage of arc erosion (6200 times, Fig. 10(d)), the surface and outer region of Ti3AlC2 underwent more severe decomposition, leading to a further reduction in its nano-mechanical properties. Concurrently, more oxides formed on the surface of Ti3AlC2, and these oxides partially enveloped the surface, contributing to an improvement in nano-mechanical properties at the interface between the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase and the Ag base. The oxide layer also served to inhibiting the outward diffusion of Al atoms and thereby limiting further decomposition of Ti3AlC2 inner region. As a result, the whole Ag/Ti3AlC2 material maintained a certain level of nano-mechanical properties and resistance to arc erosion at the later stage of arc erosion.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, the nano-mechanical properties of Ag/Ti3AlC2 material subjected to different arc erosion times were investigated to reveal valuable insights into this material’s resistance to external arc damage. Utilizing techniques such as constant loading rate indentation, creep testing, continuous stiffness measurements, and compositional analysis, we observed a gradual degradation in the nano-mechanical properties of the Ti3AlC2 reinforcing phase with increasing arc erosion times. Interestingly, the rate of this degradation appeared to decelerate over arc erosion times. Specifically, continuous stiffness measurements highlighted the uneven mechanical properties within Ti3AlC2, attributing this heterogeneity to the phase’s decomposition. This degradation process induced the de-intercalation of Al and the inward diffusion of Ag, forming an Ag(Al) solid solution, evident as a transition zone in Ti3AlC2’s outer regions.

Moreover, as the arc erosion escalated, its thermal effects penetrated deeper into Ti3AlC2, accelerating decomposition and broadening the transition zone. Concurrently, rising temperatures triggered surface oxidation on Ti3AlC2, which fortuitously improved the nano-mechanical properties at the interface between Ti3AlC2 and the Ag base, enhancing the overall properties of the Ag/Ti3AlC2 composite. Ultimately, despite the increasing severity of Ti3AlC2 decomposition as the arc erosion times further increased, more oxides formed and partially envelop the surface of Ti3AlC2, improving the nano-mechanical properties of the Ag/Ti3AlC2 composite, allowing this material system to maintain a certain level of nano-mechanical properties and resistance to arc erosion. The analysis on degradation behavior of Ti3AlC2 has important theoretical and applied value for understanding in depth the performance evolution of Ag/MAX electrical contact materials.
