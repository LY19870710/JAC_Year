## 1 Introduction

Recently, the development of energy storage electroceramics has become one of the hot topics in the research field of dielectrics [1−7]. Perovskites (ABO3) with a ferroelectric or antiferroelectric phase structure, has emerged as the top material candidate aimed for electrical energy storage applications demanding a high energy density and a high power density, as well as a performance robustness in harsh working conditions [8−12]. So far, the focus has been on modulating the polarization−electric field (*P*–*E*) hysteresis loops of these materials through chemical composition design. Doping [5,13], substitution [14], or solid solutioning [9−11,15−21], as well as engineering of defects [22−24] and heterostructures [25−30], are the main experimental methods being explored. These methods are effective in creating a relaxor microstructure showing a slim *P*–*E* loop due to its chemically inhomogeneous nanopolar regions (NPRs) [5,9−11,15−21], defect enhanced polarization responses and energy storage properties [22−24], polymorphic nanodomain structures [15−21], bilayer or multilayer films with a tilted, extended *P*–*E* loop and a high apparent dielectric strength [25−30], etc. However, a poor manufacturability has limited practical applications of the composition design approach in high energy density electroceramics.

Equivalent physical means of structure modulation to the chemical ones, including grain [31−36], strain [37], and domain engineering [38,39] approaches, have been utilized in combination with prototype perovskite electroceramics, such as BaTiO3 and Pb(Zr0.52Ti0.48)O3 (PZT), to generate manufacturing-friendly processes. These processes can take full advantage of the existing raw materials, equipments, and foundries for electroceramics, and hence promote their further development in energy storage applications. For example, Ryu’s group prepared nanograined PZT thick films (~4 μm) via the high kinetic energy deposition method of aerosol [32]. They discovered a mechanically induced relaxor ferroelectric (RFE) behavior in this normal ferroelectric, i.e., a slim and extended *P*–*E* loop with a large, unsaturated electric polarization and a boosted dielectric strength. Wang *et al*. [37] studied the effect of strain on the energy storage properties of a collection of perovskite thin films. They concluded that an in-plane tensile strain can significantly narrow down a *P*–*E* loop and enhance both the recyclable energy density and charge–discharge efficiency. Owing to a threefold rotational symmetry along the normal of a (111)-oriented epitaxial BaTiO3 film, Peng *et al*. [39] realized a nanodomain configuration with morphed structural phases in this prototype simple perovskite. Such phase degeneracy induced ultrafine ferroelectric nanodomains (1–10 nm) with low-angle domain walls, leading to an enhanced spontaneous polarization (*P*s) with a diminishing hysteresis and remnant polarization (*P*r).

Our previous investigations on preparing high energy density BaTiO3 film capacitors on Si have focused on a columnar nanograin design via a low-temperature sputtering process (350–400 ℃) with a buffer-layer technique [34,40]. This approach resulted in a densely-packed nanograin array, which shows a (001) texture with an in-plane grain diameter down to 10–20 nm, and a grain length extending through the film thickness. Such a microstructure is endowed with a high dielectric strength and a very large energy storage density (130–242 J**·**cm–3) [34,40]. However, the drawback of this nanograin design is the much-reduced relative dielectric constant, which requires a very large electric field to achieve a high energy density. This is not the ideal dielectric in applications where a high pulsed power is demanded under a low electric field. The parameter of “responsivity”, defined as the ratio between recyclable energy density and the applied electric field, *ξ* = *W*rec/*E*app, better describes such an ideal power dielectric [41]. Usually, the achievement of a high responsivity requires a composition design approach, which balances between a high applicable field/energy density with a high polarizability. In this work, we increased the deposition temperature to the limit for CMOS-compatible processing, i.e., 500 ℃, to allow a controlled nanograin growth and hence achieve an improved polarizability/dielectric constant without substantially sacrificing the dielectric strength, ultimately leading to a high energy density with a high responsivity. We will explore the microstructure−energy storage property correlations in submicron BaTiO3 films consisting of these “grown-up” grains, and compare the energy storage performance of our BaTiO3 films with those processed at lower temperatures, as well as those through the composition design.

## 2 Experimental

The Si substrates being used in this work are commercially available platinized Si, i.e., Pt/Ti/(100)Si. The Pt/Ti bilayer serves as a bottom electrode with the ~10 nm thick Ti layer enhancing the adhesion between Pt and Si. We carried out depositions of the Pt/Ti bilayers on (100)Si substrates at 300 ℃ via DC magnetron sputtering in a pure Ar atmosphere. The resulting Pt layer is highly (111) textured. The sputtering targets of Pt and Ti (*Φ* = 50 mm, *t* = 3 mm, 99.99% purity), as well as gold foils (*Φ* = 50 mm, *t* = 0.2 mm, 99.99% purity) were provided by Zhongnuo Advanced Material Technology Co., Ltd., China. The Si substrates (10 mm × 10 mm) and BaTiO3 and LaNiO3 (LNO) ceramic targets (*Φ* = 50 mm, *t* = 5 mm, 99.9% purity) were provided by Anhui Institute of Optics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The key deposition parameters of the LaNiO3-buffered and unbuffered BaTiO3 film heterostructures have been summarized in Ref. [42]. The targeted thicknesses of the bottom electrode (LaNiO3/Pt or Pt by itself) and the BaTiO3 films were set at 300 and 500 nm, respectively. After deposition of the BaTiO3 film, Au top electrodes in a dot shape (*Φ* = 0.2 mm) were sputtered through a mask on an SBC-12 sputtering machine (KYKY, China) at room temperature for electrical measurements.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) 2*θ* scans were used to characterize the phase structures and crystalline orientations of the two types of BaTiO3 films, which took place in a commercial D/Max-RC XRD diffractometer equipped with a Ni filtered Cu Kα radiation source (Rigaku, Japan). Nanostructures of the thin films, including heterostructure interfaces, nanograin morphology, and inner-grain domain structures, were analyzed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using a JEM-2010 microscope (JEOL, Japan). Surface morphology and root-mean-square roughness of the films were analyzed using a MicroNano D-5A atomic force microscope (MicroNano, China). Room temperature standard polarization−voltage (*P*–*V*) loops and pulsed polarizations (the “PUND” test) were measured at 1 kHz for the BaTiO3 films, using a Multiferroic ferroelectric (FE) tester (Radiant Technology, USA).

The frequency (*f*) dependent capacitance (*C*) (*C*–*f*) curves, from which the dielectric constant and loss tangent at zero DC bias were obtained as functions of the frequency of an applied small AC field (*V**p*–*p* = 100 mV), were measured in a QuadTech 7600 plus high-precision LCR meter (Chroma Systems Solutions, USA). The temperature dependent capacitance (*C*–*T*) curves and *P*–*V* loops were obtained by combining a temperature-controllable HFS600E-PB2 probe station (Linkam, UK) with the LCR meter and the FE tester, respectively. To evaluate the ferroelectric capacitor’s reliability, polarization cycling tests were carried out by using the fatigue mode of the FE tester. Lastly, charge−discharge experiments using a home-built RC circuit were carried out to obtain the actual discharge energy density at the device-level (on a load resistor).

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(a) is an XRD 2*θ*-scan spectra of the LNO-buffered and unbuffered submicron thick BaTiO3 films. From its much stronger diffraction peaks, the buffered BaTiO3 film showed an overall better crystallinity than the unbuffered one. Furthermore, the unbuffered film showed a polycrystalline structure with all three main diffraction peaks, (110), (00*l*), and (111), while the buffered film showed a (00*l*) texture due to the buffering effect of the (*l*00) oriented LNO [43]. These differences in crystallinity and orientation will play a vital role in the films’ ferroelectric and hence energy storage properties. These sputtered films did not vary much from each other in terms of the surface morphology, i.e., a dense and smooth surface revealed via atomic force microscopy (AFM) observations with a root-mean-square (RMS) roughness of a few nanometers [42]. In Fig. 1(b), an AFM surface scan image of the LNO-buffered BaTiO3 film is shown, which has an RMS roughness of 2.371 nm. The nanostructures in the films, including heterostructure interfaces, nanograin morphology, and inner-grain domain structures, however, changed significantly with the introduction of the LNO buffer layer.

The Pt layer, measured of ~290 nm in thickness, showed a coarse grain structure with an average in-plane grain size of ~200 nm. This grain size difference, together with the random (001)/(110) polycrystalline structure in the BaTiO3 layer and its huge lattice mismatch with that of (111)Pt, created an incoherent interface between the two adjacent layers (Fig. 2(b)). Moreover, in Fig. 2(a), *c*/*a*/*c*/*a*... type of ferroelastic domains were identified as 45°-canted stripe arrays (with respect to the film normal) inside a BaTiO3 grain (marked with a dashed rectangle). This type of domain structure is common in polycrystalline ferroelectrics of a tetragonal phase.

Meanwhile, in Fig. 2(c), BaTiO3 film in the BaTiO3/LNO/Pt heterostructure showed a continuous columnar nanograin microstructure across the film thickness. Its average in-plane grain diameter is about 30 nm, much smaller than that in the unbuffered BaTiO3 film. This changed grain morphology and reduced grain size can be attributed to a buffered growth of BaTiO3 on (001) textured LaNiO3 (LNO), which was easily grown on Pt/Si [44]. The cubic perovskite LaNiO3 has a lattice parameter very close to that of BaTiO3 (as can be seen from the XRD pattern in Fig. 1), and hence it acted as a locally coherent growth template for the latter. The total thickness of the LNO/Pt bi-layer electrode is ~300 nm (~110 nm for LNO and ~190 nm for Pt), similar to that of the Pt electrode for the unbuffered film. However, the (001)-textured nanograins formed in the thin LNO layer, had led to the formation of fine columnar nanograins in the BaTiO3 film. The locally coherent growth of BaTiO3 on LNO has been verified via a high resolution TEM image shown in Fig. 2(d). Furthermore, the inner-grain ferroelastic domains of the *c*/*a*/*c*/*a*... type were also found in the LNO-buffered BaTiO3 film (marked with a dashed rectangle in Fig. 2(c)), but with a smaller domain thickness due to the reduced grain size.

Overall, the sum total of the different microstructure characteristics (crystallinity and orientation, heterostructure interfaces, nanograin morphology, and inner-grain domain structures) should result in much different ferroelectric *P*–*E* loops of the two types of BaTiO3 films. Basically, a ferroelectric film consisting of continuous columnar nanograins with a (001) texture will have a periodic nanograin/grain boundary microstructure with aligned electric polarizations (Fig. 2(e)). The grain boundaries are usually considered as non-ferroelectric or at most weak ferroelectric, and hence they produce a polarization gradient from ~0 near the grain boundaries to ~*P*S (spontaneous polarization of the ferroelectric material) near the center of the grains [34,40]. This gradient confines the electric polarization, reducing its remnant value (at *E* = 0) and delaying its saturation under an electric field. Consequently, the LNO-buffered BaTiO3 film featuring such a microstructure will show a high saturation/maximum applicable electric field and a diminishing remnant polarization. On the other hand, the discontinuous columnar nanograin structure with a larger average grain size in the unbuffered BaTiO3 film has a much weaker confining effect on the film’s electric polarization, leading to a larger remnant polarization and an early saturation. Therefore, an improved electric energy storage performance is expected on the LNO-buffered BaTiO3 film.

Figure 3(a) shows the room temperature (RT) *P*–*E* loops (@1 kHz) of the two types of BaTiO3 films under the same field *E*m ≈ 320 MV**·**m–1, the maximum applicable electric field before the *P*–*E* loop of the unbuffered film showed a dominating contribution from the conduction current. Both films showed typical ferroelectric characteristics in their *P*–*E* loops, including a *P*–*E* hysteresis with a remnant polarization (*P*r) and a concave curvature [45]. However, the unbuffered BaTiO3 film showed signs of an earlier saturation (from tips of its *P*–*E* loop) and a larger *P*r, despite an unfavorable grain orientation for the out-of-plane electric polarization. Furthermore, this film had a larger free charge/conduction current contribution to the measured electric polarization and a lower breakdown strength. These differences are consistent with those in their microstructures. A finer nanograin microstructure with smaller inner-grain domains and a (001)-textured continuous columnar grain morphology, have created a small *P*r and delayed its saturation under an increasing field, leading to an enhanced recyclable energy density (~81 J**·**cm–3 vs. 57.1 J·cm–3). Discussion of the effects of grain shape (continuous vs. discontinuous columnar nanograins) and grain size (fine vs. coarse grains) have been carried out in our previous work [34], and the conclusions are supported again by the present work. Moreover, the effect of grain orientation on polarization saturation was insignificant as compared with the effects of grain size and shape, since the (001)-oriented grains should be easier to saturate than those with the non-polar orientations.

To elucidate the above conclusion, we performed pulsed polarization test, i.e., the Positive-UP-Negative-Down (PUND) test on both types of BaTiO3 films. This test measures the switchable ferroelectric polarization−“delta *P*”, which is approximately twice the remnant polarization of the ferroelectric material under the corresponding applied electric field. Figure 3(b) is the PUND test result for the LNO-buffered BaTiO3 film, which showed a slow increase of delta *P* in the low field region (*E* –1) and a substantial increase from ~100–310 MV·m–1, followed by a quick boost of delta *P* from 310 MV·m–1 to the maximum applicable field *E*max ≈ 431 MV·m–1. If we look at the percentage of achieved delta *P* (normalized with respect to the maximum delta *P*, delta *P*max, achieved under *E*max = 431 MV·m–1), it reached slightly below 50% at about two thirds of the maximum applicable field. This delayed saturation of ferroelectric polarization is the key for the film’s outstanding energy storage performance. Furthermore, we plotted the 1/2 delta *P*max data point, i.e., *P*r,max, the fully poled (or close to fully poled) remnant polarization, together with a series of published theoretical data points of strain-modulated ferroelectric polarization *P*(*ε*m) for BaTiO3 films [46] in Fig. 3(c). Our data point (1.9%, 8 μC·cm–2) lies very close to the extrapolated *P*–*ε*m line, indicating an intrinsic nature of the measured delta *P* at saturation, which can be attributed to the buffered film’s high degree of (001) texture. To make a fair comparison, delta *P* results of the unbuffered BaTiO3 film were plotted in both absolute value and a percentage of its predicted delta *P*max (% of pred. delta *P*max) in Fig. 3(d). The predicted delta *P*max for this (110)-textured film was estimated to be * 1/2 times of the delta P*max for the (001)-textured film, given that the BaTiO3 films are tetragonal and were both grown on Si (hence had similar residual strains). After taking this measure, we can see that although delta *P* of the unbuffered film showed similar (at low fields) or slightly larger values (at *E* ≈ 320 MV·m–1) than those of the LNO-buffered one, its percentage increase, i.e., the polarization saturation process, went way ahead of the latter. For example, at the same electric field of ~320 MV·m–1, the unbuffered film showed a ~73% saturation while the buffered one only showed ~41% saturation. These results support our proposed correlations between the film microstructure and its *P*–*E* behavior/energy storage performance.

From low to high electric field, the half *P*–*E* loops of the LNO-buffered BaTiO3 films are presented in Fig. 4(a), from which the recyclable energy density (*W*rec), charge−discharge efficiency (*η*), and responsivity (*ξ*) at each applied field *E*app can be obtained (Table 1). At *E*app = 196 MV·m–1 (*V* = 100 V), the *P*–*E* loop is a minor one and shows a negligible remnant polarization *P*r. However, the maximum polarization *P*max is also low due to the film’s low polarizability at this field (Fig. 3(b)), resulting in a low *W*rec value of ~29.7 J·cm–3 and a low *ξ* of ~151.5 J·(kV·m2)–1, together with a charge−discharge/energy efficiency of ~75%. With an increasing *E*app, *P*r and *P*max both increased, resulting in a boosted recyclable energy density *W*rec and responsivity *ξ* at the cost of energy efficiency. At the maximum *E*app of 431 MV·m–1 (beyond which the *P*–*E* loop became seriously distorted due to a dominant leakage current contribution), *W*rec increased to 161.1 J·cm–3 with an efficiency value of ~64.1%. This is a significant achievement given that the responsivity, *ξ* = *W*rec/*E*app, is as high as 373.8 J·(kV·m2)–1.

In Fig. 4(b)**,** it can be clearly seen that the responsivities of our BaTiO3 films (solid black squares) are much higher than those for the reported BaTiO3 films (solid green squares) [34,40,42], and are among the highest in recent works on other high energy density ceramics (solid red and blue squares) [1,6,10,11,14−16,18−22,25−27,29−33,35,36,38,47]. For the BaTiO3 films, due to its relatively small spontaneous polarization (*P*S), a common strategy to improve its energy storage density is to delay the polarization saturation and enhance the dielectric strength via nanograin engineering [34,40]. However, in terms of the responsivity performance, i.e., the achieved energy density per applied electric field, these low temperature deposited nanograin films are poor due to their poor polarizability/low dielectric constant (*ε*r ≈ 100) [34]. In other words, the originally high polarizability/dielectric constant of BaTiO3 was traded for a delayed polarization saturation, leading to a high energy density (~130–240 J·cm–3) with a low responsivity [34,40]. On the other hand, our recent work on thick BaTiO3 films directly deposited on Si with an enlarged grain size, showed the other extreme of a large dielectric constant accompanied by an early polarization saturation, leading to a reduced energy density (~50 J·cm–3) with a decent responsivity [42]. In contrast, the current work achieved both a high energy density and a high responsivity by finding a good balance between dielectric constant/polarizability and delay of the polarization saturation. A larger nanograin size (~30 nm) through limited lateral grain growth with the use of a buffer layer and an elevated growth temperature, is the key to achieve such an outstanding performance.

Ceramic dielectrics taking advantages from composition design should be able to achieve a good balance between energy density and responsivity, since they can be concurrently endowed with a high spontaneous polarization, a large dielectric constant, and a high dielectric strength. However, as can be clearly seen from Fig. 4(b), except for a few cases with a very complex composition (solid blue squares, with total number of cations reaching 6 or more), most of the composition-designed ceramic dielectrics (solid red squares) showed mediocre energy density or responsivity performances. These data points indicate that, on top of a degraded manufacturability, a limited property tuning capability has emerged as a concern for the composition design approach. A possible reason is the proliferation of defects, such as oxygen vacancies, in a multi-component/multi-valence system. The combination of a few ceramic components with several different cation elements usually have a macroscopic synergistic effect in property enhancement, but defects generated at the atomic scale can counteract such an effect.

In Fig. 5(a), the frequency dependent capacitance (*C*–*f*) curves were shown. The small field dielectric constants *ε*r of the LNO-buffered BaTiO3 films are in the range of ~210–227 with a weak frequency dependence, while the loss tangents are less than 3% in the full range of the measuring frequency (1 kHz–2 MHz). The loss tangent values are on par with those reported earlier on BaTiO3 films sputtered at 350 ℃ [34], while the dielectric constants *ε*r are much higher due to grain growth under an elevated temperature. Furthermore, the inset in Fig. 5(a) is a Weibull distribution chart for the dielectric strength (*E*b) of the LNO-buffered BaTiO3 film. The Weibull modulus *β* is 16.22 and the modeled *E*b is ~4.46 MV·m–1. The modeled dielectric strength is consistent with the experimentally measured *E*b through the *P*–*E* test.

Thermal stability is an important performance parameter to consider since capacitors may work under a varying environmental temperature. In Fig. 5(b), the temperature dependent capacitances (*C*–*T*) were displayed as functions of temperature at two measuring frequencies, i.e., 10 and 100 kHz. The capacitance showed small changes (±10%) in a broad temperature range between –150 and 200 ℃. From 200 to 400 ℃, the *C*–*T* curves showed a peak around 340 ℃, which is characteristic of a ferroelectric–paraelectric phase transition. This Curie temperature is much higher than that of the bulk BaTiO3 (~125 ℃), and can be attributed to the residual tensile strain in the BaTiO3 film (~1.9%), which stabilized the ferroelectric phase at elevated temperatures [48]. Consequently, this high Curie temperature endows our BaTiO3 films a good thermal stability for their applications in capacitors.

The temperature dependent *P*–*V* hysteresis loops are shown in Fig. 5(c). These results clearly indicate a stable polarization response (and hence a reliable capacitor performance) from ~−20 to ~160 ℃. At *T* = 220 ℃, the leakage current made a significant contribution to the polarization charge due to the thermally activated conduction process. While at *T* = 240 ℃, the slope of the *P*–*V* curve plummeted and the hysteresis shrank, indicating the occurrence of a ferroelectric–paraelectric phase transition. Further increase of the temperature to 280 ℃ leads to a conduction current-dominant *P–V* loop. The temperature dependence of the *P*–*V* loops is consistent with that of the capacitance/dielectric constant (*C*–*T*) shown in Fig. 5(b). Furthermore, the fatigue resistance of the LNO-buffered BaTiO3 film was evaluated via a polarization cycling test, and the results are shown in Fig. 5(d). The positive and negative maximum polarizations (+*P*max, –*P*max) at a given electric field amplitude *E*max = +/−600 kV·cm−1 were plotted as functions of cycling times (times of bi-polar switching). The remnant polarizations (+*P*r, −*P*r) were also plotted on the same graph. It can be clearly seen that, these polarization values did not show significant changes up to 109 times of bi-polar switching, corresponding to a high fatigue resistance of the ferroelectric BaTiO3 film capacitor.

We also measured the energy storage performance of the LNO-buffered BaTiO3 films at the device level in a classic RC-circuit. Figure 6(a) is the diagram for the charge−discharge RC circuitry and Fig. 6(b) is an optical image of the patterned capacitor arrays of a BaTiO3 film. In such an experiment, a circular BaTiO3 thin film capacitor (*Φ* = 0.2 mm) was fully charged at different voltages generated by a high-voltage source with the same waveform as that in the *P*–*V* test (triangular, 1 kHz), and then discharged to a series of two load resistors with a total resistance of 1050 Ω. The time-resolved discharge voltages of the small resistor R1 (50 Ω) were measured via a high-speed voltmeter (Fig. 6(a)), and the results are shown in Fig. 6(c). Discharge currents as function of time were deduced from these voltage pulses and were used to compute the discharge energy densities, which are shown in Fig. 6(d). Table 1 compares these measured discharge energy densities (*W*dis) with the recyclable energy densities *W*rec integrated from the *P*–*E* hysteresis loops. For the three applied voltages that we have both *W*dis and *W*rec values, we can see that the two are in the same order of magnitude, while the former is always smaller (~50%−60% of the latter) due to circuit losses such as those induced by stray capacitance and parasitic resistance [49]. Nevertheless, at applied fields of ~471 MV·m–1 (240 V) and ~392 MV·m–1 (200 V), the discharge energy densities reached 94.9 and 74.0 J·cm–3, respectively, indicating a great potential for real device applications. It is noted that the maximum applicable field on the FE film capacitor from the RC charge–discharge measurement is slightly higher than that from the *P*–*V* test (for both without deteriorating leakage current). This can be attributed to a faster charge−discharge rate in the device-level test [34]. Lastly, we measured the leakage current of the fully charged FE film capacitor. It rapidly increased with the charging voltage, reaching ~7 μA @ 240 V (471 MV·m–1) (Fig. 6(e)). The FE film capacitors broke down when the charging voltage was increased beyond 240 V (Fig. 6(f)).

## 4 Conclusions

In a summary, through engineering a columnar nanograin microstructure, we successfully achieved both a high energy density *W*rec ≈ 161.1 J·cm–3 and a high responsivity *ξ* ≈ 373.8 J·(kV·m2)–1 in submicron thick BaTiO3 films integrated on Si. While a simple composition and easy availability of the raw materials have warranted these film capacitors a good manufacturability, the high energy density and responsivity they demonstrated for electric energy storage are on par with some of the best composition-designed ceramic film capacitors. In addition, the BaTiO3 films showed a low loss tangent and a weak frequency dependence in their dielectric properties, as well as a broad range of working temperatures. These findings are important for the applications of film capacitors in microelectronic systems.
