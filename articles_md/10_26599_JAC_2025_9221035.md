## 1 Introduction

Optical isolators can be applied in diverse applications, including magnetic field sensors, microwave technology, and high-power lasers [1,2]. Based on the non-reciprocity of Faraday rotation, controlling the unidirectional passage of light and preventing back-reflected light from interfering with the normal output of a light source can be achieved. To do so, magneto-optical materials—which serve as core components of optical isolators—should possess excellent optical quality, large Verdet constants, and high thermal conductivities [3−5].

Terbium(III) oxide (Tb2O3) is a promising material for near-infrared and visible wavelength band isolation, exhibiting a larger Verdet constant and superior laser damage threshold than other terbium-based materials. Nonetheless, the scalability of pure Tb2O3 single crystals is limited by their high melting point and phase transition, which can present a considerable challenge for realizing their large-scale preparation. The development of transparent Tb₂O₃ ceramics represents a major advance in the field of materials science. However, the Tb2O3 ceramic manufacturing process remains in its infancy.

The primary technical challenges include the instability of the valence state and the transformation of the crystal structure at high temperatures. One approach is to introduce other rare-earth ions with higher solubilities and stabilities, such as Lu3+ and Y3+, into the lattice structure via solid-state doping [6−9]. However, the incorporation of these ions (with their lower spin magnetic moments) can result in a reduction in the Verdet constant owing to the dilution of Tb3+ ions. Previous studies have shown that certain rare-earth ions with high effective magnetic moments or effective transition wavelengths—such as Ho3+ [10], Dy3+ [11,12], Ce3+ [13−15], and Pr3+ [16,17]—can enhance the optical properties of materials through spin interactions. The quantum interaction between ions and Tb3+ ions modifies the material’s crystal field, resulting in a notable increase in the material’s Verdet constant. Dai *et al*. [18] and Li *et al*. [19] achieved 7% and 3% increases in the Verdet constant by doping terbium gallium garnet (TGG) and terbium aluminum garnet (TAG) ceramic matrices with Ce3+ and Ho3+ ions, respectively. However, the impact of high-spin magnetic rare earth ion doping on the optical qualities and magnetic photophysical properties of Tb2O3 transparent ceramics remains unexplored.

In this study, (Tb0.94−*x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 (*x* = 0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.0125, 0.015) magneto-optical transparent ceramics were successfully prepared by sintering under 5% argon–hydrogen mixed reducing atmosphere combined with hot isostatic pressing (HIP) post-treatment. The impacts of the physical phase and microstructure of the corresponding ceramics were investigated. The impacts of different Ho3+ doping concentrations on the optical quality and Verdet constant were also examined. The magneto-optical properties of the (Tb0.93Lu0.06Ho0.01)2O3 transparent ceramics were compared with those of other terbium-based materials.

### 2.1 Experimental procedures

High-purity Tb4O7 (Eternal-Element Co., Ltd.; 99.999%), Lu2O3 (Eternal-Element Co., Ltd.; 99.999%), and Ho2O3 (Eternal-Element Co., Ltd.; 99.99%) were used as raw materials, and ZrO2 (Aladdin; 99.99%) was used as a sintering aid at a concentration of 0.1 at%. The powder was mixed using mechanical ball milling at a stoichiometric ratio of 0.1 at% ZrO2: (Tb0.94−*x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 (*x* = 0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.0125, 0.015) for 24 h. Zirconia balls and pure alcohol were used as the ball milling media. The ball-milled powder was dried in a rotary evaporative dryer and sieved through a 200-mesh screen. The dried powder obtained was uniaxially pressed at 20 MPa and subjected to cold isostatic pressing at 200 MPa, followed by pre-sintering in air for 10 h to remove residual organic impurities. The resulting green body was sintered at 1500 °C for 10 h under a 5% argon‒hydrogen mixed reducing atmosphere (KSL-1700X, Hefei Kejing Material Technology Co., Ltd., China), after which HIP post-treatment was performed at 1550 °C for 4 h under a 180 MPa argon atmosphere. Finally, the ceramic samples were mirror-polished on both sides (*ϕ*15 mm × 4 mm) and thermally etched at 1400 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere for surface observation.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The phase structures of the sintered ceramic samples were characterized by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D2 Phaser, Bruker Co., Ltd., Germany) using Cu Kα radiation, with a scanning step increment of 0.05°, scanning time of 0.2 s, and a 2*θ* range of 10°–90°. The microstructures of the ceramics were analyzed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; JSM-6700, JEOL, Japan), while the phase distributions of the samples were analyzed by energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) using both face and line scans. The grain sizes of the ceramics were measured using the linear intercept method and were calculated with 200 grains. The bulk densities of the ceramic samples were determined using the Archimedes method. Linear transmittance in the 200–1400 nm range and absorbance in the 400–800 nm range were determined using a visible–near–infrared (UV–vis–NIR) spectrometer (Lambda950, PerkinElmer, USA). The Verdet constants of the ceramic samples at 633 nm were measured using a homemade Faraday effect test instrument.

### 3.1 Phase characterization and microstructure of manufactured ceramics

Figure 1(a) shows the schematic crystal structure of (Tb0.94−*x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 ceramics. Tb2O3 is classified as a cubic crystal system, where oxygen ions (O2−) form the closest packing arrangement within the cubic lattice, and terbium ions (Tb3+) occupy the octahedral interstices. 75% of Tb ions are situated at the C2 lattice position, while the remaining 25% reside at the S6 lattice sites. Holmium (Ho3+) and lutetium (Lu3+) ions are substituted for the host terbium ions (Tb3+) to create a continuous solid solution upon doping.

*
Fig. 1Structural characterizations of (Tb0.94−x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 ceramics: (a) crystalline cell structure of ceramic samples; (b) XRD pattern, where the inset in right-hand panel shows an enlarged view of main diffraction peak (222); (c) results of linear fitting of lattice constants and theoretical densities as a function of Ho3+ concentration.

Figure 1(b) depicts the XRD patterns of the ceramic target products obtained from (Tb0.94−*x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 pre-sintered compactly followed by high-temperature HIP treatment at 1550 °C. The diffraction patterns of the ceramic samples were successfully matched to the standard card of the cubic phase of Tb2O3 (JCPDS No. 43-1032), confirming the complete solid solubility of the dopant ions within the main phase (terbium oxide). This result demonstrates that no secondary-phase impurities were generated or introduced during the doping process. In the control group (Tb0.94Lu0.06)2O3, the XRD diffraction peak (222) slightly shifted compared with that of the standard Tb2O3 cards, which can be attributed to the differing radii of Lu3+ (0.848 Å) and Tb3+ (0.923 Å) ions. When the control group (Tb0.94Lu0.06)2O3 is used as the baseline, the gradual increase in the Ho3+ doping concentration results in a shift of the diffraction peaks towards higher angles.

Figure 1(c) shows the lattice constant of (Tb0.94−*x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 ceramics. The calculation of the lattice constant—based on the XRD data and Bragg equation [20]—indicates that the lattice constant decreases with increasing concentration of Ho3+ ions. This lattice-shrinkage phenomenon is consistent with Vegard’s law, which implies the formation of a homogeneous sesquioxide solid solution. Ho3+ doping induces lattice distortion owing to the substitution of Tb3+ ions by Ho3+ ions (with an ion radius of 0.894 Å).

Figure 2 shows the fracture surface field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) images of the pre-sintered ceramics. The microstructures and porosities of the ceramic samples clearly change in response to variations in the concentration of doped Ho2O3. Figures 2(a)–2(e) show the structural characterization results of (Tb0.94−*x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 (*x* = 0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.0125, 0.015) ceramics samples sintered at 1500 °C for 10 h under a reducing atmosphere. The distribution of the ceramic microstructure is relatively uniform. However, as the Ho3+ ion concentration increases further to 1.5 at%, densification of the ceramic is observed, accompanied by a significant reduction in porosity and a consequent decrease in the size of the residual pores.

The ratio of the experimental density to the theoretical density—which is the exact relative density—was determined using the Archimedes drainage method, as shown in Fig. 5(b). The theoretical density (*d*th) of the solid solution used in this study can be determined by Eq. (1):

(1) dth=16a3NA{[nMLu+mMTb+(1−m−n)MHo]×2+3MO}

where * a denotes the lattice constant;  NA denotes the Avogadro constant;  M denotes the relative atomic mass of each element;  m and  n denote the percentages of normal lattice sites occupied by plasmids [21]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the introduction of Ho3+ resulted in a reduction in the lattice constant of Tb2O3, accompanied by a corresponding decrease in the mass per unit cell. The theoretical density of (Tb0.94−x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 increases from 8.99 to 9.03 g·cm−3 with the introduction of Ho3+. The results of the calculations clearly show that the relative densities of the pre-sintered samples are gradually increased from 94.11% to 97.09%. However, the HIP-treated (Tb0.94−*x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 shows different results, with relative densities of 99.56%, 99.77%, 99.98%, 99.78%, and 99.67%, respectively. The decrease in the density of samples with a Ho concentration of 1.5 at% could be attributed to the creation of intracrystalline pores, which was observed in the FESEM images.

The EDS surface scanning results of (Tb0.93Lu0.06Ho0.01)2O3 are shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b), enabling characterization of the elemental distribution and homogeneity of the elements, as well as the solid-solution state of each ion after doping. The green spot represents Tb, whereas the yellow, red, and brown spots correspond to Lu, Ho, and Zr, respectively. Following HIP treatment at 1550 °C, a complete solid solution formed among the phases. Overall, the cationic elements were uniformly distributed, and no obvious aggregation of discrete single phases or secondary phases can be detected.

### 3.2 Performance characterizations

Figure 7 shows the optical in-line transmittance and images of the transparent ceramic (Tb0.94−*x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 with a thickness of 4 mm at wavelengths ranging from 200 to 1400 nm. All the ceramic samples exhibited a certain degree of transparency. The introduction of Ho ions renders the samples yellowish. The theoretical transmittance (*T*) for Tb2O3 can be deduced from the relationship between the refractive index (*n*) and the wavelength (*λ*) as Eqs. (2) and (3):

(3) n=(1+λ2Bλ2−A)1/2

where * n denotes the refractive index,  λ is the wavelength of light,  α is the loss coefficient, and L* is the transparent ceramic sample thickness. *A* = 0.0122 and *B* = 0.3853 are the mean dispersion parameter and intercept values, respectively, obtained from Refs. [23,24].

Under ideal conditions, the internal light loss is negligible, and the calculated refractive index and theoretical transmittance values are 1.916 and 81.25% at 1064 nm, respectively. Compared with the experimental data, the highest optical transmittance of (Tb0.93Lu0.06Ho0.01)2O3 is approximately 79.05% with a sample thickness of 4 mm, which is very close to the corresponding theoretical values. However, the linear transmittance of the blank control sample (Tb0.94Lu0.06)2O3 is comparatively diminished, possibly due to the intergranular porosity indicated by the microstructural analysis. The absorption peaks observed in the transmittance curves could be attributed to the intra-f10 transition of Ho3+ and the intra-4f8 transition of Tb3+ [10]. All of the samples exhibit no characteristic optical absorption in the 630 nm and 1 μm bands, making them appropriate for 1 μm Faraday optical isolators in laser systems based on materials with Yb3+ and Nd3+ doping [25−31]. Nevertheless, compared with the near-infrared region at 1064 nm, the transmittance in the visible region at 633 nm is significantly diminished, a reduction attributed to optical scattering induced by the presence of micropores.

To characterize and compare the magneto-optical properties of (Tb0.94−*x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 transparent ceramics, we used a homemade Faraday effect tester, as shown in Fig. 8(a). Device 1 was a laser fixed at a wavelength of 633 nm. The devices 2 and 5 represent the polarizers. The device 3 was a magnetic coil. The magnetic field intensity ranged from 0 to 0.5 T. The horizontal position was adjusted to ensure that the light path was parallel to the magnetic field and that the light passed through the magneto-optical sample. The rotation angle of the detector was adjusted to find the extinction position of the magneto-optical sample under different magnetic field strengths, which was calculated as Eq. (4):

Figures 8(b) and 8(c) show the change in the Verdet constant of ceramics with different Ho3+ doping concentrations and a comparison with a commercial TGG single crystal. The magnitudes of the Verdet constants are presented as absolute values to facilitate comparative analysis. The highest Verdet constant of (Tb0.925Lu0.06Ho0.015)2O3 was −403.8±2.9 rad·T−1·m−1, which resulted in a 6% improvement over that of (Tb0.94Lu0.06)2O3 sample without Ho3+ doping (−380.9±2.3 rad·T−1·m−1). Moreover, the values of (Tb0.94-*x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 with *x* = 0.005, 0.01, and 0.0125 were measured to be −384.2±5.6, 389.8±5.8, and 397.9±2.7 rad·T−1·m−1, respectively. The Verdet constant value of (Tb0.925Lu0.06Ho0.015)2O3 sample was more than 3 times that of TGG single crystals [32] (−134 rad·T−1·m−1 at 633 nm).

Ho-doped TGG crystals are almost twice as large as normal TGG single crystals, suggesting a generalization of the magneto-optical property enhancement effect of Ho for Tb-based materials. The magneto-optical effect level of paramagnetic ions can be explained by Eq. (5) using van Vleck–Hebb’s quantum mechanical mechanism.

Figure 8(d) shows the UV–vis absorption coefficient curves of (Tb0.94−x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 transparent ceramics. The absorption bands identified at 416, 450, 464, 537, 550, and 638 nm are associated with the 4f10 transitions from the ground state 5I8 to the excited states 5G5, 5F1–5G6, 5F2, 5F4, 5S2, and 5F5, respectively. Additionally, the absorption coefficients are positively correlated with the Ho3+ content.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, magneto-optical ceramics (Tb0.94−*x*Lu0.06Ho*x*)2O3 were prepared using reducing atmosphere sintering combined with HIP post-treatment. The XRD results showed that Ho3+ ions were introduced into the cubic lattice of Tb2O3 with a pure single solid-solution phase and that the lattice constant decreased with increasing Ho2O3 content. Moreover, trace Ho doping could facilitate the densification of ceramic materials, whereas excess doping led to the aggregation of intracrystalline pores. The highest in-line transmittance was 79.05% at 1064 nm. (Tb0.925Lu0.06Ho0.015)2O3 ceramics possessed the highest Verdet constant in this work, that is, −404 rad∙T−1∙m−1 at 633 nm, almost three times that of the TGG crystals.
