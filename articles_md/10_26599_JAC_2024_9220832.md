## 1 Introduction

Carbon fiber-reinforced silicon carbide ceramic matrix composites (Cf/SiC) are a new class of high-temperature structural and functional integrated materials possessing the advantages of metals, ceramics, and carbon compounds [1−3]. They are characterized by low density, high specific strength and stiffness, excellent high-temperature mechanical properties, elevated temperature resistance, outstanding oxidation resistance, and good chemical and thermal shock stability [4,5]. Consequently, these composites have been widely used in aviation and aerospace engine hot-end components [6,7], thermal protection systems for space shuttles [8−10], space mirrors [11], and high-speed braking devices [12−14]. Currently, Cf/SiC composites are the preferred thermal protection structural materials for key parts of new-generation hypersonic vehicles, such as nose cones, wing leading edges, and flaps [15].

However, with the continuous increase in the flight speed of hypersonic vehicles, these vehicles must withstand temperatures exceeding 1300 ℃ during aerodynamic heating [16,17]. Analysis of the cross-sectional microstructure of 2D Cf/SiC composites prepared via the chemical vapor infiltration (CVI; Fig. 1) has shown that there are numerous interlaminar pores in the vertical direction, which dramatically reduce the thermal conductivity of the composites (to 5–6 W·m−1·K−1 only [18]). Therefore, the thermal load concentrated at the tip of the hypersonic vehicle may induce thermal ablation and rapid failure of the thermal protection structural materials under ultra-high temperatures. Thus, it is very important to establish suitable methods to improve the thermal conductivity of the composite materials, strengthen the heat conductivity, and quickly transmit the local high thermal load to a low-temperature region. This can reduce the risks of damage to the local structure caused by ultra-high temperature ablation, and increase the ablation resistance and service life of the thermal protection materials.

As a novel one-dimensional nanomaterial, carbon nanotubes (CNTs) exhibit a certain degree of curvature in the hexagonal grid of carbon atoms in the form of sp2 hybridization, resulting in a spatial topology structure which can be regarded as a hollow cylinder formed by winding graphene sheets [19]. This unique structural feature endows CNTs with outstanding mechanical properties, ultra-high thermal conductivity, and excellent electrical properties, gaining considerable attention from researchers [20]. It has been confirmed both experimentally and theoretically that the tensile strength of CNTs can reach 100 GPa [21,22], while their Young’s modulus is higher than 1 TPa [23−25], and their thermal conductivity exceeds 3000 W·m−1·K−1 [26,27]. Therefore, based on the concept of multi-level scale design, CNTs are introduced into fiber-reinforced ceramic matrix composites as a nano-reinforcement phase. Their mutual overlap forms a thermal conductivity network, in which the inter-layer macropores in the preform are divided into micropores to increase the density of the composites. This strategy is promising to achieve synergistic improvement in thermal conductivity and mechanical properties of the composites.

To date, several attempts have been made to introduce CNTs into the Cf/SiC composites with the aim of enhancing their mechanical parameters and thermal conductivity. For example, Yu et al.* [28] embedded commercial multi-walled CNTs (0.0765 wt%) as raw materials in 3D preform via slurry infiltration, and then obtained Cf/SiC composites via precursor infiltration and pyrolysis (PIP) technique. The results showed that the flexural strength and room-temperature thermal conductivity of the composites doped with CNTs were 311.4 MPa and 1.81 W·m−1·K−1, respectively, exceeding those of the pristine materials by 25% and 30%. Wang *et al*. [29] introduced CNT networks into unidirectional T700SC carbon fiber preforms by slurry impregnation combined with a freeze-drying process, and prepared CNT-modified Cf/SiC composites by PIP. Compared to the composites without CNTs, the flexural strength and interfacial shear strength of the composites with 1 wt% CNTs were increased by 27% and 31%, respectively. Yang *et al*. [30] introduced 10% CNTs of different lengths into 2D plain carbon fiber preforms by a vacuum filtration process, and prepared CNT-modified Cf/SiCN composites via PIP. The effect of the initial length of CNTs on the thermal conductivity of 2D Cf/SiCN composites was studied. Compared with the unmodified composites, the thermal conductivity of the composites modified by short CNTs (with a length of 5 μm) and long CNTs (with a length of 1000 μm) increased by 10% and 31%, respectively. Wang *et al*. [31] modified carbon fibers by CNTs using a slurry impregnation process, and studied the effect of the position difference of CNTs in the carbon interphase layer on the tensile properties of Cf/SiC mini-composites prepared by PIP. The results showed that the introduction of CNTs into the carbon interphase layer can greatly improve the tensile strength and fracture work of the mini-composites, likely due to the reduction of residual thermal stress in the composites. Tulbez *et al*. [32] introduced CNTs into Cf/C preforms through ultrasound-assisted impregnation and then prepared Cf/C–SiC composites via the liquid silicon infiltration. The flexural strength and thermal conductivity of the CNT-modified composites were increased by 40% and 18%, respectively, reaching 120 MPa and 7.29 W·m−1·K−1. Hu *et al*. [33] applied the catalytic chemical vapor deposition (CCVD) technology to introduce CNTs (4 vol%) into 3D carbon fiber preforms and produce Cf/SiC composites. The results revealed that, compared with undoped composites, the flexural strength of the composites containing CNTs increased from 293 to 336 MPa, and the corresponding room-temperature thermal conductivity increased from 2.1 to 3.3 W·m−1·K−1. Hu *et al*. [34] used ferric nitrate as the catalyst, and acetylene and hydrogen as the reaction gas, along with phenolic resin and polycarbosilane as precursors to prepare CNTs-modified Cf/C–SiC composites by the CCVD process and PIP process, respectively. Compared with the unmodified composites, the flexural strength, elastic modulus, and fracture toughness of the Cf/C–SiC composites modified by 3.2 vol% CNTs were increased by 25.51%, 5.75%, and 4.57%, to 246 MPa, 9.2 GPa, and 9.15 MPa·m1/2, respectively. This can be attributed to the fact that CNTs enhance the bonding strength between the fiber and the matrix and improve the damage resistance of the matrix. Chen *et al*. [35] deposited vertically aligned CNTs on the fiber surface of pretreated 2D preform by CCVD, and prepared Cf/SiC composites via the PIP process. The Cf/SiC composites modified by CNTs exhibited high flexural strength (248.62 MPa) and thermal conductivity (16.8 W·m−1·K−1), which were 14.75% and 111.59% higher than those of the unmodified Cf/SiC composites. Furthermore, to enhance heat conduction, Cao *et al*. [36] adopted laser-assisted processing technology in combination with slurry impregnation to create heat transfer channels in the thickness direction of 2D Cf/SiC composites. It was found that the channels formed by CNTs increased the ambient-temperature thermal conductivity of the composites from 6.29 to about 20.69 W·m−1·K−1. However, the holes formed during laser processing broke the continuity of the fibers, and the resulting defects limited the bending strength of the composites to only 324.5 MPa.

It is worth mentioning that the above reports focused on elucidating the influence of adding CNTs on mechanical characteristics and thermal conductivity of the composites. However, the impact of the amount of CNTs on the microstructure evolution and comprehensive properties of composites has not been studied in detail, which is very important for the optimization of mechanical and thermal properties of the composites and cannot be ignored. In addition, a great deal of attention has been paid to the thermal conductivity of the composites at room temperature or relatively low operation temperatures. The leading edge of the hypersonic vehicle and the thermal protection tiles of the aerospace vehicle face an ultra-high temperature environment (> 1300 ℃) [16, 17]. Yet, there is still a lack of systematic research on the thermal conductivity and thermally conductive mechanism of CNTs in the composites exposed to high-temperature conditions. Therefore, these two key issues merit a thorough investigation to open up new prospects in the design, manufacture, and engineering applications of the Cf/SiC composites.

To address the above scientific problems, the CCVD technology was used herein to introduce CNTs of different contents into the 2D carbon fiber preform, and the 2D Cf/SiC composites were then prepared via CVI. The microstructure, surface morphology, and crystallinity of CNTs were characterized via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Raman spectroscopy, and X-ray diffraction (XRD), and the growth mechanism of CNTs prepared by CCVD was also studied. In addition, the kinetics of the CNT growth was proposed, and a relevant CNT growth model was also established. Furthermore, the flexural strength, fracture toughness, and high-temperature thermal conductivity of the Cf/SiC composites modified with CNTs of different contents were determined. The optimal amount of CNTs to enhance the mechanical properties and thermal conductivity of the composites synergistically was established as well. Based on the shear-lag model, the influence of the CNT content on the interfacial shear strength between the fiber and the matrix was assessed. The effect of CNTs on the thermal conductivity of the composites was investigated using Debye model and Maxwell–Eucken model. Finally, the mechanisms associated with the strength, toughness, and thermal conductivity of CNTs present in the composites were carefully analyzed.

### 2.1 Raw materials

T300 PAN-based carbon fiber cloths with a filament count of 1k were provided by Toray Co., Ltd. The carbon source used for the growth of carbon nanotubes was anhydrous ethanol of analytical grade, purchased from Tianjin Fuyu Fine Chemical Co., Ltd. The catalyst and CNT growth promoter were analytical grade ferrocene and 1,3-propane diamine, which were provided by Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Other reagents, such as acetone, were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. The protective and carrier gas was argon with purity greater than 99.999%, provided by Xi’an Weiguang Gas Co., Ltd.

### 2.2 Preparation of CNTs

Figure 2 depicts a schematic diagram of the CNT growth on carbon fiber cloths via CCVD. Pretreatment of commercial carbon cloths, including cutting and removal of sizing agent, was performed before the deposition of CNTs. In particular, large pieces of carbon cloths (600 mm × 1000 mm) were first cut into rectangular samples with dimensions of 100 mm × 45 mm, which were then soaked in acetone solution for 4 h to remove the sizing agent from the fiber surface, and finally placed in an oven at 60 ℃ for 2 h to remove excess solvent. The treated carbon cloths were placed in the isothermal reaction zone of a quartz reactor in a horizontal tubular furnace (OTF-1200X-III-C, Hefei Kejing Material Technology Co., Ltd., China). The tube furnace was heated to the growth temperature of CNTs of about 850 ℃ at a rate of 5 ℃·min−1 under the protection of argon by means of a programmable temperature controller. In this work, ferrocene as the catalyst for the CNT growth was dissolved in a mixture of 1,3-propane diamine and ethanol with a volume ratio of 1 : 4 to prepare precursor solution with a catalyst concentration of 0.01 g·mL−1. Once the furnace temperature rose to 850 ℃, the precursor solution was injected into the quartz reactor with an injection cylinder and a micro-injection pump at a rate of 10 mL·h−1, and the flow rate of argon gas was kept at 1.2 L·min−1. The precursor solution was vaporized in the low-temperature region, and CNTs were grown on the carbon fiber cloth in the carrier gas environment reaching the high-temperature reaction region. The number of CNTs was adjusted by controlling reaction time. After reaching the set growth time, the injection of the precursor solution was stopped, and the furnace power was turned off. The furnace was then cooled down to room temperature under the protection of argon gas. The above steps were repeated several times to obtain enough CNT-branched carbon cloth.

### 2.3 Fabrication of Cf/CNTs–PyC/SiC composites

The schematic diagram of the fabrication of the Cf/CNTs–PyC/SiC composites is shown in Fig. 3. First, twenty pieces of carbon fiber cloth with CNTs on the fiber surface were stacked in the same direction and clamped by a graphite mold to form a fiber preform with dimensions of 100 mm × 45 mm × 3 mm. Using propylene as a precursor, a PyC interphase with a thickness of about 200 nm was prepared on the surface of the fiber preform with CNTs through the CVI method at 950 ℃ [37]. Then, the preform with CNTs–PyC interphase was heat-treated under vacuum at 1800 ℃ for 1 h. Finally, the SiC matrix of the Cf/CNTs–PyC/SiC composites was produced via CVI at a reduced pressure (2 kPa) and a deposition temperature of about 1000 ℃ for 600 h using methyltrichlorosilane (MTS, CH3SiCl3), hydrogen, and argon as the precursor of SiC, carrier gas, and dilution gas [37], respectively. To study the effects of CNTs on the thermal and mechanical properties of the Cf/CNTs–PyC/SiC composites, the deposition time of CNTs was set at 30, 60, and 90 min, and the corresponding Cf/CNTs–PyC/SiC composites were labeled as S3, S6, and S9, respectively. In addition, traditional Cf/PyC/SiC composites without CNTs (referred to as S0) were also fabricated by the same process.

### 2.4 Characterization of materials

The bulk density and open porosity of the composites were measured by Archimedes method. The volume fraction of each phase in the composites was calculated according to Eqs. (1)–(3):

(1) ρ=ρfVf+ρCNTsVCNTs+ρPyCVPyC+ρSiCVSiC

(2) PC=1−Vf−VCNTs−VPyC−VSiC−PO

(3) PT=PO+PC

where *ρ*, *ρ*f, *ρ*CNTs, *ρ*PyC, and *ρ*SiC represent the density of the composites, the carbon fiber, the the CNTs, PyC interphase, and the CVI–SiC matrix, respectively. *V*f, *V*CNTs, *V*PyC, and *V*SiC represent the volume fraction of the carbon fiber, the CNTs, the PyC interphase, and the CVI–SiC matrix, respectively. *P*O, *P*C, and *P*T are the open porosity, the closed porosity, and the total porosity, respectively.

The morphology and microstructure of CNTs were observed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; S-4700, Hitachi, Japan) and a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200X, FEI, USA). The degree of graphitization of CNTs prepared on the surface of carbon fiber cloth using the CCVD method was characterized via an Raman spectroscope (Alpha 300R, WITec, Germany) within the wavenumber range from 100 to 4000 cm−1 by applying a 532 nm argon-ion laser source. In addition, the phase structure of CNTs was characterized by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X’Pert Pro, Philips) equipped with Cu Kα (*λ* = 1.5406 Å) radiation in the range of 10°–90°. The weight percent of CNTs (wt%) under different preparation conditions was determined according to Eq. (4):

(4) wt%=M−mm×100%

where *M* and *m* represent the weights of the carbon fiber preforms with and without CNTs, respectively; *M–m* is the weight change of the preforms before and after CCVD, evaluated by means of an electronic analytical balance with the accuracy of 0.1 mg.

The thermal diffusivity (*α*), specific heat (*c**p*), and thermal conductivity (*λ*) of the composites were measured using a laser flash apparatus (LFA-427, NETZSCH, Germany) based on Eq. (5) [38]:

(5) λ=ραcp

where *ρ* is the density. The measurements were performed on a round-shaped specimen sheet with the dimensions of *ϕ*12.7 mm × 3 mm in the temperature range from 25 to 1400 ℃ under a N2 atmosphere. The operation principle of the laser flash apparatus is illustrated in Fig. 7(a).

The flexural strength and flexural moduli of the composites were obtained through a three-point bending test on a SANS CTM 4304 machine (Sans Materials Testing Co., Ltd., China) according to the ASTM C1341 standard [39]. The specimen dimensions were 40 mm × 5 mm × 3 mm, the crosshead speed was 0.5 mm·min−1, and the outer support span was 30 mm. The related parameters were then determined as Eqs. (6) and (7):

(6) σf=3Pl2bh2

(7) Ef=kl34bh3

where σ*f and *E*f are the flexural strength and the flexural modulus, respectively; *P*, *l*, *b*, and *h* are the load, the span, the width, and the thickness of the sample, respectively; *k* is the slope of the initial straight-line segment in the load–displacement curve acquired using a mechanical test machine (SANS CTM 4304, Sans Materials Testing Co., Ltd., China).

The bending proportional limit stress of the composites was determined by the strain shift method. Based on the fitting line of the elastic deformation section of the composites, the parallel line of the linear section of the stress–strain curve was drawn by shifting 0.002% to the right. The intersection of the parallel line and the stress–strain curve gives the proportional limit stress of the composites [40].

To explore the effect of CNTs on the interfacial properties of the composites, the interfacial shear strength (*τ*i) of the composites with and without CNTs was evaluated based on the shear–lag model (Eq. (8)) [41].

(8) lC=dfσf2τi

where *d*f and *σ*f represent the diameter (7 μm) and the tensile strength (3.5 GPa) of the carbon fiber, respectively. *l*C represents the fiber pull-out length, which is obtained by the statistical method based on the SEM observation of the fracture of the bending specimen.

To evaluate the fracture toughness of the composites, single-edge notched beam (SENB) tests were performed on the above universal testing machine according to the ASTM C1421 standard [42]. The specimen dimensions were 40 mm × 5 mm × 3 mm, the crosshead speed was 0.05 mm·min−1, and the outer support span was 30 mm. The straight-through notch (2.5 mm in length) at the center of the test sample was machined by a 0.2 mm thick diamond blade. In addition, the work of fracture of the composites was obtained from the characteristic area under the load–displacement curve, which covered the range from the original point to the 10% drop of the maximum load divided by the cross section of the specimen. The relevant parameters were determined as Eqs. (9) and (10):

(9) KIC=Plhb3/2f(cb)

(10) f(cb)=2.9(cb)1/2−4.6(cb)3/2+21.8(cb)5/2−37.6(cb)7/2+38.7(cb)9/2

where *K*IC is the fracture toughness, *c* is the notch depth, and *b* is the width of the sample.

The schematic diagrams of the three-point bending tests and SENB experiments are shown in Fig. 8(a). At least five specimens of each composite were tested to ensure statistically accurate data. The cross-sections and fracture morphologies of the specimens after mechanical testing were also observed via SEM.

### 3.1 Microstructure and growth mechanism analysis of CNTs.

Figures 4(a)–4(d) depict the surface morphology of carbon fiber cloths modified by CNTs under different growth time. As can be observed in Fig. 4(a) and its inset, the diameter of the carbon fiber filament in the unmodified S0 sample was about 7 μm, and the filament surface was rough, exhibiting ridge-like protrusions and grooves. Figures 4(b)–4(d) display carbon fibers coated with numerous CNTs (the S3, S6, and S9 samples). According to the images, the coverage with CNTs caused a significant increase in the diameter of the carbon fiber filaments for the S3 and S6 samples. However, the abundant CNTs produced by the long-term deposition in the S9 sample made the boundaries between the fiber filaments indistinguishable. Figure 4(e) shows the mass ratio of CNTs to the carbon fiber cloth as a function of deposition time. The relationship between the yield of CNTs and their growth time is also displayed in Fig. 4(f). It can be seen from both figures that the CNT-to-cloth mass ratio increased with the growth time. Based on the above results, CNTs prepared via CCVD in this work underwent the vapor–liquid–solid bottom–up growth mechanism [43,44]. A schematic diagram of the relevant growth mechanism is shown in Fig. 5(n). Under a high-temperature environment, anhydrous ethanol is decomposed into small carbon-containing molecules of gas, whereas ferrocene is decomposed into Fe elemental particles. During the transport of Ar gas flow, Fe elemental particles are deposited onto the surface of the carbon fiber preform. In turn, the carbon-containing gas adsorbed on the surface of Fe particles is decomposed into carbon particles which then migrate to the interior of iron particles through different diffusion paths and get deposited to form CNT nucleation sites. Once diffused along the boundary of Fe particles, the carbon particles accumulate and rearrange at the boundary of the catalyst to form a graphene layer, which finally grows into a graphite wall of CNTs. The carbon diffused inside the Fe particles accumulates on the surface of the catalyst to form a spacer layer. As the diffusion of carbon along the boundary of catalyst particles is faster than that inside them, a typical regular bamboo-like structure of CNTs is formed with the extension of deposition time.

To eliminate the influence of metal catalysts on the mechanical and thermal properties of the composites, CNTs prepared at 850 ℃ were subjected to high-temperature heat treatment at 1800 ℃. The structure of heat-treated CNTs was examined via TEM, and the results are shown in Figs. 5(g)–5(k). According to the images, the catalyst particles at the bottom of CNTs disappeared due to volatilization under vacuum and high-temperature treatment [45]. The lattice fringes of the CNT wall after heat treatment became clearer, and the interplanar spacing in CNTs was measured to be 0.347 nm. This means that heat treatment promotes the removal of impurity atoms and the regular arrangement of carbon atoms in CNTs prepared at low temperatures, which is conducive to the ideal graphite structure transformation.

To further quantitatively characterize the crystalline structure of carbon materials, CNTs produced using the CCVD method without and with thermal treatment were analyzed via Raman spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction. For the convenience of description, pristine CNTs and heat-treated CNTs were labeled as CNTs and HT-CNTs, respectively. Figures 5(f) and 5(l) depict Raman patterns of both samples and the relevant fitting curves obtained using Gaussian function in the Origin software. Four features in the refined Raman spectra are referred to as D, G, I, and D'' bands [46,47]. The so-called D band at ~1350 cm−1 is attributed to a disordered state arising from defects, vacancies, and dangling bonds in carbon. The G band at ~1580 cm−1 originates from the in-plane lattice vibrations of C sp2 atoms. The I band and D'' bands located at around 1180–1200 cm−1 and 1480–1500 cm−1, respectively, are ascribed to the sp3-rich phase and the amorphous carbon, respectively [48]. Compared to pristine CNTs, the intensity of the G band of HT-CNTs was strengthened, while the intensity of the D, I, and D'' bands is weakened, implying that high-temperature heat treatment promoted the transformation of disordered carbon to the ordered graphite structure. Moreover, the ratio of the integrated areas of the D and G bands (I*D/*I*G) obtained from the fitting curves is a common parameter for the evaluation of the degree of crystallization of carbon materials. In this work, the *I*D/*I*G value of CNTs was calculated to be 1.80, while that of HT-CNTs was 0.54. This indicates that the graphitization degree of carbon nanotubes prepared by the low-temperature CCVD method increased during heat treatment. Figure 5(m) displays the XRD patterns of CNTs and HT-CNTs, in which the sharper diffraction peaks of the latter sample further confirmed that heat treatment improved the carbon nanotube texture. The spacing between the (002) crystal planes calculated according to Bragg equation [49] decreased from 3.5462 to 3.4730 Å, which was completely consistent with HR-TEM data, indicating that high-temperature thermal treatment can greatly improve the texture and crystallinity of carbon nanotubes.

### 3.2 Morphology of Cf/CNTs–PyC/SiC composites

The phase composition, bulk density, and porosity of the samples from S0 to S9 are shown in Table 1. With the increase in the number of CNTs, the bulk density of the composites increased first, reaching the maximum in the S6 sample, and then decreased, while the porosity exhibited the opposite trend. Figure 6 presents cross-sectional morphologies of various Cf/CNTs–PyC/SiC composites. In contrast, as shown in Figs. 6(b)–6(d), the size and amount of inter-bundle pores in the S3, S6, and S9 specimens were significantly reduced. In addition, the local magnification images (the insets of Figs. 6(b) and 6(c)) revealed a large number of CNTs wrapped around the fibers in the S3 and S6 specimens, and the pores formed by the overlap of these CNTs were filled with the SiC matrix. Moreover, the thickness of the CNT layer on the fiber surface in S3 was about 2.1 μm, while that in S6 reached 6.3 μm, indicating that increasing the CNT deposition time to a certain extent can significantly increase the amount of CNTs deposited on the fiber surface and improve the density of composites. ### 3.3 Effect of CNTs on thermal conductivity of Cf/CNTs–PyC/SiC composites

Figure 7(a) depicts the working principle of the laser flash apparatus. Thermal diffusivity, heat capacity, and thermal conductivity of the S0, S3, S6, and S9 specimens as functions of temperature from 25 to 1400 ℃ along the thickness direction are presented in Figs. 7(b)–7(d). As can be seen from Fig. 7(b), the thermal diffusivity of the composites increased first and then decreased with the increase in the addition amount of CNTs in the range from room temperature to 1400 ℃. The highest and lowest thermal diffusivity was attributed to the S6 and S9 specimens, respectively, and the difference between both values was 1.49 times that at room temperature and 1.91 times that at 1400 ℃. Compared with the unmodified S0 specimen, the thermal diffusivity of the S6 sample was significantly improved, while that of the S9 sample was dramatically reduced. Based on the cross-sectional microstructures of the composites in Fig. 6 and the data listed in Table 1, it was inferred that the introduction of an appropriate amount of CNTs (≤ 4.94 wt%) in the fiber preform yielded a high-density composite. In addition, the thermal conductivity network formed by the overlap of CNTs further enhanced the thermal diffusivity of the composites.

*
Fig. 7(a) Schematic diagram of working principle of laser flash apparatus. (b–d) Thermal diffusivity, heat capacity, and thermal conductivity of Cf/CNTs–PyC/SiC composites, respectively. (e) Thermal conductivity mechanism of S0 and S6 samples.

The heat capacity of the composites showed the same trend as the thermal diffusivity from room temperature to 1400 ℃ with the increase in the CNT content (Fig. 7(c)). In particular, the heat capacity of carbon materials was larger than that of SiC at the same temperature. The increase rate of the heat capacity of carbon materials with the temperature also greatly exceeded that of SiC [54]. Therefore, with the rising number of CNTs in the composites, the heat capacity of the composites increased significantly, reaching the maximum in the S6 specimen with CNTs of 4.94 wt%. With further increase in the CNT content, the densification of the SiC matrix was hindered. Thus, the density of the composites dropped, resulting in a decrease in their heat capacity. The gradual decrease in the heat capacity rate from room temperature to 1400 ℃ can be explained by the Debye model as Eq. (14) [55]:

(14) cp(T)=9R(TθD)3∫0θD/Tχ4eχ(eχ−1)2dχ

where c**p*, *T*, *R*, and *θ*D represent the molar heat capacity at the constant pressure, the Kelvin’s temperature, the gas constant, and the Debye temperature, respectively. When the temperature is lower than Debye temperature, the heat capacity of the composites is proportional to the cube of the temperature. On the contrary, the heat capacity remains constant at temperatures above Debye temperature, which explains the observed change in the heat capacity with temperature.

Figure 7(d) displays the temperature dependence of the thermal conductivity for the four composites based on Eq. (2). According to the plots, the S6 sample with a moderate amount of CNTs had the highest thermal conductivity, reaching 8.50 W·m−1·K−1 at room temperature and 15.90 W·m−1·K−1 at 1400 ℃. Both values were 55% and 117% higher than those of the S0 sample,. However, the thermal conductivity of the S9 sample containing excessive CNTs amount was even lower than that of the unmodified S0 sample. For Cf/CNTs–PyC/SiC composites, the thermal conduction was derived from the lattice vibrations of the composite constituents including carbon fiber, CNTs, PyC interphase, and SiC matrix. As the main heat transfer carrier, phonons realize heat conduction in composites through diffusion [56,57]. Based on the kinetic theory of gas [58], the thermal conductivity of the composites can be expressed as Eq. (15):

(15) λ=13cpνl

where * ν and l* represent the group velocity and the mean free path of the phonon, respectively.

For materials with specific composition and microstructure, the thermal conductivity is proportional to the mean free path of phonons, which is determined by phonon scattering comprising the collisions between phonons themselves and between phonons and defects, conforming to Eq. (16) [59,60]:

(16) 1l=1lp+1ld

where *l*, *l*p, and *l*d are the mean free path of phonons, the path lengths of phonon–phonon and phonon–defect collisions, respectively.

The mean free path of phonons upon interaction is significantly affected by the temperature, as seen from Eq. (17) [61,62]:

(17) l=aαγT

where *a*, *α*, and *γ* represent the interatomic distance, the expansion coefficient, and the non-harmonic coefficient, respectively. With the increase in the temperature, the phonon vibration energy increases, as do the vibration frequency and the number of collisions between phonons, whereas the mean free path and the thermal diffusivity of the materials decrease (Fig. 7(b)).

In addition, the interactions between phonons and defects such as pores in composites also greatly impact the average free path and ultimately the heat transfer efficiency of phonons. To explore the influence of pores on the thermal conductivity of the composites, the multiphase Cf/CNTs–PyC/SiC composites can be simplified to a two-phase system composed of a dense solid phase and an air phase, by assuming that the thermal conductivity of air is much lower than that of dense solid. Based on this simplified porous system, Maxwell–Eucken formula can be applied as Eq. (18) [63,64]:

(18) λeq=λs2λs+λp−2(λs−λp)νp2λs+λp+(λs−λp)νp

where *λ*eq, *λ*s, and *λ*p represent the equivalent thermal conductivities of the porous materials, the dense solid phase, and the air phase, respectively; *ν*p is the pore volume fraction.

Ignoring the thermal conductivity of air, Eq. (14) can be further simplified to

(19) λeq=λs1−νp1+νp/2

Therefore, the pores in the composites exert a decisive effect on the thermal conductivity of the materials. By reducing the number of pores in the materials, the mean free path of the phonon heat transfer can be greatly increased, whereas the defect thermal resistance can be decreased, thereby improving the thermal conductivity of the materials. The theoretical analysis also confirmed that the S6 sample with an appropriate amount of CNTs had the highest density and the lowest porosity, exhibiting excellent thermal conductivity in the whole temperature range.

In summary, the improvement in the thermal conductivity of the composites through the addition of CNTs is caused by the following three mechanisms. (i) A thermal conductivity network is formed in the composites during the deposition and overlap of CNTs with high thermal conductivity. (ii) This network skeleton with the appropriate CNT content divides the interlayer macropores in the fiber preform into relatively uniform micropores, which promotes the deposition and densification of the SiC matrix in the interlayer macropores during CVI. As a result, the density of the composites increases, along with the mean free path of phonons, heat conduction, and thermal diffusivity, while the thermal resistance decreases. (iii) Compared with SiC, the heat capacity of the carbon materials and its increase rate with temperature become higher. The introduction of an appropriate CNT amount (about 4.94 wt%) not only promotes the densification of the composites but also significantly improves their heat capacity. The positive effect of introducing CNTs on the thermal conductivity of the composites is illustrated in Fig. 7(e).

The introduction method, the amount of CNTs, and the preparation process of the ceramic matrix are the main factors leading to the large differences between the results obtained in this work and those reported in the literature. To realize the synergistic improvement of mechanical properties and thermal conductivity of the composites, the interaction among the above factors should be fully considered. In this paper, a perfect synergy between thermal and mechanical properties of Cf/SiC composites, i.e., the functional and structural integration, was successfully realized in the S6 sample with the optimal CNT addition. Therefore, optimizing the amount of CNTs is the key strategy to synergistically improve the thermal conductivity and mechanical properties of the C/SiC composites through the structural enhancement of the CNT network, the densification of the matrix, and the “rivet strengthening” effect between the fiber and the matrix.

## 4 Conclusions

Based on the multi-scale design concept, nano-sized CNTs were introduced into the fiber preform via the CCVD process. The effect of the number of CNTs on the microstructure evolution during CVI densification of the Cf/SiC composites, as well as the thermal conductivity and mechanical properties of the composites were investigated in detail. CNTs prepared in this work exhibited the vapor–liquid–solid bottom–up growth mechanism, and the yield of nanotubes exponentially increased with deposition time. The density, thermal conductivity, and mechanical properties of the composites increased first and then decreased with the increase in the CNT content. The highest bulk density and the lowest porosity were achieved at the CNT deposition time of 60 min and the CNT amount of 4.94 wt%. The thermal conductivity of the corresponding composites reached 15.9 W·m−1·K−1, the flexural strength was as high as 435.5 MPa, the flexural modulus was 143.6 GPa, and the fracture toughness was 20.3 MPa·m1/2, which were 117%, 21.8%, 67.4%, and 10.3% higher than those of the unmodified composites, respectively. The introduction of an appropriate amount of CNTs into the composites can cause splitting of interlayer macropores, thereby building a thermal conductivity network, providing nucleation and growth sites for the SiC matrix, and promoting structure densification along with rivet strengthening. The combined effect of these mechanisms is the key to achieving a synergistic improvement in the thermal conductivity and mechanical properties of CNT-modified composites.
