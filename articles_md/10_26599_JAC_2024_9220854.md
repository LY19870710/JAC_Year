## 1 Introduction

Oxides with a fluorite structure have garnered widespread attention in various fields such as thermal barrier coatings [1−5], solid ionic conductors [6−8], and high-temperature catalysis [9,10]. Among the widely utilized fluorite oxides are those based on zirconia (ZrO2), hafnia (HfO2), or ceria (CeO2), often doped with other rare earth elements [3,11,12]. These oxides exhibit characteristic traits of low thermal conductivity and high ionic/mixed conductivity, presenting compelling technical advantages [13]. Recently, the introduction of the novel concept of high-entropy materials has led to increased exploration of HEFOs in various emerging applications [9,13−18]. Their intrinsic high entropy leads to lowered Gibbs free energy at elevated temperatures, rendering them generally more stable in high-temperature environments [14]. Researchers have begun to investigate high-temperature applications of these fluorite-structured high-entropy oxides (HEOs) and have uncovered their intriguing and promising potential [19−22]. For instance, cerium-based HEOs have shown low thermal conductivity [23,24] and good calcium–magnesium–alumina–silicate (CMAS) corrosion behavior [4]. However, practical applications often require these oxides to operate at high temperatures for extended durations [3], making it crucial to assess their long-term stability under such conditions. Consequently, studies on the high-temperature stability of these oxides at their intended operational temperatures are of paramount importance.

Taking inspiration from the widely used CeO2-based fluorite oxides, cerium-based rare-earth HEOs have emerged as one of the most extensively researched and promising HEFO systems to date [25−27]. The formation of single-phase HEFOs has been widely investigated, with theoretical advancements also aiding in the prediction of such phase formations. Spiridigliozzi *et al*. [26] introduced the standard deviation of atomic radii as an indicator for predicting the formation of single-phase HEFOs, positing that greater variance among atomic radii increases the likelihood of the formation of fluorite-structured HEFO. Focused research on the synthesis and application of these oxides has indicated intriguing structural symmetry transition behaviors [25,28]. For instance, Djenadic *et al*. [28] observed that the annealing of Ce-based rare-earth HEFOs at 1000 ℃ for one hour led to a partial transformation of the space group 225 (*Fm** 3¯m*) to 206 (*Ia** 3¯), as indicated by the evolving minor peaks in the XRD spectra. Furthermore, in the application of pyrochlore HEOs as high-temperature thermal barrier coatings, a pyrochlore-to-fluorite transition was observed during the high-temperature thermal cycling [22]. These studies underscore the possibility of fluorite HEOs to undergo phase transitions to undesired products, yet the underlying mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated.

Furthermore, the inherently slow diffusion kinetics characteristic of high-entropy materials often leads to kinetic stabilization [29], which poses a risk of inaccurately assessing their long-term stability at a given temperature. This slow diffusion also contributes to prolonged diffusion-driven phase transition behaviors, implying that the phase transitions in high-entropy materials is often a time-consuming process. For instance, taking the well-known and seemingly stable high-entropy alloy known as the Cantor alloy, Otto et al*. [30] performed annealing at 500 and 900 ℃ for 500 d to explore the complete phase decomposition into three distinct compositions. Similarly, Pickering *et al*. [31] observed the evolution of the decomposed Cr-rich phase after annealing at 700 ℃ for 500 h. Consequently, the short heat treatments typically employed in HEOs’ synthesis and applications may not reveal certain intricate phase transition behaviors, owing to the inherent slow kinetics associated with these materials.

In this study, we selected representative HEFO, composed of the commonly used fluorite bases CeO2, HfO2, and ZrO2, along with rare earth oxides such as Gd2O3 and La2O3. This composition results in a significant standard deviation in cationic radii of 0.1624 Å, indicative of high propensity for the formation of a single-phase fluorite structure [26]. We designated this synthesized material as (CeHfZrGdLa)O*x* (Ce-HEO) and subjected it to prolonged annealing at its intended operational temperature to demonstrate high-temperature stability behavior of HEFO. Our investigations revealed that, following a heat treatment of 100 h, the initially single-phase Ce-HEO underwent a transformation into three distinct phases: Ce-rich HEO, La-poor HEO, and La-rich HEO. Employing extensive X-ray and electron beam diffraction techniques, coupled with the statistical elemental composition analysis from AEMs, we have comprehensively identified and characterized these phases. Additionally, we found that the evolving peaks in Raman spectra serve as a practical and convenient method to track phase decomposition. These analyses enabled us to elucidate the intricate structure-composition relationships within the decomposed product post-annealing. Our findings unveiled a noteworthy phase transition phenomenon within the fluorite-structured Ce-HEO materials. Specifically, we observed a transition from the space group 225 to 206 at the commonly utilized operating temperature of 1000 ℃. This phase transition was attributed to the segregation of elements within the materials, facilitated by the elevated temperature.

### 2.1 Material synthesis

The synthesis of Ce-HEO was accomplished using the co-precipitation-peptization method as outlined in our previous works [16,32−34]. In a typical Ce-HEO synthesis, 0.001 moles of cerium ammonium nitrate (Ce(NH4)2(NO3)6, Sigma-Aldrich, 98.5%), gadolinium nitrate hydrate (Gd(NO3)3∙H2O, Alfa Aesar, 99.9%), lanthanum nitrate hexahydrate (La(NO3)3∙6H2O, Alfa Aesar, 99.9%), hafnium chloride (HfCl4, Alfa Aesar, 98%), and zirconyl chloride octahydrate (ZrOCl2∙8H2O, Sigma-Aldrich, 98%) were initially dissolved in DI water of 30 mL. Ammonia solution (NH4OH, Merck, 25%) was added to the solution to raise the pH above 12, precipitating the desired compounds. The resulting precipitates were subsequently separated through centrifugation and washed repeatedly with DI water until reaching a final pH of 7. A mixture of 5 mL DI water and nitric acid (HNO3, Merck, 69%) in the metal ion to HNO3 ratio of 1 : 1.5 was then added to the washed precipitates and vigorously shaken. The resulting suspension underwent sonication using a probe sonicator (Sonics Vibra-Cell VCX 750, 750 watts, 20 kHz) equipped with an in-built temperature sensor. To prevent solution evaporation, a temperature threshold of 80 ℃ was set, halting sonication when the solution reached this temperature and resuming when it fell below 80 ℃. The entire sonication process lasted approximately 1.5 h, resulting in the final transparent sol. The sol was subsequently dried in an oven at 100 ℃ for 24 h. Following this, the dried powders of Ce-HEO underwent calcination in a muffle furnace (LC033K5DN5, Nabertherm™,  Sweden) at 500 ℃ for 4 h for better crystallization. The calcined powders would be considered as the as-synthesized Ce-HEO. Subsequently, the heat treatment was also conducted on the as-synthesized Ce-HEO at 1000 ℃ for 24 and 100 h to investigate the thermal stability.

### 2.2 Material characterization

The XRD analysis was performed using a multi-functional high-intensity 2-dimensional (2D) X-ray diffraction system (RIGAKU RAPID II D/Max with MicroMax 007HF Cu/Cr dual-target rotating-anode). Phase analysis was performed using the search-match software with the ICDD PDF4+ database. The lattice parameters of the phases were estimated by the whole profile matching method using the FullProf suite software (version 5.10). The morphology and elemental analysis of MPOs were examined using an analytical scanning electron microscope (SEM; VERSA 3D, FEI). Raman spectra were acquired using a Renishaw inVia Qontor with a laser wavelength of 532 nm and a laser beam spot size of 1 µm. Optical absorption spectra were recorded with a spectrophotometer (Lambda 950, PerkinElmer). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy was conducted utilizing ESXCALAB Xi+. Two analytical transmission electron microscopes (ARM 200F, JEOL) were employed for the microstructure characterization of Ce-HEO. One of these transmission electron microscopes (TEMs), equipped with a cold field emission gun and an aberration corrector for the probe-forming lens system, was used to obtain atomic resolution STEM images. The other TEM, equipped with a Schottky field emission gun and an aberration corrector for the objective lens system, was utilized to acquire high-quality HRTEM images. Both TEMs operated at 200 kV. High-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) images were captured using an annular-type detector with a collection semi-angle of ~100–269 rad. STEM-EDX elemental maps were generated to reveal the chemical composition and phase distribution. For the TEM sample preparation, MPO powders were dispersed in ethanol under sonication for 15 min, and then deposited onto a copper grid with carbon membranes. Subsequently, the copper grid was dried under an oven lamp before being loaded onto a double-tilt TEM holder for structural characterization.

### 3.1 Visual appearance and structural properties

Figure 1 depicts optical properties of Ce-HEO before and after the heat treatment at 1000 ℃. The as-synthesized Ce-HEO powders exhibit a yellow hue color as shown in Fig. 1(a). When annealing at 1000 ℃ for 24 h, the powder color gradually changes to pale white (Fig. 1(b)), and after 100 h it became pure white (Fig. 1(c)). The changes in external color are typically indicative of alterations in internal structure/composition, which, in turn, affect their optical properties [35]. Therefore, X-ray diffraction (XRD) was conducted on the annealed samples to investigate the structure evolution shown in Fig. 1(d). The results showed that the as-synthesized oxides exhibit relatively poor crystallinity with broad XRD diffraction peaks, matching well with the fluorite structure of Ce0.5Eu0.5O1.75 (PDF#04-006-5865) based on the indexed crystal planes. After the heat treatment at 1000 ℃ for 24 h, the synthesized HEO exhibited a greater driving force for crystallization and growth, resulting in improved crystallinity and narrower diffraction peaks. However, with prolonged annealing for 100 h, the single-phase fluorite structure became unstable, leading to the splitting of XRD diffraction peaks and the emergence of multiple peaks. Unlike our previous observation in quaternary oxides (CeHfGdLa)O*x* when annealing at 1500 ℃ for 12 h led to a simple splitting of peaks from the fluorite structure into two sets of peaks [34], the XRD profile after 100 h annealing at 1000 ℃ in the current quinary oxides (CeHfZrGdLa)O*x* clearly indicates a more complex phase transition process. Considering the high standard deviation value of 0.1627 Å of Ce-HEO, this could possibly indicate a multi-phase evolution which was suggested to occur at lower temperatures. We performed whole profile matching on this profile (Fig. 1(e)), where four phases are used to calculate and simulate the XRD pattern. This refinement effectively matched the experimental profile. However, it is important to note that due to the complexity of the spectrum and the introduction of more phases, the four-phases refining may not entirely reflect the actual phase decomposition. From the XRD analysis, it is evident that apart from the original fluorite structure, another phase with an *Ia* 3¯ space group evolved after high-temperature annealing, indicating structural degradation. These structural transitions and the possible formation of multiple phases are often accompanied by changes in the composition and morphology, which prompted further investigations using SEM.

### 3.2 Morphology and elemental distribution statistics

Figure 2 shows the morphology of the as-synthesized Ce-HEO powders, which consist of agglomerates assembled by ultrafine grain with uniform elemental distribution. After annealing, the typical morphology under SEM is shown in Figs. 3(a)–3(f). This characteristic is attributed to the synthesis method, where these powders are derived from pre-made HEO sol, followed by drying and calcination. These powders inherently possess extremely fine particles with an average crystallite size of approximately 3.5 nm [16], as estimated from the XRD peak profile using Williamson–Hall (W–H) method [36]. After the heat treatment of 24 h at 1000 ℃ (Figs. 3(b) and 3(c)), most of the powders still retain the morphology of the dispersed ultrafine particles. However, the number of secondary particles assembled from these ultrafine particles significantly increases, and they become denser compared to the as-synthesized state. This aligns with the kinetics of crystal growth, as at high temperatures, the crystals tend to grow and undergo sintering reactions, causing small grains to bind tightly to form larger grains by a process known as Ostwald ripening effect [37]. However, due to the characteristics of high-entropy systems, the diffusion and motion of elements become particularly challenging [38], making grain growth in these HEOs notably slower compared to conventional oxides. This delayed grain growth due to multi-principal elements is one of the reasons for HEO could be a potential candidate for high-temperature applications.

With the prolonged annealing time at 1000 ℃, the differentiation in the particle morphology within the entire system becomes more pronounced, leading to the appearance of various particle shapes. After 100 h, as shown in Figs. 3(d)–3(f), we can observe roughly three types of particle morphologies: dispersed small particles, relatively dense particles, and highly dense particles. This reflects different degrees of sintering reactions occurring during the 1000 ℃ particle annealing process, likely influenced by variations in compositions [39]. Compositional analysis of these particles by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Fig. 3(g)) indicates that the composition of the small dispersed particles (labeled as the particle 2 in Fig. 3(d)) differs significantly from the original composition (the particle 1 in Fig. 3(a)). They exhibit notably higher La content, while Hf, Zr, and Gd contents are relatively low. In contrast, in the highly-dense particle 4 shown in Fig. 3(e), the La content is particularly low, with the other four elements being relatively uniform. Similarly dense particle 5 (Fig. 3(f)) displays a La content similar to that of the particle 1, which possesses an original uniform distribution of elements, but with a significant enrichment in Ce. The element distribution in the relatively high-density particle 3 (Fig. 3(d)) is more uniform, resembling the initial powders annealed at 500 ℃ for 4 h.

The variations in elements among these differently shaped particles indicate a high-temperature-driven process of element diffusion and segregation. Consequently, we have conducted a systematic statistical analysis on the elemental distribution in the as-synthesized state and after annealing at 1000 ℃ for 24/100 h. The distribution statistics of various cations across multiple particles in each state are presented in Fig. 4. The results show that the elemental distribution in the as-synthesized state is relatively uniform (Fig. 4(a)), although a chemical fluctuation of approximately 15–25 at% still exists due to the synthesis method. This fluctuation appears to intensify with high-temperature heat treatment. Post-annealing for 24 h at 1000 ℃ (Fig. 4(b)), the range of this fluctuation expanded to about 10–35 at%. Furthermore, after 100 h (Fig. 4(b)), the chemical fluctuation significantly broadens, covering a range of 0–55 at%. These changes effectively visualize the phase decomposition process, clearly showing that elemental segregation intensifies with prolonged annealing. Additionally, to accurately quantify the distribution of cations varying with annealing, we employed the standard deviation (SD) as a metric to represent the spread of the cation’s distribution data. The corresponding SDs for each element in every state are depicted in Fig. 4(d). It is evident that Ce, Hf, Zr, and La experience the significant changes in SD during the annealing process, while Gd exhibits the most stability with the least chemical fluctuation.

The pronounced increase in SDs following annealing at 1000 ℃ indicates that the observed phase decomposition is a direct consequence of intensified elemental segregation at elevated temperatures. To gain a deeper insight into the resulting decomposition products, we meticulously analyzed the cation distribution of Ce-HEO post 100 h annealing at 1000 ℃. This was subsequently categorized into four classes and depicted in Fig. 5. The statistical results reveal that after annealing of 100 h at 1000 ℃, this HEO system decomposes into four distinct phases with different compositions: Ce-rich, La-poor, La-rich, and uniform distribution. The trend of La redistribution is most pronounced, with La enriching the La-rich phase due to accelerated diffusion at high temperatures (Fig. 5(d)), leaving behind the La-poor phase (Fig. 5(c)). Furthermore, by analyzing the distribution of Zr and Hf, it becomes evident that Zr and Hf are mutually exclusive with La. The La-poor phase (Fig. 5(c)) is enriched with a significant amount of Zr and Hf, while the La-rich phase contains relatively lower concentrations of Zr and Hf. This can be understood based on the ionic radii of these constituent elements: Ce, Hf, Zr, La, and Gd have ionic radii of 185, 155, 155, 195, and 180 pm, respectively [40]. As La possesses the largest ionic radius, according to Hume–Rothery rules [41], it does not tend to form stable solid solution with the smallest ionic radius elements, Hf and Zr. Ce also exhibits enrichment behavior. Notably, in the Ce-rich phase (Fig. 5(a)), the mutual exclusion of La with Zr and Hf is observed, with their composition percentages exhibiting opposite trends. Some phases, where no element segregation and diffusion occurred, are referred to as the observed uniform phase shown in Fig. 5(b). These element segregations result in the diverse morphologies observed in the SEM images (Figs. 3(a)–3(f) and Fig. 6). The most significant difference lies in the segregation of La, leading to notable distinctions in sintering reactions. The phases enriched with La generally exhibit limited Ostwald ripening, resulting in relatively loose and non-dense particles, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Conversely, the La-poor phases typically undergo more pronounced ripening processes, ultimately resulting in denser particles.

### 3.3 Unveiling structure–composition relationship

To further elucidate the structure–composition relationship of the observed particles with different compositions in SEM and unravel the structural evolution in XRD profiles, we employed TEM to reveal the structure–composition relationship. Figure 7 presents typical selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns and EDS mapping results for Ce-rich, La-poor, and La-rich phases. The compositions in these regions closely resemble those observed in SEM. As shown in Figs. 7(a), 7(c), and 7(e), the corresponding SAED patterns reveal critical insights. The phases with lower La content, such as Ce-rich and La-poor, as depicted in Figs. 7(a)–7(d), both retain the complete fluorite structure. This is because these phases are rich of elements that stabilize the fluorite structure, such as Ce, Hf, and Zr, which maintain the stability of the fluorite structure at high temperatures [19]. On the other hand, the La-rich phase shown in Figs. 7(e) and 7(f), exhibits a bixbyite structure with a space group of *Ia** 3¯, consistent with the structure of cubic lanthanum oxides, c-La2O3. It is noteworthy that the space group Ia** 3¯ is a derivative of the fluorite structure, which could form with the degradation of the parent fluorite Fm** 3¯m* structure [28].

*
Fig. 7SAED pattern and EDS mapping results: (a, b) Ce-rich, (c, d) La-poor, and (e, f) La-rich phases.

At elevated temperatures, the diffusion of elements intensifies, leading to possible compositional segregation. The degree of segregation influences the formation of different structures, causing the structure of La-rich particles to degrade into a structure similar to c-La2O3. This is consistent with the previously observed Fm** 3¯m* to *Ia** 3¯ phase transition in Ce-based HEFO (CeLaPrSmY)Ox* during annealing at 1000 ℃ for 1 h [28]. However, it is worth noting that in the case of (CeLaPrSmY)O*x*, the transition from *Fm** 3¯m* to *Ia** 3¯ occurs within one hour when annealed at 1000 ℃. This rapid transition can be attributed to the composition of (CeLaPrSmY)Ox*, where only one fluorite-stabilizing element, Ce, is present. Consequently, as elemental separation begins, regions lacking Ce quickly degrade to the *Ia** 3¯ structure since La, Pr, Sm, and Y do not favor the fluorite structure. In contrast, in Ce-HEO, the coexistence of Ce, Zr, and Hf leads to a delay in the macroscopic structural transition. This results in the phase transition being observed only after prolonged annealing. The structure evolution of the Ce-rich and La-rich phases can be observed through changes in SAED patterns shown in Figs. 7(a) and 7(e). The ( 5¯4¯0¯), ( 4¯4¯0¯), ( 4¯0¯0¯), ( 2¯2¯2¯) polycrystalline ring of the Ia** 3¯ structure in La-rich particles highly coincides with the ( 3¯3¯3¯), ( 2¯2¯0),( 2¯00), ( 1¯1¯1¯),... polycrystalline ring of the Fm** 3¯m* structure in Ce-rich and La-poor particles. Only after symmetry lowering in La-rich particles, the (* 4¯3¯1¯) and ( 2¯1¯1¯) polycrystalline rings start to evolve from the original fluorite-structure’s diffraction pattern. This confirms the evolutionary relationship between these two structures.

In a fluorite structure, cations occupy the regular sites of an FCC lattice, and oxygen anions occupy the tetrahedral interstitial sites. This arrangement results in a highly symmetrical structure. Consequently, numerous derivatives of the fluorite structure, each with lower symmetries, can emerge under varying compositional or thermal conditions. However, due to their structural similarities, these derivative structures often exhibit comparable polycrystalline diffraction patterns, making them hard to distinguish. Previous studies have already highlighted the potential of HEFOs to adopt different configurations under specific compositional and thermal conditions. These include the fluorite itself, pyrochlore with the Fd*3*m* symmetry [22], and bixbyite with *Ia** 3¯ symmetry [28]. To ascertain the structural information of the decomposed La-rich particles, we conducted an in-depth TEM investigation. Figure 8 presents high-resolution TEM images taken from different zone axes for La-rich particles along with their corresponding SAED patterns. By comparing these patterns with the standard SAED patterns for Ia** 3¯ along the same zone axis, we confirmed that the decomposed La-rich particles belong to the Ia** 3¯ structure, rather than the pyrochlore structure observed in previous literature [22,42]. This reaffirms that the post-transition phase of this HEO is highly composition-dependent. Following decomposition, La-rich HEO predominantly adopts the bixbyite structure of c-La2O3, while Ce-rich and La-poor HEO predominantly retains the fluorite structure of CeO2.

### 3.4 Spectroscopic investigation of decomposition

Raman spectroscopy can offer vital information complementary to diffractions in oxide materials [43,44]. Figure 10 displays the Raman spectra of Ce-HEOs under different conditions, compared with the fluorite structure of CeO2, the bixbyite structure of Gd2O3, and hexagonal La2O3. As seen in Fig. 10(a), within the range of 200–800 cm−1, the Raman spectra among all Ce-HEOs show approximately three Raman peaks. These include a Raman peak around 290 cm−1, which corresponds to the T1u TO mode of the fluorite structure, a peak at around 460 cm−1 representing the F2g vibration mode of the Ce-based fluorite structure, and a vibration mode at approximately 600 cm−1. It is worth noting that in the as-synthesized Ce-HEO, which could be considered Ce-based oxides, the Raman peak at around 600 cm−1 is typically associated with oxygen vacancies [25]. These vacancies originate from the 3+ oxidation states of the constituents La, Gd, working in synergy with surface defects on as-synthesized nano grains, resulting in the activation of the T1u LO mode at around 600 cm−1. However, as the temperature increases, the concentration of trivalent elements La and Gd in the Ce-rich, La-poor fluorite phase decreases, leading to a reduction in charge balance-induced oxygen vacancies, and the grains undergo sintering, reducing surface defects. At this stage, the Raman peak at around 600 cm−1 should be identified as the F2g peak of the fluorite structure based on ZrO2 [45], originating from the La-poor phase enriched with a substantial amount of Zr and Hf, formed with the elemental segregation at high temperatures.

*
Fig. 10Raman spectra of annealed Ce-HEOs: (a) 200–800 cm−1 range; (b) 200–4500 cm−1 range.

Notably, it can be observed that the three fluorite structured phases, Ce-rich, La-poor, and uniform, all have corresponding Raman peaks, whereas the La-rich phase with the space group Ia** 3¯ does not exhibit clear corresponding Raman peaks in the range of 200–800 cm−1. These peaks do not match with the standard Raman peak around 360 cm−1 for Gd2O3 with the space group of Ia* 3¯. Therefore, we examined the distribution of Raman peaks over a wider range, as shown in Fig. 10(b), covering a range of 200–4500 cm−1. It is evident that after annealing at 1000 ℃ for 100 h, many high-intensity Raman peaks are observed within the range of 1000–4500 cm−1. Typically, Raman bands for rare earth oxides are found in the lower wavenumber range, which is why most studies in the literature investigate these vibrational modes at below 1000 cm−1 [46]. The peaks at high wavenumber in rare earth oxides often originate from fluorescence signals induced by laser excitation. For instance, the intense band around 2500 cm−1 in bixbyite-structured Gd2O3 arises from impurities of other rare earth oxides inevitably introduced. Similar fluorescence signals have also been detected in bixbyite-structured Eu2O3 and Sc2O3 [47]. In the case of Ce-HEO, these high-intensity fluorescence bands are absent in the as-synthesized state but begin to evolve and become particularly intense as the La-rich phase with the bixbyite structure becomes prevalent after prolonged heat treatment. These fluorescence bands are analogous to those in bixbyite-structured rare earth oxides like Gd2O3, likely due to the similar electron transition characteristics of bixbyite-structured rare earth oxides, resulting in similar fluorescence signals. These signals from Raman spectra may also serve as indicators of the extent of high-temperature decomposition reactions, especially as the La-rich bixbyite phase may be challenging to accurately detect from XRD due to less content and compositional complexity.

To further track the changes in the valence states of elements during phase decomposition, we conducted an XPS study. The XPS spectra of cations present in both the as-synthesized and heat-treated Ce-HEO are displayed in Fig. 11. Due to the multivalent nature of Ce [25], we fitted the sub-bands of 3d electrons of Ce in the XPS spectra to uncouple the information about Ce3+ and Ce4+. The presence of three spin–orbit doublets at binding energy (BE) of 881.9–900.5, 888.5–907.0, and 897.9–916.3 eV suggest a predominant presence of Ce4+ ions. On the other hand, the peaks located at BE of around 904 and 886 eV, indicative of Ce3+, point to a significant presence of Ce3+ [48]. This may arise from the abundance of oxygen vacancies [49], originating from surface defects or charge balance, which could lead to the formation of Ce3+ due to nearby charge redistribution [50]. Other elements, La, Hf, Gd, and Zr, with no possible change in valence, were identified as La3+, Hf4+, Gd3+, and Zr4+. After heat treatment, despite the phase separation, there is no significant change in the valence states of the metallic elements. Ce still maintains a coexistence of a large amount of Ce3+ and Ce4+, but the characteristic peaks representing Ce3+ have significantly intensified, indicating an increase in Ce3+ ions. Given the high surface sensitivity of XPS, it is plausible that the increase in Ce3+ ions originates from the segregation process, where Ce is continually separating from lower-valence La and Gd ions. This segregation leads to negatively charged oxygen vacancies on the surface that are no longer neutralized due to the absence of low-valent cations. Consequently, the excess charge of these oxygen vacancies is redistributed [50,51], resulting in the formation of the lower-valence-state Ce3+ cations. As for the other metallic ions, they displayed no significant valence state alterations post-high-temperature treatment and phase decomposition.

### 3.5 Electronic property

Further, to understand the consequences of phase decomposition and segregation, we examined one of the most fundamental properties of oxide materials: the energy bandgap. Employing UV–Visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy, the bandgaps of the annealed Ce-HEOs are assessed, and the resulting absorption spectrum and corresponding Tauc plot are presented in Fig. 12. Since no band structures had been previously reported for this type of mixed-phase oxides (MPOs), we adopted the bandgap type of CeO2 [52], which is indirect, to generate Tauc plot. Consequently, the *n* factor in the Tauc plot equation [53] was determined to be 2.

The analysis of the absorption spectra reveals that all annealed Ce-HEOs exhibit semiconductor characteristics, with their absorption edges falling within the range of 430–480 nm. Tauc plots derived from the absorption spectra (as shown in the inset of Fig. 12) were used to calculate the bandgap widths. The calculated bandgap widths for Ce-HEO of the as-synthesized state, annealed at 1000 ℃ for 24 h, and annealed at 100 h were 2.92, 3.02, and 3.25, respectively. It is evident that as the annealing duration increases, the bandgap width gradually increases. This can be attributed to morphological changes and compositional alterations occurring at high temperatures. At elevated temperatures, nanoparticles undergo sintering reactions and grow larger. Ultrafine nanoparticles typically exhibit more surface oxygen vacancies due to the large surface area, resulting in a smaller bandgap width [54]. This trend is also reflected in Fig. 12, where prolonged annealing at high temperatures leads to particle growth, reduced oxygen vacancies at the surface, and wider bandgap. Furthermore, with extended annealing time of 100 h, the grains continue to grow, while the compositional changes also occur. While these changes did not alter their semiconductor nature, the bandgap widens further. It is worth mentioning that the annealed Ce-HEO before and after phase decomposition, i.e., annealed for 24 and 100 h, has a relatively minor change in energy bandgap width of 0.23 eV. This indicates a possible stability of high-temperature properties of HEO that despite the splitting of the compositions into a number of cubic phases, the overall properties are still maintained in a similar manner.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, high-temperature stability of typical HEFO was investigated in this work. Through prolonged heat treatment at 1000 ℃, we identified and confirmed the structural degradation in the initially single-phase (CeHfZrGdLa)O*x* HEFO. This structural degradation can be attributed to intensified element diffusion at high temperatures. At 1000 ℃ for the heat treatment of 100 h, we found from AEMs that La and Ce in (CeHfZrGdLa)O*x* HEO spontaneously segregated, forming La-rich, La-poor, and Ce-rich phases. The La-rich particles transformed into a pyrochlore structure, resulting in evolving diffraction peaks in the XRD spectra. Such decomposition leads to significant changes in the morphology of Ce-HEO. However, the decomposition led to an insignificant energy gap, which could possibly indicate a property stability of Ce-HEOs over a wide range of compositions. This work holds significant practical implications for the potential utilization of fluorite-structured HEO in high-temperature applications.
