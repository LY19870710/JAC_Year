## 1 Introduction

The rapid advancements in artificial intelligence, 5G communication, and Internet of Things have propelled society into an era dominated by high-frequency electromagnetic signals [1]. These technologies, while revolutionizing information transmission and interconnectivity across diverse fields such as communication, energy, and healthcare, have also led to an unprecedented surge in electromagnetic wave (EMW) radiation [2−4]. Excessive EMW exposure not only interferes with the reliable operation of sensitive electronic equipment but also poses potential risks to human health [5−7]. Moreover, in the defense sector, the growing importance of electromagnetic stealth has driven demand for materials capable of effectively attenuating stray electromagnetic radiation [8−10].

To meet the growing demand for efficient EMW management, extensive research has been devoted to the development of high-performance EMW absorbing materials. Among various candidates, carbon-based materials—such as carbon nanotubes, carbon nanofibers, and graphene—have garnered significant attention because of their low density, tunable electrical conductivity, and chemical stability [11−15]. In particular, reduced graphene oxide (rGO), which has a large specific surface area and abundant defect sites, is highly promising for EMW absorption [16,17]. When assembled into aerogel architectures, rGO not only inherits these intrinsic advantages but also gains a hierarchically porous network characterized by ultralow density and continuous conductive pathways [18−21]. This unique structure enables multiple internal scattering paths, prolonged transmission paths, and improved impedance matching, positioning rGO aerogels (GAs) as highly attractive platforms for lightweight and broadband EMW absorption systems [22−24].

Despite their structural advantages, rGO aerogels still face significant challenges in achieving both strong low-frequency absorption and broad effective bandwidths (Fig. 1(a)) [18,25−27]. Fundamentally, carbon-based materials dominated by conduction loss tend to exhibit a sharp increase in the imaginary part of the permittivity (* ε″) at low frequencies, resulting in severe impedance mismatch and diminished absorption capability (Fig. 1(b)) [28,29]. According to dielectric dispersion theory, effective low-frequency absorption requires a large dielectric dispersion (Fig. 1(c)). Theoretically, strong interface polarization can lead to a high real part of the permittivity ( ε′) and enhance its frequency dispersion behavior [25,30,31]. Moreover, broadband microwave absorption is fundamentally influenced by the frequency-dependent behavior of the permittivity. This behavior arises from the relaxation and polarization of dipoles and charges, which manifests as a distinct peak in the ε*″ curve. These polarization peaks are often associated with two impedance matching points located on either side of the peak frequency, resulting in dual reflection loss minima (Fig. 1(b)). Therefore, the development of strong interface polarization is crucial for the design of materials capable of both low-frequency and broadband EMW absorption. To overcome the limitations of single-phase dielectric systems, heterogeneous interface engineering has become an effective strategy for enhancing interfacial polarization loss [32−36]. This method leverages the abundant defects and dipole centers at interfaces to improve dielectric relaxation. However, conventional approaches relying on phase-type modifications face inherent limitations, which hinder the further enhancement of polarization effects [37]. Therefore, achieving a stronger synergistic polarization relaxation response has emerged as a critical challenge in the field.

In this study, hierarchical heterogeneous interface engineering was proposed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which interfacial modulation governs EMW absorption performance. With the rGO aerogel as the structural framework, a lightweight porous rGO@zeolitic imidazolate framework-67 (ZIF-67)@polysilazane/ZIF-67 (GMSM)-derived magnetic graphene‒SiCN aerogel with a density of 72.7 mg∙cm−3 was successfully fabricated via a stepwise confinement strategy. This process involved the in situ* crystallization of ZIF-67, impregnation with a polysilazane nanolayer embedding ZIF-67 onto the rGO framework, and subsequent confined pyrolysis. The pyrolysis of ZIF-67 generated Co/CoO nanoparticles uniformly anchored onto the rGO framework, thereby constructing two-dimensional (2D)–zero-dimensional (0D) heterointerfaces that induced natural resonance and eddy current dissipation. Upon the subsequent incorporation of a conformal SiCN ceramics layer, integrated 2D–0D–2D hierarchical interfaces were established. The rationally designed hierarchical heterostructure promotes strong interfacial polarization and synergistic dipolar/defect relaxation, simultaneously optimizing low-frequency impedance and inducing dual reflection-loss minima, thereby achieving both pronounced low-frequency EMW absorption and an extended effective bandwidth. Notably, the GMSM-800 composite achieved a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −60.63 dB at a low frequency of 5.52 GHz and a maximum effective absorption bandwidth (EABmax) of 7.89 GHz at a reduced matching thickness of only 2.4 mm, demonstrating considerable promise for next-generation high-performance EMW absorbing materials.

### 2.1 Chemicals

GO nanosheets were obtained from Jiangsu XFNANO Materials Tech Co., Ltd.; liquid polysilazane (PSZ) was purchased from Kuberd Chemical (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.; tert-butanol (TBA), cobalt nitrate hexahydrate, and 2-methylimidazole were obtained from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

### 2.2 Synthesis of GMSM-derived magnetic graphene‒SiCN aerogels

The previously synthesized rGO aerogel was used as the base material [38]. To prepare GA@ZIF-67 (GM), 0.7275 g of Co(NO3)2·6H2O was dissolved in 50 mL of methanol under stirring for 30 min. The GA was then immersed in this solution and soaked for 24 h. Subsequently, 1.642 g (20.00 mmol) of 2-methylimidazole was dissolved in methanol with stirring for 30 min, and the resulting solution was added to the above Co2+-loaded GA system and left undisturbed for another 24 h to allow *in situ* growth of ZIF-67 on the aerogel skeleton. After solvent exchange and removal of excess tert-butanol, the sample was subjected to freeze-drying (cold trap temperature: −65 °C; sample temperature: 18 °C; vacuum level: 1.5 Pa) to yield GM. To synthesize the GMSM, the as-obtained GM was immersed in a tert-butanol solution containing polysilazane and ZIF-67 nanoparticles (ZIF-67 content was 1 wt% relative to polysilazane), and the polysilazane concentration was controlled by dispersing 1 g of polysilazane in 1 mL of tert-butanol. After soaking, the sample was freeze-dried again to remove tert-butanol, yielding the GMSM [39]. For comparison, rGO@ZIF-67@polysilazane (named as GMS) was prepared following the same procedure but without the addition of ZIF-67. The GMSM sample was subjected to pyrolysis at 600, 700, 800, and 900 °C at a heating rate of 2 °C/min and held for 3 h under a nitrogen atmosphere (0.1 MPa) to obtain GMSM-600, GMSM-700, GMSM-800, and GMSM-900, respectively. For comparison, GA, GM, and GMS were also annealed at 800 °C to obtain GA-800, GM-800, and GMS-800.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The microstructures of the GA, GM, GMS, and GMSM samples were investigated by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Regulus 8230, Hitachi) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200X, Thermo Scientific) operated at 200 kV. The phase compositions were analyzed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/teX Ultra 250, Rigaku) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5406 Å) operated at 40 kV. Raman spectra were collected using a Raman microscope with a 532 nm excitation laser. The surface chemical states were examined by an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher) with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (*hv* = 1486.6 eV). The electromagnetic parameters of the samples were measured using a vector network analyzer (5324A, Agilent) based on the coaxial line method in the frequency range of 2‒18 GHz. For sample preparation, the aerogels were impregnated with paraffin wax at 80 °C under vacuum conditions to ensure uniform infiltration. After cooling, the composites were cut into concentric ring shapes with an inner diameter of 3.04 mm and an outer diameter of 7.00 mm. These prepared ring samples were then used for electromagnetic parameter measurements. The magnetic properties of the samples were tested by a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM; LakeShore 7404, Labtop Technology).

### 2.4 Radar cross-section (RCS) simulation

The RCSs of GA-800, GM-800, GMS-800, and GMSM-800 samples were simulated via CST Studio Suite 2019. The simulation model consisted of a square structure (200 mm × 200 mm) composed of an upper absorbing layer and a bottom perfect electric conductor (PEC) layer. The thickness of the PEC layer was set to 1.0 mm. The absorber layer thickness was set to 4.6 mm at 5.52 GHz to match the typical quarter-wavelength design criteria. Open boundary conditions were applied in all directions, and the scattering behavior was analyzed by defining the *θ* and *ψ* angles in spherical coordinates.

### 3.1 Fabrication and characterization

The structural evolution of the composite aerogels is illustrated in Fig. 1(d). Initially, ZIF-67 nanocrystals were uniformly anchored onto the rGO aerogel via an *in situ* crystallization method, forming a homogeneous dispersion of particles throughout the porous carbon skeleton, as observed in the SEM images (Figs. 1(e) and 1(f)). This spatially regulated growth facilitates strong interfacial coupling between ZIF-67 and rGO network, thereby establishing a robust foundation for subsequent interface design [40]. Upon thermal annealing at 800 °C, the composite underwent distinct morphological evolution (Figs. 1(g) and 1(h)). The initially smooth surface transformed into a roughened and wrinkled texture, mainly resulting from the pyrolysis of the embedded ZIF-67 nanocrystals, which experienced structural collapse and volumetric shrinkage [41]. Notably, the hierarchical porous structure of the GA remained well preserved throughout this transformation. With further interfacial engineering via impregnation of a liquid polysilazane precursor followed by pyrolysis, a conformal and continuous SiCN ceramics nanolayer was deposited onto the GA framework (Figs. 1(i) and 1(j)). The ceramics shrinkage during high-temperature treatment led to pronounced wrinkling of the carbon scaffold, while gas release from the thermolysis of the intermediate ZIF-67 phase gave rise to distinct bubble-like surface features [26]. The additional incorporation of ZIF-67 into the outermost polysilazane precursor promoted localized crystallization during pyrolysis. This process, in concert with the co-pyrolysis of ZIF-67 and the SiCN ceramics matrix within the aerogel network, resulted in a more extensively deformed microstructure characterized by enhanced folding and denser, more prominent bubble structures (Figs. 1(k) and 1(l)), thereby constructing a hierarchical heterogeneous interfacial architecture within the magnetic carbon-ceramics aerogel.

The structural and chemical evolution of the aerogel composites under thermal treatment and compositional tuning was analyzed using XRD, Raman spectroscopy, and XPS analyses. As shown in Fig. 2(a), increasing the pyrolysis temperature from 600  to 900 °C promoted the crystallization of the cobalt-based phases. In particular, the diffraction peaks corresponding to metallic Co (JCPDS No. 15-0806) and CoO (JCPDS No. 78-0431) become sharper and more intense, indicating increased crystallinity with increasing temperature [42]. The compositional variation also influences the structural characteristics of the composites. As illustrated in Fig. 2(b), the GA-800 sample displays a distinct (002) diffraction peak of graphitic carbon, reflecting a well-preserved carbon lattice structure. However, when ZIF-67 is incorporated, the graphitic peak intensity is reduced in GM-800, accompanied by the appearance of well-defined Co and CoO diffraction peaks, confirming the *in situ* generation of cobalt-based nanocrystals derived from ZIF-67 [43]. Upon coating the framework with SiCN ceramics, the XRD pattern of GMSM-800 transitions into a broad, featureless hump centered at 20°–30°, indicative of an amorphous ceramics network [44]. Raman spectroscopy provides further insight into the carbon structural transformation (Figs. 2(c) and 2(d)). The absence of D (~1350 cm−1) and G (~1580 cm−1) bands in GMSM-600 is likely attributed to strong fluorescence signals arising from uncarbonized organic components at low temperatures. In contrast, the GMSM samples pyrolyzed above 700 °C exhibit prominent D and G bands. Notably, the *I*D/*I*G ratio increases from 1.32 to 1.57 with increasing pyrolysis temperature, suggesting an increasing density of defects and disordered regions [45]. This defect generation is closely linked to the synergistic confined pyrolysis of ZIF-67 and SiCN precursors, which induces structural distortion and enhances dipole polarization and interfacial relaxation, thereby improving electromagnetic attenuation. Compositional differences also play a pivotal role in regulating carbon disorders. For GA-800, the *I*D/*I*G ratio reaches 1.33, reflecting a relatively high defect density resulting from thermal reduction. Interestingly, the presence of cobalt nanocrystals in GM-800—formed during the decomposition of ZIF-67—catalyzes the graphitization of the carbon matrix. This catalytic effect increases the ordering of the carbon phase and suppresses defect formation, yielding a significantly lower *I*D/*I*G ratio of 1.18. However, once the carbon framework is coated with SiCN ceramics in GMS-800, the *I*D/*I*G value increases markedly to 1.55. This increase is attributed primarily to mechanical stresses arising from volumetric shrinkage during pyrolysis and the structural mismatch between the ceramics layer and the carbon skeleton. Moreover, the polymer-to-ceramics transformation of SiCN introduces free carbon and dangling bonds, further contributing to defect accumulation. In addition, this structural disorder can be partially alleviated through further compositional tuning. When ZIF-67 is reintroduced into the outermost ceramics precursor to form GMSM-800, the *I*D/*I*G ratio decreases slightly. This may be due to the confined co-pyrolysis of ZIF-67 and polysilazane, which promotes local crystallization of the SiCN ceramics. XPS analysis further revealed the surface chemical state of GMSM-800 (Fig. 2(e)). Peaks corresponding to C=C (284.6 eV), C–O (286.6 eV), C=O (287.8 eV), O–C=O (289.1 eV), and C–Si bonds (283.5 eV) are observed in the C 1s spectrum (Fig. 2(f)) [46]. The relatively weak C–Si signal reflects the incomplete conversion into SiC at this pyrolysis temperature. The Si 2p spectrum reveals strong Si–O (103.2 eV) bonding, attributed to residual oxygen-containing groups from rGO and possible oxygen release from ZIF-67 decomposition, alongside Si–N (102.3 eV) and Si–C (101.5 eV) bonds that confirm the formation of the SiCN ceramics network (Fig. 2(g)). In the N 1s spectrum, the presence of both N–Si (397.9 eV) and N–C (399.6 eV) peaks further validates the successful synthesis of Si–C–N ternary ceramics through the thermolysis of the polysilazane precursor (Fig. 2(h)) [31]. Finally, the Co 2p spectrum displays well-resolved peaks for Co2+ and Co3+ along with their satellite features, confirming the coexistence of metallic Co and CoO species, which are expected to synergistically increase magnetic loss contributions (Fig. 2(i)) [47].

The structural features and interfacial characteristics of the aerogel composites are shown in Fig. 3. The GM-800 sample displays well-dispersed nanoparticles derived from the decomposition of ZIF-67 (Figs. 3(a) and 3(b)). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) analysis of the GM-800 nanoparticles reveals distinct lattice fringes with spacings of 0.177 and 0.205 nm, corresponding to the (200) and (111) planes of metallic Co [48], respectively (Fig. 3(c)). Additionally, lattice spacings of 0.213 and 0.246 nm are observed, which can be indexed to the (200) and (111) planes of CoO, respectively (Fig. 3(d)). For the GMS-800 sample, the TEM images reveal increased thickness and contrast, which can be attributed to the conformal coating of the SiCN ceramics layer (Figs. 3(e) and 3(f)). Despite the predominantly amorphous nature of the SiCN matrix, HRTEM analysis confirms the presence of embedded crystalline phases of Co and CoO (Figs. 3(g) and 3(h)). Benefiting from the hierarchical interface system, GMSM-800 has developed a more intricate and hierarchical interfacial structure. Compared with those of GMS-800, low-magnification TEM images reveal a similar thickened morphology but with a higher concentration of ZIF-67-derived residues (Figs. 3(i) and 3(j)). Furthermore, metallic Co and CoO nanoparticles are again observed (Figs. 3(k) and 3(l)) and are embedded within the surrounding amorphous carbon layers. These carbon shells are presumed to originate from free carbon released during the crystallization of SiCN ceramics, a process that is catalytically facilitated by the cobalt species in the outermost layer [31]. Additionally, heterogeneous interfaces among different components inevitably induce lattice defects and dislocations, and lattice mismatch generates strain fields that affect charge transport and distribution, thereby enhancing polarization loss. The energy dispersive X–ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping results reveal the uniform distribution of the various elements inside the composite material (Fig. 3(m)).

### 3.2 Electromagnetic parameters and microwave absorption properties

Consequently, a systematic investigation was conducted to evaluate the electromagnetic parameters and corresponding EMW absorption properties of the aerogels. As illustrated in Fig. 4(a), the * ε′ value gradually decreases with increasing frequency, which is indicative of characteristic frequency-dispersion dielectric behavior [49]. An intriguing phenomenon in the  ε″–f* relationship of the GMSM system is the gradual shift of the * ε″ peak with increasing pyrolysis temperature. The  ε″ displays pronounced peaks at distinct frequencies—approximately 13.4 GHz for GMSM-700, 11.2 GHz for GMSM-800, and 5.8 GHz for GMSM-900 (Fig. 4(b)), with the peak positions shifting progressively toward the lower-frequency region as the pyrolysis temperature increases. The tangent loss exhibits a similar trend, with evident peaks at corresponding frequencies (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). Such fluctuations in both  ε″ and tangent loss across specific frequency ranges are typically attributed to polarization relaxation processes [50−52]. The integration of hierarchical heterogeneous interfaces within the aerogel matrix substantially modulates both the location and intensity of polarization peaks, underscoring the pivotal role of interfacial engineering in tailoring dielectric behavior [53]. To further clarify the underlying loss mechanisms, the total dielectric loss was decomposed into contributions from conduction loss ( εc″) and polarization loss ( εp″) by fitting the experimental data using the least squares method [54]. As shown in Fig. 4(c), the values of  εp″/ εc″ for all the GMSM samples exceed 1 across the 2–18 GHz frequency range, indicating that polarization loss is the dominant loss mechanism over conductivity loss. Moreover, with increasing pyrolysis temperature, the relative contribution of polarization loss gradually increases, suggesting enhanced dielectric relaxation behavior (Fig. 4(d)). This phenomenon is consistent with the observed variations in the Cole–Cole plots of the GMSM samples prepared at different temperatures (Fig. 4(e)), indicating that with increasing degrees of pyrolysis, more heterogeneous interfaces are formed in situ*, which significantly prolongs the dielectric relaxation time [55]. These semicircles all appear around the frequencies at which relaxation peaks appear in the imaginary part. The surrounding heterogeneous interfaces may facilitate electron aggregation and transfer, which is inferred to give rise to local potential variations and enhanced interfacial polarization [56]. The real part (* μ′) and the imaginary part ( μ″) of the composite aerogels are shown in Fig. 4(f). Generally, the peak located at frequencies below 10 GHz is attributed to natural resonance, whereas exchange resonance accounts for the peaks occurring at frequencies above 10 GHz [57−59]. Natural resonance is the primary contributor in the low-frequency range, originating from cobalt-based nanocrystals derived from ZIF-67. In addition, magnetic eddy currents also contribute significantly to magnetic loss within the studied frequency band, leading to energy dissipation [48,60,61]. The C*0 values remain constant across the 8–18 GHz range, implying that the eddy current loss mechanism also contributes to the magnetic absorption performance of the GMSM samples (Fig. 4(g)) [62]. The VSM was used to test the magnetic properties of GMSM samples (Fig. S2 in the ESM). GMSM-800 exhibits enhanced magnetic properties, as evidenced by its well-defined hysteresis loop with an *H*c of 357.4 Oe and an *M*s of 3.36 emu/g. Generally, higher *M*s and *H*c values can help the material generate resonance effects at high frequencies, thereby increasing its capacity to attenuate EMWs [63]. The attenuation coefficient increases with increasing pyrolysis temperature, indicating that a higher polarization relaxation ability leads to greater dielectric loss (Fig. 4(j)). In contrast, the impedance matching exhibits an opposite trend, and its continuous deterioration can lead to impedance mismatch, which hinders EMW penetration and ultimately degrades the overall absorption performance [64−67]. The reflection loss (RL) values of the composites were calculated based on the electromagnetic parameters using transmission line theory to evaluate their EMW absorption properties. Generally, RL ≤ −10 dB indicates that more than 90% of the incident EMW is absorbed, and the corresponding frequency range is referred to as the effective absorption bandwidth (EAB). The EMW absorption properties of GMSM-600 are relatively poor, exhibiting a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −14.34 dB (Fig. 4(h)). As the pyrolysis temperature increases, GMSM-700 displays a continuous broadband characteristic with dual absorption peaks. This observation suggests a potential correlation between the microwave absorption performance and the multi-scale heterogeneous interfaces present in the GMSM samples. Specifically, GMSM-700 demonstrates excellent EMW absorption performance, with an RLmin of −61.56 dB and an EABmax of 7.03 GHz (Fig. 4(i)). Additionally, GMSM-800 exhibits a notable advantage in terms of low-frequency EMW absorption performance, achieving an RLmin of −60.63 dB at 5.52 GHz, with an EAB of 2.12 GHz, ranging from 4.68 to 6.80 GHz (Fig. 4(k)). When the thickness is 2.4 mm, the sample exhibits a broad EAB of 7.89 GHz. Owing to impedance mismatch, GMSM-900 exhibits the poorest microwave absorption performance, achieving an RLmin of −10.21 dB and an EAB of merely 0.21 GHz (Fig. 4(l)).

Notably, a pronounced drop in * ε′ is observed at approximately 11 GHz for GMSM-800, suggesting a strong relaxation process at this frequency. This phenomenon can be attributed to enhanced dipole polarization, as well as the formation of complex hierarchical interfaces. When the oscillation frequency of the electric field exceeds the relaxation time of the dipoles, they fail to respond effectively, resulting in a sharp decrease in  ε′ and a corresponding peak in the imaginary part [50]. As shown in Fig. 5(b), both GA-800 and GM-800 display a monotonic decline in  ε″ across the measured frequency range, with no evident polarization peaks. In contrast, GMS-800 and GMSM-800 exhibit prominent polarization peaks centered at approximately 11.2 GHz. This behavior is associated with the presence of a well-defined 2D–2D heterogeneous interface between the graphene nanosheets and the SiCN ceramics layer, which facilitates strong interfacial polarization. Notably, the ε*″ peak intensity in GMSM-800 is significantly greater than that in GMS-800, further confirming that the outer ZIF-67-derived Co species may locally promote the crystallization of SiCN, thereby modulating the interfacial charge dynamics. As shown in Fig. 5(c), GA-800 and GM-800 exhibit relatively high conduction loss in the low-frequency region, which may lead to impedance mismatch and reduced EMW absorption efficiency in this range. In contrast, GMS-800 and GMSM-800, characterized by stronger polarization capacity, show significantly lower conduction loss at low frequencies, suggesting improved impedance matching under these conditions. Moreover, compared with the GA-800 and GM-800 composites, which primarily feature 0D–2D heterointerfaces, the introduction of 2D–2D heterointerfaces in GMS-800 and GMSM-800 leads to a substantial increase in the proportion of polarization loss (Fig. 5(d)). To explore the effects of hierarchical heterogeneous interface engineering on EMW absorption performance, the reflection loss of GA-800, GM-800, GMS-800, and GMSM-800 aerogels was investigated. As shown in Fig. 5(e), although GA-800 has a relatively wide EAB of 6.59 GHz, its RLmin is only −25.48 dB owing to its relatively simple loss mechanisms. The inherent 3D conductive network constructed from rGO en sures favorable impedance matching; however, the lack of strong dipole or interfacial polarization severely restricts the intrinsic attenuation capacity. Upon loading ZIF-67-derived cobalt-based nanocrystals, GM-800 demonstrates a moderate increase in EMW absorption intensity, with RLmin improving to −35.98 dB (Fig. 5(f)). In addition, GMS-800 benefits from the synergistic integration of ZIF-67 derivatives and the two-dimensional SiCN ceramics framework. The simultaneous construction of both 0D–2D and 2D–2D heterointerfaces significantly increases the dielectric loss contributions through enhanced interfacial polarization and dipolar relaxation [68]. As a result, GMS-800 achieves a substantial RLmin of −62.6 dB and a broad EAB of 6.67 GHz (Fig. 5(h)). However, owing to insufficient optimization of low-frequency impedance matching, the EMW absorption performance in the sub-6 GHz range remains unsatisfactory. Remarkably, GMSM-800 demonstrates outstanding EMW attenuation performance across both low- and high-frequency regions, with efficient impedance matching and enhanced loss mechanisms coexisting over a broad spectrum (Figs. 5(g) and 5(i)). This stems from the hierarchical design of multiscale heterogeneous interfaces—comprising 0D–2D, 2D–2D, and outer-layer 0D–amorphous ceramics couplings—formed via confined co-pyrolysis of ZIF-67 and the SiCN precursor. These interfaces not only amplify interfacial polarization and promote multi-site relaxation but also locally redistribute the electromagnetic field, thereby enabling the simultaneous realization of enhanced low-frequency attenuation and broadband effective absorption [69]. Comparative analyses further underscore the advantages of the design of hierarchical heterogeneous interfaces in GMSM-800. As illustrated in Fig. 5(j), the minimum reflection loss peak of GMSM-800 occurs at a significantly lower frequency than those of most reported dielectric-type EMW absorbers, reflecting its superior low-frequency attenuation capability. Moreover, Fig. 5(k) shows that, relative to other graphene-based absorbers, GMSM-800 achieves a broad effective absorption bandwidth with a relatively small material thickness, demonstrating its potential for lightweight and efficient EMW attenuation applications.

### 3.3 RCS simulation

To further assess the EMW attenuation capabilities under far-field conditions, RCS simulations were conducted on the basis of a typical stealth evaluation model, as illustrated in Fig. 6(a) [70]. The simulation employed a dual-layer configuration comprising a PEC substrate and an absorber coating [71]. Plane wave incidence was applied at 5.52 GHz with varying angles (*θ*) to evaluate the backscattering intensity across the range from −90° to 90°. As depicted in the three-dimensional scattering intensity maps, GA-800, GM-800, and GMS-800 exhibit similar RCS distributions, with only slight reductions in RCS values of 12.87, 13.43, and 13.59 dB for GA-800, GM-800, and GMS-800, respectively, indicating limited effectiveness in far-field electromagnetic suppression (Figs. 6(b), 6(c), and 6(e)). In contrast, GMSM-800 demonstrates a pronounced attenuation of radar echoes, with a markedly diminished RCS profile over the entire angular range (Fig. 6(f)). Particularly at the normal incidence angle (*θ* = 0°), GMSM-800 achieves a substantial RCS reduction of 27.73 dB∙m2 compared with the PEC reference surface (Fig. 6(d)). These results provide compelling evidence of the significant benefits of enhanced polarization loss enabled by hierarchical heterogeneous interface engineering.

### 3.4 EMW absorption mechanism

The mechanism behind the excellent EMW absorption performance of the GMSM-800 magnetic carbon-ceramics aerogels is illustrated in Fig. 7. First, the coating of a low-dielectric SiCN ceramics onto high-dielectric graphene sheets creates a graded impedance structure [72]. Combined with the inherent low-density and hierarchical porous structure of the aerogel, this configuration significantly improves the impedance matching between free space and the absorbing medium, thereby facilitating the entry of incident EMWs into the material with minimal reflection [73,74]. Once inside, the three-dimensional porous network offers a tortuous path that facilitates multiple internal scattering and extended propagation paths [75]. Second, the abundant heterogeneous interfaces formed *in situ* during the co-pyrolysis of the ZIF-67 and SiCN precursors play a critical role in enhancing the interfacial polarization. These interfaces include both nanoscale 0D–2D assemblies, such as ZIF-67-derived Co/CoO nanocrystals anchored on SiCN or graphene sheets, and extended 2D–2D contacts between the SiCN and graphene layers. The resulting multi-scale interface architecture disrupts structural continuity and generates a high density of interfacial dipoles. Under alternating electromagnetic fields, these dipoles undergo delayed relaxation, resulting in pronounced interfacial polarization loss and effective dissipation of electromagnetic energy [76]. In addition to interfacial polarization, defect-induced polarization also plays a vital role in electromagnetic energy dissipation. The rGO nanosheets possess numerous defects, oxygen-containing functional groups, and dangling bonds, providing active sites for defect-induced polarization. Additionally, lattice mismatch between different components can lead to lattice defects and dislocations near the interfacial region. These defect sites promote local charge transfer and result in charge accumulation or depletion, thereby enhancing defect-induced polarization [28,77]. Furthermore, various dipole polarization mechanisms also play significant roles. The introduction of polar functional groups and the coexistence of multiple phases increase the density of polarization centers, facilitating dipolar relaxation under alternating electromagnetic fields [55]. Third, the graphene framework enables the migration of free electrons under external electromagnetic fields, thereby contributing to conduction loss. At the heterojunction interfaces—particularly those formed between graphene, SiCN, and ZIF-67-derived domains—localized energy barriers induce electron hopping. This interplay between migrating electrons within the carbon network and interfacial hopping electrons results in increased conduction loss. Finally, the incorporation of ferromagnetic Co/CoO nanocrystals derived from the thermal decomposition of ZIF-67 enhances the magnetic loss characteristics [12,78]. These magnetic domains are spatially distributed, forming a multiscale magnetic network that facilitates diverse magnetic loss mechanisms. At low frequencies, natural resonance originating from cobalt-based nanocrystals predominates. Additionally, the presence of conductive pathways and ferromagnetic components enables the formation of eddy currents, thereby promoting energy attenuation, particularly in the mid- to high-frequency range [79]. Therefore, the combination of these synergistic dissipation mechanisms accounts for the outstanding EMW absorption performance of the GMSM-800 magnetic carbon-ceramics aerogel.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, a stepwise confinement strategy was successfully employed to construct hierarchical heterogeneous interfaces within graphene-based aerogels. This approach enabled the simultaneous integration of ZIF-67-derived Co/CoO nanocrystals and a SiCN ceramics nanolayer into the rGO network, resulting in abundant 0D–2D and 2D–2D interfacial architectures. The resulting hierarchical interfaces significantly enhanced dipole and interfacial polarization. As a result, the rGO@ZIF-67@polysilazane/ZIF-67-derived magnetic graphene‒SiCN aerogel achieved outstanding low-frequency electromagnetic wave absorption (−60.63 dB at 5.52 GHz) and a broad effective absorption bandwidth of 7.89 GHz with a thickness of only 2.4 mm. This work demonstrates the great potential of step-confined growth as a powerful interface engineering strategy for the development of lightweight, low-frequency, and broadband EMW absorbers.
