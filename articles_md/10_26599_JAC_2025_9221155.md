## 1 Introduction

In practical application scenarios such as unmanned aerial vehicle skins, armored vehicle hulls, and precision electronic equipment enclosures, there is an urgent demand for multifunctional materials that can simultaneously achieve microwave–infrared compatible stealth, thermal insulation, and certain compressive strength [1−10]. These scenarios require materials to not only effectively attenuate radar signals in key frequency bands and suppress infrared radiation signatures but also withstand mechanical stress from external environments while maintaining stable thermal management performance to avoid overheating-induced functional degradation [11−13]. However, traditional single-function materials struggle to balance these integrated requirements: Conventional stealth materials often lack thermal insulation capabilities, whereas thermal management materials rarely meet stealth and mechanical performance standards simultaneously [14].

Metastructures, as artificially engineered structures with subwavelength periodicity, offer unique advantages in multifunctional integration [15]. In our previous studies, a triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) structure with mathematically defined continuous surfaces was used to optimize impedance matching by adjusting the distribution of effective permittivity and permeability at the macroscale while providing inherent mechanical strength to ensure compressive capacity [16].

Within this context, polysilazane was selected as a precursor material. Its ability for photopolymerization-based three-dimensional (3D) printing enables the precise fabrication of complex TPMS structures [17,18]. Subsequent high-temperature pyrolysis converts this polymer-derived ceramic precursor into an inorganic TPMS-SiCN ceramic, which serves as the core material responsible for the microwave absorption functionality [19].

To achieve thermal insulation and infrared stealth capabilities, phase change materials (PCMs) have also been introduced, among which paraffin wax is selected for its superior properties: It exhibits high latent heat during phase transition, enabling efficient thermal buffering [20−22]; its phase transition temperature matches the operating range of target application scenarios (e.g., mechanical operating temperature of 50–90 °C), ensuring practical applicability.

Importantly, a porous SiOC ceramic is integrated between the TPMS-SiCN ceramic and the paraffin wax, serving as a bridge between the two materials. The micro/nanopores of the ceramic physically constrain paraffin to mitigate leakage during liquefaction, addressing a key limitation of traditional PCM-containing frameworks [12,23,24]. Furthermore, the porous SiOC ceramic introduces abundant micro/nano interfaces, which enhance electromagnetic wave attenuation through mechanisms such as multiple reflections and scattering [19].

This study successfully developed an integrated material incorporating SiCN ceramics (wave-absorbing functional component), porous SiOC ceramics (microscopic porous medium), and paraffin wax (phase change component). We aim to leverage the synergistic effects across different scales: Macroscopically, the TPMS metastructure regulates electromagnetic wave propagation; microscopically, the porous interfaces enhance electromagnetic wave loss and thermal storage capacity; and, as a dynamic thermal component, paraffin responds to temperature changes to maintain thermal stability.

Experimental results showed that the composite material achieves a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −31.39 dB in the X-band, which is a significant improvement over that of pure SiCN ceramics. Its effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) covers 96% of the X-band (4.05 GHz), and the RLmin is below −20 dB in the 9.48–10.44 GHz range, enabling absorption of over 99% of electromagnetic waves. When heated at 88.5 °C (exceeding the phase transition temperature) for 42 min, the composite material maintains a temperature difference of 36.6 °C from the heating environment, exhibiting thermal insulation performance. The infrared stealth capability of this composite is achieved through the regulation of its surface temperature, which effectively suppresses outward thermal radiation emission—the primary source of detectable infrared signatures.

### 2.1 Preparation of SiCN ceramic metastructures

First, polysilazane (PSZ; WHC 1800; Guangzhou Winhigh Chemical & Technology Co., Ltd., China) was mixed for 30 min with a photosensitive resin (P200, Shenzhen SUMAOPAI Technology Co., Ltd., China) at a 1 : 1 weight ratio. Then, entrapped air bubbles in the solution were completely removed to obtain raw materials (UV-PSZ), which were suitable for DLP printing via a DLP ceramic 3D printer (Shenzhen SUMAOPAI Technology Co., Ltd., China). Finally, the UV-PSZ solution is poured into the 3D printer vat to print the green body. The printer used a UV light source with a wavelength of 405 nm, printing each layer with a thickness of 0.05 mm. The bottom layer has an exposure time of 55 s, and the remaining layer has an exposure time of 8 s. The green body printed in this study is a gyroid structure with a three-dimensional size of 40 mm × 17 mm × 5.5 mm and a design porosity of ~89 vol%. All the green bodies were cleaned via ultrasonication to remove residual resin and placed in a UV curing box for secondary curing. All the processes were carried out in the dark.

The green bodies were pyrolyzed in a N2 environment in a tube furnace. The samples were heated from room temperature to 200 °C for 2 h at a rate of 0.5 °C per minute to crosslink them, after which they were heated to 400 °C for 2 h at the same rate. The samples were then prepyrolyzed at 600 °C for 2 h at a heating rate of 1 °C·min−1 to obtain a gyroid structure that had not yet fully transformed into a SiCN ceramic (the end temperature for the polymer-to-ceramic transformation is typically approximately 800 °C) [18].

### 2.2 Preparation of composite materials

First, methyltrimethoxysilane (MTMS) and triethoxyvinylsilane (TEVS) were combined at a 1 : 1 molar ratio. The precursors (MTMS and TEVS, Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) were mixed and hydrolyzed with anhydrous ethanol, deionized water, glacial acetic acid, and DMF at a molar ratio of 1 : 3 : 4 : 0.5 : 0.2 at room temperature for 6 h. Then, ammonia was added, adjusting the pH of the solution to 9. Then, the prepyrolyzed SiCN ceramic was immersed in the solution and allowed to stand, after which the solution transformed into a wet gel, which filled the prepyrolyzed SiCN ceramic. The sample was then aged with anhydrous ethanol at 60 °C for 48 h. After that, the sample underwent four cyclohexane exchanges within 24 h. Finally, after freeze-drying via a vacuum freeze dryer (FD-1A-50+, BoYiKang (Beijing) Instrument Co., Ltd., China), the sample was pyrolyzed at 1200 °C for 2 h in a nitrogen atmosphere at a heating rate of 1 °C·min−1 to obtain a SiOC microporous ceramic (MC)-filled SiCN ceramic sample (MCGS). Notably, this heat treatment also completes the polymer-to-ceramic transformation of the gyroid structure, resulting in the formation of a SiCN ceramic material. The ceramic with a gyroid structure, obtained by pyrolyzing the preprepared SiCN ceramic according to this pyrolysis procedure, is named GS. The preparation process of MC is identical to that of MCGS, except that the prepyrolyzed SiCN ceramic is not immersed. The purpose of the prepyrolysis approach is to obtain a sample in which most of the organic component (the photopolymer) is decomposed before infiltration of the solution used to generate the microporous material, avoiding cracking due to the high amount of gas released during the full pyrolysis treatment. Furthermore, the use of a prepyrolyzed (and not fully pyrolyzed) gyroid structure as the component to be infiltrated by the microporous material-forming solution reduces the shrinkage mismatch that occurs during pyrolysis between the 3D-printed component and the infiltrated microporous material, leading to a composite with a low amount of overall defects. ### 2.3 Characterizations

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) was used to evaluate the crystalline phase assemblage of the samples. A Cu target was used as the radiation source in the 2*θ* = 10°–80° range. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Nova Nano SEM 400, FEI, USA) and a Raman spectrometer (XploRA Nano, HORIBA, France) were used to analyze the structural evolution of the carbon in the materials. The microstructure of the sample was obtained via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai F20, FEI, USA). An X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; ESCALAB Xi+, Thermo Scientific, USA) was performed using Al-Kα X-rays. The bonding of the samples was obtained via a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FT-IR; Nicolet iS 5, Thermo Fisher, USA). The pore structure of the MCs was analyzed via a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET; ASAP2460, Micromeritics, USA) and mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP; AutoPore IV 9520, Micromeritics, USA) methods. The mass loss of the samples at high temperature in an air atmosphere (the heating rate was 5 °C∙min−1, and the temperature was increased to 900 °C) was measured by a thermal gravimetric analyzer (TGA; HCT-4, Beijing Hengjiu Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd., China). In this study, a universal testing machine (Instron 5969, Illinois Tool Works Inc., USA) was used to evaluate the mechanical properties of the samples. The compression test sample size was 9 mm × 9 mm × 9 mm. The samples were tested at a rate of 0.5 mm·min−1, and the reported data are the average of 5 tests. The thermal conductivity of PW and P-MCGS was measured via a thermal conductivity meter (TPS2500S, Hot Disk® Instrument, Sweden). The sample size was 20 mm × 20 mm × 3 mm, and the test temperature points were 25 and 58 °C. An infrared thermal imager (Testo869, Testo Inc., Germany) was used to record temperature changes.

The P-MCGS materials were processed to a size of 22.86 mm × 10.16 mm × (2–3) mm, and the waveguide method was used to obtain the electromagnetic parameters of the X-band. The electromagnetic parameters of the samples were determined via a vector network analyzer (VNA; N5244A, Agilent, USA). The RL of the composites was calculated via transmission line theory (Eqs. (1) and (2)) [25]:

(1) RL(dB)=20log10|(Zin−1)/(Zin+1)|

(2) Zin=μrϵrtanh(j2πfdcμrϵr)

where *Z*in is the input impedance of the absorber, and the parameters affecting it include the frequency (*f*), speed (*c*) of electromagnetic wave (EMW) transmission in vacuum, and the thickness (*d*) of the absorber. It is also affected by the complex permeability (*μ*r) and complex dielectric constant (*ε*r) of the material.

For the XRD, TEM, Raman, and XPS characterizations of the MC sample, ground powdered MC was used; the remaining characterizations were conducted using a bulk MC that met the testing requirements.

### 3.1 Microstructure and chemical composition

Figure 2(a) shows the XRD patterns of the MC and GS samples. There is a typical amorphous hump (2*θ* ≈ 24.5°) in the figure. There are some graphitic (2*θ* = 26.4°) and turbostratic (2*θ* = 43.5°) carbon phases in the GS ceramics, whereas the presence of a β-SiC phase (2*θ* = 35.6°) needs to be further confirmed (see below) [26]. The P-MCGS sample has two distinct diffraction peaks at 2*θ* = 21.17° and 2*θ* = 23.53°, which correspond to the (110) and (200) crystal planes of paraffin, respectively, as shown in Fig. 2(b), which is consistent with the characteristic diffraction peak position of pure paraffin (JCPDS No. 40-1995) [27]. Since both MC and GS are typically amorphous, the diffraction peaks in the XRD pattern of P-MCGS are mainly the diffraction peaks of paraffin. Moreover, the development of the composite material occurred only through physical interactions, with no chemical reactions between its constituents [28]. The TEM analysis confirmed the presence of both graphitic and turbostatic carbon as well as β-SiC (Figs. 2(c)–2(e)). The heterogeneous interface between the crystalline phases and the amorphous matrix, as well as between the different crystalline phases, increases the dielectric polarization loss and enhances the EMW absorption performance [29].

The carbon phase in the materials was further analyzed by Raman spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 2(f). The three samples have two peaks at approximately 1340 and 1590 cm−1, corresponding to the D and G bands, respectively (Table 2). The D band represents the vibration of sp3 atoms in disordered graphite, and the G band represents the in-plane vibration of sp2 atoms in a two-dimensional hexagonal lattice. I*D/*I*G values are commonly used to reflect the degree of graphitization of carbon in a material [30]. The *I*D/*I*G value of GS is the lowest, indicating that GS has a relatively high degree of graphitization, which is beneficial for increasing dielectric loss and achieving high complex permittivity [31]. This also shows that the SiCN structural ceramic material in P-MCGS plays a major role in the microwave absorption process.

SEM images show the morphology of the GS, MC, and P-MCGS samples. The GS surface (Fig. 3(a)) provides evidence of the stairstepping effect, which is characteristic of the vat photopolymerization technology adopted [32]. The micropores in the section (Fig. 3(b)) are caused by the release of a large volume of gas during high-temperature pyrolysis, but the overall structure is free of defects such as cracks [25]. As shown in Fig. 4(a), MIP analysis revealed that the pores in the MC were mainly macropores, with a porosity of approximately 77.79%. The pore size distribution is 85–6291 nm, which is widely distributed. To exclude pore compression caused by high pressure in the mercury intrusion test, a BET test was also carried out, as shown in Figs. 4(b) and 4(c). Figure 4(b) shows a typical type IV isotherm with an H3 hysteresis loop [31]. When *P*/*P*0 > 0.9, the high-pressure area of the sample increases sharply, which also indicates the existence of a macroporous structure [33]. Figure 4(b) shows that there may be micropores and mesopores in the obtained MC. In combination with SME, high-porosity porous foam with an interconnected pore structure is formed inside the MC. This allows PW (or other PCMs) to be absorbed and encapsulated in a porous network, preventing PW from leaking at the melting temperature. In addition, the highly porous structure enhances the interfacial polarization, which induces multiple attenuations of the EMW [34]. PW is infiltrated into the pores by a vacuum impregnation process above its melting temperature. Figures 3(e) and 3(f) show that the pores are filled with paraffin and tightly bonded to the gyroid matrix, which keeps its structure intact. Although PW fills the pores of the MC, it is a wave-transparent material; that is, the pore structure present within the solid SiOC phase can still cause multiple reflections of electromagnetic waves in the material. Figure 4(d) shows the TG data under an air atmosphere. The sample underwent slight oxidation, resulting in a minor weight gain. The PW no longer exhibited a mass change at 650 °C. MC/PW (1.5 h), MC/PW (2 h), and MC/PW (2.5 h) correspond to samples where the MC was vacuum-impregnated with paraffin for 1.5, 2, and 2.5 h, respectively. The paraffin filling ratios of these three samples are 50.27%, 64.22%, and 64.8%, respectively. The MC is completely filled with PW when the impregnation duration is 2 h.

XPS was used to study the elemental composition and electronic state of each element in the three materials, as shown in Fig. 5. Si 2p, C 1s, N 1s, and O 1s were detected in all samples (Fig. 5(a)). The high-resolution Si 2p spectrum can be divided into three peaks, namely, 102.75, 103.61, and 104.30 eV (Fig. 5(b)), corresponding to Si–N, Si–C, and Si–O, respectively. The high-resolution C 1s XPS spectrum (Fig. 5(c)) shows four distinct peaks at 284.80, 285.3, 286.35, and 289.22 eV, corresponding to C-sp2, C-sp3, C–Si, and C–O, respectively [35−37]. The sp3/sp2 carbon area ratios derived from XPS deconvolution calculations exhibit a consistent ranking order across the three samples, fully corroborating the Raman spectroscopic analysis. The high-resolution N 1s spectrum can be divided into two peaks, at 398.63 and 400.52 eV, corresponding to N–Si and N–O, respectively. Among them, the N–O peak increase in the P-MCGS material occurred because the siloxane bond (Si–O–Si) may break at high temperatures, resulting in the formation of active species such as silicon free radicals and oxygen free radicals [38]. As shown in Fig. 5(g), the characteristic absorption band of Si–O–Si bonds progressively attenuates with increasing processing temperature [39]. The formation of oxygen-containing active species provides a relatively O-rich environment so that polysilazane forms relatively more N–O bonds during the ceramization process. The high-resolution O 1s spectrum can be divided into three peaks, namely, 530.95, 532.92, and 533.27 eV, corresponding to O–N, O–Si, and O–C, respectively. The O atom in the framework is due to the reaction of silazane with environmental moisture during the 3D printing process [26].

### 3.2 Microwave absorption performance and mechanism

Generally, the two crucial parameters closely linked to the EMW absorption performance of a material are the complex permittivity (*ε*r, *ε*r = *ε*′−j*ε*″) and complex permeability (*μ*r, *μ*r = *μ*′−j*μ*′), where the real part (*ε*′, *μ*′) and imaginary part (*ε*″, *μ*″) represent the storage capacity and dissipation capacity of electromagnetic energy [40], respectively, as shown in Fig. 6. The material is a nonmagnetic material, so the real part and the imaginary part of the complex permeability are set to 1 and 0, respectively. According to the analysis of electromagnetic parameters, first, the real part (Fig. 6(a)) and imaginary part (Fig. 6(b)) of the dielectric constant show a downward trend, which is attributed to the dispersion effect [41]. Second, according to Fig. 6(b), the *ε*″ value of GS is greater than that of the other two materials; that is, the loss capacity of the SiCN ceramic material is greater than that of the other two materials, which also corresponds to the Raman analysis above. The MC has the weakest loss ability toward EMW. To understand the possible loss mechanism (polarization loss and conduction loss) in the composite, Cole–Cole plots (Figs. 6(c) and 6(d)) are drawn [42]. The semicircle and trailing can be clearly seen in the diagram. The semicircle represents the polarization relaxation behavior in the material [43], and the trailing indicates that there is conduction loss in the materials [44]. The attenuation coefficient (*α*) can be introduced for the comprehensive loss capacity of materials [45]. Figure 6(e) shows that both the GS and P-MCGS materials have strong comprehensive loss abilities in the X-band.

The reported wave absorption mechanism of P-MCGS is summarized and explained in Fig. 10. First, the MC porous structure enhances the impedance matching of the material. Therefore, the incident EMWs can generate multiple reflections and scattering within the material, and then, the GS ceramic material absorbs the EMWs due to its high absorption performance. The attenuation of EMWs comes from (i) the heterogeneous interface formed by the nanosized crystalline phases (such as C and β-SiC), and the accumulated charge leads to polarization loss at the interface due to the different dielectric constants; (ii) the turbostratic carbon layer defects produce polarization relaxation and dipole polarization, thereby enhancing the attenuation of electromagnetic wave energy; (iii) the existence of several phases helps to form a conductive path in the variable-frequency electromagnetic field, resulting in conduction loss. We should stress that additive manufacturing provides a suitable method for effectively controlling the overall EMW absorbance performance of components, beyond merely altering the intrinsic properties of the materials that constitute them by modifying their microstructure or composition. The architecture of the solid phase can be modified at will, generating, for example, metamaterials based on different structural units, producing parts with graded architectures, or combining various types of building blocks. Furthermore, the amount of solid material within each building block unit, as well as the size/thickness of the individual features, can also vary over a wide range of values.

### 3.3 Thermal insulation, infrared stealth, and mechanical properties

The performance of the P-MCGS composite in terms of thermal insulation was also investigated (Fig. 11), as it can also play the role of infrared stealth by blocking heat conduction [49,50]. The real-time temperature changes of the samples under different heating configurations (58 and 90 °C) are shown in Figs. 11(a) and 11(d). The temperature change was directly recorded by a thermal infrared (IR) camera, and the corresponding time–temperature maps were drawn (Figs. 11(b) and 11(e)). The P-MCGS material exhibited appropriate shape stability at a heating temperature of 58 °C, below the paraffin solid–liquid transition temperature (Fig. 11(a)). After heating for 150 s, the material temperature only increased by 7.6 °C, which was 22.2 °C lower than the heating temperature, indicating a certain degree of heat insulation. Figure 11(c) also shows that the thermal conductivity of PW and P-MCGS at 25 and 58 °C is low, with a difference of less than 9%, indicating that the composite material has similar thermal insulation properties to PW [27]. This finding also indirectly shows that the addition of MC and SiCN ceramics has little effect on the physical properties of paraffin. In addition, when P-MCGS is heated at 90 °C, above the solid–liquid transition temperature of PW, after 150 s, the material temperature only increases by 12.6 °C, which is 45.9 °C different from the heating temperature. The heat storage effect is evident (Figs. 11(d) and 11(e)), and the shape of the sample is relatively stable at high temperatures, indicating that the liquid paraffin remains effectively trapped within the porous microstructure of the sample. As shown in Figs. 11(g) and 11(h), the thermal insulation effect of the material filled with only PW in the pores of GS is slightly worse than that of P-MCGS, and some obvious paraffin leakage can be observed in Fig. 11(i) under the same heating conditions. In Figs. 11(a), 11(d), and 11(g), “M” at the bottom right corner of each image represents the manual scale range mode, which is set to a range of 0–100 °C, and “E0.95” indicates that the default emissivity of the IR camera is 0.95.

## 4 Conclusions

This study used additive manufacturing to fabricate an EMW 3D absorbing metamaterial based on a gyroid building block. The material was infiltrated with a precursor solution to generate a porous ceramic, which was then impregnated with a phase change material (paraffin) to develop a multifunctional composite. While the composite possessed excellent microwave absorption properties, the thermophysical properties of the composite were similar to those of paraffin wax. The microwave absorbing material supports the mechanical rigidity of liquefied PCM, and the MC not only enhances the microwave absorption behavior of the composite but also effectively prevents the leakage of the phase change material at temperatures higher than its melting point. This design method inspires a promising, versatile, and feasible strategy that combines mechanically stable EMW-absorbing structural materials that provide stability to PCM-based thermal management systems for the production of novel composites with satisfactory heat insulation performance and infrared stealth ability.
