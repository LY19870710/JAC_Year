## 1 Introduction

Solid-state lighting (SSL) combining phosphor materials with blue LED/LD chips is currently the most efficient and energy-saving lighting technology [1−3]. Compared with traditional incandescent lights and fluorescent or metal halide lamps, SSL has the advantages of nontoxicity, high luminous efficiency and long service life [4−6]. Phosphor materials, as the core material of solid-state lighting, are required to have high luminous efficiency, high thermal conductivity and high luminescence saturation thresholds [7,8]. LuAG:Ce phosphor ceramics have excellent quantum efficiency (> 90%) [9,10] and higher thermal conductivity (5–15 W∙m−1∙K−1) [11,12] compared to phosphor in silicone (PiS; 0.1–0.4 W∙m−1∙K−1) [13,14] and phosphor in glass (PiG; 1–3 W∙m−1∙K−1) [15].

Despite these advantageous properties, LuAG:Ce transparent ceramics (TCs) possess a refractive index as high as 1.84, which inherently constrains the critical angle (*θ*c) to a relatively small value at which light propagates inside a material [16−18]. Consequently, this high refractive index results in intensified total internal reflection (TIR), leading to a substantial decline in the efficiency of light extraction, as documented in [19,20]. The mathematical expression linking the critical angle (* θc) and the refractive indices of two light propagation media, specifically the incident medium ( n1) and the adjacent medium ( n2), is formulated as Eq. (1) [21,22]:

(1) θc=arcsinn2n1

where  n1 represents the refractive index of the medium in which the light is incident, and  n2 represents the refractive index of the adjacent medium in which the light attempts to refract. If  n1 >  n2, the critical angle  (θc)determines the maximum angle of incidence at which light can still refract into the second medium; beyond this angle, total internal reflection (TIR) occurs [23]. In the context of LuAG:Ce transparent ceramics (TCs), assuming that they serve as the incident medium ( n1 =1.82−1.84) and that the adjacent medium has a lower refractive index (e.g., air with  n2 ≈ 1.0), the critical angle can be computed via the above expression [24−26]. This angle characterizes the threshold beyond which TIR dominates, affecting the light extraction efficiency.

To address the issue of strong total internal reflection (TIR) and subsequent light extraction inefficiency in LuAG:Ce transparent ceramics (TCs), the strategy of introducing scattering centers can offer a promising solution [27]. These scattering centers serve to effectively change the optical path of light propagating within the ceramics. By increasing the probability that the angle of incidence (θ*) will be less than the critical angle (* θc), the likelihood of light being refracted out of the ceramic instead of undergoing TIR is increased [28−30]. The BaAl2O4 phase stands out as a promising candidate for exceptional scattering centers in ceramic phosphors. Its hexagonal crystal structure, coupled with a low refractive index of 1.66, effectively mitigates the TIR effect and enhances light extraction. Tian et al*. [31] synthesized BaAl2O4–YAG:Ce composite phosphor ceramics (CPCs) through a solid-state reaction method. These CPCs demonstrated luminous efficiencies (LEs) of 102–112 lm∙W−1 under 300 mA blue LED excitation. Our research group [32] prepared BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce composite powders via the coprecipitation method and subsequently obtained composite phosphor ceramics via vacuum sintering. Under 10 W blue LD reflection excitation, the 3 wt% BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce CPCs achieved LE of 208 lm∙W−1.

Although the coexistence of pores and BaAl2O4 secondary phases can contribute to mitigating total internal reflection and enhancing light efficiency, the excessive number of pores leads to a decline in the optical quality of phosphor ceramics. Notably, the number and size of pores increase with increasing BaAl2O4 content owing to the partial melting of BaAl2O4 during sintering, which results in pore aggregation within the ceramic matrix. When the sintering temperature is close to the melting point of BaAl2O4 (1827 °C), the nanoparticle size of the powder leads to high surface energy and a large number of surface atoms. Owing to their incomplete coordination, these atoms exhibit high activity, thereby requiring much lower internal energy for melting [33]. Therefore, on the basis of the above research results, an experiment was designed to prepare BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce ceramics via the solid-phase reaction technique, and novel findings were obtained. On the one hand, as the BaAl2O4 content increased, the relative density of the composite ceramics tended to increase. This method differs from the coprecipitation method, where the melting of the BaAl2O4 phase during sintering leads to a decrease in the relative density. In addition, BaAl2O4 serves a dual function as both a sintering aid and a structure-regulating agent: Forming secondary phases acts as a scattering center. At a BaAl2O4 content of 3 wt%, the LuAG:Ce phases exhibit a unique multiscale hierarchical structure—characterized by the coexistence of large and fine grains, with the pores and secondary phases dispersed among them. This structure may contribute to enhancing the luminescence performance under LED/LD excitation. The phase composition, microstructure, optical, and luminescence properties of the BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce ceramics were also investigated.

## 2 Experimental

Ceramic samples with the chemical formula of *x* wt% BaAl2O4–LuAG:0.4 at% Ce (*x* = 1, 3, 5, 10) were prepared via a high-temperature solid-state reaction method. Commercial Y2O3 (99.999%, Shanghai Sinian Metal Materials Co., Ltd.), Lu2O3 (99.999%, Jining Zhongkai New Materials Co., Ltd.), α-Al2O3 (99.99%, Fenghe Ceramic Co., Ltd.), CeO2 (99.995%, Fujian Changting Golden Dragon Co., Ltd.), and BaO (99.99%, Aldrich Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) were used as raw materials. The above raw powders were weighed according to the stoichiometric ratio and then mixed by ball milling for 12 h in ethanol, with 0.8% TEOS (99.999%, Alfa Aesar) and 0.08% MgO (99.998%, Alfa Aesar) used as sintering aids. The powder mixtures were dried at 70 °C for 2 h, sieved through a 200-mesh screen and calcined at 600 °C in air for 4 h to remove organic impurities. Then, the mixed powders were pressed into disks with a diameter of 20 mm under 20 MPa and cold-pressed at 250 MPa for 3 min. The pretreated disks were sintered at 1700 °C in a vacuum atmosphere (at the level of 10−4 Pa) for 10 h and annealed at 1450 °C in air for 10 h. Eventually, both sides of the composite ceramic samples were polished for further measurements.

The phase identification of the ceramic samples was carried out via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan) in the 2*θ* range of 15°–75° using nickel-filtered Cu Kα radiation. The microstructures of the nanopowders and the thermally etched surfaces of the ceramics were characterized via field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; SU8220, Hitachi, Japan). The average grain sizes of the constituent phases were calculated via the linear intercept method. The relative density of the BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce CPCs was calculated according to the Archimedes principle. The theoretical full densities of these CPCs were obtained according to the rule of the mixtures, i.e.,  ρ=vBaAl2O4ρBaAl2O4+vLuAGρLuAG, where  vBaAl2O4 and  vLuAG are the volume fractions of BaAl2O4 and LuAG, respectively, and  ρBaAl2O4 and  ρLuAG are the theoretical densities of pure BaAl2O4 and LuAG, which are 4.37 and 6.73 g∙cm−3, respectively [32]. The total transmittance over the wavelength range from 250–800 nm was recorded via a UV–VIS–NIR spectrophotometer (Cary-5000, Varian Medical Systems, USA). The photoluminescence excitation (PLE) and PL emission spectra were recorded via an FLS920 fully functional fluorescence spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments, UK). The PL decay time was obtained via a steady-state transient fluorescence phosphorescence spectrometer (FLS980, Edinburgh Instruments, UK). The variable-temperature PL spectra were measured via a FluoroMax-4 spectrofluorometer (JY FM-4, HORIBA, Japan). The ceramic samples were mounted onto a 465 nm LED chip-on-board (COB) module and equipped with a 450 nm LD source in reflection mode. The related luminous efficiency (LE), luminous flux (LF), correlated color temperature (CCT), color rendering index (CRI or Ra), and PL spectra at room temperature (RT) were measured via an integrating sphere connected to a CCD spectrometer (OHSP-350, Hangzhou Hopoo Light and Color Technology, China).

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1 shows the XRD patterns of the *x* wt% BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce (*x* = 1, 3, 5, 10) CPCs. The diffraction patterns of the CPCs show strong LuAG phase diffraction peaks corresponding to the standard card (PDF#73-1368). When the content of BaAl2O4 is low, the BaAl2O4 diffraction peak can hardly be observed, which may be attributed to the low diffraction peak intensity [34]. As the content of BaAl2O4 increases, diffraction peaks can be gradually observed, which correspond to the standard BaAl2O4 (PDF#17-0306).

The FESEM micrographs of the BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce CPCs are depicted in Figs 2(a)–2(d). With increasing BaAl2O4 content in the CPCs, the number of pores tends to decrease, suggesting an increase in ceramic densification. Additionally, compared with the grain size of 8.4 μm in the 1 wt% BaAl2O4 samples, the ceramics with 3 wt% BaAl2O4 contents exhibit significant variations in grain size, with large and small grain clusters intertwined (Fig. 2(b)). The sizes of the larger grains can reach approximately 27.9 μm, and the smaller grains are approximately 2.0 μm in size. Notably, EDS analysis revealed that these grains, regardless of size, consisted of Lu, Al and O with Lu/Al ratios close to 3 : 5, indicating the presence of LuAG:Ce phases (Figs. 3(b) and 3(c)). In areas where larger grains are distributed, BaAl2O4 secondary phases are observed, as confirmed by EDS analysis (Figure 3(d)). The above phenomena may be associated with solid-state reactions and densification behaviors during sintering. First, the solid-state reaction temperature of BaO and Al2O3 to form BaAl2O4 is approximately 600 °C, which is lower than the temperature (900−1100 °C) required for the solid-state reaction between Lu2O3 and Al2O3 to form LuAG. Furthermore, the melting point of BaAl2O4 (~1827 °C) is lower than that of LuAG (~2010 °C), suggesting a lower densification driving force and sintering activation energy for BaAl2O4 during sintering. Second, the BaAl2O4 phase, which forms and grows first during sintering, tends to "attract" smaller LuAG grains to "attach" and grow due to the better fluidity of the lower-temperature BaAl2O4 phase, potentially facilitating interactions with the higher-temperature LuAG phase. This leads to a lower interfacial energy between the two phases, which is favorable for the sintering of LuAG. Therefore, the BaAl2O4 secondary phase is predominantly observed in areas with larger grains. A further increase in the BaAl2O4 content led to a larger proportion of BaAl2O4 forming an independent secondary phase (Figs. 2(c) and 2(d)). With increasing secondary phase particle content, the resistance to grain growth in the primary LuAG phase increases, resulting in a reduction in the average grain size of the primary phase. Concurrently, the increased presence of secondary phase particles weakens the grain‒to‒grain interactions, improving the uniformity of the grain size distribution. This observed trend is consistent with the grain coarsening scaling laws governing polycrystalline systems containing secondary phases [35]. Third, as shown by the EDS analysis in Figs 3(e) and 3(f), Ba, Al, Si, Lu, and O are significantly enriched at the triangular grain boundaries, which further indicates that BaAl2O4 acts as a sintering aid. As the BaAl2O4 content increases from 5 to 10 wt%, the grain size of LuAG gradually becomes more uniform and increases from 4.4 to 8.0 μm, accompanied by further densification and a reduction in porosity. In addition, on the basis of the EDS mapping results of the 10 wt% BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce ceramics shown in Figs. 4(a)–4(e), the elements O, Al, Ba, and Lu exhibit a good uniform distribution. This also indicates that the phases of LuAG:Ce and BaAl2O4 are uniformly distributed when a high content of BaAl2O4 is added.

Figure 5(a) shows the total transmittance curves of BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce CPCs with a thickness of 0.4 mm. The total transmittance at 800 nm is approximately 3%–10%. The absorption bands between 420 and 500 nm and between 350 and 420 nm can be attributed to the 4f → 5d1 and 4f → 5d2 transitions of Ce3+, respectively [36]. According to the relative density of x* wt%BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce CPCs (*x* = 1, 3, 5, 10), the porosities of the CPCs are 22.5%, 21.0%, 20.5%, and 15.7%, respectively.

The PLE and PL spectra for the 0.8 mm thick BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce samples are depicted in Fig. 6(a). As the BaAl2O4 content increases, the intensity of PLE/PL continuously increases, reaching a maximum in the 10 wt% BaAl2O4 sample. For the 0.4 mm thick samples, the highest PLE/PL intensity was obtained for the 3 wt% BaAl2O4 sample (Fig. 6(b)). This is because the 0.8 mm samples are opaque, and the light collected by the instrument primarily originates from reflected and scattered light at the sample surface. In samples with basic opacity, the role of pores as scattering centers is negative. With increasing BaAl2O4 secondary phases serving as scattering centers, the luminous efficiency increases, leading to an increase in the PLE/PL intensity. In contrast, the PLE/PL intensity measured for the 0.4 mm thick samples is influenced by both the transmittance and scattering centers. On the one hand, a portion of the light in samples with higher transmittance can directly pass through the sample. On the other hand, the uniform distribution of secondary phases or pores as scattering centers also affects the luminous efficiency of the ceramics. Compared with the uneven distribution of BaAl2O4 as the secondary phase, the increased and more uniform distribution of pores has a positive effect on the luminous efficiency of the ceramics. This may explain why the PLE/PL intensity is greater for the 3 wt% BaAl2O4 ceramic with a thickness of 0.4 mm. On the basis of the PL decay time of the 3 wt% BaAl2O4 sample (Fig. 6(c)), the PL decay time (56 ns) is shorter than that of the LuAG:Ce phosphor ceramics (59 ns) [37], indicating that Ba2+ ions partially enter the LuAG lattice. However, this reduction in decay time is significantly less effective than that observed in LuAG:Ce,Ca (48 ns) [38]. Figure 6(d) shows the PL spectra of the 3 wt% BaAl2O4 sample measured at various temperatures, revealing that the PL intensity at 225 °C is 76.6% of the RT intensity under excitation at 450 nm.

Figure 7 shows the luminescent properties (LE, LF, CCT, CRI, and PL spectra) of BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce CPCs with thicknesses of 0.8 and 0.4 mm under excitation with a 465 nm LED operated at 0.8 W. Figure 7(a) shows that the LE and LF for CPCs with thicknesses of 0.8 mm continually increase with increasing BaAl2O4 content. For the 0.4 mm thick CPCs, the 3 wt% BaAl2O4 sample presented optimal values of LF = 70.7 lm and LE = 88.4 lm/W, whereas CCT was maintained at approximately 5699 K for all the samples, as shown in Fig. 7(b). On the basis of the PL spectra excited by the 450 nm LED shown in Figs. 7(c) and 7(d), the extraction intensity of yellow‒green light clearly aligns with the trends in LF and LE, indicating that the ceramic thickness, porosity, and secondary phases collectively influence blue light absorption and yellow‒green light extraction. First, reducing the thickness increases the light extraction efficiency of the transmissive LED (enhanced transmittance, as the 0.8 mm sample is opaque). Second, the pores and secondary phases act as scattering centers, helping mitigate total internal reflection (TIR) and enabling more light to be extracted from the ceramics, thereby increasing the light extraction efficiency. However, excessive porosity and the presence of secondary phases can limit the transmittance of a ceramic, resulting in a reduction in the light output. The refractive index of the pores is approximately 1, which is significantly lower than that of LuAG:Ce (ranging from 1.82–1.84). This lower refractive index of the pores enhances the scattering capability (stronger than that of BaAl2O4), thereby increasing the probability that the emitted light meets the condition θ* c within the composite ceramics and consequently increases the extraction efficiency of yellow–green light. BaAl2O4 has a refractive index of 1.66. As a composite phase, its refractive index is lower than that of LuAG:Ce (1.82–1.84). Like pores, they can also reduce the total internal reflection within the matrix material, thus increasing the extraction efficiency of yellow–green light. Moreover, it helps to suppress the excessive growth and formation of an excessive number of pores. According to Rayleigh–Mie scattering theory, if the pores are too large, their optical scattering ability will decrease (The scattering direction of light will become more concentrated, rather than diverse). As shown in Fig. 8, the color coordinates primarily cluster in the yellow‒green light regions around (0.32, 0.57) and (0.33, 0.60).

To evaluate the luminescent performance of BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce CPCs in laser lighting applications, all CPC samples were tested under excitation with 0.92 and 4.3 W reflective laser diodes (LDs) at 450 nm. Under LD excitation at 0.92 W, both the LE and LF exhibit similar trends as those under LED excitation. The 0.8 mm thick sample with 10 wt% BaAl2O4 achieves LF of 254.2 lm and LE of 277 lm/W. However, as the thickness is reduced to 0.4 mm, both LF and LE decrease, with the optimal LF and LE occurring in the 3 wt% BaAl2O4 sample at 217.9 lm and 237.4 lm/W, respectively (Fig. 9(a)). On the one hand, this is due to the decrease in the number of luminescent centers in the ceramics as the sample thickness is reduced, resulting in a lower conversion efficiency of yellow–green light and subsequently a lower LE and LF. On the other hand, uniformly distributed pores can act as scattering centers to effectively reduce total internal reflection, thereby increasing light extraction efficiency. In the 3 wt% BaAl2O4 samples, LuAG:Ce with both small and large grain sizes is uniformly distributed (Fig. 2(b)), with small LuAG:Ce grains interspersed with uniform pores and large LuAG:Ce grains interspersed with BaAl2O4 as secondary phases serving as scattering centers. This structure may offer advantages for light extraction, particularly when the sample has a certain transmittance after thickness reduction, allowing it to leverage its structural advantages more effectively to improve LE and LF. As seen from the PL spectra excited by the 450 nm LD in Figs. 9(e) and 9(f), the yellow‒green emission peak of the 3 wt% BaAl2O4 sample is stronger, indicating a superior yellow‒green light conversion efficiency under the same blue light excitation. When the LD power is increased to 4.3 W (Fig. 9(b)), both the 0.8 and 0.4 mm samples containing 3 wt% BaAl2O4 exhibit optimal LE and LF values, which may also be attributed to the combined effect of the pores and the secondary phases present in the 3 wt% BaAl2O4 samples, and the optimal LF can reach 1015 lm. Consequently, optical scattering centers play a beneficial role in enabling more light within the ceramics to meet the exit angle requirement (θ* c), thereby increasing the light extraction efficiency. In summary, the optical pass path and scattering effects are two important factors for the light extraction efficiency of BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce phosphor ceramics. For LED lighting, reducing the thickness from 0.8 to 0.4 mm improves the light extraction efficiency by enhancing the total transmittance, according to the luminous efficiency of the LED performance (Figs. 7(a) and 7(b)). However, for LD lighting, the 0.4 mm ceramics have a lower luminous efficiency than the 0.8 mm ceramics do, so further reducing the thickness leads to a further decline in the performance of LDs, so a 0.4 mm thickness can balance the optical pass path and optical scattering effects to achieve acceptable performance for LEDs and LDs. In Table 1, compared with other phosphor ceramics, 3 wt% BaAl2O4-LuAG:Ce with a thickness of 0.4 mm has a greater LE when it is applied in solid-state lighting. The BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce ceramics excited by a 0.92 W LD exhibit a CCT of approximately 15,000 K and CRI between 50 and 60 (Fig. 9(c)). As the LD power increases, the CCT increases, whereas the CRI remains at a relatively low level (Fig. 9(d)). This is due to the lack of red components in LuAG:Ce and the increase in the number of blue components in the spectrum. The color coordinates in Fig. 10 are mainly concentrated in the pale green (palewhite) light region.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, the solid-state reaction method effectively suppresses the melting of BaAl2O4 during high-temperature sintering in BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce composite phosphor ceramics (CPCs), increasing densification with increasing BaAl2O4 content. However, despite their higher density, CPCs with lower BaAl2O4 contents (e.g., 3 wt%) and a porous microstructure exhibit superior luminescent properties. This unique structure likely improves light extraction and luminous flux, making it a promising phosphor material. Under excitation with a 0.92 W blue LD, the 3 wt% BaAl2O4 –LuAG:Ce CPCs (0.4 mm thickness) demonstrate an optimal luminous efficiency of 237 lm/W and a luminous flux of 218 lm. Under excitation with a 4.3 W blue LD, the 3 wt% BaAl2O4–LuAG:Ce CPCs (0.8 mm thick) achieve an optimal luminous flux of 1015 lm. These results suggest that the designed composite ceramics hold promise as phosphor converters for high-power laser lighting applications.
