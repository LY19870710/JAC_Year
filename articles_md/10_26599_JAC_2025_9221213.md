## 1 Introduction

The rapid development of modern communication and wireless detection technologies has increased the requirements for electromagnetic wave (EMW) functional materials [1,2]. In particular, EMW-absorbing materials are expected to achieve broadband attenuation of incident waves while maintaining structural and chemical stability under corrosive or extreme service conditions, such as marine atmospheres, high humidity, and high-temperature oxidative environments [3−5]. This dual demand presents great challenges to material design. Dielectric ceramics such as SiC [6], high-entropy diborides [7], and TiB [8] are well known for their outstanding thermal and chemical stability, resulting in excellent corrosion resistance. However, their intrinsic dielectric properties limit their ability to regulate impedance matching, thereby constraining the effective absorption bandwidth (EAB). In contrast, magnetic loss materials, especially Fe-based compounds, can effectively broaden the EAB via magnetic dissipation governed by the Planck–Rozanov limit [9−12], thereby exhibiting a complementary effect with dielectric loss. However, they are prone to oxidation and corrosion, leading to rapid performance degradation and poor long-term reliability [13,14]. Achieving both the bandwidth-broadening function of magnetic components while retaining the corrosion resistance of ceramic systems remains an unsolved challenge.

Polymer-derived ceramics (PDCs) offer a powerful platform for developing advanced ceramic absorbers owing to their molecular-level design flexibility, which enables simultaneous control over composition, microstructure, and functionality [15−17]. The incorporation of magnetic species into PDC matrices provides, in principle, a pathway to achieve broadband absorption [18]. Moreover, the polymer-to-ceramic conversion process provides opportunities to generate micro- and nano-scale architectures, such as hollow structures and aerogels, that increase electromagnetic dissipation through multiple scattering, interfacial polarization, and magnetodielectric coupling [19−21]. However, most template-free PDCs typically form dense and featureless particles, restricting interfacial polarization and magnetodielectric coupling. These morphologies originate from polymer-to-ceramic conversion, in which bond scission and fragment volatilization drive surface energy minimization and hinder secondary structure formation [22]. Therefore, strategies to engineer hierarchical architectures *in situ* during pyrolysis are essential for enhancing the EAB of PDC materials.

Addressing this challenge requires a molecular-level design of precursors that can not only introduce magnetic elements homogeneously but also guide structural evolution during pyrolysis. Previous approaches have focused largely on physically blending Fe-based nanoparticles [23] or salts [24,25] into polymer matrices, which inevitably suffer from agglomeration, inhomogeneity, and weak interfacial bonding, resulting in poor control of the final microstructure and compromised corrosion resistance. In contrast, chemically coupling magnetic components directly into the polymer backbone holds promise for achieving uniform elemental distribution and a higher degree of crosslinking, thereby facilitating the controlled formation of nanostructures during subsequent pyrolysis [26]. This strategy offers significant potential for balancing the trade-off between broadband EMW absorption and corrosion resistance.

Herein, we report a strategy for the design and synthesis of hierarchical silicon–boron–carbon–nitrogen–iron composite ceramic absorbers (h-SiBCNFe) based on ferric-crosslinking chemistry. Specifically, the homogeneous incorporation of Fe was achieved through secondary crosslinking between iron(III) acetylacetonate (Fe(acac)3) and the Si–H bonds in polyborosilazane (PBSZ), which simultaneously increased the degree of polymer crosslinking. During subsequent pyrolysis, this dual effect played two critical roles: (i) The highly crosslinked polymer backbone promoted the nucleation of hierarchical microstructures, and (ii) the *in situ* catalytic activity of Fe drove the growth of carbon nanotubes and facilitated the formation of Fe*x*Si*y* phases via reaction with Si. Remarkably, the resulting ceramics exhibited a unique “mushroom-like” hierarchical architecture, in which the “stems” of carbon nanotubes grew out from the SiBCN ceramic particles, whereas the “caps” were composed of carbon-encapsulated Fe*x*Si*y* nanoparticles. Unlike conventional PDCs, the C@Fe*x*Si*y*@SiBCN hierarchical architecture synergistically combines SiBCN dielectric and magnetic losses with abundant interfacial polarization and magnetodielectric coupling arising from the secondary C@Fe*x*Si*y* structure. This strategy establishes a new pathway for the development of ceramic EMW-absorbing materials that combine durability with broadband absorption performance, holding significant promise for applications in aerospace, communications, defense, and related fields.

### 2.1 Materials

Dichloromethylvinylsilane (DCMVS, 97%), dichloromethylsilane (DCMS, 98%), borane-dimethylsulfide complex (2.0 M in tetrahydrofuran), hexamethyldisilazane (HMDZ, 98%), iron(III) acetylacetonate (Fe(acac)3, 99%), and anhydrous xylene (98%) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd., China, and used without further purification.

#### 2.2.1 Synthesis of PBSZ–Fe ceramic precursors

Hyperbranched polyborosilazane (PBSZ-SiH) was synthesized under an argon atmosphere via the Schlenk technique as previously reported [27]. In a typical procedure, 3 mol DCMVS was reacted with 1 mol borane-dimethylsulfide complex at 0 °C for 12 h to yield tris(dichloromethylsilylethyl)borane (TDSB). Subsequently, 1 mol DCMS and excess HMDZ were added, and the mixture was allowed to undergo polycondensation for 4 h under continuous argon flow (flow rate: 50 mL·min−1). After the reaction, the system temperature was gradually increased to 180 °C, and the unreacted monomers and byproducts were removed via vacuum distillation. Upon cooling to room temperature, a pale yellow viscous liquid was obtained, denoted as PBSZ-SiH (60% yield).

The PBSZ–Fe precursor was prepared by crosslinking PBSZ–SiH with Fe(acac)3 under an argon atmosphere. Specifically, 6 g of Fe(acac)3 was dissolved in 100 mL of anhydrous xylene, followed by the addition of 12 g of PBSZ–SiH. The mixture was magnetically stirred at 140 °C for 72 h. After the reaction, the solvent and byproducts were removed by vacuum distillation, yielding a dark red solid polymer (PBSZ–Fe, 75% yield).

#### 2.2.2 Preparation of h-SiBCNFe ceramics

The obtained PBSZ-Fe precursor was transferred into a tubular furnace under argon protection and heated to 400 °C at a rate of 2 °C·min−1, followed by a 2 h dwell for crosslinking. The crosslinked samples were then ball-milled for 4 h and sieved through a 400-mesh screen to obtain fine powders. Finally, the powders were pyrolyzed at 1200, 1300, and 1400 °C for 3 h under flowing argon (flow rate: 100 mL·min−1), with a heating rate of 5 °C·min−1. The resulting ceramics were designated h-SiBCNFe-*T*, where *T* denotes the pyrolysis temperature.

#### 2.3.1 Characterization

The molecular structures of the ceramic precursors were verified via a 1H nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer (1H-NMR; Bruker Avance 400, Bruker BioSpin, Germany) and a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FT-IR; Nicolet iS 10, USA). The ceramic yield and thermal properties of the polymer were tested via a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA; STA 449F3, Netzsch Group, Germany). The analysis was conducted over a temperature range of 30–1400 °C, with a heating rate of 10 °C·min−1 in an argon atmosphere (gas flow rate: 50 mL·min−1). The phase composition and crystal structure of the pyrolyzed ceramics were resolved via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Bruker D8 Advance, Germany) and a Raman spectrometer (Renishaw Invia spectrometer, USA; excitation source: 514.5 nm Ar laser). An X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; Thermo ESCALAB 250XI, USA) was used to characterize the elements and their chemical states. The morphology and microstructural features were observed via a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; Zeiss Sigma 300, Germany) and a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM; FEI Talos F200X, USA) with an energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS; JED 2300, Japan).

The electrochemical stability was assessed in a 5.0 wt% NaCl solution via an electrochemical workstation (CHI760E, Shanghai Chenhua Co., Ltd., China), where the open-circuit potential (OCP), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and Tafel polarization curves were recorded. The working electrodes were prepared by mixing the ceramic absorbers with polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF; powder, No. A08946, Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd., China) at a 4 : 1 mass ratio. The electromagnetic parameters were determined by a vector network analyzer (VNA; MS46322B, Anritsu, Japan) in a 7/3 transmission line. Test samples were prepared by mixing ceramic powders with paraffin wax (the addition amount of ceramic powders was 20 wt%) and pressing them into toroidal samples with an outer diameter of 7.0 mm and an inner diameter of 3.04 mm. The electromagnetic parameters were resolved according to the measured S parameters via the Nicolson–Ross–Weir method [28]. Radar cross-section (RCS) simulations were performed via CST Microwave Studio with a time-domain solver to evaluate the reflection characteristics of the coated absorbers [29].

#### 2.3.2 First-principles calculations

First-principles calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) were carried out via the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP). The projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotentials and the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) were employed to optimize the structures and calculate the free energies [30]. The cutoff energy for the plane-wave basis set was set to 450 eV, and a Monkhorst–Pack *k*-point mesh of 2×2×1 was used for Brillouin zone sampling in the adsorption energy calculations. The electronic self-consistent convergence threshold was set to 1×10−5 eV, and all atomic positions were fully relaxed until the residual force on each atom was less than 0.02 eV·Å−1. A vacuum layer of 15 Å along the *z*-axis was applied to eliminate spurious periodic interactions.

### 3.1 Preparation of h-SiBCNFe ceramics

The h-SiBCNFe ceramics were synthesized via a PDC approach designed to achieve *in situ* incorporation and homogeneous dispersion of metallic iron within the SiBCN matrix through molecular-level control (Fig. 1).

*
Fig. 1Schematic illustrations of preparation of h-SiBCNFe ceramics. Initially, a PBSZ–SiH precursor containing Si–H bonds was constructed through Brown hydroboration and polycondensation; subsequently, it coordinated with Fe(acac)3 to form a Si–O–Fe cross-linked structure (PBSZ–Fe), ultimately leading to in situ* generation of uniformly dispersed Fe*x*Si*y* nanophases within SiBCN matrix after pyrolysis.

The fabrication involved three consecutive stages. First, Brown hydroboration between the vinyl groups of DCMVS and the B–H bonds of a borane–dimethyl sulfide complex afforded a multifunctional TDSB intermediate containing Si–C–B bonds. Subsequent polycondensations with DCMS and HMDZ yielded a hyperbranched PBSZ-SiH precursor, integrating Si–C–B–N units into the main chain. 1H-NMR spectra (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) revealed signals at 4.0–4.8 ppm assigned to Si–H groups, confirming the presence of reactive sites for iron coordination. Additional resonances at 0.6–1.8 ppm corresponded to residual vinyl groups, while weak peaks at 0–0.41 ppm were attributed to byproducts such as –CH3. The FT-IR spectrum showed a distinct Si–H stretching band at 2140 cm−1, confirming the designed molecular structure of PBSZ-SiH. Together, these results verified the synthesis of a polymer suitable for subsequent iron coordination.

Second, the PBSZ-SiH precursor was then reacted with Fe(acac)3 at 140 °C to produce an iron-crosslinked precursor (PBSZ-Fe). The reaction proceeded through Si–H/Fe–O bond cleavage and recombination, leading to Si–O–Fe linkages within the polymer backbone [31]. This reaction primarily proceeded through nucleophilic addition and hydrosilylation, forming Si–O–Fe bonds between hydropolysilazane and metal acetylacetonates, while the acetylacetonate ligand was eliminated. This not only introduced iron species but also promoted a three-dimensional (3D) crosslinked network, enhancing structural stability during pyrolysis. The PBSZ-SiH (P0) precursor exhibited significant mass loss between 180 and 700 °C, after which the mass stabilized, resulting in a final ceramic yield of approximately 22% (Fig. S2 in the ESM). Upon the introduction of iron into the polymer, a similar mass loss was observed within the same temperature range, but the ceramic yield increased significantly. The yield for the PBSZ-Fe (P1) precursor reached approximately 46.7% at 1400 °C. This enhancement is attributed primarily to partial crosslinking reactions between Fe(acac)3 and PBSZ, where the resulting network structure effectively increased the ceramic yield from the precursor. FT-IR analysis (Fig. S3 in the ESM) confirmed this transformation: The disappearance of the Si–H absorption band at 2140 cm−1 and the emergence of a new band at 929.4 cm−1, characteristic of Si–O–Fe, directly confirmed the coordination-driven incorporation of iron.

Third, the PBSZ–Fe precursor underwent crosslinking at 400 °C, during which dehydrocoupling between the Si–H and N–H groups increased the network density and improved the thermal stability. Upon pyrolysis at elevated temperatures, the Si–O–Fe bonds decomposed and rearranged to form nanoscale Fe*x*Si*y* phases that were homogeneously dispersed within the amorphous SiBCN matrix. This rationally designed molecular-to-ceramic pathway thus yielded h-SiBCNFe ceramics with compositional uniformity and robust structural integrity.

### 3.2 Hierarchical structures of h-SiBCNFe ceramics

The phase compositions of h-SiBCNFe ceramics pyrolyzed at different temperatures were examined via XRD (Fig. 2(a)). Compared with SiBCN ceramics [32], the h-SiBCNFe ceramics exhibited distinct structural evolution after pyrolysis at 1200–1400 °C due to the *in situ* incorporation of Fe. The newly formed dominant crystalline phases included graphitic carbon, β-SiC, and Fe–Si compounds, with Fe3Si being the most prominent [33]. The peak at 2*θ* = 26.1° was assigned to graphitic carbon (PDF#98-000-0231), attributable to the Fe-catalyzed conversion of disordered sp3 carbon (from PBSZ decomposition) into ordered sp2 graphitic carbon. β-SiC reflections (PDF#01-075-0254) appeared at 2*θ* = 35.6°, 41.7°, 59.6°, and 71.4°. Mechanistically, the addition of carbon in an Fe–Si melt significantly reduced the surface tension at the liquid–SiC interface, thereby providing a lower energy barrier for the nucleation of SiC [34]. The Fe–Si compounds are predominantly Fe3Si (PDF#04-004-6815), with minor amounts of Fe5Si3 (PDF#97-016-1131) and FeSi (PDF#04-007-2551). Characteristic peaks were observed at 2*θ* = 45.5°, 52.4°, and 84.0° for Fe3Si; 47.1° for Fe5Si3; and 50.2° and 79.8° for FeSi. This phase evolution was attributed to solid-state reactions between Fe and Si near 1100 °C, which first yielded Fe3Si. With increasing temperature, supersaturation of Si/C in the Fe–Si–C liquid promoted solidification of Fe5Si3 and FeSi [35]. With increasing pyrolysis temperature, the diffraction peaks of graphitic carbon and β-SiC became more intense, and the crystallinity of the Fe–Si phases improved, reflecting the increased catalytic activity of Fe at elevated temperatures.

The structural evolution of the carbon in the ceramics was further investigated by Raman spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 2(b), h-SiBCNFe ceramics pyrolyzed at 1200–1400 °C displayed two distinct peaks at ~1350 cm−1 (D-band) and ~1580 cm−1 (G-band). The D-band reflects structural defects within the carbon lattice, and the area under this peak represents the defective carbon content. Conversely, the G-band signifies the stretching vibration of the hybridized carbon atoms in the plane and indicates the density of these atoms on the basis of their peak area. The intensity ratios (I*D/*I*G) commonly used to assess structural order and defect density in carbonaceous materials decreased from 1.221 (1200 °C) to 1.123 (1300 °C) and 1.078 (1400 °C), indicating enhanced graphitization at higher temperatures due to the catalytic effect of Fe. Notably, all ratios remained above 1, confirming the presence of abundant defects and heterostructures. This high defect density not only provides interfacial polarization centers to strengthen dielectric loss but also interrupts π-conjugated networks, creating local conductivity gradients that generate microcurrent pathways and markedly improve electromagnetic attenuation [36].

The surface chemical states were further examined using XPS. The full survey spectrum confirmed the presence of Si, B, N, C, and Fe, indicating successful incorporation of Fe (Fig. 2(c)). High-resolution spectra (Figs. S4–S6 in the ESM) were analyzed to determine the valence states of the elements in h-SiBCNFe. In the Fe 2p region, a distinct peak appears at 711.4 eV (Fe 2p3/2), whereas the peaks at 715.0 and 725.3 eV correspond to the satellite peaks of Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2, respectively. The Si 2p peak at 101.1 eV was attributed to Si–C bonds, and the C 1s spectrum exhibited a peak at 283.0 eV, corresponding to C–Si bonds, both confirming SiC formation. Additionally, the C 1s spectrum showed a peak at 284.8 eV (C–C), indicating the presence of graphitic carbon. The N 1s spectrum revealed a dominant peak at 398.7 eV, whereas the B 1s spectrum showed a major peak at 191.1 eV, both associated with B–N bonds, along with minor contributions from C–N and Si–N bonds. Oxygen signals, likely resulting from surface adsorption during handling/testing, were detected, but no bulk oxygen-containing phases were observed by other techniques and were not discussed further.

The morphologies of the SiBCN and h-SiBCNFe-1300 samples are shown in the SEM images (Figs. 2(d) and 2(e)). Compared with pure SiBCN, the h-SiBCNFe-1300 sample exhibited a “mushroom-like” architecture, where the h-SiBCNFe ceramic matrix served as the substrate, the carbon nanotubes acted as the stems, and the Fe*x*Si*y* magnetic phases encapsulated by the carbon layers formed the caps. This unique hierarchical structure introduced abundant heterointerfaces and enabled multiple reflections between carbon nanotubes, thereby enhancing the EMW absorption ability of the ceramics [37]. The spherical Fe*x*Si*y* particles observed at the tips of the carbon nanotubes were consistent with the tip-growth catalytic mechanism. During this process, the carbon nanotubes precipitated from the bottom of the Fe*x*Si*y* particles and continued to elongate, pushing the particles away from the substrate. Growth continued as long as the Fe*x*Si*y* surface remained exposed and catalytically active. Once the particles were fully encapsulated by graphitic carbon, the catalytic activity was lost, and nanotube growth terminated [38].

To further analyze the heterostructures at the core–shell interfaces, TEM characterization was conducted. As shown in Fig. 2(f), the Fe*x*Si*y* nanoparticles were completely encapsulated by graphitic carbon layers, indicating the formation of a core–shell structure, which demonstrated the effectiveness of the precursor molecular design in tailoring microstructures. The corresponding EDS elemental mapping revealed a uniform distribution of C, N, and B around the Fe*x*Si*y* particles, further confirming the successful construction of the core–shell architecture. HRTEM (Fig. 2(g)) revealed graphitic layers with a thickness of approximately 74 nm and a lattice diffraction fringe interlayer spacing of approximately 0.33 nm, which were indexed to the C (002) planes. This can be attributed to accelerated graphitization at 1300 °C, where carbon species agglomerated to form continuous carbon layers. In addition, lattice fringes corresponding to Fe3Si (220, 0.20 nm) and Fe3Si (111, 0.32 nm) were clearly observed in the red region (Fig. 2(g)), while SAED (Fig. 2(h)) further confirmed the presence of Fe5Si3 (211, 0.19 nm) and SiC phases, which is in good agreement with the XRD results. Although SiC lattice fringes were not observed in HRTEM, their diffraction rings corresponding to the (111), (220), and (222) planes were identified in the SAED patterns. Moreover, structural defects were observed in the carbon layers (Fig. S7 in the ESM), which may originate from the incorporation of B and N into the carbon network. These defects introduced dipoles that enhanced interfacial polarization loss.

The magnetic properties of the ceramics were further evaluated via VSM (Fig. S8 in the ESM). The Fe*x*Si*y* phases formed *in situ* during pyrolysis imparted distinct magnetic characteristics to the ceramics. Increasing the pyrolysis temperature resulted in higher saturation magnetization and lower coercivity, indicating soft magnetic behavior with rapid magnetization/demagnetization dynamics, thus contributing to strong magnetic loss [39]. Lorentz electron holography (Fig. 2(i)) revealed strong stray magnetic fields in the h-SiBCNFe ceramics. Uniform magnetic coupling zones were observed between the Fe*x*Si*y* phases and graphitic carbon layers, indicating that the carbon shells did not hinder the extension of stray magnetic field lines from the Fe*x*Si*y* cores. These flux lines not only radiated into free space, enabling long-range magnetic interactions, but also coupled with adjacent magnetic particles through domain alignment, thereby contributing to increased magnetic loss [40].

Taken together, the pyrolysis process promoted the catalytic formation of SiC and graphitic carbon, while Fe reacted with Si to form Fe*x*Si*y* magnetic phases. The graphitic carbon layers catalytically generated during pyrolysis completely encapsulated the Fe*x*Si*y* particles, giving rise to well-defined core–shell structures and a “mushroom-like” morphology. These structural features, combining magnetoelectric synergy, interfacial polarization, multiple reflections, and inert encapsulation barriers, endowed the h-SiBCNFe ceramics with both excellent EMW attenuation capability and outstanding corrosion resistance.

### 3.3 EMW absorption performance of h-SiBCNFe

To evaluate the EMW absorption capability, composite samples prepared by mixing ceramic powders with paraffin were tested using transmission line theory. The real parts of the complex permittivity (*ε'*) and permeability (*µ'*) represent the ability to store electromagnetic energy, whereas the imaginary parts (*ε''* and *µ''*) correspond to dielectric and magnetic loss. All samples exhibited a frequency (*f*)-dependent decrease in *ε'* with increasing frequency, which is consistent with classical dielectric relaxation behavior, indicating the presence of multiple polarization mechanisms, such as interfacial polarization and dipole relaxation [41].

As shown in Fig. 3(a), the complex permittivity of h-SiBCNFe ceramics increased significantly with increasing pyrolysis temperature. This trend was attributed mainly to the enhanced catalytic activity of Fe at higher temperatures, which facilitated the formation of more ordered carbon structures and consequently increased the electrical conductivity [42]. Notably, h-SiBCNFe-1300 and h-SiBCNFe-1400 presented relatively high *ε''* values, which arose from strong polarization relaxation and conductive loss mechanisms, including interfacial polarization and dipole reorientation induced by structural defects, demonstrating excellent dielectric loss capacity. In particular, the h-SiBCNFe-1300 sample combined a relatively high *ε''* with a moderate *ε'*, implying superior impedance matching and rendering it an efficient candidate for EMW absorption applications. As shown in Fig. 3(b), the real μ'* and imaginary *μ''* for all three samples remained relatively low, ranging from 0.99–1.38 and 0.01–0.19, respectively, and exhibited noticeable fluctuations across the measured frequency range. These variations could be ascribed to the suppression of magnetic domain wall motion within this frequency window, accompanied by natural or exchange resonance, which introduced pronounced oscillations in the magnetic loss behavior [43]. Typically, the eddy current loss coefficient (*C*0)–*f* curve is employed to identify different magnetic loss mechanisms in materials. If magnetic loss arises solely from eddy current loss, the *C*0 value remains constant with frequency. The calculated *C*0–*f* curves of h-SiBCNFe-1300 are shown in Fig. S10 in the ESM. Within the frequency range of 2–18 GHz, multiple fluctuation peaks appeared in these curves. The pronounced peaks observed between 2.0 and 7.0 GHz were attributed to natural resonance losses caused by the static magnetic energy within magnetic domains, whereas the weaker peaks in the 7.0–18.0 GHz range corresponded to exchange resonance resulting from magnetic energy exchange between domains under high-frequency electromagnetic fields. As the frequency increased, the *C*0 value tended to stabilize, indicating that the composites exhibited a combination of eddy current loss, natural resonance, and exchange resonance mechanisms [44]. Additionally, the interaction between stray magnetic fields at the Fe*x*Si*y*/carbon interfaces and localized charge accumulation may also modulate magnetic loss behavior, especially at higher frequencies where interfacial polarization becomes more pronounced.

The EMW absorption properties calculated using transmission line theory further confirmed the performance differences among the samples. As shown in Fig. 3(e), h-SiBCNFe-1300 exhibited the most balanced and superior absorption capability, combining strong attenuation with a broad effective bandwidth. At a matching thickness of 2.7 mm, the EAB reached 8.16 GHz (9.84–18 GHz), while a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −32.3 dB was achieved at 2.0 mm, demonstrating excellent absorption within the target frequency range. In contrast, h-SiBCNFe-1200 and h-SiBCNFe-1400 showed inferior performance (Figs. 3(d) and 3(f)). Owing to insufficient intrinsic loss, the RLmin of h-SiBCNFe-1200 reached only −8.3 dB. Although h-SiBCNFe-1400 generated more loss components after high-temperature pyrolysis, its severe impedance mismatch restricted performance; the EAB was 3.28 GHz at 2.0 mm thickness, while the RLmin reached only −13.6 dB, indicating limited attenuation capability. Furthermore, the construction of “mushroom-like” and core–shell structures facilitated multiple scattering and interfacial polarization, endowing the ceramics with broadband absorption across 2–18 GHz, particularly at higher frequencies. In summary, h-SiBCNFe-1300 exhibited the most favorable electromagnetic wave absorption performance among the three samples, benefiting from its moderate complex permittivity and near-matched permeability, which effectively mitigated the impedance mismatch.

To further assess the EMW dissipation capability of the ceramics under practical conditions, RCS simulations were performed to evaluate the reflection behavior and stealth efficiency at different incident angles (−90° Fig. 3(g)). The results revealed that all three h-SiBCN-Fe coatings significantly reduced the scattering signals of the PEC substrate, confirming their strong ability to absorb incident EMW energy (Fig. S11 in the ESM). Figure 3(h) presents the RCS reduction values at representative incident angles (0°, 30°, 60°, and 90°). Among the tested samples, h-SiBCNFe-1300 exhibited the largest reduction, reaching 18.0 dB·m2 at normal incidence (0°), which is consistent with the reflection loss measurements. Overall, these findings demonstrated that h-SiBCNFe ceramics, particularly h-SiBCNFe-1300, can simultaneously achieve efficient dissipation of electromagnetic energy and substantial suppression of the EMW scattering intensity, highlighting their excellent EMW absorption.

To gain deeper insight into the EMW absorption mechanism at the heterointerfaces of h-SiBCNFe ceramics, first-principles calculations based on DFT were performed to systematically analyze the interfacial charge behavior and polarization characteristics at the electronic structure level. By constructing a C@Fe3Si heterojunction model, the work function (*W*f) and differential charge density were calculated in detail, thereby elucidating the critical role of charge redistribution and interfacial electric fields in governing electromagnetic dissipation.

As shown in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b), the work functions of C and Fe3Si were determined to be 5.37 and 4.68 eV, respectively. Upon contact, the lower work function of Fe3Si corresponded to a higher Fermi level, which drove spontaneous electron transfer from Fe3Si to C. This process continued until the Fermi levels equilibrate across the interface, forming a thermodynamically stable state and establishing a built-in electric field directed from Fe3Si to C (Fig. 4(c)). This interfacial field not only promoted polarization but also served as the driving force for dipole relaxation, thereby enhancing the dielectric relaxation and electromagnetic energy conversion efficiency. Differential charge density mapping (Fig. 4(d)) further confirmed significant charge redistribution at the heterojunction interface: yellow regions indicate electron accumulation, whereas cyan regions indicate electron depletion. Quantitative Bader charge analysis revealed a net transfer of 1.329e from Fe3Si to C units within the interface region, demonstrating strong electronic interactions. This multilevel charge transfer substantially increased both the local dipole moments and carrier concentration.

Collectively, these results indicate that charge transfer induced by work function differences at the heterointerface triggers strong interfacial polarization relaxation while simultaneously improving conductivity. This synergistic increase in the dielectric and conductive losses at the electronic scale effectively optimized the macroscopic absorption performance of h-SiBCNFe ceramics, providing a solid theoretical foundation for their application as advanced EMW absorption materials. The “mushroom-like” structure facilitated multistage reflection/scattering of the incident EMW, optimized impedance matching, and attenuated the EMW. The 3D carbon framework served as a conductive network for electron transport, enhancing conduction loss. The Fex*Si*y* magnetic phase in the core–shell structure promoted natural resonance through magnetic nanoparticles, reinforcing magnetic loss. The multiscale architecture provided a large surface area, accommodating electric dipoles under an external field. The heterogeneous interfaces between the carbon layers and Fe*x*Si*y* phases generated additional dipoles, dissipating electromagnetic energy. This multiscale structural design achieved excellent EMW absorption capability.

### 3.4 Corrosion resistance of h-SiBCNFe

In harsh marine atmospheres or saline immersion environments, EMW absorbers must combine strong EMW absorption with excellent corrosion resistance to ensure long-term service stability. To systematically evaluate the corrosion resistance of h-SiBCNFe ceramics under chloride-containing conditions, carbonyl iron powder (CIP), a widely used commercial absorber, was selected as a benchmark material. A series of electrochemical measurements were conducted in a 5.0 wt% NaCl solution, and the results are shown in Fig. 5.

*
Fig. 5Corrosion resistance of h-SiBCNFe. Electrochemical corrosion parameters of CIP and h-SiBCNFe ceramics pyrolyzed at different temperatures in 5.0 wt% NaCl solution: (a) OCP curves, (b) Tafel polarization curves, and (c) Nyquist plots; comparison of (d) corrosion current densities; (e) corrosion resistance and (f) polarization resistance. The OCP is an indicator of the thermodynamic stability of materials in corrosive media, with higher OCP values suggesting lower tendencies for spontaneous corrosion. Compared with CIP, all h-SiBCNFe ceramics exhibited significantly higher OCP values (Fig. 5(a)), indicating their intrinsically superior corrosion resistance. Among them, h-SiBCNFe-1300 showed the most positive OCP, indicating the highest thermodynamic stability. Kinetic parameters, including corrosion potential (E*corr) and corrosion current (*I*corr), were obtained from Tafel polarization curves. A higher *E*corr indicates greater resistance to corrosion initiation, while a lower *I*corr corresponds to slower corrosion rates [45]. As shown in Fig. 5(b), the h-SiBCNFe ceramics outperformed the CIP ceramics in terms of both parameters. Notably, h-SiBCNFe-1300 achieved the highest *E*corr (0.033 V) and the lowest *I*corr (0.63 μA·cm−2), confirming that corrosion kinetics were significantly suppressed. This performance was attributed to the core–shell structures that formed dense physical barriers, effectively impeding the penetration of corrosive media. The polarization resistance (*R*p), extrapolated from Tafel fitting, further confirmed this trend. The h-SiBCNFe-1300 ceramic exhibited the highest *R*p value (Fig. 5(f)), exceeding that of CIP by more than threefold, which is consistent with the other electrochemical results.

EIS was further employed to probe the interfacial corrosion behavior after immersion in a 5.0 wt% NaCl solution for 1600 s. Nyquist plots (Fig. 5(c)) revealed that the h-SiBCNFe samples exhibited substantially larger capacitive semicircle radii than the CIP samples, indicating higher interfacial resistance and reduced charge-transfer activity. In the Bode phase angle–frequency plots (Fig. S12(a) in the ESM), the impedance (*|Z|*0.01) modulus at 0.01 Hz was significantly greater for the h-SiBCNFe ceramics than for the CIP ceramics. Furthermore, the h-SiBCNFe ceramics showed more stable phase behavior across the relevant frequency range, again demonstrating their superior corrosion resistance. Equivalent circuit fitting of the EIS spectra (Fig. S12(b) in the ESM) was performed via Z-View software. *R*s, *R*c, and *R*ct represent the solution resistance, coating resistance, and charge-transfer resistance, respectively, while *Q*c and *Q*dl correspond to constant phase elements. The fitting results confirmed that h-SiBCNFe ceramics, particularly h-SiBCNFe-1300, presented significantly higher *R*ct values (Table S1 in the ESM), reflecting the role of graphitic carbon layers in effectively suppressing corrosive penetration and hindering charge transfer during electrochemical reactions. Taken together, these electrochemical results demonstrated that h-SiBCNFe ceramics, especially h-SiBCNFe-1300, possessed corrosion resistance far superior to that of conventional CIP absorbers and FeSiAl-based materials (*I*corr = 2.16 μA·cm−2, *E*corr = −0.045 V) [13]. Their unique core–shell architecture and dense graphitic protective layers endow them with outstanding long-term stability in aggressive marine environments, underscoring their potential for practical application in corrosion-resistant EMW absorption.

To clarify the adsorption behavior of Cl− on the surface of the core–shell structures and its influence on corrosion resistance, DFT calculations were carried out (Figs. 5(g)–5(i)). On the basis of the XRD and TEM results, the dominant crystal phases in the core–shell regions were identified as C(002) and Fe3Si(220). Accordingly, the adsorption energies of Cl− on these two surfaces were calculated. The adsorption energy (*E*ads) reflected the interaction strength between Cl− and the surface: a more positive value indicated weaker adsorption and higher resistance of Cl−, whereas a more negative value suggested stronger binding and greater corrosion susceptibility [46].

The results revealed that the adsorption energy of Cl− on the C (002) surface was +0.62 eV, a relatively high positive value. Thermodynamically, this indicated that Cl− adsorption on the carbon surface required overcoming an energy barrier and was thus unlikely to form a stable adsorbed state. In contrast, the adsorption energy of Cl− on Fe3Si (220) was −3.17 eV, a significantly negative value, suggesting spontaneous and strong chemisorption. In this case, Cl− readily bonds to the Fe3Si surface, disrupting the passive film and initiating localized corrosion reactions. For the C@Fe3Si heterojunction, the adsorption energy of Cl− was calculated as +0.23 eV, as shown in Fig. 5(i). Although slightly lower than that on pure carbon, it remains positive, indicating that the carbon layer continues to inhibit Cl− adsorption at the heterojunction interface. Therefore, the heterostructure effectively prevented the ingress of Cl− and thus maintained high corrosion resistance. These theoretical findings aligned well with the three-electrode measurements, further validating the proposed corrosion-protection mechanism of h-SiBCNFe ceramics.

The “mushroom-like” h-SiBCNFe ceramics achieved an optimized trade-off between EMW absorption and anticorrosion performance and demonstrated significantly enhanced properties relative to those of most previously reported EMW-absorbing anticorrosive materials, as shown in Fig. 6(c). On the basis of the above discussion, Fig. 6(a) shows the schematic EMW absorption mechanism of h-SiBCNF ceramics, which involves multiple reflections, conduction loss, magnetic loss, and dipole polarization. First, the “mushroom-like” architecture facilitated multistage reflection/scattering of the incident EMW, thereby optimizing impedance matching and effectively attenuating the EMW. Subsequently, the three-dimensional carbon framework served as a fundamental conductive network, acting as an electron transport channel to further increase conduction loss. In addition, the FeSi magnetic phase within the core–shell structure significantly promoted natural resonance through magnetic nanoparticles, thus reinforcing the magnetic loss capability. Moreover, the multiscale architecture endowed the material with a large specific surface area, enabling the accommodation of abundant electric dipoles under an external electric field. Moreover, plentiful heterogeneous interfaces between the carbon layers and Fe*x*Si*y* magnetic phases generated additional dipoles driven by interfacial potential differences, which acted as polarization centers to dissipate electromagnetic energy. For the anticorrosion mechanism of h-SiBCNFe ceramics (Fig. 6(b)), the graphitic carbon shell encapsulating the Fe3Si core functions as an inert physical barrier, effectively preventing Cl− penetration toward the highly active metallic substrate. This not only decreased the local concentration of Cl− at the interface but also delayed the initiation of corrosion reactions.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, this work reports an h-SiBCNFe ceramic EMW absorber with a hierarchical C@Fe*x*Si*y*@SiBCN architecture. Interfaces between carbon nanotubes and encapsulated Fe*x*Si*y* enhance interfacial polarization, whereas the coexistence of dielectric SiBCN and magnetic Fe*x*Si*y* enables magnetodielectric coupling, broadening the absorption bandwidth. The “mushroom-like” morphology promotes multiple scattering, and the carbon-encapsulated Fe*x*Si*y* within the robust SiBCN matrix ensures corrosion resistance. Together, these features result in a combination of broadband EMW absorption (EAB = 8.16 GHz, RLmin = −32.3 dB) and superior corrosion resistance (*I*corr = 0.63 μA·cm−2, *E*corr = 0.033 V). More importantly, this work establishes a “precursor-to-structure” paradigm, where ferric-mediated secondary crosslinking simultaneously ensures compositional uniformity and guides hierarchical microstructure development during pyrolysis. The resulting h-SiBCNFe exemplifies how structural complexity in PDCs can be programmed at the precursor stage, providing new insights for the development of functional PDC absorbers.
