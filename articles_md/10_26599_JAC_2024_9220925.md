## 1 Introduction

The rise in global industrialization and urbanization has intensified water contamination challenges in the 21st century [1]. Effluents from textile processes and harmful microorganisms from food industry wastewater further endanger human health and ecosystems [2,3]. Given these urgent environmental challenges, there is a strong push for innovative solutions to treat pollutants sustainably and ensure safety. Tribocatalysis, which harnesses mechanical energy for chemical reactions, is an emerging field with applications ranging from energy generation to material science [4−13]. The efficiency of tribocatalysis strongly depends on the selection of materials and their inherent properties [7]. The introduction of defects into catalysts offers valuable prospects for increasing tribocatalytic efficiency [6]. These defects can contribute additional impurity energy levels, which aid in electron transfer, diminish electron–hole recombination, and enhance the degradation of pollutants [14−20].

Strontium titanate (SrTiO3, STO), a versatile centrosymmetric perovskite-type oxide, is gaining recognition for its outstanding characteristics, including chemical stability, biocompatibility, resistance to photocorrosion, and an ideal bandgap width [21−24]. In SrTiO3, the flexoelectric effect originates from electric polarization induced by a strain gradient. This indicates that non-uniform mechanical deformation of the material results in the generation of an electric field [25]. Significantly, the flexoelectric properties of SrTiO3, which are further improved through doping and defect engineering, open up a broad spectrum of applications in areas such as energy harvesting, sensing, MEMS, tunable electronics, and nanotechnology [26−31]. However, the interplay between the tribocatalytic performance of doped SrTiO3 variants and their electronic structure, particularly when integrated with flexoelectric properties, remains largely unexplored. This area represents a significant knowledge gap, which is essential for the advancement of sophisticated tribocatalytic systems.

The unique electronic properties and catalytic behavior of rhodium significantly boost the photocatalytic efficiency and stability of SrTiO3. Carbon doping introduces oxygen vacancies, enhancing the flexoelectric effect and synergizing with rhodium to further advance photocatalysis. This study investigated the tribocatalytic capabilities of both uni-doped (Rh-doped and C-doped) and bi-doped (Rh&C co-doped) SrTiO3 for degrading Rhodamine B (RhB) dyes. Our goal is to explore how doping and defects collectively increase degradation effectiveness, offering new insights for evolving wastewater treatment technologies.

### 2.1 Synthesis of uni- & bi-doped SrTiO3 tribocatalysts

Micron-sized uni- and bi-doped SrTiO3 particles were synthesized using a conventional solid-state reaction method. For the preparation of uni-doped SrTiO₃, first, the starting materials included strontium carbonate (SrCO3, 99.5%), titanium oxide (TiO2, 99.9%), rhodium oxide (Rh2O3, 99.8%), and graphite (C, 99.95%), all sourced from Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd. Second, a stoichiometric mixture of 1% Rh2O3 to 0.99% SrTiO3 was prepared and denoted as Rh-STO [23]. Another mixture was created with a certain percentage of C to SrTiO3 (the proportion of added carbon is 25% of the SrCO3 content [28]), abbreviated as C-STO. Each mixture underwent ball milling at 260 r/min for 4 h. Third, the milled powders were dried for 12 h, pressed into disk pellets, and sintered at 1000 °C with a heating rate of 2 °C/min for 10 h in air, followed by natural cooling. Finally, the sintered pellets were crushed and ball-milled at 300 r/min in ethanol for 24 h, yielding micron-sized uni-doped SrTiO3 particles, i.e., Rh-STO and C-STO. For the bi-doped SrTiO3 preparation, first, a mixture of 1% Rh2O3 to 0.99% SrTiO3 was blended with carbon, maintaining the same carbon proportion relative to SrCO3 as in C-STO. Second, the synthesis process was identical to the uni-doped SrTiO3 procedure mentioned above, resulting in Rh and C co-doped STO particles, abbreviated as Rh&C-STO.

### 2.2 Characterizations

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; MiniFlex/600, Rigaku, Japan), a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM; FEI Tecnai G2 F20 S-Twin, USA) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDS) were applied to observe the microstructure, surface morphologies, and EDS spectra of the particles. Moreover, Raman spectra were collected with 532-nm excitation using a Raman microscope (Renishaw inVia, UK) at room temperature (RT). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed using a Thermo ScientificTM NexsaTM system (USA) with Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV). Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra were obtained using a Bruker EMXplus spectrometer. The ultraviolet–visible (UV‒Vis) diffuse reflectance spectra of the powder catalysts, along with the dye concentrations in the reaction solutions, were measured using a Shimadzu UV-2600i UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Japan).

For the measurement of carrier density, Mott–Schottky plots were obtained at a frequency of 10³ Hz under room temperature conditions by using an electrochemical workstation (CHI 660E, CH Instruments Inc., China) equipped with a three-electrode system consisting of a working electrode, a platinum counter electrode, and an saturated calomel reference electrode (SCE). The working electrode was ITO-coated glass (20 mm × 20 mm × 1 mm) covered with 10 mg of catalyst particles. A 0.5 mol/L Na2SO4 aqueous electrolyte was used and illuminated with simulated solar light using a custom-made XENON setup (300 W, PLS-SXE300+/UV, PerfectLight, China).

### 2.3 Tribocatalytic evaluation of uni- and bi-doped SrTiO3 particles

The degradation performance of uni- and bi-doped SrTiO3 particles (namely, Rh-STO, C-STO, and Rh&C-STO) was tested against RhB dye at a concentration of 5 mg/L and at room temperature. For comparison, micron-sized commercial SrTiO3 was prepared by ball milling 99.5% pure SrTiO3 (average particle size ~5 μm) from Sigma‒Aldrich, USA, for 24 h. This milled sample, referred to as STO, was also simultaneously tested for RhB dye degradation. Experiments were carried out in darkness and under solar light from the XENON lamp as described in our previous work [6]. In all the RhB degradation experiments described here, a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) magnetic stirring bar measuring 8 mm in diameter and 20 mm in length was used. The stirring speed was consistently maintained at 1200 r/min (equivalent to 20 Hz) throughout the tribocatalytic process. Additionally, to ensure adsorption equilibrium between the dye and the catalyst, the solution was pre-stirred for 1 h using the aforementioned PTFE stirrer before conducting the tribocatalytic tests. The degradation steps are as follows: First, 50 mg of the selected catalyst (either STO, Rh-STO, C-STO, or Rh&C-STO) was dispersed in 50 mL of RhB solution within a 100 mL flat-bottomed glass vessel. Then, under dark conditions, a 3 mL sample of the suspension was extracted every hour; under solar light, samples were taken every half hour. Each sample was then centrifuged at 6000 r/min for 10 min to obtain a clear supernatant. Finally, the absorption peak of the dyeat ~554 nm was determined using the same UV‒Vis spectrophotometer.

### 3.1 Microstructural characterization and Raman spectroscopy analysis

Figure 1(a) presents the XRD patterns of uni- and bi-doped SrTiO3 materials synthesized at 1000 °C for 10 h. For Rh-STO, Figs. 1(b), 1(b1), and 1(b2) display the surface morphology and microstructures. EDS analysis is shown separately in Fig. 1(c). Additionally, Raman spectra of various catalysts at RT are shown in Fig. 1(d). As shown in Fig. 1(a), the XRD patterns feature distinct and well-defined peaks, indicative of their crystalline nature with cubic symmetry. Notably, the introduced dopants do not induce any discernible changes in the average crystal structure. The XRD patterns confirmed the formation of a pure phase, devoid of any stray phase, retaining the cubic perovskite structure characteristic of commercial SrTiO3 crystals.

Furthermore, a detailed Rietveld refinement using MAUD software was performed to determine the crystal symmetry, stoichiometric formulas, and precise lattice parameters of the representative Rh-STO and C-STO samples. As illustrated in Fig. S1 and Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), the refinement confirmed the crystallization of the samples in a cubic symmetry, specifically within the Pm** 3¯m* space group. For Rh-STO, the analysis accurately identified the lattice positions of the constituent elements: strontium (Sr), titanium (Ti), oxygen (O), and rhodium (Rh), each occupying their expected sites in accordance with the stoichiometric formulas. Notably, the refinement also revealed that Rh atoms were preferentially located at the titanium (Ti) sites, indicating that substitutional doping could bestow unique electronic and structural properties on the Rh-STO sample. In the case of C-STO, the analysis pinpointed the lattice positions of strontium (Sr), titanium (Ti), and oxygen (O), all aligning with their anticipated sites consistent with the stoichiometric formulas. Interestingly, the refinement indicated the absence of carbon atoms, suggesting that carbon may have evaporated during the sintering process.

Additionally, the morphology and crystalline structure of the Rh-STO sample were examined using TEM, as depicted in Figs. 1(b)–1(b2). Figure 1(b) reveals that the Rh-STO particles are micron-sized with an irregular shape, and their average grain size is less than 500 nm. HRTEM images and the corresponding indexed fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns, shown in Figs. 1(b1) and 1(b2), identify the (100), (002), and (120) lattice planes of Rh-STO, with *d*-spacings of 0.384, 0.194, and 0.171 nm, respectively. For example, the *d*-spacing for the (100) lattice plane of Rh-STO is 0.384 nm, which is consistent with the refinement results presented in Table S1(a) in the ESM. This observation contrasts with the corresponding plane in standard SrTiO3 (PDF No. 35-0734), which exhibits a *d*-spacing of 0.390 nm. Clearly, the lattice planes in the Rh-STO particles exhibit contraction compared to those of the standard SrTiO3 crystal structure. Although doping SrTiO3 with Rh3+ ions, which replace Ti4+ ions, is typically expected to maintain or slightly expand the lattice parameter due to the slightly larger ionic radius of Rh3+ 0.665 Å (compared to that of Ti4+ 0.605 Å) [24], the observed contraction of lattice planes in Rh-STO defies these expectations. This unexpected contraction is attributed to the presence of oxygen vacancies. The removal of oxygen ions leads to closer packing of the surrounding metal ions, consequently reducing the lattice spacing [6,15]. This phenomenon is also observed in the C-STO sample, as detailed in Table S1(b) in the ESM. Additionally, enlarged XRD patterns for the catalysts, focusing on the 2*θ* degree between 45° and 48°, as shown in Fig. S1(c) in the ESM, evidently demonstrate that the peak (200) of doped catalysts is subtly shifted to the right compared to that of commercial STO, indicating lattice contraction. These findings will be further explored in the subsequent section.

EDS analysis of the Rh-STO particles confirms the successful diffusion of Rh3+ into the SrTiO3 lattice, forming a homogeneous solid solution, as illustrated in Fig. 1(c). Similarly, the morphologies of commercial STO and C-STO particles, as shown in Figs. S2(a) and S3(a) in the ESM, were observed via TEM. These morphologies exhibit an irregular shape, with an average grain size of less than 500 nm. Additionally, the HRTEM images and indexed FFT patterns in Figs. S2(a1) and S2(a2) in the ESM reveal the (100), (011), and (111) lattice planes of commercial STO, with corresponding *d*-spacings of 0.390, 0.276, and 0.225 nm, respectively, which are consistent with those of standard SrTiO3. Furthermore, EDS analysis was conducted on C-STO samples. This analysis, particularly focusing on the carbon-free region of the copper grid (marked with a red circle in Fig. S3(b) in the ESM), indicates that carbon has evaporated, leaving no detectable residue in the EDS spectrum for this study.

The Raman spectrum offers insights into the vibrational modes of a crystal, with shifts or the emergence of new modes potentially indicating changes in crystal structure or composition. As displayed in Fig. 1(d), the Raman spectra of various uni- and bi-doped STO samples, including commercial STO measured at RT, show several broad bands, labeled 1–7, corresponding to 2nd-order scattering [32−36]. Notably, new lines labeled A, B, and C are detected at approximately 176, 544, and 800 cm−1, respectively. These are assigned to the TO2, TO4, and LO4 modes [35]. In comparison to those of pristine STO and C-STO, the peaks associated with Rh doping in SrTiO3 (i.e., the TO2, TO4, and LO4 modes for Rh-STO and Rh&C-STO) exhibit increased prominence and sharpness. Additionally, a peak at ~500 cm−1 is observable in the Raman spectra of Rh-STO and C-STO. Although the precise cause of this enhancement remains unclear, it is possible that the incorporation of dopant ions and the formation of defects, such as oxygen vacancies, contribute to the emergence of defect-induced vibrational modes in Raman spectra [33].

The OV concentrations are approximately 20%, 29%, 32%, and 29% for STO, Rh-STO, C-STO, and Rh&C-STO, respectively, as detailed in Table 1. Both Rh-STO and Rh&C-STO exhibit similar concentrations of surface oxygen vacancies, while C-STO demonstrates the highest concentration of these vacancies. The concentration of STO decreases between those of Rh-STO and C-STO. Second, titanium is predominantly in the +4 oxidation state (Ti4+), but the presence of Ti3+ ions is also noted, showing distinct binding energy values compared to those of Ti4+. These values shift slightly with the nature of the dopant. For instance, the Ti 2p binding energy in commercial STO (463.87 and 458.08 eV for Ti 2p) serves as a baseline. Rh-STO shows a slight decrease in the Ti 2p binding energy (463.30 and 457.82 eV), which is attributed to Rh3+ ion charge compensation and the partial reduction of Ti4+ to Ti3+. Additionally, higher oxygen vacancy concentrations correlate with slightly higher Ti3+ binding energies. For example, C-STO, with its high OV concentration, shows Ti3+ binding energies of 462.20 and 457.20 eV, a slight increase compared to those of commercial STO, Rh-STO, and Rh&C-STO, indicating the presence of Ti3+ in the XPS spectra commonly associated with oxygen vacancy formation [37,39−44]. In the case of the Rh-STO catalyst, Rh3+ ions are substituted for Ti4+ ions in the lattice, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This substitution introduces a deficit of one positive charge, typically balanced by oxygen vacancies ( OV⋅⋅), leaving behind two electrons (2 e−). Two electrons are captured near the oxygen vacancy, one of which reacts with Ti4+ to form Ti3+. The reaction can be represented by Reactions (1) and (2) in terms of the Kröger–Vink notation [37]:

(2) 2TiTi→2TiTi′+OV⋅⋅

Furthermore, shifts in the Sr 3d binding energy are less pronounced than those in the Ti 2p binding energy, reflecting the limited role of strontium in redox processes. These shifts in the Sr 3d binding energy are typically subtler than those in the Ti 2p binding energy, as illustrated in Figs. 4(a2)–4(d2). Nevertheless, subtle changes can still occur due to alterations in the lattice environment resulting from the introduction of dopants, which may cause distortions or strain. Additionally, Fig. S4 in the ESM displays the full XPS spectra of the Rh-STO and Rh&C-STO catalysts and the Rh 3d peaks, confirming the presence of all the elements in the various catalysts.

### 3.4 EPR, UV‒Vis, and Mott–Schottky analyses of electronic structures of catalysts

Figure 5 displays the EPR and UV–Vis absorption spectra, the Mott–Schottky curve, and a diagrammatic representation of the electronic structure of the catalysts.

*
Fig. 5Analysis of electronic properties and carrier density of catalysts: (a) EPR and (b) UV‒Vis spectra; (c) Mott–Schottky curves; (d) sketched electronic structures.

g*-factor is a sensitive parameter that often shows only minor variations due to doping, especially in materials such as SrTiO3, which is known for its weak magnetic properties [45]. EPR spectroscopy, which is highly responsive to unpaired electrons, reveals this subtlety. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the EPR spectra of both uni- and bi-doped SrTiO3, as well as commercial STO, indicate the prominent presence of oxygen vacancies in all the samples. Notably, the *g*-factor is measured at 2.003 for both uni- and bi-doped SrTiO3, which is slightly different from the value of 2.004 for STO. The minor deviation between the doped and undoped SrTiO3 (2.003 vs. 2.004) indicates that doping introduces minimal alterations to the magnetic environment of the electrons, resulting in comparable *g*-factor values across various doping types [46,47]. Additionally, the largest *g*-factor observed in C-STO suggests a higher concentration of unpaired electrons or localized electron states, which could be due to carbon-induced defects, i.e., oxygen vacancies [28,29]. The similarities in the *g*-factors of Rh-STO and Rh&C-STO indicate that Rh3+ doping, either alone or in combination with carbon, does not significantly alter the concentration of unpaired electrons compared to that of C-STO. Additionally, a notable contrast is observed in the detection of Ti3+ using XPS, compared to the absence of distinct Ti3+ signals in the EPR spectra. The lack of Ti3+ signals in the EPR spectrum could be attributed to their low concentrations, which potentially fall below the EPR detection threshold but are still discernible via XPS.

Figure 5(b) displays the UV‒Vis absorption spectra of various catalysts, with their band gap values estimated and provided in Table 1 [6,7]. The energy band gap of a material significantly influences its light absorption characteristics; generally, a smaller band gap enables more efficient absorption of visible light by requiring less energy to excite electrons from the valence to the conduction band [23,37]. Among the catalysts, Rh&C-STO exhibits the smallest band gap (~2.79 eV), suggesting its ability to absorb lower-energy, longer-wavelength light, as evidenced by the highest absorption peak within the 400–700 nm range. Rh-STO (~3.03 eV), with a marginally larger band gap, demonstrates the second-highest absorption, underscoring that Rh3+ doping effectively narrows the band gap, thereby enhancing visible light absorption. Additionally, as mentioned above, we contribute to the flexoelectric effect induced by crystal contraction and the presence of oxygen vacancies [48−52], which could further enhance electron‒hole separation. This, in turn, results in significantly higher absorption in the UV‒Vis spectra presented here.

In contrast, commercial SrTiO3 (~3.15 eV) and C-STO (~3.07 eV) exhibit lower absorption in this range. Notably, an increase in the oxygen vacancy concentration in both STO and C-STO does not significantly reduce the band gap or enhance the visible light absorption efficiency, which clearly differs from the effects observed with Rh doping. This leads to two important insights. First, oxygen vacancies, as defects, can play a dual role in tribocatalysis. On one hand, they modify the electronic structure, acting as shallow donor states that facilitate the production of * ⋅O2− (superoxide radicals) and promote electron‒hole separation, as noted in Ref. [6]. On the other hand, they also act as recombination sites for electron‒hole pairs. As indicated in Rh-STO, although oxygen vacancies can induce a flexoelectric field, this study highlights that the crucial aspect is the interplay and balance between dopant-induced states within their band structures, the oxygen vacancy concentration, and the resultant flexoelectric field during tribocatalysis.

Figure 5(c) shows the Mott–Schottky plots for various catalysts measured at a frequency of 103 Hz. These plots exhibit a positive slope when 1/C*² is plotted against the applied voltage, confirming that the catalysts are n-type semiconductors with electrons as the majority carriers [6]. The flat band potential (*V*fb) is determined from the *V*-axis intercept. The electron carrier concentration (*N*d) is derived from the slope of the Mott–Schottky plot, with the calculation method detailed in a previous work by the authors [6]. The *N*d values for all catalysts are summarized in Table 1. As illustrated in Fig. 5(c), C-STO has the lowest carrier concentration and lowest *V*fb of ~2.18 eV, while commercial STO has the highest carrier concentration and lowest *V*fb. Rh-STO and Rh&C-STO have intermediate carrier concentrations between those of C-STO and natural STO. According to the visible light spectrum in Fig. 5(b), the high concentration of surface oxygen vacancies in C-STO might function as traps for charge carriers, potentially diminishing the efficiency of light absorption and lowering the energy required to excite an electron. The electronic structures of the various catalysts are illustrated in Fig. 5(d). The conduction band (CB) and valence band (VB) of pure STO are approximately −3.09 and 0.054 eV, respectively, and a noticeable shift in these positions is observed for both uni-doped and bi-doped STO. For instance, C-STO shows CB and VB values of approximately −2.13 and 0.93 eV, respectively. However, for the Rh-STO and Rh&C-STO catalysts, there is no notable change in the CB and VB positions, with CB ranging from approximately −2.63 to −2.49 eV and VB from approximately 0.39 to 0.29 eV. Alterations in the positions of VB and CB suggest that dopants or defects introduce new energy states, thereby modifying the photocatalytic properties in tribocatalysis.

As reflected in Figs. 2 and 3, C-STO exhibits a RhB degradation efficiency similar to that of commercial STO in tribocatalysis under both solar and nonsolar light irradiation, ranking the lowest among the tested materials. Moreover, Rh&C-STO shows slightly better performance than both STO and C-STO. In contrast, Rh-STO exhibits the highest efficiency in degrading RhB under identical conditions. The phenomena observed can be attributed to the introduction of oxygen vacancy defects by C doping in this study, which predominantly act as recombination centers for electron–hole pairs. Furthermore, an optimal energy band gap of approximately 3.0 eV, along with a balanced defect–tribocatalysis interaction, is crucial for the efficient degradation of RhB.

### 3.5 Computed electronic state densities of various catalysts

Consequently, the diverse structures of SrTiO3 doped with carbon at oxygen sites and of Rhodium at titanium sites were modeled, along with those of commercial STO, using a 2 × 2 × 2 supercell derived from the cubic perovskite structure. This supercell size was chosen to minimize defect interactions. The electronic structures were computed using the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) and density functional theory (DFT), employing the projector augmented wave (PAW) method [53,54]. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional was used for exchange-correlation energy, with the structures optimized until convergence criteria for energy and force were met, setting a kinetic energy cutoff at 400 eV [55], as shown in Fig. 6.

This discrepancy could be due to the limitations of DFT in accurately predicting band gaps in semiconductors [56].

However, Fig. 6(a) confirms that commercial STO is a semiconductor in nature, with no electronic states at the Fermi level (*E*f). In contrast, for Rh-STO and Rh&C-STO, electronic states are present at *E*f, indicating the creation of new energy states in Rh-doped STO catalysts. For C-STO, a weak new energy state is observed in the valence band, suggesting the formation of new energy states between the VB and CB compared to natural STO.

Rh-doped SrTiO3 displays distinct electronic characteristics compared to those of natural STO. An electronic state at the Fermi level (*E*f) involving O, Ti, and Rh is observed between the CB and VB. In contrast, in natural STO, the electronic state of O is situated in the VB, while Ti is in the CB, with no electronic states noted between these bands (Fig. S5(b) in the ESM). The introduction of Rh seems to create intermediary electronic states, potentially altering the material's electronic and optical properties—a factor that could significantly impact its application in tribocatalysis, both with and without solar light irradiation.

Tribocatalysis operates under both solar irradiation and dark conditions, relying on critical interactions within CB and VB of the catalysts. All catalysts exhibit a CB potential more negative than −2.13 eV (Fig. 6(d)), placing it beneath the reductive potential of  O2/⋅O2− (−0.33 V vs normal hydrogen electrode (NHE)) [6,16]. Consequently, electrons in the CB can reduce dissolved  O2 to form  ⋅O2−, which is essential for RhB degradation. Conversely, the standard oxidative potential of  OH−/⋅OH (1.99 eV vs. NHE) exceeds the VB potentials of all the catalysts, which remain below 0.93 eV, suggesting that direct conversion of VB holes to  ⋅OH radicals is theoretically improbable.

In the absence of solar light (Fig. 7(a)),  ⋅OH radical generation is hypothesized to occur through a two-step process. Electrons, derived from friction-induced effects, oxygen vacancies, or both, reduce  O2 to  ⋅O2− on the catalyst surface, leading to the formation of  H2O2, which then decomposes into  ⋅OH radicals [6,16].

Under solar irradiation (Fig. 7(b)),  ⋅OH radicals form through two pathways: the dark process and a light-induced mechanism exploiting Rh-STO’s energy band gap (~3.03 eV), which spans from visible light (409 nm) to the UV range, thereby enhancing RhB degradation tenfold under light compared to dark conditions. Additionally, solar irradiation induces a blueshift in Rh-STO, which contributes to increasing  ⋅OH radical production and facilitating C–O bond cleavage [2].

The behaviors of the Rh-STO and Rh&C-STO catalysts under varying conditions provide insights into their tribocatalytic and photocatalytic behaviors. Both catalysts, which are rich in Ti3+ ions and oxygen vacancies, exhibit comparable dark degradation efficiencies for RhB, suggesting defect-induced activities at similar levels. The difference in degradation efficiency under solar irradiation, with high performance in Rh-STO, underscores the impact of defect interactions with light. The improved efficiency may stem from better energy state alignment with the solar spectrum or more effective charge carrier separation, emphasizing the role of defect engineering in photocatalyst performance in tribocatalysis.

Additionally, our work elucidates the role of the flexoelectric field in promoting electron–hole separation under solar irradiation, as depicted in Figs. 7(c)–7(e). Figure 7(c) illustrates how Rh3+ doping at Ti4+ sites in SrTiO3 causes oxygen vacancies, as detailed in Reactions (1) and (2). The TEM image in Fig. 7(d) confirms the presence of a polarization domain attributable to flexoelectric effects, and Fig. 7(e) shows the simulated flexoelectric field in the cubic structure of Rh-SrTiO3.

Importantly, the visible polarization domain in Rh-SrTiO3, as evidenced by the TEM image, is partly due to flexoelectric influences. This aspect is of particular relevance in centrosymmetric semiconductors where flexoelectric responses are crucial, as discussed in previous studies [27−28,31]. The intrinsic flexoelectric coefficients in solids are typically small; thus, substantial flexoelectric responses require the presence of large strain gradients. Our findings suggest that the structural changes induced by Rh3+ doping, namely, oxygen vacancies and crystal contraction, contribute to these necessary strain gradients, facilitating the observed flexoelectricity [48,49]. Figure S6 in the ESM shows the HRTEM domain image and indexed FFT, revealing that the (100) lattice plane and [010] direction are consistent with the susceptibility of the (100) plane to flexoelectricity [50−52]. As we have demonstrated, C-STO can also lead to crystal lattice contraction, coupled with the presence of oxygen vacancies. Consequently, a flexoelectric field is likely to be present in the C-STO catalyst [28,29]. However, for both C-STO and Rh&C-STO, the efficiency of tribocatalysis is significantly decreased, suggesting that the impact of the flexoelectric field is inherently constrained by the electronic structure of the catalyst. Therefore, the enhanced degradation of RhB by Rh-STO through tribocatalysis under solar light irradiation likely involves a complex interplay and balance among the electronic structure, defects, and internally induced electric fields, such as the flexoelectric field in SrTiO3. These aspects will be further investigated in our future work.

In the case of STO and C-STO without solar light irradiation, the degradation of RhB in the initial 24 h of stirring is predominantly driven by electrons induced through triboelectrification, leading to the formation of  ⋅O2−. As the stirring time increases, the degradation efficiency becomes increasingly dominated by surface oxygen vacancies. The oxygen vacancies, which act as the dual effect, can synergistically enhance the degradation performance by facilitating the separation of electron–hole pairs that act as shallow states [6]. Consequently, after 48 h, C-STO demonstrates a significantly greater dye degradation capacity than STO. However, when exposed to solar light irradiation, surface oxygen vacancies serve as recombination traps for electron–hole pairs during the 2-h stirring process. Consequently, C-STO and STO demonstrate almost identical degradation efficiencies. This observation implies and confirms that the electronic structure of the catalysts is crucial for the effective degradation of RhB through tribocatalysis.

The redox process proposed for RhB degradation via tribocatalysis utilizing Rh-STO as the model catalyst is outlined below and described in Reactions (3)–(10):

(3) Rh−STO→Frictione−

(a) Without solar light irradiation (Reactions (4)–(7)):

(4) O2+e−→⋅O2−

(5) e−+HO2→HO2−

(6) HO2−+H+→H2O2

(7) H2O2+2e−→⋅OH+OH−

(b) With solar light irradiation (Reactions (8)–(10)):

(8) Rh−STO+hv→h++e−

(11) ⋅O2−/⋅OH+dye→Decomposition

As a result, it becomes clear that defects introduced by dopants such as Rh are fundamental for enhancing the degradation process through tribocatalysis when exposed to solar light. Furthermore, the synergistic impact of these defects is crucial in achieving improved tribocatalytic efficiency. This includes attaining an optimal electronic structure and maintaining a balance between the defects and tribocatalytic activity. Finally, the flexoelectric field, which originates from these dopants and defects, plays a significant role in augmenting the separation of electron–hole pairs, especially under solar irradiation.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, we underscore the crucial interplay among defects, energy states, and flexoelectric fields in enhancing the tribocatalytic degradation of RhB using centrosymmetric uni- and bi-doped SrTiO3 as catalysts. Our findings reveal that defects introduced by dopants such as Rh (Rh-STO) significantly enhance tribocatalytic performance under solar irradiation. This improvement is achieved by modifying energy states to attain the optimal energy band gap and interacting with OH− and dissolved O2 in the solution to generate redox-active species on the surface. Additionally, the emergence of a flexoelectric field due to these defects introduces a complex dimension that profoundly affects the reactivity and efficiency of the tribocatalytic process. However, the efficiency of tribocatalysis is inherently linked to the electronic structure of the catalyst. Therefore, achieving an optimal electronic structure through the introduction of these defects, coupled with a balanced defect-tribocatalytic process, is vital for the efficient degradation of RhB.
