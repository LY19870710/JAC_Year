## 1 Introduction

Recognized as crucial functional components, microwave dielectric ceramics (MWDCs) have gained widespread industry recognition for their essential role in developing communication equipment such as filters, relays, and antennas [1−5]. Currently, owing to the implementation of fifth-generation communication technology and the development of sixth-generation communication, the pursuit of dielectric materials with high-speed signal transmission characteristics has become urgent [6−10]. This urgency has significantly stimulated the development of microwave ceramics with low dielectric constants (*ε*r 10−16]. In addition, driven by the rapid progression of communication technology, the operating frequency has been shifting to higher regions, indicating a huge application potential in future communication systems in the terahertz band [17−19]. Therefore, great attention has been given to exploring ceramics that can be applied not only in the microwave band (low *ε*r, high *Q×f* (*Q* is quality factor, and *f* is the resonant frequency of 13.5 GHz), and near-zero resonant frequency (*τ*f)) but also in the terahertz band.

As a polycrystalline material, SiO2 was found to exhibit unique dielectric properties, particularly its extremely low dielectric constant [61]. Based on different phase states, two forms, quartz and cristobalite, were identified, with dielectric constants of 4.44 and 3.81, respectively [27]. These characteristics are attributed primarily to two key factors: first, the minimal ionic polarizability of Si4+ ions (0.87 Å3), which plays a crucial role in reducing the dielectric constant; second, the [SiO4] tetrahedron structure, composed of Si–O covalent bonds, which significantly affects dielectric properties of SiO2 [25,62]. As a result, the dielectric constants of most silicate ceramic materials are generally found to be below 10. For example, Ba2ZnSi2O7 (*ε*r = 8.09, *Q×f* = 26,600 GHz, and *τ*f = −51.4 ppm/°C), Sr2MgSi2O7 (*ε*r = 8.3, *Q×f* = 55,000 GHz, and *τ*f = −48 ppm/°C), and BaCo2Si2O7 (*ε*r = 9.26, *Q×f* = 31,135 GHz, and *τ*f = −92.1 ppm/°C) have been reported previously [26,52,56]. Additionally, ceramics such as SrY2Si3O10 (*ε*r = 9.3, *Q×f* = 64,100 GHz, and *τ*f = −31 ppm/°C) and Li4SrCaSi2O8 (*ε*r = 8.2, *Q×f* = 90,094 GHz, and *τ*f = −79.6 ppm/°C) were synthesized. These microwave ceramics exhibited low relative dielectric constants [24,25]. However, although the dielectric constant of aforementioned microwave ceramics can meet the requirements for high-speed information transmission, the other two parameters are not ideal: either the quality factor is not high enough or the frequency temperature coefficient is too large. In addition, low-dielectric materials in the THz band are relatively rare. Therefore, further exploration and innovation are still needed to find silicate microwave ceramics with excellent performance.

In the exploration of silicate microwave ceramics, a large number of barium-based silicate microwave dielectric ceramics were listed by Du et al*.* [28], who reported that the dielectric properties of other ceramics were not ideal except for those of barium-based silicate ceramics containing rare earth elements. Subsequently, various barium-based silicate ceramics containing rare earth elements were studied. It was found that BaYb2Si3O10 had a higher *Q×f* value (*Q×f* = 95,601 GHz) than other microwave ceramics. The introduction of the rare earth element Yb greatly improved the properties of microwave dielectric ceramics. This property inspired the exploration of the microwave dielectric properties of Yb-based silicate ceramics.

Rare earth disilicate (RE2Si2O7) was widely studied because of its broad range of technical applications [29,72]. The structural characteristics of rare earth elements were attributed to the gradual filling of the 4f electron shells from lanthanum (La) to lutetium (Lu). These 4f electrons are tightly surrounded by the atomic nucleus, resulting in a weak shielding effect on the outer electrons from the nuclear charge [30]. As a result, RE cations are highly polarized and positively charged and form strong chemical bonds with ligands such as O2− or F− [31]. Among them, Yb2Si2O7 (YSO) was identified as a polycrystalline material that transformed from the α-phase to a stable β-phase at high temperatures. Fan *et al*. [32] promoted this phase transition by adding sintering aids, but it was found that this significantly reduced its dielectric properties, which cannot meet the demands of high-speed signal transmission. Therefore, based on the above information, optimization of the preparation process of Yb2Si2O7 to obtain stable β-phase ceramics and further exploration of its dielectric response in the microwave and terahertz bands were deemed necessary.

In this work, we reported the facile synthesis of Yb2Si2O7 and its dielectric responses in the GHz and THz bands. In addition, we conducted a systematic investigation on the correlations among the microstructure, chemical bonds, and vibration modes influencing the dielectric properties of Yb2Si2O7. Yb2Si2O7 ceramics sintered at 1500 °C exhibited outstanding microwave dielectric properties, featuring *ε*r = 7.57, *Q×f* = 78,645 GHz, and *τ*f = –13.5 ppm/°C. Similarly, the dielectric performance in the terahertz band was also outstanding (*ε*r = 8.13, *Q×f* = 112,758 GHz). Furthermore, a high bending strength (*σ* = 164.8 MPa) and a low thermal expansion coefficient were observed, indicating strong potential for practical applications [30]. The impressive dielectric performance in both the GHz and THz bands was observed, indicating that Yb2Si2O7 should be considered a promising candidate for next-generation wireless terahertz mobile communication systems. This work would pave the way for exploring dielectric ceramics for the next generation of wireless communication.

## 2 Experimental

YSO ceramics were synthesized using high-purity SiO2 (99.99%, Aladdin, China) and Yb2O3 (99.99%, Aladdin, China) as starting materials via the conventional solid-state method. The materials were weighed proportionally and subjected to ball milling in a nylon container for 12 h, using ethanol as the solvent and zirconia beads as the milling media. Afterward, the mixed slurry was dried in an oven for 8 h, and the resulting powder was calcined at 1300 °C in a muffle furnace. Subsequently, 9 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA; Aladdin, China) was added for grinding, and the powder mixture was then pressed into cylindrical forms with dimensions of 6 mm in height and 15 mm in diameter. The ceramic samples were obtained by sintering the cylinders at 1450–1550 °C. The sintered samples were finally polished and thermally etched for 0.5 h at 80 °C below the sintering temperature.

The crystal structure of the ceramic samples was systematically studied by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; SmartLab, Rigaku, Japan) and in-depth processing with GSAS software. The experiment employed a copper target as the radiation source with a tube voltage of 30 kV and a tube current of 40 mA. The scanning speed of 1.8 (°)·min−1 and step angle of 10°–70° were utilized to ensure precise data acquisition. The crystal structure was further verified using a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2 F20 S-Twin TMP, USA) operated at 200 kV. Additionally, the mechanical properties of the samples were tested using an universal testing machine (CMT6104, MTS, China). The density of the samples was measured using the Archimedes drainage method to ensure accuracy. The vibration modes of the lattice and their influence on the dielectric properties were explored in detail through Raman spectroscopy (200–1000 cm−1). Moreover, the microstructural characteristics of the samples were observed in detail by a scanning electron microscope (JSM-6010, JEOL, Japan). The dielectric response of YSO ceramics in the terahertz band was studied by a terahertz time-domain spectroscopy (THz-TDS) system (Z3, Zomega Terahertz Co., USA). Additionally, the microwave dielectric properties of YSO were evaluated by exciting the TE011 resonance mode and employing the Hakki–Coleman dielectric resonance technique [60]. ## 3 Results and discussion

The XRD results of the YSO ceramics sintered at 1450–1550 °C are presented in Fig. 1. Compared with the standard card (PDF#00-025-1345), the main phase β-Yb2Si2O7 was determined to have a monoclinic structure with a space group of *C*2/*m*. In addition, weak secondary peaks corresponding to α-Yb2Si2O7 were observed in the XRD patterns of the samples sintered at 1450–1475 °C. To further investigate the crystal structure, the XRD data were refined using GSAS software. A very small amount of α-Yb2Si2O7 phase (0.66–0.84 wt%) was present in the samples sintered in the temperature range of 1450–1475 °C, indicating that a small portion of the α phase in the YSO ceramics had not yet transformed into the β phase. The refined spectra are shown in Fig. 2. Table 1 provides a comprehensive list of the parameters derived from the refinement. Notably, all *R* factors (Reliability factors, such as *R*Wp and *R*p) in Table 1 were less than 6%, and the goodness-of-fit (*χ*2) values were refined to be close to 2%, confirming the reliability of the data and the stability of the crystal structure [33]. Furthermore, the concentration of α-Yb2Si2O7 phase was calculated to be as low as 0.66–0.84 wt%, which had a limited influence on the dielectric response of YSO ceramics. As shown in Fig. 1(b), the diffraction peaks of YSO initially shifted towards higher angles with increasing sintering temperature, followed by a shift toward lower angles. These shifts in the diffraction peaks were due to changes in the cell volume and a high correlation with the displacement of the Raman peak [64,67,68]. In addition, as shown in Table 1, as the sintering temperature increased, the cell volume of the sintered sample first decreased from 277.64 to 276.75 Å3 and then increased to 277.69 Å3. This change was attributed to the variation in the interplanar spacing with increasing sintering temperature [58,63]. As shown in Fig. 3, when the sintering temperature was changed, the distance between crystal planes with the same crystal plane index in the high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images of the sintered samples was slightly different, which corresponded to the change in cell volume. Similar variation patterns have also been reported in other ceramic systems, such as Ca2LaNbO6, Ca3Sc2Ge3O12, and Sr2V2O7 [64−66].

To study its sintering characteristics, the microstructure of YSO ceramics was observed after thermal etching, as illustrated in Fig. 6. When the sintering temperature was increased from 1450 to 1500 °C, the average grain diameter of the sintered sample increased from 3.6 to 7.1 μm, resulting in a dense microstructure, and the maximum relative density reached 96%. However, when the sintering temperature exceeded 1500 °C, the grain size continued to increase, but the abnormal growth of the grains led to deterioration of the microstructure and a decrease in density. The average grain size of the sample sintered at 1550 °C increased to 8.45 μm, and the corresponding relative density decreased to 92.7%. This typical sintering characteristic is common in most microwave ceramic materials [33−35]. Notably, the variation in relative density showed a similar trend to that of the Q×f* value, as depicted in Fig. 7. Therefore, the microscopic characteristics of the samples changed with the variation in the sintering temperature, reflecting changes in the relative density and being closely related to the *Q×f* value [36].

It is widely recognized that several factors commonly influence the dielectric properties of ceramics. In addition to the external factors (density, impurities, etc.) embodied in the sintering process, there are also some internal factors (lattice vibration, filling fraction, etc.) [37]. Kim *et al*. [38] found a strong correlation between the packing fraction and the *Q×f* value, as the packing fraction corresponds to the strength of the lattice vibration. Therefore, the packing fraction at different sintering temperatures was calculated using Eq. (2) [39]:

(2) Packing fraction(%)=Volume of the atoms in the cell×ZVolumeoftheunitcell

The *Z* value for YSO was 2, with the corresponding results displayed in Fig. 7(b). With increasing sintering temperature, the packing fraction increased steadily to a maximum value of 57.36% at 1500 °C. Subsequently, as the temperature continued to increase, the packing fraction began to decrease. According to Eq. (2), the packing fraction was inversely proportional to the cell volume. The cell volume of the sample initially decreased and then increased with increasing sintering temperature, leading to an increased packing fraction first and a subsequently decreased packing fraction. Notably, the change trend of the relative density was the same as that of the *Q×f* value. Therefore, during the sintering process, the packing fraction and relative density affected the *Q×f* value together.

Figure 8(a) shows the Raman spectra of the samples sintered at different temperatures. All the vibration peaks observed from the Raman spectrum were almost identical, indicating that the change in sintering temperature had a limited effect on the crystal structure, thereby demonstrating good structural stability [33]. The Raman spectra were fitted using Gaussian and Lorentz methods, and 11 Raman modes were identified. According to the lattice vibration theory, the observed Raman peaks were as follows [72]: in the range of 277–522 cm−1, the characteristic peak indicated the O–Yb–O bending mode, accompanied by a slight distortion of the [SiO4] tetrahedron. The band near 668 cm−1 corresponded to the O–Si–O mode vibration, primarily manifested by the mutual movement of two Si atoms in the [SiO4] tetrahedron, while the terminal oxygen atoms moved in the opposite direction. In addition, the strong peak at 922 cm−1 was due to the movement of two Si atoms along another Si–O bond, vibrating in opposite directions. The vibration characteristic of the Raman peak at 954 cm−1 was that the two Si atoms moved toward each other, while the terminal oxygen ions moved outward [40]. The results are shown in Fig. 8(b). Notably, as the temperature increased, the highest Raman peak shifted slightly. As shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), the Raman peak at 922 cm−1 initially blue-shifted from 922 cm−1 for samples sintered at 1450 °C to 924 cm−1 for samples sintered at 1500 °C, which was highly correlated with a decrease in cell volume [67]. Afterwards, the Raman peak began to redshift from 924 cm−1 for samples sintered at 1500 °C to 923 cm−1 for samples sintered at 1550 °C, corresponding to an increase in cell volume [68]. As the volume decreased, the blue-shift occurred because more energy was required for lattice vibrations, and vice versa for the red-shift. This was consistent with findings in many other studies [5,21,22]. Moreover, the correlation between the full width at half maximum (FWHM) value of the Raman active mode and the damping coefficient (*γ*) can indicate the intrinsic loss (tan*δ*) due to anharmonic vibration. Figure 9(a) depicts the change in *ε*r for YSO with increasing sintering temperature. As the sintering temperature rose, *ε*r initially increased and then decreased, which closely resembled the change in relative density shown in Fig. 6(a). Therefore, there was a close relationship between the relative density and *ε*r. The greater the sample’s densification was, the higher its dielectric constant was. Additionally, *ε*r is typically influenced by porosity and polarizability [43]. The significant correlation between porosity and density strongly influences *ε*r. Consequently, Eq. (5) was used to correct the sample’s *ε*r [44]:

The correction value of *ε*r, denoted as *ε*corr, was calculated based on the measured value *ε*r and porosity *p*. The calculated results are depicted in Fig. 9(a), and the *ε*corr was observed to fluctuate within the range of 7.72–8.4. As the sintering temperature increased from 1450 to 1500 °C, the porosity decreased, and *ε*corr gradually approached the measured value. When the sintering temperature was 1500 °C, the *ε*corr of the sample was determined to be 8.05, which was close to the measured value of 7.57. As the sintering temperature further increased, an increase in porosity was caused by the overall sintering process, leading to a difference between the *ε*corr and the measured *ε*r value [39]. The *α*(Yb3+), *α*(Si4+), and *α*(O2−) values were 3.58, 0.87, and 2.01 Å3, respectively. *V*m represents the molar volume of the original unit cell of YSO ceramics. Interestingly, *ε*theo of the sample sintered at 1500 °C was found to be 7.83, which was almost the same as the measured value. This demonstrates that ionic polarizability plays a key role in the determination of *ε*r [46]. Figure 9(b) shows the variation in *τ*f of YSO ceramics with respect to the sintering temperature. The *τ*f values of YSO ceramics varied slightly, ranging from −17.8 to −13.5 ppm/°C. This indicated that *τ*f was less influenced by the sintering temperature.

Numerous studies indicate that intrinsic factors, including *f*i, *U*b, and *E*b, play crucial roles in determining microwave dielectric properties [47−49]. Hence, the correlation between the chemical bond characteristics and microwave dielectric properties of YSO ceramics was studied through complex chemical bond theory. The crystal structure of YSO ceramics was divided into Eq. (8) according to the Phillips–van Vechten–Levine (P–V–L) theory:

(8) Yb2Si2O7=Yb(1)3/2O(2)4/3+Yb(1)2/3O(3)4/31+Yb(1)2/3O(3)4/32+Si(1)1/2O(1)+Si(1)1/2O(2)2/3+Si(1)O(3)4/3

Table S1 in the ESM presents the *U*b and *E*b values of samples sintered at 1450–1550 °C. Generally, a higher *U*b indicates increased crystal stability and reduced loss from anharmonic vibrations, resulting in a higher *Q×f* value [75]. As shown in Table S1 in the ESM, the *U*b and *Q×f* values exhibited the same trend with increasing temperature, which is consistent with previous research findings [33]. Additionally, *τ*f is associated primarily with the bond energy (*E*b) formed by chemical bonds and electronegativity [76]. The results in Table S1 in the ESM indicate that a higher *E*b value corresponds to a *τ*f value closer to zero. The *f*i, *E*b, and *U*b values of YSO ceramics sintered at 1500 °C are listed in Table 2, and the relevant calculation equations are presented in the ESM. Based on the data in Table 2, Fig. 10(a) presents a statistical analysis on the contributions of various chemical bonds to the *f*i value. The results show that the contributions of the Yb–O bond and Si–O bond to *f*i were 64.94% and 35.06%, respectively. Therefore, the Yb–O bond played a more significant role in determining the *ε*r value. The percentage of *U*b in each chemical bond in YSO ceramics is shown in Fig. 10(c). Notably, the *U*b percentage of the Si–O bond (67.28%) was considerably higher than that of the Yb–O bond (32.72%). This indicated that the Si‒O bond significantly impacted the *Q×f* value. Furthermore, the results in Fig. 10(b) show that the Si‒O bond accounted for a higher proportion (64.96%) of the contribution to *E*b. It could be inferred that *τ*f was more affected by the Si–O bond.

## 4 Conclusions

YSO ceramic materials with a low dielectric constant and low loss were reported and synthesized via facile solid-state reaction technology. XRD and TEM analysis confirmed that the YSO ceramics were crystallized in a monoclinic structure with *C*2/*m* space group throughout the entire sintering temperature range. A dense microstructure was observed in the ceramics, with a relative density of 96% for the sample sintered at 1500 °C. Moreover, it exhibited the best microwave dielectric properties: *ε*r = 7.57, *Q×f* = 78,645 GHz (*f* = 13.5 GHz), and *τ*f = −13.5 ppm/°C. As for the dielectric response in the GHz band, the dielectric constant was influenced by the relative density and ion polarizability, while the increase in the *Q×f* value relied on increasing the relative density and packing fraction. In addition, Raman analysis indicated the dominance of the SiO4 tetrahedron in the Raman vibrations. The FWHM of the strong peak at 922 cm−1 was inversely proportional to the *Q×f* value. Based on the analysis of P-V-L theory, the Yb‒O bond plays a leading role in the *ε*r value of YSO. The Si–O bond predominantly influenced *U*b and *E*b, exerting a greater impact on the *Q×f* value and *τ*f value. This finding was consistent with the results from the Raman analysis. As for the dielectric response in the THz band, excellent dielectric properties of YSO ceramics at 1 THz were exhibited: *ε*r = 8.13, and *Q×f* = 112,758 GHz.
