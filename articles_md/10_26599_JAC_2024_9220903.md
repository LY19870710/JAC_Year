## 1 Introduction

Ceramic matrix composites (CMCs), specifically SiCf/SiC composites, are emerging as promising substitutes for nickel-based alloys in aeroengine hot-end components due to their superior mechanical properties, low density, and remarkable high-temperature stability [1,2]. However, their susceptibility to calcium–magnesium–alumino–silicate (CMAS) corrosion and water vapor reduction in combustion settings restricts broader application [3,4]. A solution to this issue is the applications of environmental barrier coatings (EBCs) on SiCf/SiC composites [5−9].

Globally, rare earth silicates (RE2SiO5 and RE2Si2O7) are preferred for use as environmental barrier coatings [10−13]. The thermal expansion coefficient (CTE) of β-type rare earth pyrosilicates (Yb2Si2O7, Lu2Si2O7, and Sc2Si2O7) closely aligns with that of CMCs and the Si bonding layer, offering excellent thermal shock resistance [14,15]. However, these pyrosilicates exhibit inadequate corrosion resistance. For example, Lu2Si2O7 and Sc2Si2O7 do not produce an apatite product in the CMAS corrosion process, permitting direct penetration of CMAS molten salt along the grain boundaries [16,17]. Yb2Si2O7 struggles to form a dense, continuous product layer in reaction with CMAS, allowing inward erosion by the corrosive medium through nondense layers [18]. Conversely, X2-type RE monosilicates (X2-RE2SiO5) exhibit superior corrosion resistance [19,20], making them another preferred choice for EBCs on SiCf/SiC composites. The product layer thickness of various monosilicates corroded by CMAS at 1300 °C is less than 100 µm [19]. However, CTE of most X2-type monosilicates, approximately (6.0–7.8)×10−6 K−1 at 1400 °C [21], does not match CTE of SiCf/SiC CMCs ((4.5–5.9)×10−6 K−1) [22], leading to thermal stress mismatches between the coating and the substrate and potentially causing coating spalling [23].

To develop silicate materials with both appropriate CTE and strong resistance to CMAS corrosion, the strategy of crafting multiphase silicates (RE2SiO5/RE2Si2O7) has been identified as a viable method. The water vapor corrosion resistance [10], CMAS corrosion resistance [24], and thermophysical properties [25] of Yb2SiO5/Yb2Si2O7 multiphase silicate coatings have been systematically investigated by Zhong *et* *al*. Their findings confirmed that multiphase silicates can effectively synthesize the advantageous properties of Yb2Si2O7 and Yb2SiO5. Although there are numerous design solutions available for rare earth element components in monosilicates [26−30] and pyrosilicates [31−34], the quest for achieving optimal performance in multiphase silicates through component design is still unclear. For pyrosilicates to adopt a β-phase and monosilicates to an X2-type phase, thereby ensuring an appropriate CTE, the selection of rare earth elements with smaller ionic radii is crucial [35,36]. Furthermore, to decrease thermal conductivity, incorporating multiple elements is essential to increase compositional complexity [37−39]. Achieving optimal CMAS corrosion resistance necessitates limiting the average ionic radius of rare earth elements within a certain range [40]. It is vital to balance the ion radii, avoid sizes that are too small, which could enable direct CMAS penetration, and avoid excessively large ion radii, which may result in an overly thick corrosion layer.

In this research, a meticulous selection of four rare-earth elements from the lanthanide series was conducted, focusing on those with the smallest ionic radii. Starting with Lu, elements with progressively closer ionic radii were incorporated, enhancing the variety of elements from one to four. This results in small chemical bond lengths and atomic spacings, which play a crucial role in achieving low CTE for the silicates [35]. Additionally, the introduction of point defects by multiple elements can reduce the thermal conductivity. The average radius of the rare earth ions is also tightly controlled within a specific range to optimize the corrosion resistance of the CMAS. Consequently, four distinct multiphase silicates were synthesized: Lu2SiO5/Lu2Si2O7, (Lu1/2Yb1/2)2SiO5/(Lu1/2Yb1/2)2Si2O7, (Lu1/3Yb1/3Tm1/3)2SiO5/(Lu1/3Yb1/3Tm1/3)2Si2O7, and (Lu1/4Yb1/4Tm1/4Er1/4)2SiO5/(Lu1/4Yb1/4Tm1/4Er1/4)2Si2O7, with the general formula (*n*RE1/*n*)2SiO5/(*n*RE1/*n*)2Si2O7 (*n* = 1, 2, 3, and 4), using the solid-phase method. The phase structures, thermal expansion coefficients, thermal conductivities, and especially CMAS corrosion resistances of the materials were tested and analyzed. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) was used to observe the interface between the corrosion product layer and the silicate block, and a reaction–diffusion model was proposed to explain the corrosion mechanism of CMAS on silicates. The findings of this study provide a crucial foundation for the development of environmental barrier coatings suitable for use in extreme environments.

### 2.1 Synthesis of materials

Four multiphase ceramics with the general formula (*n*RE1/*n*)2SiO5/(*n*RE1/*n*)2Si2O7, where RE represents Lu, Yb, Tm, or Er, were synthesized using a solid-phase method. Starting materials included Lu2O3, Yb2O3, Tm2O3, Er2O3 (99.99%, *d*50 = 5±2 μm, Shanghai Heli Rare Earth Material Co., Ltd., China), and SiO2 (99.99%, *d*50 = 27.91 μm, Slnopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) powders. Each RE2O3 was used in equal molar ratios, with the combined RE2O3 to SiO2 molar ratio set at 1.5 : 1. The chemical reaction formula is shown in Eq. (1):

(1) 3RE2O3+2SiO2=RE2SiO5+RE2Si2O7

The powders were uniformly mixed in a vertical nylon tank using a ball mill (QM-3SP4, Nanjing Nanda Instrument Co., Ltd., China), employing ethanol and zirconia balls as the dispersion media. The resulting slurry was dried at 120 °C for 5 h and subsequently sieved. The sieved powders were placed in circular molds (15 and 20 mm in diameter) and square molds (5 mm × 25 mm) and cold-pressed for 20 s at 200 MPa. Following cold pressing, the green billets were sintered in a muffle furnace (JXR1800-20, Shanghai Dingpu Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., China), first at 1500 °C for 5 h and then at 1700 °C for 10 h, to create multiphase ceramic blocks. CMAS powders with a molar ratio of Ca33Mg10Al13Si44 were synthesized via the solid-phase method utilizing CaO, MgO, Al2O3, and SiO2 as the starting materials. Following the stoichiometric ratio, the mixture was thoroughly blended in a vertical nylon tank using ball milling for 4 h. The resulting slurry was dried at 120 °C and sieved through a 20-mesh sieve. The mixed powders were subsequently heated to 1400 °C for 4 h in a muffle furnace, followed by gradual cooling to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min. The samples were then allowed to cool to room temperature, resulting in the formation of a CMAS block. This block was subsequently ground to obtain CMAS powders.

### 2.2 CMAS corrosion

The milled CMAS powders were combined with ethyl alcohol before being uniformly applied to the surface of the blocks. CMAS was maintained at approximately 35 mg/cm2 through repeated coating and drying cycles. These CMAS-coated blocks were then placed in a muffle furnace under controlled conditions and maintained at 1400 °C for 50 h and at 1500 °C for both 10 and 50 h, respectively.

### 2.3 Characterizations

For phase composition analysis of the four types of multicomponent RE2SiO5/RE2Si2O7 multiphase ceramic blocks before and after corrosion, an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany) was employed. The X-ray power utilized was 40 kV and 40 mA, and a 0.6 mm evanescent slit was used for data collection. The data were recorded over a 2*θ* range of 10°–110° at a scanning rate of 0.8 (°)/min and from 10° to 80° at a rate of 10 (°)/min. The XRD data were processed using the FullProf 2023 software to determine the lattice parameters and atomic occupancy of the four types of multiphase silicates. A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Magellan 400, FEI, USA) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS) and an EBSD attachment was used for the comprehensive analysis of the micromorphology, chemical composition, and orientation information of the ceramic blocks before and after corrosion. The microstructure of the (Lu1/4Yb1/4Tm1/4Er1/4)2SiO5/(Lu1/4Yb1/4Tm1/4Er1/4)2Si2O7 block was examined using a transmission electron microscope (TEM; HF5000, Hitachi, Japan). CTEs of the four types of multiphase ceramics were measured using a thermal dilatometer (DIL 402SE, NETZSCH, Germany) over a temperature range of 100 to 1400 °C with a controlled heating rate of 10 °C/min. CTEs were calculated using Eq. (2) [36]:

(2) CTE=ΔL/LΔT

where  L,  ΔL, and  ΔT denote the sample length at room temperature, the change in length, and the temperature difference, respectively.

The experimental density ( ρexp) was measured using the Archimedes drainage method. The theoretical density ( ρtheo) was calculated using lattice parameters and atomic occupancy obtained from Rietveld refinement results. The relative density and fractional porosity ( φ) were then determined based on the values of  ρexp and  ρtheo (Eq. (3)) [37]:

(3) φ=1−ρexp/ρtheo

The thermal diffusivity ( α) of the samples was assessed across a temperature range of 25–1000 °C using a laser thermal conductivity meter (LFA467, NETZSCH, Germany). The heat capacity ( cp) of the four multiphase ceramics was determined through a high-temperature specific heat tester (MHTC96, SETARAM, France). The experimental thermal conductivity ( k) of the samples was then calculated based on (Eq. (4)) [37]:

(4) k=αcpρ

The thermal conductivity of a 100% dense sample ( k0) can be estimated using Eq. (5) [37] to reduce the impact of porosity on the measured thermal conductivity ( k).

(5) kk0=1−4φ/3

The thermal diffusivity,  α, can be computed using Eq. (7) as per the Debye model [36,41−43].

(7) α=13vmΛ

where  vm and  Λ denote the mean sound velocity and the mean phonon free path, respectively. As  vm remains largely unaffected by the temperature, the variation in the thermal diffusivity primarily stems from the temperature dependence,  Λ. The average  Λ is calculated through various phonon scattering mechanisms, as demonstrated in Eq. (8) [38,43,44]:

(8) 1Λ=1Λphonon+1Λdefect+1Λboundary+∑i1Λi

where  Λphonon,  Λdefect,  Λboundary, and  Λi represent the mean phonon free paths related to intrinsic lattice scattering due to Umklapp phonon‒phonon interactions, defects, grain boundaries, and other factors, respectively.  Λdefect,  Λboundary, and  Λi demonstrate minimal dependence on the temperature [45−47]. Consequently, the temperature variation in  Λ is primarily influenced by  Λphonon at different temperature levels. In Eq. (9),  Λphonon characterizes the anharmonic Umklapp phonon‒phonon scattering, which elucidates intrinsic thermal conduction. As  vm exhibits almost no temperature dependence, the linear relationship between  α and  Λ has been adjusted using Eq. (9) [36,37]:

(9) 1α≈1Λ≈Cexp(θDbTn1/3)+D=(bCn1/3θD)T+(D−12C)

where  θD denotes the Debye temperature;  b is a constant;  n denotes the number of atoms per primitive cell. C* and *D* are two temperature independent parameters. The linear relationship described in Eq. (9) between the inverse thermal diffusivity ( α−1) and the temperature ( T) remains valid when both intrinsic and extrinsic phonon scattering mechanisms are present. By linearly fitting the values of inverse thermal diffusivity ( α−1) in the temperature range of 25–800 °C (Fig. 6(e)), it was observed that  α−1 for (2RE1/2)2SiO5/(2RE1/2)2Si2O7 and (4RE1/4)2SiO5/(4RE1/4)2Si2O7 at 1000 °C were lower than the fitted curve. This difference may be attributed to increased heat conduction at higher temperatures due to thermal radiation effects. However, (1RE1/1)2SiO5/(1RE1/1)2Si2O7 and (3RE1/3)2SiO5/(3RE1/3)2Si2O7 were minimally affected by thermal radiation. To mitigate the influence of thermal radiation, intrinsic  α values were derived from the fitted values of the inverse thermal diffusivity ( α−1) (Fig. 6(f)). Subsequently, the intrinsic lattice thermal conductivity for various samples was calculated, as illustrated in Figs. 6(g) and 6(h). Notably, the intrinsic thermal conductivity gradually decreased with increasing the temperature and exhibited a decreasing trend in tandem with increasing solid solution element concentration. According to Eq. (9), the slope of the fitted inverse thermal diffusion coefficient curve reflects the intrinsic thermal resistance of the Umklapp phonon‒phonon scattering process. As the slope increases, the scattering of phonons becomes stronger. The decrease in the slope with an increase in the type of dopant (Fig. 6(e)) suggests a gradual reduction in the degree of phonon‒phonon scattering. This reduction is related to the fact that heavier elements tend to cause stronger scattering. Consequently, the decrease in the thermal diffusivity with an increasing number of elemental species in the low-temperature range is primarily attributed to the effect of extrinsic phonon scattering mechanisms.

Phonon scattering resulting from point defects is a significant component of extrinsic phonon scattering mechanisms. The introduction of local ion charge disorder, which occurs when multiple elements occupy the same position within a multicomponent sample with varying ionic radii and masses, is the primary cause of increased phonon scattering. Specifically, the impact of atomic weight and ionic size disorders on the mean phonon free path, denoted as  Λdefect, can be quantified using Eqs. (10) and (11) [36−38,48]. Equations (11) and (12) facilitate the determination of the influence of atomic weight and ionic size disorders on  Λdefect.

(10) 1Λdefect=a34πv4ω4c(ΔMM)2

(11) 1Λdefect=2ca3ω44πv4J4γ2(ΔRR)2

where  a3 represents the volume per atom;  v represents the transverse wave speed;  ω represents the phonon frequency;  c represents the defect concentration per atom;  J is a constant;  γ represents the Gruneisen parameter.  M represents the atomic mass;  R represents the ionic radius;  ΔM represents the atomic mass difference;  ΔR represents the ionic radius difference. High values of mass disorder ( g) and size disorder ( δ) are essential for reducing the thermal conductivity. The values of  g and  δ for a particular atomic occupancy can be calculated using Eqs. (12) and (13), respectively [37,49,50]:

(12) g=∑i=1nci(Mi−M¯M¯)2

(13) δ=∑i=1nci(Ri−R¯R¯)2

where  n represents the type of element,  i represents the  ith element,  c represents the molar content,  M¯ represents the average atomic mass, and  R¯ represents the average ionic radius. The values of mass disorder and size disorder for the four silicate types were computed using Eqs. (12) and (13) (Fig. 6(i)). As the number of elemental species increased, both the mass disorder and size disorder values exhibited an upward trend, consequently amplifying phonon scattering.

### 3.3 Thermal expansion coefficients

The thermal expansion rates and CTEs of multiple multiphase silicates were measured, as illustrated in Fig. 7. At 1400 °C, CTEs of the four silicates were approximately in the range of (5.26–5.55)×10−6 K−1. A linear relationship was established between the elemental species and the average ionic radius as variables, and the coefficients of thermal expansion of the four samples were measured at 400, 800, and 1200 °C (Figs. 7(c) and 7(d)). Generally, CTE increased with increasing elemental species, but this increase slowed with increasing the temperature. CTE is directly proportional to the atomic spacing in the lattice and inversely proportional to the lattice energy [51,52]. Elevated temperatures lead to an increase in atomic spacing, resulting in higher CTEs. Furthermore, when RE3+ ions with relatively large ionic radii are introduced into silicates, the atomic spacing of the silicates gradually increases, leading to a decrease in the lattice energy and a gradual increase in CTE. It is essential to match CTE of materials used for EBC with CTE of the CMC substrate to relieve thermal stress during thermal cycling. SiCf/SiC is the most commonly used ceramic matrix composite, and CTEs of all four samples closely align with CTE of SiC in a range of (4.5–5.9)×10−6 K−1 [22].

## 4 Discussion

Dissolution–reprecipitation is currently widely accepted as the mechanism for apatite product formation [19,53−56]. When exposed to molten CMAS, silicate particles dissolve to produce REO1.5 and SiO2, which subsequently diffuse into CMAS. This leads to the precipitation of Ca–RE–Si–O apatite crystals until the concentration of REO1.5 in CMAS reaches a saturation point. Consequently, differences in the corrosion resistance of silicates against CMAS are typically explained from a thermodynamic perspective, where the size of RE ions can significantly influence the overall energetics of the apatite structure. The general trend indicates that the enthalpy of apatite formation becomes more stable with an increase in the ionic radius of RE3+ ions [57]. Hence, meeting the necessary thermodynamic conditions for apatite formation from rare earth element (REEs) with small ionic radii is more challenging [16,20,40,58]. Numerous studies have also demonstrated that the thickness of the recession layer for RE silicates decreases nearly linearly as the ionic radius or average ionic radius of RE3+ ions decreases [19,40]. Furthermore, the differences in the compositions of various rare earth elements in apatite formed from multicomponent silicates have been observed, with higher contents of rare earth elements possessing larger ionic radii [16,20,40]. As depicted in Fig. 17(a), the contents of rare earth elements in apatite formed through the corrosion of the four multiphase silicates at 1400 °C for 50 h follow this pattern. The formation of apatite within CMAS may be associated with this mechanism, in which supersaturated elements precipitate to create apatite as the liquid-phase field contracts during cooling [59]. However, this mechanism does not adequately explain the phenomenon of apatite growth and extension over time. Once a corrosive layer forms, the contact between CMAS and silicate is obstructed, preventing the effective dissolution of silicate in CMAS.

Indeed, the corrosion of silicates by CMAS follows a typical reaction–diffusion process. As depicted in Figs. 18(a1) and 18(a2), when elements such as Ca and Si from CMAS diffuse into the silicate phase, driven by the chemical potential gradient, if the concentration of these diffused elements surpasses the solid-solution limit of the original silicate, an intermediate phase (apatite) forms in the surface layer of the silicate during the progression of diffusion. This results in abrupt changes in the elemental composition on both sides of the phase interface associated with the apatite phase. Subsequently, the elements from CMAS continue to diffuse into the silicate phase using apatite as a pathway, as illustrated in Fig. 18(b). Figure 9(a) shows the diffusion of Ca from CMAS along grain boundaries into the monosilicate phase. However, at this point, the solid-solution limit is not reached, and the phase transition does not occur. If the solubility limit of Ca in the silicate phase is reached, the transformation into apatite will continue (Fig. 18(b)). Diffusion initiates at grain boundaries due to the higher atomic energy present there. As illustrated in Fig. 18(c), the transition from silicate to apatite also progresses from the outermost layer inward. According to our EBSD and EDS results shown in Figs. 9−13, many apatites are typically encapsulated with small grains of monosilicates or pyrosilicates within them, which are silicates that have not undergone a complete phase transition. When CMAS and silicate are ideal diffusion couples with compositions independent of diffusion at both ends, the elements in CMAS will continuously diffuse downward due to the chemical potential gradient, leading to the infinite growth of apatite. However, in practical service environments and experiments, the amount of CMAS remains constant. As corrosion progresses, the Ca : Si, RE : Ca, and RE : Si ratios in CMAS change, as shown in Figs. 17(d)–17(f). Notably, the formation of apatite involves the continuous consumption of Ca, causing the Ca : Si ratio in CMAS to continuously decrease with apatite formation. The chemical equations for the reaction of RE monosilicates and pyrosilicates with CaO or SiO2 in CMAS to form apatite are as Eqs. (14) and (15):

Monosilicates and pyrosilicates do not exhibit significant reaction preferences when the Ca content is sufficient. Therefore, the chemical equation for the formation of apatite from multiphase silicates can be expressed as Eq. (16):

(16) 4RE2SiO5+4RE2Si2O7+4CaO(CMAS)=2Ca2RE8(SiO4)6O2

As a result, the involvement of Si from CMAS during the prereaction period is minimal. Previous research has indicated that when the Ca : Si ratio in CMAS reaches a certain critical value, the reaction to form apatite ceases, which is linked to the rare earth elements present in the silicate [40]. In our study, the thickness of apatite formed by (1RE1/1)2SiO5/(1RE1/1)2Si2O7 remained constant (Fig. 17(g)), which also suggested that its apatite-forming reaction reached a point of completion. Therefore, when the Ca content is insufficient, SiO2 participates in the reaction as Eq. (17):

(17) RE2SiO5+SiO2(CMAS)=RE2Si2O7

Consequently, a two-phase region composed of apatite and pyrosilicate forms beneath the apatite, as illustrated in Fig. 18(a3). After the formation of pyrosilicates from monosilicates with an insufficient Si content, the Ca content is insufficient to entirely transform all pyrosilicates into apatite. As shown in Figs. 10(a), 10(b), 11(g), 12(h), 16(b), and 16(c), a two-phase region comprising apatite and pyrosilicate exists between the apatite and silicate phases. Similarly, if the calcium content continues to decrease and CMAS does not penetrate the block, only elemental silicon undergoes downward diffusion to react with the monosilicates, forming pyrosilicates primarily in the corrosion layer (Fig. 16(a)), as demonstrated in Fig. 18(a4). The smaller the ionic radius of the rare earth element within the silicate is, the faster the aforementioned phenomenon occurs.

Therefore, when discussing CMAS corrosion resistance, it is essential to consider both thermodynamic and kinetic factors [57,60]. It is well known that rare earth elements with smaller ionic radii tend to diffuse more rapidly. Consequently, rare earth elements with small ionic radii may actually have a detrimental effect on a material’s resistance to CMAS corrosion, which contrasts with the conclusions drawn solely from thermodynamic factors. Additionally, we conducted a semiquantitative analysis of the rare earth element content in CMAS by EDS (Fig. 17(b)) and consistently found higher concentrations of REEs with small ionic radii. We performed a side-by-side comparison of the total RE content in residual CMAS and observed a decrease with an increase in the average rare-earth ionic radius of the silicate (Fig. 17(c)), clearly illustrating the influence of the ionic radius on the diffusion preference of REEs. Furthermore, the elements in the CMAS diffuse in the opposite direction to the rare earth elements, and the comparison of the residual CMAS thickness can reflect the rate of diffusion or penetration. This thickness tends to increase with increasing average ionic radius of the REEs in the silicate (Fig. 17(h)). Moreover, direct penetration of CMAS into (1RE1/1)2SiO5/(1RE1/1)2Si2O7 and (2RE1/2)2SiO5/(2RE1/2)2Si2O7 was observed at 1400 and 1500 °C (Figs. 9(a) and 15(b)), respectively, whereas (3RE1/3)2SiO5/(3RE1/3)2Si2O7 and (4RE1/4)2SiO5/(4RE1/4)2Si2O7 did not directly penetrate even at 1500 °C (Figs. 15(c) and 15(d)). Both observations suggest that CMAS is more prone to diffuse or penetrate silicates with smaller rare earth ionic radii.

To increase the thrust-to-weight ratio of engines, the next generation of coating materials will be utilized at increasingly high temperatures. As illustrated in Fig. 17(i), the viscosity of CMAS decreases with increasing temperature, indicating increased fluidity. Furthermore, the diffusion coefficient of elements follows Eq. (18) [61]:

(18) D=D0exp(−QRT)

where  D denotes the diffusion coefficient;  D0 denotes the diffusion constant;  Q denotes the diffuse activation energy;  R denotes the gas constant;  T denotes the thermodynamic temperature. The diffusion coefficient is faster at 1500 °C compared to that at 1400 °C. As the service temperature increases, kinetic factors will gradually come to the forefront and should therefore be taken into consideration when designing coating materials. The corrosion after 10 h at 1500 °C is more severe than the corrosion after 50 h at 1400 °C (Figs. 17(g) and 17(f)). The hindrance of diffusion by rare earth elements with large ionic radii plays a crucial role in the design of silicate materials, contrary to previous findings. It is imperative to prevent CMAS penetration into the coating materials and minimize its average ionic radius to enhance the enthalpy of apatite generation. For instance, in the case of prolonged corrosion (50 h), the thickness of the product layer is lower in (3RE1/3)2SiO5/(3RE1/3)2Si2O7 than in (4RE1/4)2SiO5/(4RE1/4)2Si2O7 when the CMAS penetration is absent (Fig. 17(g)).

At temperatures of 1400 and 1500 °C, CMAS directly penetrated the (1RE1/1)2SiO5/(1RE1/1)2Si2O7 and (2RE1/2)2SiO5/(2RE1/2)2Si2O7 blocks, respectively. In contrast, (3RE1/3)2SiO5/(3RE1/3)2Si2O7 and (4RE1/4)2SiO5/(4RE1/4)2Si2O7 exhibited remarkable CMAS corrosion resistance, showing no direct penetration even at 1500 °C. The corrosion layer thickness of several multiphase silicates was less than 100 μm after 50 h of corrosion at 1400 °C and less than 200 μm after 10 h of corrosion at 1500 °C. The corrosion layer thickness of this series of multiphase silicates falls essentially between that of monosilicates and pyrosilicates corroded under similar conditions [11,16,19,40]. These multiphase silicate materials, especially (3RE1/3)2SiO5/(3RE1/3)2Si2O7 and (4RE1/4)2SiO5/(4RE1/4)2Si2O7, demonstrate significant potential as environmental barrier coatings suitable for use in extreme environments.

## 5 Conclusions

In this study, four types of multiphase silicates were synthesized using the solid-phase method. The thermal conductivity and thermal expansion coefficient of these four silicates were investigated, and their CMAS corrosion resistance was tested at 1400 and 1500 °C. The key findings are as follows:

1) All four multiphase silicates contain both monosilicate and pyrosilicate phases, with monosilicates existing in the X2-type and pyrosilicates in the β-type. The content of both phases is close to a 1 : 1 ratio.

2) The thermal conductivity decreases as the component complexity increases in the low-temperature range due to the external phonon scattering mechanism. However, it gradually approaches a similar level in the high-temperature range for all four silicates due to the weakening of intrinsic phonon‒phonon scattering.

3) The thermal expansion coefficient of the four silicates gradually increases with increasing average ionic radius of the RE elements, which is attributed to a decrease in the lattice energy. Nevertheless, CTEs of all four silicates are compatible with ceramic matrix composites.

4) Direct penetration of CMAS into (1RE1/1)2SiO5/(1RE1/1)2Si2O7 and (2RE1/2)2SiO5/(2RE1/2)2Si2O7 was observed at 1400 and 1500 °C, respectively. In contrast, (3RE1/3)2SiO5/(3RE1/3)2Si2O7 and (4RE1/4)2SiO5/(4RE1/4)2Si2O7 did not directly penetrate even at 1500 °C, indicating superior CMAS corrosion resistance.

5) The corrosion mechanism of CMAS on silicates was explained using a reaction-diffusion model. It was proposed that rare earth elements with large ionic radii play a crucial role in resisting CMAS corrosion, especially as the temperature of aero-engines increases.
