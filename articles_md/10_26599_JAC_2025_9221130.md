## 1 Introduction

Tantalum carbide (TaC) is a representative member of the metal carbide family and a representative ultrahigh-temperature ceramic. It possesses a range of exceptional physical and chemical properties, including an extremely high melting point (~3880 °C), high hardness (> 15 GPa), excellent corrosion resistance, and strong thermal stability [1−5]. These features make TaC especially suitable for use in harsh environments, particularly in aerospace applications, where it is commonly used as a throat insert in rocket engine nozzles [6−9]. Moreover, TaC also has good electrical conductivity and stable performance at high temperatures [10,11]. The combination of covalent, ionic, and metallic bonds in TaC endows it with excellent stability, adjustable properties, and metal-like conductivity. Its ability to dissipate electromagnetic energy through dielectric loss is due mainly to its high conductivity, whereas its magnetic loss is related to the splitting of Ta atom d orbitals under electromagnetic fields [12,13]. However, the very high electrical conductivity of pure TaC can lead to impedance mismatch, which weakens its ability to absorb electromagnetic waves (EMWs) [14].

To address this limitation, a coshell structure with a Ta2O5 or C shell was employed in this study to reduce the electrical conductivity of TaC and improve impedance matching. This architecture allows the material to synergistically utilize its inherent dielectric and conductive losses for efficient EMW absorption, converting incident electromagnetic energy into thermal energy and mitigating electromagnetic pollution [15]. In addition, the one-dimensional structure of TaC nanorods enables them to act as nanoantennas, which enhances interfacial polarization and multiple reflections, thereby improving EMW absorption performance [16]. In aerospace engineering, a key challenge is the lack of material that can withstand extremely high temperatures while also absorbing EMWs efficiently. When the temperature exceeds 2800 °C, TaC undergoes oxidation to form viscous and dense Ta2O5. This oxidation product can uniformly cover the TaC surface, acting as a protective barrier by reducing the interfacial oxygen partial pressure and inhibiting further oxidation [17]. Therefore, one-dimensional TaC nanorods hold considerable promise for use in next-generation ultrahigh-temperature EMW-absorbing material. To date, various methods have been developed to synthesize TaC, such as carbothermal reduction [18,19], magnesiothermic reduction [20,21], the sol–gel method [22,23], self-propagating high-temperature synthesis (SHS) [24,25], and chemical vapor deposition (CVD) [26]. Among these methods, microwave-assisted synthesis stands out as a fast, energy-saving, and cost-effective method, showing strong potential for large-scale TaC production [27].

Compared with traditional heating methods such as tubular furnaces, which rely on heat transfer through conduction, convection, and radiation, microwave heating works differently. It uses high-frequency electromagnetic fields to directly heat the material through dielectric and magnetic losses. This leads to fast and uniform heating throughout the sample. When used in carbothermal reduction, microwave heating offers clear advantages: faster reaction rates, lower synthesis temperatures, shorter processing times, and improved material diffusion [28]. These benefits have led to wide interest in the use of microwave techniques for preparing advanced ceramic. For example, Zhang *et al*. [29] used microwave heating to make silicon carbide (SiC) powders and studied the effect of forming pressure on the resulting product. Huang and colleagues [30,31] prepared Al2O3–ZrO2 ceramic via microwave sintering and analyzed how the sintering temperature influenced their microstructure. Collectively, these studies highlight the energy efficiency, environmental sustainability, and enhanced reactivity of microwave processing, which enables reduced synthesis temperatures and accelerated reaction kinetics [32]. Notably, Hassine *et al*. [33] further confirmed the suitability of microwave methods for synthesizing TaC by exploring the influence of temperature on reaction outcomes. However, research on the controlled and scalable preparation of one-dimensional ceramic material via microwave techniques is still limited.

Herein, we propose a new and efficient strategy to synthesize one-dimensional TaC nanorods via a molten salt-assisted carbothermal reduction method under microwave heating. We systematically studied the effects of key parameters, such as the heating temperature and the molar ratios of the raw material, on the formation and morphology of the products. In particular, the synthesis was conducted without using a protective atmosphere to evaluate its effect on the phase composition and purity of the products. Finally, the electromagnetic wave absorption performance and related attenuation mechanisms of the obtained TaC nanorods were thoroughly investigated.

### 2.1 Raw material

Activated carbon (C, analytical grade, Henan Ruoweisheng Environmental Protection Materials Co., Ltd., China), tantalum pentoxide (Ta2O5, analytical grade, Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China), sodium chloride (NaCl, analytical grade, Tianjin Deen Reagent Co., Ltd., China), and nickel powder (Ni, analytical grade, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) were employed as precursor material in the synthesis of TaC nanorods.

### 2.2 Preparation of the precursor mixture

The synthesis conditions, including the molar ratios of the reactants, heating temperatures, and duration of thermal treatment, are outlined in Table 1. The precursor material was first manually ground in batches using an agate mortar for 30 min to achieve initial homogenization. This was followed by high-energy ball milling for an additional 30 min using a mixer/mill (SPEX SamplePrep 8000 M, Cole-Parmer, USA), facilitating enhanced dispersion of the constituents. The homogenized mixtures were subsequently loaded into alumina crucibles and subjected to microwave-assisted heating to initiate the synthesis of the resulting TaC.

### 2.3 Preparation of TaC nanorods

Microwave-assisted synthesis was carried out via a 915 MHz industrial microwave furnace (WY25L, Nanjing Sanle Microwave Equipment Co., Ltd., China). The heating rate, target temperature, and holding time were precisely controlled by adjusting the input power. Initially, the input power was set to 3 kW and then gradually increased by 0.2 kW per minute until it reached 5 kW. The temperature was monitored in real time via an infrared pyrometer, which was calibrated for accurate readings above 600 °C. To better understand the system’s behavior during heating, the input power, reflected power, and temperature were recorded every 30 s.

To improve the heating efficiency in the early low-temperature regime (≤ 800 °C), an additional heating setup was used. It consisted of four SiC rods (15 mm in diameter and 150 mm in height), which were arranged evenly around the crucible at 90° intervals. To reduce the temperature gradient and prevent damage to the crucible during rapid microwave heating, a two-layer crucible system made of alumina was used. The precursor material were placed inside a smaller inner crucible, which was then placed into a larger outer crucible filled with carbon powder. The carbon provided thermal insulation and protected the sample from oxidation. Before microwave exposure, the whole assembly was placed inside an insulating container. After the reaction, any remaining carbon was removed through heavy liquid separation, and high-purity TaC nanorods were obtained.

### 2.4 Characterization

The phase composition of the synthesized TaC samples was identified via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; SmartLab, Rigaku, Japan) using Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 0.15408 nm). Microstructural analysis was carried out via a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; JSM-IT800, JEOL, Japan). High-resolution microstructural characterization was further conducted via a spherical aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope (TEM; Spectra 200, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operated at 300 kV. The elemental composition, including oxygen (O), nitrogen (N), hydrogen (H), carbon (C), and sulfur (S), was quantified via an oxygen/nitrogen/hydrogen analyzer (EMGA-930, HORIBA, Japan) and a carbon/sulfur analyzer (EMIA-Pro, HORIBA, Japan). An X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific, USA), equipped with an Al Kα excitation source (*E* = 1486.68 eV), was employed to probe the chemical states and bonding environments. The electromagnetic parameters of the TaC samples were evaluated in the 2–18 GHz frequency range via a vector network analyzer (E5080B, Keysight Technologies, USA) to measure the complex permittivity and permeability, thereby enabling assessment of the EMW absorption behavior.

Reaction (2) begins at approximately 400 °C, and once the oxygen inside the crucible is completely consumed, excess carbon begins to react with CO2 above 700 °C, producing CO gas [35]. As the temperature increases, Ta2O5 reacts with NaCl, as described in Reactions (3) and (4), resulting in the formation of Na2Ta4O11 and gaseous species such as TaOCl3 and TaO2Cl.

Moreover, carbon is highly soluble in liquid nickel (Ni). Under microwave irradiation, the applied electromagnetic fields significantly enhance the diffusion rate of carbon atoms into the liquid Ni droplets. This rapid diffusion ensures a continuous carbon supply, which is essential for the growth of TaC nanorods [36,37]. As the reaction continues, tantalum- and carbon-containing intermediates accumulate within the Ni droplet. Once saturation is reached, TaC begins to nucleate and grow into rod-like structures under the catalytic effect of the Ni droplet (Reactions (5)–(7)). The gaseous byproducts generated in this process also play a role in promoting nanorod growth. When the supply of Ta or C in the Ni droplet becomes insufficient, the VLS process gradually stops, and nanorod growth terminates. Finally, the Ni droplet at the tip evaporates, leaving behind a fully formed TaC nanorod.

The phase composition and chemical analysis of the TaC nanorods are presented in Fig. 2. As shown in Fig. 2(a), XRD patterns confirmed the successful synthesis of TaC across all the tested molar ratios. However, at a reduced holding temperature of 1100 °C, a considerable amount of the Na2Ta4O11 intermediate phase appears, along with a notable presence of Ta2O5 (Fig. 2(b)). As the reaction temperature increases to 1200 °C, both Na2Ta4O11 and Ta2O5 are largely converted, leaving only faint residual peaks, indicating that elevated temperatures promote a more complete transformation into TaC. These findings suggest the critical influence of the reaction temperature on the phase purity and yield of TaC in the microwave-assisted synthesis process.

The elemental composition of the synthesized sample is shown in Fig. 2(c), which reveals minor amounts of residual oxygen and carbon in the nanorods. The presence of oxygen is likely attributed to partial oxidation during synthesis. This may lead to either the substitution of carbon atoms within the TaC lattice by oxygen or the dissolution of oxygen into the face-centered cubic (FCC) TaC structure [38,39].

To further investigate the surface chemistry, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted, as shown in Figs. 2(d)–2(f). The C 1s spectra exhibited a binding energy at 283.1 eV, which is indicative of C–Ta bonding. The O 1s peak at 530.3 eV corresponds to O–Ta bonds. In the Ta 4f spectra, the peaks at 26.8 and 28.7 eV are assigned to Ta–O (Ta 4f7/2 and Ta 4f5/2), confirming the presence of oxygen-bound tantalum [40−42]. These results indicate partial incorporation of oxygen into the TaC lattice, and the Ta2O5 peaks may arise from surface oxidation of TaC or incomplete conversion of residual Ta2O5 during synthesis.

The microscopic morphology of the TaC nanorods is presented in Fig. 3. Among the samples, the optimal nanorod formation is observed for the TaC-2 sample. The droplet-shaped tips shown in Fig. 3(b) support a VLS growth mechanism [19]. As shown in Fig. 3(a), reducing the molten salt content significantly suppresses nanorod formation under microwave heating because an insufficient liquid phase is required for VLS growth [43]. Initially, nanorods grow via the VLS pathway, but with extended reaction time, the product tends to become more granular. Conversely, excessive NaCl content (Fig. 3(c)) decreases the nanorod yield, likely due to increased system viscosity at high temperatures, which hinders nanorod elongation and reduces their aspect ratio.

The optimal molar ratio for nanorod production was identified as TaC-2. When the molten salt content in the raw material was reduced, microwave heating resulted in minimal nanorod formation (Fig. 3(a)). The droplet tip of the nanorods (Fig. 3(b)) suggests that the growth mechanism follows the VLS model [19]. The VLS mechanism requires a specific concentration of molten salt [43], and insufficient molten salt impedes the VLS process. Initially, nanorods can grow via the VLS mechanism; however, as growth continues, TaC transitions to a more granular form. Conversely, when the NaCl content is excessively high, the whisker yield decreases (Fig. 3(c)). This can be attributed to the alteration of the sample viscosity at elevated temperatures, which disrupts nanorod growth and reduces the aspect ratio.

To gain further insight into the internal structure, TEM and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping were performed on the TaC-2 sample. Figure 3(d) shows a cross-sectional view of a nanorod prepared via a focused ion beam (FIB). The crust-like morphology is highlighted in Fig. 3(e), whereas the EDS elemental maps (Figs. 3(g)–3(i)) reveal that the outer layer is primarily composed of carbon. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns are shown in Fig. 3(f). The SAED pattern taken along the [001] zone axis shows distinct diffraction spots corresponding to the (020), (002), and (022) planes, confirming the single-crystalline nature of the nanorods. The interplanar spacing of the (002) plane was measured to be 0.229 nm, deviating by only 2.6% from the theoretical value.

Furthermore, a thin oxide layer, approximately 4 nm thick, is observed on the outer layer of the nanorods (Fig. 3(f)). According to the results of the combined XPS and EDS analyses, this oxide layer likely formed during the cooling stage because of exposure to ambient oxygen. Moreover, some nanorods exhibit an ~8 nm thick carbon shell (Figs. 3(k) and 3(l)), which is attributed to the segregation of supersaturated carbon during nanorod growth [44,45]. This carbon layer acts as an effective diffusion barrier, preventing coarsening and oxidation while also suppressing interparticle sintering and agglomeration.

### 3.2 Microwave heating behavior analysis

The microwave heating profiles are presented in Fig. 4. The molten salt-assisted carbothermal reduction method, combined with the use of Ni particles as a catalyst, clearly achieves high microwave heating efficiency. The reaction temperature was increased from room temperature to 1300 °C within approximately 28 min. In the initial stage, from ambient temperature to 600 °C (achieved within 11 min), a fast temperature increase is observed. This rapid heating is attributed primarily to the metallic nature of the Ni particles, which reflect microwaves at lower temperatures, thereby suppressing microwave absorption and promoting the repeated reflection of incident waves. The uniformly dispersed Ni particles also increase the local electromagnetic field concentration and improve the coupling between microwaves and carbon, effectively accelerating the conversion of microwave energy into heat [46].

The microwave heating behavior varies with different NaCl contents. As the NaCl content increased, the time required to heat the samples from room temperature to 600 °C decreased. For example, the TaC-1 sample takes 14 min, whereas the TaC-2 and TaC-3 samples require only 11 min. This enhanced heating is attributed to the ionic nature and low melting point of NaCl, which allows it to absorb microwaves effectively and act as a thermal promoter during the heating process. The subsequent heating from 600 to 1300 °C also shows composition-dependent behavior. The TaC-2 sample has the shortest heating duration of 17 min, followed by TaC-1 (20 min) and TaC-3 (23 min). In this temperature range, heating is governed not only by microwave absorption by NaCl but also by its chemical interaction with Ta2O5. This reaction produces volatile tantalum-containing intermediates, which enhance local dielectric and plasma effects, thereby increasing microwave energy absorption and accelerating the increase in temperature.

When the NaCl content is too low, insufficient volatile Ta species are generated, resulting in reduced microwave absorption efficiency and prolonged heating. Conversely, as the NaCl content increases, more gaseous Ta intermediates are formed, which improves microwave‒plasma coupling and reduces the heating time. However, an excessive amount of NaCl may deteriorate the impedance matching of the reaction system, thereby diminishing microwave absorption and again increasing the overall heating time [47].

### 3.3 EMW absorption performance of TaC nanorods

The complex dielectric constant (*ε*r = * ε′ − j ε″) is a critical parameter for evaluating the EMW absorption performance of material [48]. The real part ( ε′) reflects the material’s ability to store EMW, the imaginary part ( ε″) indicates the material’s dissipative characteristics with respect to EMW, and j is the imaginary unit. The frequency-dependent variation in  ε′ for TaC nanorods is presented in Fig. 5(a). The  ε′ of the TaC-2 sample clearly has the highest ε*′ value, whereas TaC-1 has the lowest value. This trend corresponds to differences in the nanorod yield and aspect ratio. A higher yield and greater aspect ratio increase the specific surface area, which in turn enhances interfacial polarization, thereby contributing to a higher *ε*′ value for TaC-2 [49].

*
Fig. 5Electromagnetic parameters of TaC: (a) real part of the dielectric constant, (b) imaginary part of the dielectric constant, (c) tangent loss, and (d–f) Cole‒Cole semicircular curves of TaC-1, TaC-2, and TaC-3, respectively.

Similarly, the  ε″ values also increase with increasing nanorod yield and aspect ratio, as shown in Fig. 5(b). According to free electron theory [50],  ε″ can be approximated as  ε″ ~ 1/2ρfε*0π, where *ρ* is the resistivity, *f* is the frequency of the EMW, and *ε*0 is the dielectric constant in vacuum. The elongated and interconnected structure of the TaC nanorods facilitates the formation of conductive pathways, thereby lowering the resistivity and enhancing conductive losses. The overall dielectric loss capability of a material is commonly evaluated by the dielectric loss tangent, tan*δ**ε* = * ε″/ ε′, which is illustrated in Fig. 5(c) [51]. However, it is important to note that a higher tan δ**ε* does not always correspond to better EMW absorption. This is because high dielectric loss can impair impedance matching, which is critical for effective energy attenuation [49,52].

The polarization relaxation behavior of the material can be further explained through the Debye dipole relaxation mechanism [53].

(8) (ε′−εs+ε∞2)2+(ε″)2=(εs−ε∞2)2

The static dielectric constant (*ε*s) and the relative dielectric constant at the high-frequency limit (*ε*∞) are used to construct Cole‒Cole semicircles, which represent various polarization phenomena and conductive losses in the TaC nanorod/paraffin composite absorber.

Figures 5(d)–5(f) display the Cole‒Cole semicircles of the TaC/paraffin composites. The TaC nanorods exhibit four to five semicircles in the Cole‒Cole plot, indicative of multiple polarization mechanisms, including interfacial polarization, dipole polarization, defect-induced polarization, and conductive loss. Each semicircle corresponds to a different relaxation process. The presence of four to five semicircles indicates that multiple polarization mechanisms are active, including interfacial polarization, dipolar polarization, defect-related polarization, and conductive losses. Specifically, the TaC-1 sample has four semicircles, whereas TaC-2 and TaC-3 each have five semicircles, suggesting that the nanorod morphology enhances the interfacial polarization efficiency. Moreover, the short linear tail observed in the Cole‒Cole plots implies that, in addition to polarization, conductive loss also contributes to the dielectric behavior of TaC nanorods [54]. This multimechanism synergy underpins the superior dielectric properties and EMW absorption potential of the optimized nanorod-based samples.

To provide a more direct evaluation of the EMW absorption performance of TaC, the reflection loss of these composites can be calculated via transmission line theory [55], as described by Eq. (9):

(9) RL=20log|Zin−Z0Zin+Z0|

where the input impedance (*Z*in) and free-space impedance (*Z*0) characterize the properties of the absorber. A reflection loss (RL) value below −10 dB indicates that 90% of the incident EMW is absorbed, with a lower RL indicating superior EMW absorption performance of the material [56]. Another key metric is the effective absorption bandwidth (EAB), which is defined as the frequency range over which the RL remains below −10 dB. A wider EAB reflects broader spectral absorption capability. The variations in RL, absorber thickness, and impedance matching (*Z*) with frequency are presented in Fig. 6.

As the absorber thickness increases, the frequency at which the minimum RL occurs shifts toward lower frequencies, in accordance with the quarter-wavelength cancellation theory [57].

(10) tm=nλ4=nc(4fm|εr||μr|)n

where *t*m is the matching thickness, *n* = 1, 3, 5, ... *λ*, is the wavelength of the EMW, *c* is the speed of light, *f*m is the matching frequency, *ε*r is the complex permittivity, and *μ*r is the complex permeability. When the thickness corresponds to an odd multiple of one-quarter of the wavelength, a 180° phase shift occurs between the incident and reflected waves within the absorber, resulting in destructive interference at the air–absorber interface and enhanced absorption [58].

Impedance matching is another critical factor for achieving effective EMW absorption.

(11) Z=Zin/Z0=μrεrtanh(j2πfdμrεrc)

The electromagnetic frequency (*f*), absorber thickness (*d*), and speed of light in free space (*c*) are the key parameters influencing impedance matching. Impedance matching denotes the alignment of the material’s surface impedance with that of free space. Effective impedance matching ensures that more incident EMWs are absorbed rather than reflected. Ideal impedance matching occurs when the ratio of *Z*in to *Z*0 approaches 1, with studies suggesting that optimal impedance matching values typically fall between 0.8 and 1.2 [59].

As shown in Figs. 6(a)–6(c), the TaC-2 sample exhibited the best EMW absorption performance among the three samples owing to its high nanorod yield and large specific surface area, which promoted interfacial polarization and increased dielectric loss. At an absorber thickness of 1.0 mm, TaC-2 achieves a maximum effective absorption bandwidth (EABmax) of 3.0 GHz, along with an RLmin of −30.5 dB.

However, increasing the thickness leads to a narrower frequency range where the impedance matching ratio lies between 0.8 and 1.2, thereby reducing the EAB. In comparison, the TaC-3 sample, although still exhibiting a nanorod morphology, has a lower aspect ratio and lower surface area than TaC-2. As a result, it has a diminished EMW absorption capability. At a thickness of 1.1 mm, TaC-3 achieves an EABmax of 3.0 GHz and an RLmin of −27.5 (Figs. 6(d)–6(f)).

The TaC-1 sample, which contains only a small fraction of rod-like structures and predominantly irregular, interconnected particles, has the lowest specific surface area and thus has the weakest absorption performance. At a thickness of 1.5 mm, EABmax is reduced to 1.0 GHz, with an RLmin of −22.9 dB (Figs. 6(g)–6(i)).

Figures 7(a) and 7(b) display the 3D surface and corresponding 2D contour projection of the RL as a function of frequency and absorber thickness for the TaC-1 sample. At an optimal thickness of 1.5 mm, an EAB of 1.0 GHz is achieved with an RLmin of −22.9 dB.

Figures 7(c) and 7(d) illustrate the impedance matching behavior of TaC-1, with the impedance matching region (defined as |Z*in/*Z*0| ≈ 0.8–1.2) highlighted by dashed lines. The discontinuity observed in this region suggests localized impedance mismatches that hinder EMW attenuation. This is attributed to the high aspect ratio and large surface area of the TaC-2 nanorods, which facilitate polarization and impedance matching.

For the TaC-3 sample, the RL surface and contour maps (Figs. 7(i) and 7(j)) show that a comparable EAB of 3.0  GHz and RLmin of −27.5 dB are achieved at a thickness of 1.1 mm. This deviation is attributed to the reduced aspect ratio and specific surface area of the TaC-3 nanorods, which require increased material thickness to maintain effective impedance matching and maximize the absorption efficiency.

Owing to the inherent electrical conductivity of TaC [64], the interconnected nanorods form a three-dimensional conductive network, enabling continuous charge transport and promoting strong conductive loss. Polarization losses arise from various mechanisms, including dipolar, ionic, interfacial, and electronic polarizations. Among these, ionic and electronic contributions are negligible at frequencies below 100 GHz because of their limited response times [58,65]. Instead, dipolar relaxation—originating from intrinsic dipoles and impurity-induced dipoles (such as oxygen substitutions in the TaC lattice)—dominates, facilitating energy dissipation via Debye-type relaxation processes.

Moreover, the presence of surface Ta2O5 or carbon layers introduces abundant defects at heterointerfaces, which further enhance dipolar polarization [66]. Strong interfacial polarization also occurs at the carbon/TaC and Ta2O5/TaC interfaces in the core‒shell structure. These heterogeneous interfaces act as sites for charge accumulation, generating localized electric fields that intensify dielectric relaxation and improve energy conversion efficiency [67]. Therefore, the excellent impedance matching, combined with the synergistic effects of conductive loss and multiple polarization mechanisms, accounts for the superior EMW absorption performance observed in TaC nanorods.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, TaC nanorods were successfully synthesized via a molten salt-assisted carbothermal reduction method coupled with microwave heating. The effects of the synthesis temperature and precursor molar ratio on the phase composition, morphology, and EMW absorption properties were systematically investigated. One-dimensional TaC nanorods with high yield and structural uniformity were obtained under optimal conditions of Ta2O5 : C : NaCl : Ni = 1 : 8 : 2 : 0.08 at 1300  °C for 20 min. The resulting nanorods exhibited excellent electromagnetic wave absorption performance, with an RLmin of −30.5 dB and an EABmax of 3.0 GHz at a simulated absorber thickness of only 1.0 mm. Electron off-axis holography revealed localized charge accumulation at Ta2O5/TaC interfaces, indicating strong interfacial polarization. RCS simulations further confirmed significant attenuation of backscattered EMW signals for a 1.0 mm TaC-coated surface, highlighting the material’s practical potential for stealth and shielding applications. Mechanistic analysis attributed the enhanced EMW attenuation to the combined effects of conductive loss, interfacial polarization, dipolar relaxation, and defect-induced polarization. These findings demonstrate that microwave-assisted synthesis offers a scalable and energy-efficient strategy for fabricating high-performance, high-temperature EMW absorbing material based on TaC nanorods.
