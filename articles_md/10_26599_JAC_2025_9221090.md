## 1 Introduction

Ceramic coatings, characterized by their tailored microstructures, exceptional high-temperature stability, and engineered functionalities, have been widely explored for extreme environmental applications [1−5]. For example, Li *et al.* [4] demonstrated that refurbishing 4-tert-butylcatechol (TBCs) effectively mitigated oxidation-induced failure by suppressing mixed oxide layer growth and redirecting crack propagation pathways under 1100 °C cyclic conditions. Among them, the inert oxide SiO2 has been employed to improve the oxidation resistance of TiAl alloys [6−8], Nb alloys [9,10], Ni superalloys [11,12], steel [13,14], and carbon [15,16] because of its superior thermal stability and chemical inertness [7,17,18]. To protect TiAl alloys from oxidation attack, a variety of silicon-based coatings, such as pure SiO2 coatings [7,8,19], enamel coatings [20−22], and silicide coatings [23,24], have been developed by actively or passively forming SiO2. Conventional SiO2 coatings can be obtained by spraying [25], spinning [8], and immersion [6]. Although these pure SiO2 coatings can improve the oxidation resistance of TiAl alloys to some extent, they still suffer from inadequate adhesion, insufficient densification, and crack sensitivity [8,17]. By adding other components containing K2O [26], Na2O [27], and B2O3 [28], the densification ability and rigidity of silicon-based coatings can be promoted due to their enhanced liquidity. However, the structural stability of the coating decreases, which is detrimental to its high-temperature protection performance. In addition, aluminum silicide-based coatings can protect TiAl alloys from oxidation attack through the formation of a stable oxide scale, which acts as an effective oxygen diffusion barrier at elevated temperatures [29,30]. Zhao *et al.* [30] reported that a silicon-aluminizing coating can effectively improve the high-temperature oxidation resistance of TiAl alloys by forming alumina-based scales with high thermal stability and good adhesion. To improve coating densification or suppress phase transitions, silicide coatings can also be modified by HfO2 [31] or Yb2O3 [32], resulting in superior high-temperature performance.

Recently, electrodeposition has been proposed to fabricate SiO2 coatings on TiAl alloys [19,33]. The electrodeposited SiO2 coating adhered well to the TiAl substrate and exhibited excellent densification ability, resulting in significant enhancements in the high-temperature oxidation resistance. Owing to the use of KNO3 in the precursor electrolyte, a certain amount of K+ ions are contained in the SiO2 coating [19]. The introduction of K+ ions into SiO2 disrupts the continuous Si–O network structure, leading to the formation of isolated [SiO4] tetrahedral units and free K+/O2− species. This process converts bridging oxygen (Si–O–Si) into nonbridging oxygen (Si–O−), thereby reducing the connectivity of [SiO4] tetrahedra and significantly decreasing melt viscosity [34]. Thus, potassium silicate can fill in cracks and voids, promoting the sintering and densification of the coating [35]. However, the preferential accumulation of alkali metal cations can lead to the precipitation of a large amount of cristobalite [36−38]. The substantial volume changes of cristobalite during the heating and cooling stages may induce crack formation, adversely impacting the high-temperature protective performance of the coating [39,40]. Moreover, the oxygen diffusion coefficient of the coating also increases due to the introduction of alkali metals [41].

To solve this issue, controlling the precipitates of cristobalite and promoting the stability of the SiO2 coating is crucial. It has been reported that Al3+ can be associated with alkali ions, inhibiting their transport in the glass phase due to the coupling effect [42]. This interaction effectively suppresses the crystallization of cristobalite [36−38]. Consequently, additives containing aluminum, such as Al2O3, are frequently used as modifiers to enhance the SiO2 network structure [43]. However, owing to the high stability of Al2O3, it is challenging for Al2O3 to enter the SiO2 network and stabilize the element K before the crystallization of the SiO2 coating. Thus, in this study, Al nanoparticles with high surface activity were introduced into the SiO2 coating. These Al nanoparticles can rapidly stabilize the K element after densification, preventing its migration and therefore significantly inhibiting cristobalite precipitation. Additionally, the inclusion of Al nanoparticles can reduce the consumption of Al in the substrate and suppress the precipitation of the Z-phase.

In this study, we developed Al-modified SiO2 coatings (Al‒SiO2 coatings) via coelectrodeposition. Figure 1(a) displays the formation mechanism of the Al-modified SiO2 coatings. Through the application of a cathodic potential, water molecules and dissolved oxygen at the electrode interface are electrochemically reduced to catalytically generate OH− ions *in situ* [44]. These OH− ions facilitate the condensation reaction of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), leading to the formation of a SiO2 coating on the TiAl alloy surface. During this process, the hydroxylated surface of the Al nanoparticles adsorbs protons [45], inducing their cathodic migration, which enables their encapsulation within the SiO2 coating to form an Al‒SiO2 composite coating. This study focused on the oxidation behavior of Al‒SiO2 coatings at 900 °C. The effects of the incorporated Al nanoparticles on the densification and crystallization processes of the SiO2 coating were revealed. The stability of substitutional and interstitial Al atoms in the SiO2 lattice and K@SiO2 lattice (SiO2 lattice doped with interstitial K atoms) was simulated via density functional theory (DFT). This approach provided insights into the high-temperature oxidation mechanism of the Al‒SiO2 coating.

### 2.1 Materials and coating preparation

The γ-TiAl ingot, with a nominal composition of Ti–48Al–2Nb–2Cr (all in at%), was synthesized via vacuum melting. Flake samples (15 mm × 15 mm × 1.2 mm) were machined from the ingot via electrical discharge wire cutting. The substrate was subsequently polished with 220-grit SiC sandpapers, followed by a cleaning process using deionized water and ethanol.

To achieve a novel structure with suppressed cristobalite precipitation and well-dispersed Al nanoparticles, Al‒SiO2 coatings were prepared with Al nanoparticle concentrations of 0.1 and 1 g·L−1 in the precursor for comparative analysis. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image and size distribution of the Al nanoparticles are displayed in Fig. 1(b), showing sizes ranging from 80 to 200 nm. The specific statistical methods are as follows: In the SEM image of the Al nanoparticles, at least 100 particles were selected, and the areas of these particles were measured via ImageJ software. The equivalent circular diameter (ECD) corresponding to the area of each particle (*A*) was subsequently statistically analyzed via Eq. (1):

(1) ECD=2Aπ

This conversion standardizes the particle size representation by assuming a circular geometry with an identical projected area.

As shown in Fig. 1(c), the Al‒SiO2 coatings were prepared via coelectrodeposition, with details of the electrodeposition process outlined in our previous work [33]. After prehydrolysis, the Al nanoparticles were added to the precursor solution and ultrasonically dispersed for 20 min. The samples were then electrodeposited at 1 mA·cm−2 for 500 s via an electrochemical workstation (CHI 630b, Shanghai CH Instruments Co., Ltd, China). After electrodeposition, the samples were removed from the precursor solution, thoroughly rinsed, and blow-dried with warm air. Owing to the use of KNO3 as an electrolyte, electrodeposited coatings incorporate a certain amount of K. Typically, the residual K content in the SiO2 coating is approximately 13.12 wt%, which was analyzed via an electron-coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometer (ICP-AES; OPtima8300, PerkinElmer, USA). The Al–SiO2 coatings were denoted as 0.1Al–SiO2 and 1Al–SiO2 coatings on the basis of the Al nanoparticle content (0.1 and 1 g·L−1) in the precursor solution. For comparison, the SiO2 coating was fabricated using identical electrodeposition parameters.

Prior to oxidation testing, the SiO2 and Al–SiO2 coatings were heat-treated at 900 °C in a vacuum environment for 1 h to promote the densification of the coating, thereby effectively encapsulating the modified nanoparticles.

### 2.2 Oxidation test

Isothermal oxidation tests of the SiO2 and Al‒SiO2 coatings on the Ti‒48Al‒2Nb‒2Cr alloy were conducted at 900 °C in laboratory air for 100 h. The samples were loaded into the furnace (KSL-1200X, Hefei Kejing Materials Technology Co., Ltd, China) after they reached 900 °C. After oxidation for different durations (5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 h), the samples were directly removed and cooled in air. The mass gains of the three coatings were measured by a microbalance with a sensitivity of 10−5 g. Three replicate samples were tested for each condition to ensure reliability.

### 2.3 Characterization

The micromorphology of the samples was characterized by an SEM (Phenom XL, Thermo Scientific, USA) and an electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA; JXA-8530F Plus, JEOL Ltd., Japan). The structures of the oxide scales of the three coatings were analyzed via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Tecnai G2 F30, FEI, USA). The samples were cut into thin slices in a predetermined orientation and prepared on a focused ion beam (FIB) workstation via an *in situ* lift-out technique. The phase identification of the oxidized samples was performed via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, USA) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 0.154056 nm) at 40 kV and 40 mA. *In situ* X-ray diffraction was conducted at temperatures up to 900 °C for 1 h to study the phase precipitation behavior of the SiO2 coating. The residual stress of the SiO2 and Al‒SiO2 coatings was measured via a micro confocal Raman spectrometer (inVia, Renishaw, UK; 25 W, 785 nm). The adhesion strength between the coating and substrate was evaluated via an automatic scratch tester (WS-2005, Lanzhou Zhongke Kaihua Technology Development Co., Ltd, China).

### 2.4 Simulation method

In this study, the stability of substitutional and interstitial Al atoms in the SiO2 lattice, as well as in the K@SiO2 lattice (SiO2 lattice doped with interstitial K atoms), was explored via first-principle calculations. The SiO2 lattices doped with substitutional and interstitial Al are denoted as the Si*x*−1AlO2*x* lattice and the Al@SiO2 lattice, respectively. The K@SiO2 lattice doped with substitutional and interstitial Al atoms is denoted as the K@Si*x*−1AlO2*x* lattice and the AlK@SiO2 lattice, respectively.

The simulation was implemented via DFT with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional and the 6-31G* basis set. Dispersion correction is considered through the DFT-D3 (BJ) method. The energy cutoff of the plane wave basis set is 350 Ry, and the rel_cutoff is chosen as 50 Ry. The SiO2 bulk model contains 3 × 3 × 3 primitive cells, corresponding to optimized simulation supercells of 15.182 Å × 20.738 Å × 16.624 Å and 90° × 90° × 120°. Periodic boundary conditions are applied in all three directions. The self-consistent field (SCF) convergence criterion is set to 1.0×10−6, the maximum force convergence value is set to 4.5×10−4 Hartree·bohr−1, and the optimization method is Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS). The simulation is implemented in the quickstep package of the CP2K software [46], and the input file is generated through the Multiwfn 3.8(dev) software [47].

### 3.1 Microstructure of the Al‒SiO<sub>2</sub> coatings

Figure 2 shows the surface microstructure of the electrodeposited Al‒SiO2 coatings. Regardless of whether Al nanoparticles are added, all the coatings appear loose and porous, with aggregated spherical silica particles approximately 1 μm in diameter (Figs. 2(d)–2(f)). The amorphous nature of the silica particles was confirmed by the characteristic halo pattern in the XRD patterns (Figs. 2(h)–2(j)). The energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) results revealed the existence of some Al-enriched areas in the 1Al–SiO2 coating (spot 3 in Fig. 2 and Table 1). Localized Al enrichment reaches 11.9 at%, which is much greater than that of the SiO2 coating and 0.1Al–SiO2 coating (spots 1 and 2 in Fig. 2 and Table 1). The signal of Al in the SiO2 coating without the addition of Al nanoparticles (spot 1 in Fig. 2 and Table 1) may originate from the substrate, likely due to its porous structure and the EDS penetration depth at the micron level [48,49]. In addition, the elemental mappings also revealed Al-enriched regions on the surface of the 1Al‒SiO2 coating (Fig. 2(g)).

As shown in Fig. 3(a), the SiO2 coating becomes continuous. Some dark precipitates with sizes of several micrometers appear after thermal treatment at 900 °C for 1 h. Notably, numerous cracks are found in these precipitates (Fig. 3(d)). Table 2 lists the EDS results for the spots shown in Fig. 3. These precipitates consisted of Si, O, and trace amounts of K and Ti (spot 2 in Fig. 3(d) and Table 2). Combined with the XRD patterns (Fig. 3(h)), these precipitates are inferred to be composed mainly of cristobalite. For the bright regions, the elements K, Si, and O can be detected (spot 1 in Fig. 3(d) and Table 2). These bright areas are considered glass phases that are based on the composition of the K2O‒SiO2 system [26,50]. With the addition of a small amount of Al nanoparticles (0.1%), a scattered distribution of dark precipitates can be observed (Figs. 3(b) and 3(e)), which is different from the concentrated distribution of precipitates in the SiO2 coating (Fig. 3(a)). Additionally, the distribution area of dark precipitation has also decreased. For the 1Al–SiO2 coating, nearly no dark precipitates or cracks emerged after thermal treatment (Figs. 3(c) and 3(f)). The element mappings demonstrate that Al is evenly distributed on the coating surface (Fig. 3(g)). Both the 0.1Al–SiO2 coating and the 1Al–SiO2 coating had higher Al contents than did the SiO2 coating (spots 3–6 in Fig. 3 and Table 2). Moreover, the XRD patterns indicate a weaker intensity of the cristobalite peak in the 0.1Al–SiO2 and 1Al–SiO2 coatings than in the SiO2 coating. Combining the SEM images (Figs. 3(a)‒3(f)) and XRD results (Figs. 3(h)‒3(j)), it can be concluded that the precipitation of cristobalite is inhibited by the incorporated Al nanoparticles.

The modification effect of the 1Al–SiO2 coating is nonuniform due to Al agglomeration, resulting in two typical regions (Fig. 5). As shown in Figs. 5(a1)–5(h1), the region with relatively well-dispersed Al maintains a continuous and dense coating structure, where Al particles are surrounded by diffusion zones with lower Al contents, indicating that Al has begun to enter the network of the SiO2 coating during heat treatment. In contrast, Figs. 5(a2)–5(h2) demonstrate the impact of Al agglomeration on the coating structure. The agglomerated Al regions contain pores while simultaneously affecting the surrounding SiO2 coating, which fails to achieve complete densification and retains porosity and cracks. These structural defects may adversely affect the high-temperature oxidation resistance of the 1Al–SiO2 coating.

### 3.2 High-temperature performance of Al‒SiO<sub>2</sub> coatings

Figure 6(a) shows the oxidation kinetic curves of the SiO2 coating (curve 1), 0.1Al–SiO2 coating (curve 2), and 1Al–SiO2 coating (curve 3). During the initial oxidation stage, the mass gains of all the coatings rapidly increase. For the first 5 h, the order of mass gain from high to low was the 1Al‒SiO2 coating, 0.1Al‒SiO2 coating, and SiO2 coating. This can be attributed to the oxidation of the incorporated Al nanoparticles. After oxidation for 10 h, the oxidation rates of all the coatings decreased, and a parabolic growth mode was observed. The final mass gains of the SiO2, 0.1Al‒SiO2, and 1Al‒SiO2 coatings are 0.352±0.029, 0.221±0.033, and 0.307±0.063 mg·cm−2, respectively. Compared with TiAl alloys coated with other ceramic coatings in the literature, the Al‒SiO2 ceramic coating developed in this study offers superior performance in high-temperature oxidation environments. For example, Bik *et al.* [53] developed a SiOC coating with an oxidation mass gain of 2.74 mg·cm–2 after oxidation at 900 °C for 100 h. As the load increases from 0 to 40 N, the insulating coatings are compromised by the diamond stylus. The contact between the diamond stylus and the substrate results in a change in the voltage. Consequently, the critical loads of the oxidized SiO2, 0.1Al–SiO2, and 1Al–SiO2 coatings can be determined by the sudden change in the voltage. ## 4 Discussion

Owing to the use of KNO3 as the electrolyte, K+ is contained in the electrodeposited SiO2 coating (Fig. 2(g)). During thermal exposure to air, the reaction between K+ and SiO2 leads to the formation of potassium silicate, which can improve the fluidity of the coating and fill the pores [35,62]. However, potassium silicate inevitably leads to the crystallization of the SiO2 coating at high temperatures according to the K2O–SiO2 binary diagram [63]. This phenomenon has also been reported in enamel coatings [43]. Cristobalite undergoes significant volume changes during the heating and cooling stages because of its polymorphic transformation between high- and low-temperature forms [39,40], thus inducing the generation of cracks. To solve this problem, Al nanoparticles are incorporated into the SiO2 coating. The effect of Al can be attributed to two factors. One is optimizing the organizational structure by inhibiting the formation of cristobalite. The other method involves reducing the consumption of Al in the substrate and suppressing the precipitation of the Z-phase.

### 4.1 Effect of Al nanoparticles on microstructure of SiO<sub>2</sub> coating

The electrodeposited SiO2 coating without the incorporation of Al nanoparticles has good densification ability at 900 °C. As shown in Fig. 14, during thermal treatment, the porous structure of the SiO2 coating disappears and gradually transforms into a smooth and compact coating. This is beneficial for reducing the channels for the inward diffusion of oxygen and strengthening the protection properties of the coating. However, some cracks emerge after thermal treatment (Fig. 14(f)). To discern the factors contributing to crack formation, the phase compositions of the electrodeposited SiO2 coatings were analyzed *in situ* during oxidation at 900 °C. The peak intensity of cristobalite increases with increasing oxidation time (Fig. 14(g)). Moreover, the cracks are mainly distributed in the dark regions, which have the Raman characteristic peaks of cristobalite (Fig. 15). Therefore, it can be concluded that the formation of cristobalite in the SiO2 coating can induce cracking. In addition, SiO2 has many crystallographic forms, such as quartz, tridymite, and cristobalite [64]. The reason why the electrodeposited SiO2 coating tends to precipitate into cristobalite at 900 °C can be attributed to the lower Gibbs free energy of cristobalite than the other variants [64].

On the one hand, according to the K2O‒SiO2 binary diagram, potassium silicate in the SiO2 coating can form a liquid phase during the thermal treatment process, promoting the densification of the coating [63]. With the incorporation of Al nanoparticles, the liquid phase consisting of potassium silicate transforms into stable potassium aluminosilicate at 900 °C. The generation of potassium aluminosilicate has been demonstrated by Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 12). Owing to its higher melting temperature and better thermal stability than those of potassium silicate [42], potassium aluminosilicate has a positive effect on improving the barrier properties of the Al‒SiO2 coating. Thus, compared with the SiO2 coating, the Al‒SiO2 coating exhibited improved oxidation resistance (Fig. 6). In addition, the Ti content in the Al‒SiO2 coating is lower than that in the SiO2 coating (spots 1‒6 in Fig. 7 and Table 3). The 0.1Al‒SiO2 coating has smooth and continuous surfaces after thermal treatment (Fig. 3), indicating that an appropriate amount of incorporated Al nanoparticles does not hinder the densification process of the SiO2 coating.

On the other hand, the transport of alkali ions, such as K+ in the SiO2 coating, leads to precipitation and the growth of cristobalite [36−38]. With the addition of Al nanoparticles, the distribution of cristobalite is adjusted. For the 0.1Al‒SiO2 coating, the aggregated distribution of cristobalite in the SiO2 coating transforms into a dispersed state (Fig. 7). The Raman spectra show the reduced residual stress of cristobalite in the SiO2 coating caused by the introduction of Al nanoparticles (Figs. 12(a) and 12(b)). It can be inferred that this optimized distribution of cristobalite in the 0.1Al–SiO2 coating helps adjust the residual stress, inhibiting the tendency to crack. For the 1Al–SiO2 coating after thermal treatment, cristobalite and cracks are hard to find (Fig. 3(c)). The small change in residual stress explains why the 1Al–SiO2 coating has a low tendency to crack (Fig. 12(c)). Owing to the coupling effect of Al3+ and K+, KAl4+ will substitute for Si4+ in the aluminosilicate melt [42], hindering the transport of K+ in the glass phase and thus inhibiting the crystallization of cristobalite [36−38]. The formation of potassium feldspar (KAlSi3O8) is shown in Fig. 12(c). In addition, after oxidation for 100 h, the EDS results show that the stoichiometric ratio of K : Al : Si : O is approximately 1 : 1 : 3 : 8 (spot 6 in Fig. 7 and Table 3), further confirming the existence of potassium feldspar. The effects of Al and K atoms on the SiO2 network structure are discussed in Section 4.2.

### 4.2 Effects of Al atoms on SiO<sub>2</sub> and K@SiO<sub>2</sub> lattices

In this work, the stability of substitutional and interstitial Al atoms in the SiO2 lattice and K@SiO2 lattice (SiO2 lattice doped with interstitial K atoms) was explored via first-principles simulation. As shown in Fig. 16(a), the optimized lattice structure of SiO2 has dodecagonal channels formed by Si–O bonds with a side length of 1.63 Å and a channel diameter of 5.12 Å. Figure 16(b) shows the optimized lattice structure of the SiO2 lattice doped with substitutional Al, which is denoted as Si*x*−1AlO2*x*. The Al‒O bond of the corresponding channel extends to 1.77 Å, resulting in a slight distortion of the dodecagonal channel. The channel diameter of Al···Si in the Si*x*−1AlO2*x* lattice is 5.18 Å, whereas the channel diameter of Si···Si is compressed to 5.11 Å. As shown in Table 4, the Mulliken charge of Al in Si*x*−1AlO2*x* is 0.859, indicating that the Al atom tends to lose electrons. The polarity of Al is weaker than that of Si in the bulk SiO2 lattice, with a value of 1.114. Compared with that in the bulk SiO2 lattice, the positive charge of Si in the Si*x*−1AlO2*x* lattice is slightly increased to 1.162, and the negative charge of O is also increased, from −0.557 to (−0.567)–(−0.576). With the substitutional Al, the covalency of the corresponding Si‒O chemical bond in the Si*x*−1AlO2*x* lattice weakens slightly, but the covalency of the Al‒O bond is slightly stronger than the covalency of the Si‒O bond in the bulk SiO2 lattice. Figure 16(c) shows the optimized lattice structure of the SiO2 lattice doped with interstitial Al, which is denoted as the Al@SiO2 lattice. The O atoms in the Al@SiO2 lattice are not chemically bonded to Al, and the interstitial Al atoms greatly increase the corresponding channel size. The channel diameter is expanded to 6.27 Å, whereas the diameters of adjacent channels are compressed to 5.04–5.08 Å. The interstitial Al atom tends to gain electrons because the Mulliken charge of Al in Al@SiO2 is −0.523, which is opposite to the charge transfer trend of substitutional Al. As a result, the charge distribution near the channel in the Al@SiO2 lattice changes dramatically compared with that in the bulk SiO2 lattice. The positive electricity of Si is increased to 1.203–1.314, whereas the negative electricity of O is increased to (−0.571)–(−0.627). This greatly weakens the covalency of the corresponding Si–O in the Al@SiO2 lattice. The solution energy of the SiO2 network doped with a substitutional and interstitial Al atom can be given as Eqs. ### 4.3 Effect of Al nanoparticles on selective oxidation of TiAl alloys

In thermodynamic terms, for both the SiO2 coating and the Al‒SiO2 coating, the selective oxidation of Al to form Al2O3 can occur at the coating/substrate interface. The oxidation reaction of an alloying element M from the alloy can be expressed as Reaction (8):

(8) (2xy)M+O2→(2y)MxOy

According to the Ellingham diagram [67], standard Gibbs free energy change (* ΔGr0) of Al to form Al2O3 ( 43Al+O2→23Al2O3,ΔGr0(900∘C)=−866.6kJ/mol) at 900 °C is lower than that of Ti to form TiO2 ( Ti+O2→TiO2,ΔGr0(900∘C)=−722.56kJ/mol). When the oxygen atoms approach the substrate, they tend to react with Al. With the assistance of the electrodeposited SiO2 coating, the oxygen partial pressure at the coating/substrate interface can be reduced. According to the van’t Hoff equation, the change in Gibbs free energy during oxidation can be expressed as Eq. (9) [68]:

(9) ΔG=RTlnpo2′po2

where  po2′ refers to the equilibrium oxygen partial pressure of the oxidation reaction or decomposing pressure of the oxide at the reaction temperature, and  po2 represents the oxygen partial pressure of the oxidation conditions. When  po2′ po2 and ΔG*0 po2′ of Al is smaller than that of Ti at 900 °C [67]. Thus, the preferred situation is that the oxygen partial pressure of the oxidation environment is between  po2′ of Al and  po2′ of Ti, which accelerates the selective oxidation of Al. However, the actual oxide growth process is also related to the activities of Ti and Al. The γ-TiAl alloy is composed of γ-TiAl, α2-Ti3Al, and trace amounts of β-Ti [69]. According to Rahamel et al*. [70], who studied the activities of Ti and Al in the Ti3Al + TiAl system, the activity of Ti * (aTi=0.475)was greater than the activity of Al  (aAl=5.0×10−3) at 900 °C. This occurred because Ti has high activity in α2-Ti3Al, whereas Al has high activity in γ-TiAl [71]. As Al undergoes oxidation, α2-Ti3Al is continuously produced. Thus, for the sample without SiO2 coating, the growth rate of TiO2 is increased, leading to the formation of loose TiO2 + Al2O3 oxide. Apart from the low oxygen partial pressure at the coating/substrate interface, the SiO2 coating can lower the activity of Ti by forming Ti5Si3. The formation of Ti5Si3 can consume Ti, reducing the generation of α2-Ti3Al and therefore decreasing the activity of Ti. Ti5Si3 and Al2O3 can be generated via Reaction (10) [72]:

(10) 5Ti+4Al+3SiO2=Ti5Si3+2Al2O3

The standard free energy of Reaction (9) at 900 °C is calculated to be −1115 kJ·mol−1, according to the following equation  ΔGr0(T)=ΔHr0(298K)−TΔSr0(298K). In the equation,  ΔGr0 denotes the standard Gibbs free energy change of the reaction,  ΔHr0 represents the standard enthalpy change of the reaction, and  ΔSr0 stands for the standard entropy change of the reaction. T* indicates the temperature, typically expressed in Kelvins. The incorporated Al nanoparticles can further suppress the consumption of Al, decreasing the formation of a brittle Z-phase (Ti3Al and Ti5Al3O2), which has high Ti activity. In addition, the thickness and structure of the Al2O3 layer are affected by the incorporated Al nanoparticles. This phenomenon is related to the kinetic process of selective external oxidation, which has four steps [73]: (i) the reactant gas molecules approach the surface; (ii) the surface physically adsorbs reactant gas molecules; the adsorbed molecules dissociate into oxygen atoms, which are subsequently chemisorbed; (iii) the chemisorbed oxygen reacts with active alloying elements to form an oxide nucleus on the surface; (iv) reactant elements are supplied to the surface via diffusion to develop the oxide.

The incorporated Al nanoparticles can affect the steps (i) and (iv). In the step (i), owing to the adjustment of the incorporated Al nanoparticles to precipitate cristobalite, the number of oxygen diffusion channels, such as cracks and pores, decreases in the Al–SiO2 coating (Fig. 3), avoiding direct contact between the oxygen and the substrate. In step (iv), the Al nanoparticles in the coating can reduce the Al concentration gradient and chemical incompatibility between the coating and the substrate. Thus, the aluminum diffusion flux decreases according to Fick’s second law (Eq. (11)) [74]:

(11) ∂c∂τ=∂∂x(D∂c∂x)

In Eq. (11), *D* is referred to as the diffusion coefficient (m2/s), *c* represents the volume concentration of the diffusing substance (component) (atom/m3 or kg/m3), *τ* represents the diffusion time (s), and ∂*c*/∂*x* denotes the concentration gradient. Therefore, the diffusion rate (∂*c*/∂*τ*) of Al in the Al–SiO2 coating decreases, and the thickness of the Al2O3 layer decreases with increasing content of Al nanoparticles (Figs. 10 and 11).

The good oxidation resistance of the Al‒SiO2 coating is due to two factors. On the one hand, the reduced proportion of cristobalite reduces the residual stress after 100 h of oxidation and inhibits the occurrence of cracks and other defects (Figs. 12 and 18(f)). On the other hand, during oxidation, potassium silicate transforms into potassium aluminosilicate, which has a higher melting point and better stability. The Al in the Al‒SiO2 coating originates from the incorporated Al nanoparticles and diffuses outwardly from the TiAl alloy. The existence of potassium aluminosilicate is confirmed by the Raman spectra (Fig. 12(c)). The melting point of potassium aluminosilicate is above 1000 °C. The elemental maps in Fig. 20 show that K and Al significantly overlap and are predominantly distributed in the amorphous SiO2 network surrounding the cristobalite phase, which is generally consistent with the DFT simulation scenario (Figs. 16 and 17). The doped Al atoms preferentially exist in substitutional positions, generating shorter Al‒K bonds, preventing the migration of K, and stabilizing the K@SiO2 lattice, which is beneficial for reducing the degree of crystallization and promoting the barrier properties of the SiO2 coatings. In addition, the incorporation of Al nanoparticles can increase the content of Al in the SiO2 coating (spots 1, 4, and 6 in Fig. 3 and Table 2), reduce the chemical mismatch between the coating and the substrate, decrease the diffusion rate of Al from the substrate to the coating, reduce the thickness of the selective alumina layer at the interface, and suppress the precipitation of the brittle Z phase in the Al-poor layer (Figs. 10 and 11). Moreover, compared with the SiO2 coating, the Al‒SiO2 coatings exhibit superior interfacial adhesion to the substrate (Fig. 13).

## 5 Conclusions

1) The porous structure of the electrodeposited SiO2 coating allows for the accommodation of incorporated Al particles. Both the SiO2 coating and the Al‒SiO2 coating can be densified after thermal treatment. The EMPA results demonstrate the successful incorporation of Al nanoparticles within the SiO2 coating.

2) Al nanoparticles effectively refine the cristobalite distribution in the SiO2 coating, which helps reduce the residual stress and thereby hinders the formation of cracks.

3) The Al‒SiO2 coating demonstrated superior oxidation resistance, with a parabolic oxidation constant that was 32% lower than that of the SiO2 coating. After oxidation for 100 h, the Al‒SiO2 coating has a lower Ti content than the SiO2 coating does, indicating enhanced barrier properties.

4) The SiO2 lattice with a doped Al atom is unstable. However, the incorporation of a substitutional Al atom in the K@SiO2 lattice is exothermic and occurs spontaneously. Substitutional Al forms a shorter Al‒K bond (2.91 Å) than does the Si‒K bond (3.39 Å), which can mitigate K-induced channel distortion and hinder the migration of K, thereby reducing the degree of crystallization and promoting the barrier properties of K@SiO2.

5) The consumption of Al in the substrate and the precipitation of the brittle Z-phase are inhibited in the Al‒SiO2 coating, which is beneficial for diminishing the activity of Ti.
