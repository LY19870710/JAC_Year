## 1 Introduction

The coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) in crystalline materials is a vital characteristic that has significant impacts on various scientific and engineering fields [1,2]. These materials are characterized by highly ordered atomic arrangements and exhibit unique thermal expansion behaviors that are essential for applications in the electronics, aerospace, construction, and advanced technological sectors [3]. For example, in integrated circuit packaging systems, multilayer ceramic substrates [4] must have CTE values that closely match those of silicon chips (3.2 ppm/°C) [5] and printed circuit boards (14–17 ppm/°C) [6]. This prevents solder joint failure and reliability degradation caused by thermal mismatch, particularly during temperature fluctuations [7,8]. Similarly, in aerospace engineering, structural materials used in satellites and hypersonic vehicles require strict regulation of the CTE values to function effectively in environments with extreme temperature changes. For instance, satellites operating in space experience extreme temperature gradients between sun-facing surfaces and shaded surfaces. A mismatch in thermal expansion among materials can lead to structural deformation, instrument displacement, or even system failure [9].

Understanding the thermal expansion behavior of materials is critical for predicting and controlling the dimensional stability of components under varying temperature conditions. The Grüneisen parameter quantifies the sensitivity of lattice vibrational frequencies to changes in lattice volume, particularly reflecting how lattice vibrational mode frequencies depend on variations in lattice constants [10]. In the context of thermal expansion, this parameter acts as a bridge, connecting the phonon spectrum characteristics of a material to its macroscopic thermal expansion behavior. When the CTE is calculated theoretically, the Grüneisen parameter can be used to estimate the thermal expansion behavior of materials across a range of temperatures [11]. Additionally, the fundamental bond characteristics of crystal structures, such as bond ionicity and bond covalency, have been systematically calculated via the Philips–Van Vechten–Levine (P–V–L) model [12−15]. By integrating these parameters, Zhang *et al*. [16] proposed a simplified calculation method for the CTE values of binary AIBVII, AIIBVI, and A*m*B*n* crystal structures. This advancement represents a significant step toward correlating the electronic structure and chemical bonding with the thermal expansion properties of materials.

The CTE of a crystal material can be obtained through first-principles calculations, which delve into the intricacies of the crystal structure and chemical bond characteristics [17]. However, these calculations are often encumbered by the complexities of the crystal structures and computational procedures involved. Recent studies have demonstrated that parameter settings can significantly influence the accuracy of CTE predictions, with the choice of exchange-correlation functional and calculation methods being particularly critical. To address these challenges, researchers are eager to adopt data-driven machine learning methods to predict thermal expansion properties with minimal reliance on experimental data [18−22]. For instance, the support vector regression model (SVM) is employed to predict the CTE value of the RE2Si2O7 crystal, with verification conducted specifically for the RE = Sm system [23]. The predicted CTE value of 10.39 ppm/°C aligns closely with the experimental value of 11.55 ppm/°C. In contrast, a suite of machine learning models is utilized to estimate the CTE value for perovskite oxides by adopting the ten most influential features. Notably, the SVM model demonstrates good predictive accuracy, achieving a coefficient of determination (*R*2) value of 0.88 [24]. Additionally, the extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) model has been used to investigate the negative thermal expansion behaviors in A2M3O12 crystals, achieving an acceptable *R*2 value of 0.81 [25].

Based on the aforementioned studies, data-driven machine learning models are believed to be able to predict the CTE of crystal materials [26]. However, owing to the complexity of constructing strongly correlated features and choosing models, the prediction performance of the CTE, such as the mean absolute error (MAE), the *R*2 values and the cross-validation result, has been undesirable. Artificial neural network (ANN) models offer distinct advantages in probing the CTE of crystal materials. These models excel at handling high-dimensional nonlinear relationships and are suitable for capturing the intricate physical and chemical properties of crystal materials [27]. Additionally, they adopt multi-layered nonlinear transformations to learn the complex mapping between input features and output CTE target, enabling highly accurate data-driven predictions. More importantly, fewer experimental data are needed, by which strong generalization can be achieved by optimized network architectures and training strategies.

In this study, we constructed features related closely to binary crystals and employed artificial neural network methodologies to predict the thermal expansion properties of binary crystals. By integrating interpretability techniques, we analyze key features within the crystal structure and their interactive effects influencing the CTE. Results demonstrate that the proposed method enables high accuracy in predicting the CTE of binary crystals.

### 2.1 Artificial neural network model construction and feature selection

To apply machine learning techniques for investigating the determinants of thermal expansion characteristics, a robust feature set and comprehensive dataset are essential. In this study, twelve features were developed to encompass various aspects of the element types, chemical compositions, and crystal structures of binary crystals. These features include the total number of elements (TNE), ordinal number of cations (ONC), ordinal number of anions (ONA), cation radius (CR), relative molecular weight (RMW), total electronegativity (TE), bandgap (BG), formation energy (FE), unit cell volume per atom (UCPA), average bond length (ABL), cation valence state (CVS), and cation coordination number (CCN). A detailed explanation of these features is provided in Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

The dataset includes parameters from several binary crystals, such as NaCl-type, CaF2-type, rutile-type, CsCl-type, A2B3-type, and α-ZnS-type crystals, which are presented in Table S2 in the ESM. The distributions across different crystal structures and space groups are shown in Fig. 1.

Pearson correlation analysis was performed on the twelve features to investigate the degree of linear correlation among them, with the results visualized in the correlation plot shown in Fig. 2. Consequently, we deem them critical for predicting CTE values and retain these highly correlated feature groups without exclusion. Furthermore, the selected machine learning models—random forest (RF), least-squares boosting (LSBoost), Gaussian process (GP), and back-propagation neural network (BP-NN) regression models—are all suitable for handling features with high linear correlations.

Specifically, we employed the POA to perform an adaptive architecture search for a multilayer perceptron (MLP), using an intelligent evolutionary mechanism to discover the highest-performing network configuration. Our method encodes the number of neurons in each hidden layer as components of a multidimensional vector, with each neuron count constrained to an integer between 2 and 25 to balance model capacity and computational efficiency. During initialization, candidate parameter combinations are randomly sampled from this predefined search space, and each combination defines a complete network structure. For each candidate, we constructed the corresponding neural network and evaluated it on a validation set by computing the MAE value of its predictions, which serves as the fitness value guiding the optimization. The algorithm then uses a prey-tracking strategy to steer candidates toward more promising regions and employs a time-varying disturbance coefficient (which decays over iterations) to finely adjust solutions, thus overcoming local optima and achieving a thorough global search. Ultimately, this process provides the optimal number of neurons in each hidden layer for a fixed network depth. As shown in Table 3, we applied the above algorithm to search for and determine the optimal number of neurons in each layer for various fixed hidden layer depths. However, the corresponding 8-fold cross-validation results are less favorable, particularly when the number of hidden layers is 7, where the MAE of cross-validation is approximately sixfold greater than that of the test set. Therefore, considering both the test set performance and cross-validation stability, the BP-NN model exhibits superior predictive performance when the number of hidden layers is set to 2.

### 2.2 Analysis of feature interaction effects

To address the black-box limitation of the BP-NN model, which prevents effective analysis of how the crystal structure impacts the CTE values of binary crystals A*m*B*n*, the SHAP interpretability technique was used to rank feature importance and analyze potential interaction effects [31]. Figure 5(a) displays the SHAP values of different features across various instances (samples). The color intensity reflects the magnitude of the SHAP value, with red indicating a positive impact and blue indicating a negative impact. The CVS feature has a strong positive impact in many instances, particularly in those with larger instance numbers, suggesting that the CVS feature makes a significant positive contribution to the predicted CTE values. In contrast, features such as BG and CR appear in both blue and red across some instances, indicating that their impact varies depending on the context. Above Fig. 5(a), a line chart illustrates the overall trend of the predicted CTE values. This line rises and falls in correspondence with the color changes in the heatmap. The importance of features is shown in Fig. 5(b). However, due to the limited sample size, directly calculating SHAP values via the test set may result in unstable estimates of feature importance. To address this, the entire dataset for this analysis was utilized. In subsequent sections, we validated the consistency of feature importance rankings by combining the results from an 8-fold cross-validation of the MAE values with the experimental data. This integrated approach ensures a robust evaluation of feature relevance. The features were prioritized based on their significance in influencing the predicted CTE value in the whole dataset, with the ABL, CVS, TNE, and TE features emerging as the most four critical factors. Their contributions reach 20%, 17%, 12%, and 12%, respectively. However, the influence of these features on the CTE is different: a high ABL or TNE feature value tends to have a negative impact on the model, while a high CVS or TE value shows a positive impact on the model. It is reported that the Grüneisen parameter measures the sensitivity of the phonon frequency to system volume changes, reflecting the correlation between the bond length and volume changes. The positive Grüneisen parameter indicates that the phonon frequency decreases with increasing volume, which is usually related to the elongation of the bond length. In diamonds, the Grüneisen parameter is always positive, ranging from 0.46 to 1.46, which is related to the relative rigidity of the carbon–carbon bonds in diamonds [32]. The bond length is sensitive to stretching and bending, resulting in elongation as the main manifestation of bond length changes, leading to a positive CTE. Additionally, changes in the electronegativity of II–VI group semiconductors also affect the Grüneisen parameter. Specifically, as the electronegativity of VI group elements decreases (from O to Te), the negative value of the Grüneisen parameter gradually increases, leading to more significant negative thermal expansion behavior [33]. A lower electronegativity implies stronger covalent bonding properties, which can lead to a decrease in the interaction forces between atoms, reducing the frequency of lattice vibration modes. The lower vibration frequency is correlated with the negative value of the Grüneisen parameter, promoting the negative thermal expansion effect. The force plots in Fig. 5(c) show the exact distributions of the SHAP values for two samples, and sample 1 and sample 12 are chosen as examples. The baseline indicates the initial prediction value, and it gradually moves to the actual value by accumulating the contributions of all the features.

SHAP analysis reveals distinct feature contribution patterns in CTE value prediction across the two samples. In sample 1, six features demonstrate statistically significant influences, with the BG feature exhibiting the most substantial positive impact (SHAP value = 7.56). In contrast, the CR and CVS features made relatively minor contributions (SHAP value ≤ 1), indicating their limited predictive importance in this context. Notably, sample 12 presents a contrasting feature importance profile, where the UCPA feature displays the strongest association because of its comparatively larger SHAP value of approximately 39.51. Comparative analysis between samples reveals divergent interaction patterns: the BG feature is the main positive influencing factor in sample 1. On the contrary, the UCPA feature has the most significant effect on the predicted CTE values in sample 12. This finding indicates that the importance of features varies across different samples and the CTE value prediction for different samples relies on different combinations of features. This sample-dependent variability in feature importance underscores the necessity of model interpretation and suggests that the optimal CTE prediction requires dynamic feature weighting based on material-specific characteristics.

To discover the tipping points in the influences of the predicted CTE values, the generalized additive model (GAM) that captures the nonlinear relationship, was used to fit trends among the features with SHAP values, which includes the main effect of a single feature and the sum of the interaction effects between that feature and all other remaining features [34]. Figure 6 shows the SHAP dependence plots for the features, showing different relationships between the feature values and their corresponding SHAP values. The blue trend lines represent the GAM fit, and the shaded areas indicate the confidence intervals. The red dots mark the tipping points, and the trend line intersects with the zero SHAP value line. The fitting trends among the top six most important features are in an acceptable range of R*2 = 0.84–0.92 and show distinct tipping points between positive and negative effects, reflected by the blue and orange areas. For instance, in Fig. 6(a), the ABL value exerts a positive predictive influence on the CTE outcomes when the ABL value is low (e.g., below 2.1); however, once the ABL value surpasses the tipping point, the direction of its impact reverses, shifting from a positive to a negative association. The relationship between the chemical bond properties and the CTE can be explained as follows: an inverse relationship exists between the bond length and bond strength; for example, as the bond length increases, the bond strength decreases. This weakening of the bond strength typically enhances the CTE, which is consistent with the trend observed in Fig. 5(b). However, when the bond length is stretched beyond a critical threshold, the asymmetry of lattice vibrations, as predicted by the Grüneisen law, can attain a critical threshold. This critical asymmetry alters the vibrational mode of the lattice, thus inverting the direction of the CTE and resulting in the tipping point. According to Eq. (1), the trend of the change in the bond valence (νM–O) is opposite to that of the bond length (*d*M–O) [35], which is also testified by Fig. 6(b). Generally speaking, the influence direction of features on the CTE prediction values can be understood.

The SHAP interaction values of the features were analyzed and shown in Fig. 7 to understand the possible interaction effects [36,37]. The interaction points between the ABL and TNE features are predominantly located in the positive region, indicating that when the interaction value between these two features is high, they tend to make a positive contribution to the predicted CTE value. Conversely, in the combination of the ABL and CR features, the distribution of interaction values is more dispersed, with contributions that are both positive and negative. This phenomenon suggests that the underlying interactive effect between them is non-monotonic and may exert different influences on the model depending on the level of interaction value. A lower interaction magnitude may contribute positively to the model, whereas elevated interaction levels could potentially exhibit either a negative impact or statistically insignificant contributions.

The *p*-values associated with the ABL and CVS feature pairs are the lowest (0.00016), highlighting the most substantial interaction effect between the two features. Specifically, when the ABL value is at a lower level (e.g., 1), an increase in the CVS value leads to a shift in the direction of the interaction effect, transitioning from negative to positive. However, as the ABL value increases, the interaction effect tends to approach zero, irrespective of changes in the CVS value. The interaction effect between the ABL and TNE features is quite different. When the value of the ABL feature is low (e.g., 1), a low TNE value can result in a positive interaction effect between the two variables. As the value of ABL increases, a higher TNE value is necessary to produce a positive interaction effect. Furthermore, a significant interaction effect between the UCPA and CCN feature pairs is also demonstrated, which indicates that while the total influence of the UCPA and CCN features on the CTE prediction values is relatively minor, the impact of their interaction cannot be overlooked in predictive modeling. Additionally, when the UCPA value ranges between 10 and 20 and the CCN value is either low or high, the interaction values between these two variables show a relatively dispersed pattern, exhibiting both positive and negative directions. However, once the UCPA value exceeds 20, the interaction value gradually strengthens when the CCN value is low. By integrating the SHAP analysis, the influence direction and intensity of features on the CTE value prediction can be intuitively observed, which enhances the transparency of the BP-NN model’s decision-making process.

### 2.3 Synergistic influences of multiple features on the model

Based on the above SHAP analysis, the contribution of features to individual samples and the contribution of features to model prediction have been clarified. To comprehensively understand the overall trend of the CTE prediction values affected by the collaborative changes in single and multiple features, ALE analysis is adopted [39]. ALE analysis calculates the influence of each feature on model predictions by assessing local impacts cumulatively. This technique segments features into intervals, measures changes in predicted values within each interval, accumulates these changes, and generates a cumulative effect curve, providing a comprehensive understanding of how features affect CTE predictions. By calculating local effects, ALE analysis overcomes the limitations imposed by the assumption of variable independence and mitigates the confounding effects of correlations. This approach offers precise explanations for the relationships among the features. Using ALE analysis allows us to demonstrate the impact of individual features and their combinations, which is particularly useful for features that exhibit relevance. It isolates the pure impact of a single feature on the variations in the CTE value, thus providing a deep understanding of the feature’s contribution.

Figure 9 presents the first-order ALE curves, which explain the impact of individual features on the predicted CTE values. Through these plots, the effects of several features exhibit distinct nonlinear relationships with the CTE prediction. Features such as the CVS and ABL have a stronger influence on model outputs, whereas the BG, ONA, and UCPA have weaker effects. By analyzing the feature distribution (depicted as blue vertical lines below the *x*-axis), the data density across different feature value ranges becomes apparent. For instance, CVS data are predominantly concentrated in lower value ranges and sparsely distributed in higher ranges, potentially amplifying uncertainty in ALE estimates for high-value intervals. The ALE plot provides an intuitive visualization of each feature’s independent contribution to the model’s predictions, thereby facilitating the identification of key drivers of model behavior and clarifying how specific features shape the final CTE value. This analysis is instrumental in understanding the underlying mechanisms of the model’s decision-making process and enhances the interpretability of the model’s outputs.

### 2.4 Theoretical/experimental verification

The CTE value obtained via P–V–L theory calculation is based on the structural parameters from the Bohr Materials Database. The calculation process can be found in the ESM. The feature value used in this BP-NN model is also determined by the same structural information. The theoretical/experimental CTE value is retrieved from the first-principal calculation reported previously and our experimental result. For experimental verification, starting powder of Sm2O3 (99.9%) was added to a 3 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) solution to prepare green cylinders under a pressure of 20 MPa. The cylinders were fired at 650 °C to remove the binder and then sintered at 1350 °C for 4 h. For crystal structure determination, an X-ray diffraction equipment (Philips X’Pert ProMPD, the Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation and a scanning range of 20°–70° was used. The linear expansion coefficient was measured by a dilatometer (DIL 420C, Netzsch, Germany).

As shown in Table 4, eleven binary materials, which cover five distinct crystal structures, were selected to verify the BP-NN model’s generalization performance. It is shown that the BP-NN model yields relatively high predicted CTE values compared with those calculated by the P–V–L theory even when the same structural parameters are used, demonstrating greater accuracy and closer alignment with the theoretical/experimental results. Based on the comparisons in Table 4, the absolute errors among the selected eleven binary crystals are all less than the 8-fold cross-validation MAE value, and the CTE prediction model simplifies a lot of theoretical calculation workload and can be applied to other binary crystals. Notably, microdefects, such as dislocations and grain boundaries, may also alter the strength of atomic interactions and displacement modes, affecting the CTE value. Additionally, the type, concentration, and distribution of impurities significantly affect the CTE value. More importantly, some materials undergo phase transitions when the temperature changes, which significantly alters the thermal expansion behavior of the material. Therefore, the rule for using this BP-NN model is that the crystal structure type and structural parameters should be clarified. Meanwhile, the BP-NN model is currently not applicable to phase transition situations.

## 3 Conclusions

A BP-NN model integrated with SHAP and ALE interpretability techniques was adopted to predict the CTE of binary crystals A*m*B*n* and simultaneously identify key influencing features. Through the Pearson feature selection method, nine features were identified for model training. The BP-NN model consists of an input layer with nine key features and two hidden layers with 25 and 14 neurons, which exhibits exceptional performance with a low MAE value of 1.503 and a high *R*2 value of 0.9834, suggesting a robust generalization capability for CTE prediction. SHAP and ALE analyses indicate that both the ABL and CVS features have a high influence, with 44% contribution to the model’s predictability, and that the features with low contributions also play a crucial role in enhancing the prediction accuracy. Given its high efficiency and accuracy, the proposed BP-NN model is suitable for predicting the CTE of binary crystals A*m*B*n*.
