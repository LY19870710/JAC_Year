## 1 Introduction

Sustainable materials and technologies are being developed more quickly owing to increasing energy and environmental issues [1,2]. Fuel cells are considered an environmentally friendly method for producing energy with high efficiency and low pollution because they can directly transform fuel energy into electricity [3]. Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) have garnered significant attention as one of the most promising fuel cell types owing to their all-solid-state construction and flexibility in fuel selection. Moreover, owing to their high operating temperatures, SOFCs can avoid water management issues and reduce costs using metal oxides rather than noble metals as electrode catalysts [4]. Nonetheless, the typical operating temperature of conventional SOFCs is above 800 °C, which is excessively high and causes several issues, including interfacial diffusion and a short fuel cell lifespan [5]. Consequently, lowering the operating temperature of SOFCs to an intermediate range is necessary [6].

The primary reason for the high operating temperatures of conventional SOFCs is the use of yttria stabilized zirconia (YSZ) as an electrolyte. As YSZ exhibits adequate conductivity only at high temperatures, the low conductivity of YSZ at intermediate temperatures increases the ohmic resistance [7], limiting the operation of SOFCs at intermediate temperatures [5]. Utilizing novel electrolytes with high ionic conductivities at intermediate temperatures is crucial for developing SOFCs that can operate at intermediate temperatures [8]. Proton-conducting electrolytes are promising electrolyte sources for SOFCs because they exhibit higher conductivities and lower activation energies than YSZ [9−12]. These fuel cells are referred to as protonic ceramic fuel cells (PCFCs) [13] or proton-conducting SOFCs (H-SOFCs) [14].

While the high ohmic resistance issue can be resolved by utilizing proton-conducting electrolytes, lowering the operating temperature necessitates developing highly catalytic cathodes at intermediate temperatures because cathode performance largely determines fuel cell performance. The design of high-performance cathodes has received considerable attention in the H-SOFC community in recent years, with excellent results [15−17]. Most of these effective cathodes are made using cobaltite components. Despite their high catalytic activity, cobaltite materials have several disadvantages, such as large thermal expansion and cobalt evaporation [18]. In contrast, ferrite materials have been used in H-SOFCs because ferrite oxide cathodes have lower overpotentials than cobaltite cathodes [19]. SrFeO3-based ferrite materials have attracted the most interest because changing their structure and characteristics with appropriate dopants is simple [20−23]. Nb-doped SrFeO3−*δ* (SFO) has been used for oxygen-ion-conducting SOFCs (O-SOFCs) [24,25]. While Nb doping results in increased chemical stability [26], it still has mediocre effects on O-SOFCs [25,27]. Furthermore, the solid-state reaction approach is typically used to prepare Nb-doped SrFeO3−*δ* and other Nb-containing oxides [27]. This method requires high temperatures to generate pure-phase materials, which usually increases the particle size of the materials and subsequently decreases the cathode activity. Considering all these aspects, using Nb-doped SrFeO3−*δ* in H-SOFCs seems unreasonable. However, the current study aims to overcome this limitation using first-principles calculations and experimental methods to examine and compare the performances of SrFeO3−*δ* cathodes for H-SOFCs with and without Nb doping. To improve the performance of the Nb-doped SrFeO3−*δ* cathode for H-SOFCs, additional manipulations were accomplished by adding oxygen vacancies (VO) to the oxide and preparing the nanosized Nb-doped materials via an efficient wet chemical method.

## 2 Experimental

SFO and SrFe0.9Nb0.1O3−*δ* (SFNO) oxides were prepared using a solid-state reaction technique. All the starting materials employed in this study were analytical reagents (ARs). Briefly, stoichiometric proportions of starting materials (SrCO3, Fe2O3, and Nb2O5) were mixed by ball milling, with ethanol used as the solvent without dispersant. The metal oxides were mixed by ball milling for 24 h at 400 r·min−1. The mixture was dried in an oven (DHG-9030, Shanghai Yiheng, China) at 60 °C and then calcined at various temperatures, and the phase compositions of the materials formed at various temperatures were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD). The oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) on the SFO and SFNO surfaces was studied using density functional theory (DFT) calculations via the VASP software [28]. The cutoff energy was set to 520 eV. The convergence criteria for energy and force were 10−5 eV and 0.05 eV·Å−1, respectively. The *k*-points were sampled at 3 × 3 × 1 using the Monkhorst–Pack method. A vacuum layer of 15 Å was added to avoid interactions with other slabs. Additional details of the calculations can be found in prior studies [29,30].

Fuel cells with proton-conducting electrolytes of BaCe0.7Zr0.1Y0.2O3−*δ* (BCZY) were constructed to assess the effects of Nb doping on the SFO cathode performance of H-SOFCs. Half-cells were formed by copressing and cosintering the BCZY electrolyte membrane with the NiO+BCZY anode substrate [31]. The weight ratio of NiO to BCZY in the anode ranged from 6 to 4. Subsequently, the BCZY electrolyte surface was coated with the SFO or SFNO cathode and co-fired for 30 min at 900 °C in a high-temperature furnace (KSL-1100X-S, HF-Kejing, China), while single-phase SFO or SFNO was used without the formation of a composite cathode. Using H2 as the fuel, the manufactured fuel cells were tested under operational conditions, and their performance was recorded using an electrochemical workstation (Squidstat Plus, Admiral, USA). The fuel cells were tested at temperatures ranging from 600 to 700 °C. The flow rate of H2 fuel was set to 30 mL·min−1, with static air employed as the oxidant. All electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed under open-circuit voltage (OCV) conditions in the frequency range of 1 MHz to 0.1 Hz.

The SFNO cathode was optimized by introducing VO into SFNO using the microwave sintering technique. Microwave sintering was performed during the cathode co-firing process. The SFNO cathode slurry was deposited on the electrolyte and co-fired with the half-cell in a microwave sintering furnace (HY-MF3016, Huae Microwave Technology, China) rather than in a conventional high-temperature furnace (KSL-1700X-A1, HF-Kejing, China) to fabricate the cathode layer in the full cell. The same BCZY/BCZY+NiO half cells were used in this study. Microwave sintering was conducted in a microwave sintering furnace, with the heating rate set at 10 °C·min−1 until the temperature reached 900 °C and was maintained for 30 min. The temperature was then automatically reduced to room temperature. The fuel cell, including the microwave-sintered SFNO cathode, was tested under the previously stated conditions. The as-prepared SFNO powders were fired again using conventional microwave sintering techniques to examine the effect of microwave sintering on the VO in the oxide. The VO content of the SFNO powders produced by these two sintering techniques was examined using iodometric titration and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). XPS measurements were carried out under a vacuum at a pressure of 10−9 Pa.

Given that the solid-state reaction method was used to prepare all the SFNO materials mentioned above, a wet chemical approach was used to prepare the SFNO powders to reduce the particle size of the materials. Solid-state reactions are commonly used to prepare Nb-containing oxides because Nb2O5 is typically used as the starting materials [27,32], and no other materials, such as niobium nitrate, are available. Ammonium niobate oxalate was used as the starting materials, and H2O2 was used to oxidize the oxalate anions to prevent the precipitation of strontium oxalate. Citric acid was then added to the complex Nb cations in the solution. The Nb cation solution was then mixed with stoichiometric amounts of Sr(NO3)2 and Fe(NO3)3 using citric acid as the complexing agent, resulting in the formation of a transparent solution. After evaporating the water, the solution ignited, creating ash. SFNO powders were produced by firing these ashes for 3 h at 1100 °C. The surface areas of the SFNO powders prepared using sol–gel and solid-state reaction processes were compared using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) technique. The grain sizes of the as-prepared SFNO powders were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The solid-state-reaction-prepared SFNO cathode was compared to a sol–gel cathode by assessing the fuel cell performance, and SEM was used to examine the microstructures of the fuel cells.

## 3 Results and discussion

The XRD patterns of the SFNO produced using a solid-state reaction technique and fired at various temperatures are shown in Fig. 1(a). After firing the SFNO powders at 1000 °C, impurities were clearly observed. The impurity-corresponding peaks decrease with increasing firing temperature and vanish entirely for the powders at 1300 °C, indicating the formation of a pure-phase SFNO after calcination at 1300 °C. The structure of SFNO is comparable to that of typical Nb-free SFO. However, as Fig. 1(b) illustrates, the peaks of SFNO move to lower angles relative to those of SFO, indicating an increase in the lattice volume of SFO following Nb doping. The XRD Rietveld refinement of SFO and SFNO, as shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), revealed a cubic crystal structure with a space group of *Pm** 3¯m*. The obtained lattice parameter of SFO is *a* = *b* = *c* = 3.873 Å with a volume of 58.095 Å3, which is smaller than that for SFNO (*a* = *b* = *c* = 3.895 Å with a volume of 59.091 Å3), further demonstrating that the lattice volume expands after Nb doping. By partially substituting Nb for Fe in SFNO, the lattice volume expands owing to the larger ionic radius of the Nb5+ (64 pm) cations than that of the Fe cations (61 pm for Fe2+ and 55 pm for Fe3+). Furthermore, doping SFO with high-valence Nb may suppress the valence of Fe cations, increasing the average radius of the Fe cations in the lattice and increasing the lattice volume.

By measuring the conductivities of SFO and SFNO, the conductivity of SFNO was found to be greater than that of SFO, as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM, suggesting that doping SFO with Nb can enhance the conductivity of the materials. The conductivities of both materials reach tens of S·cm−1 at intermediate temperatures, which are sufficient for use as cathodes for H-SOFCs. In addition, doping SFO with Nb partially mitigated the high thermal expansion of the SFO. Figure S3 in the ESM shows the thermal expansion coefficient (TEC) curves of SFO and SFNO. TEC of SFO was slightly complicated, showing four different slopes. TEC of SFO is 15.1×10−6 °C−1 from room temperature to approximately 330 °C. Then, TEC increases to 47.81×10−6 °C−1 from 330 to approximately 400 °C. TEC between 400 and 600 °C is 18.45×10−6 °C−1, and the value increases again to 41.86×10−6 °C−1 from 600 to 1000 °C. In contrast, there were two slopes for TEC of SFNO. TEC of SFNO is 16.99×10−6 °C−1 from room temperature to 650 °C. Then, TEC increases to 34.64×10−6 °C−1 from 650 to 1000 °C. TEC of SFNO is lower than that of SFO at both low and high temperatures, making it more thermally compatible with the electrolyte.

Considering that SFNO can be synthesized successfully with improved conductivity and a more compatible TEC, investigating how SFO, both with and without Nb doping, affects ORR of H-SOFCs could provide more insights. DFT calculations were performed to examine the reaction process. Surface models of SFO and SFNO were built, as shown in Fig. 2(a). ORR for H-SOFCs includes O2(g) → *OOH → *O → *OH → H2O reactions [33]. Therefore, the Gibbs free energy changes of ORR at U* = 0 and 1.23 V were calculated for the chosen surface models to evaluate the thermodynamic reaction process. The potential determining step (PDS) of SFO occurs during the first hydrogenation process (O2(g) → *OOH), with a calculated ORR overpotential (* ηDFTORR) of 0.77 V, as shown in Fig. 2(b). SFNO shows a better ORR catalysis performance compared to Nb-free SFO, in which PDS is the second hydrogenation step (*OOH → *O) with a  ηDFTORR of 0.58 V, as shown in Fig. 2(c). Doping with SFO leads to a lower ORR overpotential, potentially benefiting the cathode reactions. Therefore, studying the differences in performance between H-SOFCs using SFO and SFNO cathodes could be important.

The fuel cell performances of the H-SOFCs with SFO and SFNO cathodes are shown in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b). Although a single-phase SFO or SFNO cathode was utilized, the chemical compatibility of SFO (or SFNO) and BCZY was investigated by considering the contact between the SFO (or SFNO) cathode and the BCZY electrolyte, as shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM. The phases of the SFO+BCZY and SFO+BCZY composite powders remained unchanged following cosintering, indicating the high chemical compatibility between SFO (or SFNO) and BCZY. At 600, 650, and 700 °C, the peak power density (PPD) of the SFO cell reaches 305, 403, and 605 mW·cm−2, respectively. In contrast, PPD for the SFNO cell clearly increases and reaches 370, 674, and 957 mW·cm−2 under identical testing conditions. The potential of each cell at PPD was similar. For instance, the same potential of 0.51 V corresponds to PPD at 700 °C. OCVs of the cells were approximately 1 V, which was smaller than the theoretical value, probably owing to the partial reduction of Ce4+ to Ce3+ in the BCZY electrolyte under a reducing atmosphere. Nevertheless, the advantage of tailoring the SFO cathode for H-SOFCs using the Nb-doping technique is demonstrated in Fig. 3(c), where PPD of SFNO cell was greater than that of the SFO cell at all testing temperatures. The EIS images of the two cells tested at 700 °C are displayed in Fig. 3(d). The capacitance of the high-frequency arc in the EIS plot is on the order of 10−5 F·cm−2 [34], which is typical for electrode processes and much higher than that of electrolytes (10−12 to 10−9 F·cm−2) [35]. As a result, the EIS semicircles do not include the electrolyte contribution and instead display the electrode polarization resistance. The polarization resistance (R*p) of SFO cells is determined to be 0.123 Ω·cm2, while the *R*p of SFNO cells is 0.084 Ω·cm2. The reduced *R*p for SFNO also indicates faster cathode reactions. The semicircles of the EIS plot for the SFNO cathode at high and low frequencies are smaller than those for the SFO cathode, implying that Nb doping improves charge carrier transport, as well as the adsorption and dissociation of O2 [36,37], which is consistent with the lower ORR energy barrier for SFNO compared with SFO, as determined by DFT calculations.

SFO materials were prepared with Nb doping concentrations of 5%, 10%, and 20%. Figure S5 in the ESM shows the XRD patterns of SrFe0.8Nb0.2O3, SrFe0.9Nb0.1O3, and SrFe0.95Nb0.05O3. The preparation of SrFe0.8Nb0.2O3 produces impurities. In contrast, pure phases were observed for SrFe0.9Nb0.1O3 and SrFe0.95Nb0.05O3, indicating that the Nb doping concentration in SFO was less than 20%. The performance of H-SOFCs with the SrFe0.95Nb0.05O3 cathode was further investigated, and the results are shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM. PPDs of these cells were determined to be 829, 527, and 350 mW·cm−2 at 700, 650, and 600 °C, respectively. Although their performance was higher than that of the Nb-free SFO cathode, it was clearly lower than that of the SrFe0.9Nb0.1O3 cathode, indicating that a doping concentration of 10% Nb in SFO was optimal.

Following testing, the morphologies of SFO and SFNO cells were examined to rule out the possibility that their differences in performance were a consequence of microstructural issues. The results are shown in Fig. 4. Both cells had trilayer structures with a porous anode, a dense layer of electrolyte, and a porous cathode. For both cells, the electrolyte thickness is almost the same, approximately 14 μm. The discrepancy in the electrolyte grain size is most likely attributable to sample preparation for SEM observations. The electrolyte membrane can crack along or through the grain boundaries, resulting in various grain morphologies [18]. Both cell types exhibited comparable microstructures. Even after testing, there was no delamination in the electrode connections to the electrolyte, including the cathode and anode. Consequently, the different cathodes employed, rather than microstructure-related problems, are the reason for the performance disparity between the SFO and SFNO cells.

Although using an SFNO cathode instead of a conventional SFO cathode results in a substantial performance improvement, the performance is still not as good as that of some recently reported cathodes for H-SOFCs [38−40]. To increase the competence of the SFNO cathode in the community, greater performance improvement is needed. Given that VO has been shown to increase electrode ORR activity [41,42], the SFNO cathode performance can be further enhanced through defect engineering in conjunction with the introduction of VO. The microwave sintering technique can introduce VO into oxides [43,44]. Hence, by applying this technique to the materials, VO can be readily added to SFNO. Microwave sintering can cause a reduction in the metal cations in the lattice [45,46], thereby creating extra VO. SFNO materials are subjected to microwave and conventional sintering techniques to validate this idea. The pure-phase SFNO powders were prepared by the solid-state reaction method, and microwave sintering and conventional sintering were further used to treat the pure-phase SFNO powders at 900 °C for 30 min to study the influence of microwave sintering on VO. The VO contents of the SFNO materials treated by various sintering techniques were examined using XPS, as shown in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b). The surface VO of the materials can be determined by comparing the oxygen species linked to surface VO (O22− or O−) to lattice oxygen (Olat) [47,48]. The higher the VO content is, the larger the ratio. A greater VO content was obtained for SFNO using microwave sintering, as evidenced by the ratio of 0.49 for conventionally sintered SFNO and 0.66 for microwave-sintered SFNO. A higher VO content may also contribute to enhanced protonation because VO production is a prerequisite for protonation [49], thereby enhancing the use of the materials as a cathode for H-SOFCs.

The iodometric titration method [50] is another technique used for analyzing the oxygen defect content of SFNO materials treated in various ways. The concentration of VO was assumed to correlate with the average valence of iron ions (p*+), which was determined using the iodometric titration method. Samples (0.1 g) were dissolved in a 2 M HCl solution containing 1 g KI. Due to the strong oxidation of * Fep+,  I− can be oxidized to  I2 according to the equation  Fep++(p−2)I−→Fe2++(p−2)2I2. Nitrogen was used to empty the air in the unit throughout the titration procedure because the oxygen in the air readily oxidizes  I−. Using the starch as an indicator, the amount of generated  I2 can be titrated using the consumed volume of standard  Na2S2O3 solution (0.01 M) according to the equation of  I2+2S2O32−→2I−+S4O62−. Knowing the average valence state of  Fe ions, the nonstoichiometry of oxygen can be calculated based on the electric neutrality principle. As demonstrated in Fig. 5(c), the oxygen defect density for conventionally sintered SFNO was 0.39, which increased to 0.46 when the SFNO materials were fired under microwave sintering conditions. XRD Rietveld analysis was performed to determine the exact lattice parameters of SFNO (Fig. S7 in the ESM). After microwave sintering, the lattice parameter of SFNO synthesized by the solid-state method is a* = *b* = *c* = 3.899 Å, which is larger than that of SFNO without microwave sintering (*a* = *b* = *c* = 3.895 Å). The increased lattice parameter of SFNO after microwave sintering implies the partial reduction of cations [45,46] and thus, the creation of VO. All these data show that VO has been effectively added to SFNO using the microwave sintering method and that the performance of the cathode for H-SOFCs made using this approach can be assessed and compared with that of the cathode prepared using the conventional method.

Despite the increased VO content, microwave sintering did not affect the morphology of the SFNO powders (Fig. S8 in the ESM). The morphologies of the SFNO powders before and after microwave sintering are similar, which is expected because the phase formation temperature for the SFNO powders is 1300 °C, and subsequent microwave sintering at a much lower temperature of 900 °C normally cannot change the morphology of the powders.

DFT calculations were used to investigate the impact of VO in SFNO on the cathode performance. The structures of SFNO generated via microwave and conventional sintering are shown in Fig. 6(a). A microwave sintering technique was used to introduce VO. In this model, one oxygen atom is removed from the lattice—a common strategy for creating a model with VO for DFT calculations [33]. VO plays a crucial role in the ORR activity because it influences several important features, such as redox couples, surface chemistry, and the electronic structure of the catalyst materials [41]. Thus, the effect of VO on the cathode activity was determined. After the introduction of VO, the energy of the *OOH intermediate is regulated to a lower value, and meanwhile, the last hydrogenation process (*OH  →  H2O) becomes PDS, with a * ηDFTORR of 0.54 V, as shown in Fig. 6(b), which is lower than that of SFNO without VO (0.58 V) (Fig. 2(c)), suggesting that the synergistic regulation of the Nb dopant and VO together adjusts the local electron environment via the Sr–Nb–Sr active site and thus further reduces the ORR overpotential. Considering all the free-energy diagrams (Figs. 2(c) and 6(b)) under comprehensive analysis, VO plays a vital role in enhancing the catalytic ability. The electronic states changed by VO in the bulk were also evaluated. Large band gaps are observed for the spin-down electrons in the SFNO system, as shown in Fig. 6(c). When the O atom is removed, more energy states occur above Fermi energy level, which is considered to be the defect energy level. Thus, the conductivity of the catalyst was significantly enhanced. In summary, VO can improve the catalytic performance and conductivity, thus potentially enhancing the cathode performance. Conductivity enhancement with increasing VO was also demonstrated by experimental studies, as shown in Fig. S9 in the ESM. The conductivity of SFNO sintered by microwave sintering was greater than that of SFNO sintered by conventional sintering, suggesting that microwave sintering can improve the conductivity of SFNO, which is consistent with the DFT calculation results.

A microwave-sintered SFNO cathode was used to fabricate an H-SOFC to verify the predictions made by DFT simulations. The SFNO phase formation procedure was performed on a conventional high-temperature sintering surface, whereas microwave sintering was used for cathode layer construction during fuel cell fabrication, which did not introduce extra procedures in fuel cell fabrication for introducing VO because the cathode layer fabrication (sintering) procedure is needed for all SOFC fabrication. Figure 7(a) shows the fuel cell performance. At 600, 650, and 700 °C, the PPD of the cell reaches 566, 876, and 1143 mW·cm−2, respectively. Figure 7(b) illustrates the benefits of using the microwave sintering method for SFNO fabrication by comparing the PPD of cells with that of SFNO cathodes prepared using conventional and microwave sintering methods. The microwave-sintered SFNO allows a greater PPD than the conventionally sintered SFNO over the entire testing temperature range, consistent with the DFT results described above, which show that adding VO can further lower the ORR energy barrier. According to Fig. 7(c), R*p of the cell using the microwave-sintered cathode is 0.072 Ω·cm2, which is less than that of the cell using the conventionally sintered SFNO cathode (0.084 Ω·cm2). The improved cathode catalytic activity of SFNO with the addition of VO is indicated by its lower *R*p. The EIS plots were further analyzed using the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) technique, and the results are shown in Fig. S10 in the ESM. For both cathodes, three frequency peaks were observed: high (P1), medium (P2), and low (P3). P2 is related to the anode response [51] and was comparable for both cells. In contrast, the microwave-sintered SFNO had lower P1 and P3 values than conventionally sintered SFNO. P1 is linked to proton migration [51]. As indicated previously, microwave sintering produces more VO, which may aid in the protonation of the materials. As a result, microwave-sintered SFNO is likely to have a smaller P1. P3 represents the oxygen adsorption and dissociation processes [51]. The reduced P3 for microwave-sintered SFNO indicates an improvement in O2 adsorption and dissociation compared with conventionally sintered SFO, which is advantageous for producing VO for electrodes [41].

Notably, while the SFNO cathode processed by the microwave sintering method can improve fuel cell performance, the solid-state reaction approach is still used to prepare SFNO powders. The solid-state reaction process often requires a high calcination temperature to prepare pure-phase powders, resulting in materials with large grain sizes. In contrast to the solid-state reaction approach, which generates powders with larger grain sizes, the wet chemical pathway can prepare the materials at a considerably lower temperature, thereby reducing the particle size [52]. However, because selecting Nb precursors is challenging, Nb-containing oxides are often prepared using solid-state reaction techniques [27]. This study makes it possible to successfully prepare SFNO materials using a sol–gel cathode. The specifics are given in the experimental section. Figure 8(a) shows the results of the XRD analysis performed on the sol–gel-produced SFNO powders to determine their phase purity. After firing at 1100 °C, the sol–gel approach produces SFNO powders with a pure phase structure. Although the phase structure is the same as that of the SFNO powders prepared using the solid-state reaction method, the solid-state reaction method requires a calcination temperature of 1300 °C to produce a pure phase of SFNO, which is 200 °C higher than that required by the sol–gel method. Compared with the solid-state reaction approach, the sol–gel method significantly lowers the phase formation temperature of SFNO, which reduces the difficulties associated with its preparation. When the calcination temperature for the sol–gel SFNO was reduced to 1000 °C, some small impurities could be observed, and no pure-phase SFNO was obtained, as shown in Fig. S11 in the ESM, implying that a calcination temperature of 1100 °C is required even when using the sol–gel method to prepare SFNO.

Furthermore, the low phase formation temperature enabled by the sol–gel approach could theoretically result in a decrease in the grain size of SFNO. The surface areas of SFNO synthesized using solid-state and sol–gel techniques are shown in Fig. 8(b). For SFNOs prepared by solid-state and sol–gel procedures, the surface areas are 0.54 and 1.24 m2·g−1, respectively, showing that the wet chemical process greatly improves the surface area of the materials. This process increases the surface area of SFNO and decreases its grain size, which could result in more active reaction sites when SFNO is utilized as the cathode.

To study the influence of microwave sintering on the lattice parameters of the sol–gel-prepared SFNO powders, XRD Rietveld analysis was performed on the sol–gel-prepared SFNO powders before and after microwave sintering, as shown in Fig. S12 in the ESM. The as-prepared SFNO has a lattice parameter of a* = *b* = *c* = 3.901 Å, which is smaller than that of SFNO after microwave sintering treatment, with *a* = *b* = *c* = 3.907 Å. Similar results were observed for the previously reported solid-state-reaction-prepared SFNO powders, as mentioned above, implying that the formation of VO as a result of partial cation reduction also occurs for sol–gel-prepared SFNO following microwave sintering.

The performance of the H-SOFC employing the sol–gel-prepared SFNO cathode is shown in Fig. 10(a). Although the sol–gel powder precursor was calcined in a conventional high-temperature furnace to obtain a pure phase, microwave sintering was applied in the cathode layer co-firing (fabrication) procedure. The PPD of the cell reaches 871, 1205, and 1764 mW·cm−2 at 600, 650, and 700 °C, respectively. As demonstrated in Fig. 10(b), the performance was greater than that of the fuel cell employing the solid-state reaction-produced SFNO cathode, indicating the benefit of sol–gel-prepared SFNO in increasing fuel cell performance. Figure 10(c) depicts the trilayer structure of the fuel cell, which is comparable to that of the cell using the SFNO cathode prepared via a solid-state process (Fig. 7(c)). Given the same microstructures of both cells, the varied preparation methods of the cathodes should be the main cause of the discrepancy in fuel cell performance. Figure 10(d) shows the EIS plots of the cell using the sol–gel-prepared SFNO cathode, indicating that the *R*p of the cell is 0.045 Ω·cm2. By preparing the SFNO cathode using the sol–gel process, *R*p decreased, and the fuel cell performance increased without altering the cathode composite. A fuel cell employing an SFNO cathode prepared via a combination of microwave sintering and sol–gel preparation methods exhibited high-level performance, equivalent to or even better than that of many recently reported cathodes for H-SOFCs [53−61]. In addition to having an excellent fuel cell output, Fig. 10(e) illustrates the sufficient stability of the cell during operation. The cell operated steadily for more than 200 h without any signs of degradation, indicating that the SFNO cathode exhibited good operational stability. Figure S13 in the ESM shows that the cathode maintained good contact with the electrolyte even after the long-term test. The chemical stability of SFNO was further investigated under CO2 and H2O atmospheres, and the results are shown in Fig. S14 in the ESM. The structure of SFNO remained unaltered, indicating good chemical stability. The composition of SFNO, which is free of Ba, may explain its good stability. Furthermore, Nb doping has been reported to improve the chemical stability of oxides [26,62]. As a result, the modification of Nb-doped SrFeO3−*δ* can be seen as a “one stone, two birds” tactic that improves the efficiency of the conventional SFO cathode for H-SOFCs while simultaneously preserving outstanding operational stability, offering an intriguing approach to the development of new cathodes for H-SOFCs. This study presents an interesting method for developing high-performance cathodes for H-SOFCs. Starting with the conventional SFO cathode, Nb doping can improve the performance of the SFNO cathode over that of the conventional SFO cathode. The SFNO cathode was further modified by microwave sintering to introduce VO, which has been shown to improve fuel cell performance. Finally, a sol–gel process was used to improve the microstructure of the SFNO powders. The performance of the SFNO cathode was considerably enhanced by combining composition modification (Nb doping), VO introduction (microwave sintering), and microstructure optimization (sol–gel synthesis), demonstrating the importance of integrating different parameters to improve the cathode performance of H-SOFCs.

## 4 Conclusions

SFNO was proposed as a cathode for H-SOFCs, and further manipulations were performed to enhance its performance. When Nb was doped into SFO instead of Nb-free SFO, the ORR energy barrier decreased considerably, thereby improving the cathode performance. VO was added to the oxide via microwave sintering, further improving the SFNO cathode. Compared with the conventional sintering approach, experimental investigations have shown that microwave sintering can successfully incorporate VO into oxides. VO enhances the catalytic activity of the cathode and considerably lowers the ORR energy barrier. Consequently, at 700 °C, the fuel cell performance increased from 957 to 1143 mW·cm−2. Although synthesizing nanosized Nb-containing oxides is technically difficult, further improvement was achieved by preparing SFNO via a wet chemical approach rather than the conventional solid-state reaction method. The effective sol–gel approach for preparing SFNO reduced the material grain size, which increased the number of active sites available for cathode reactions and enhanced the fuel cell performance to 1764 mW·cm−2 at 700 °C. The Nb doping strategy, which combined microwave sintering and sol–gel preparation procedures, was proven to be highly effective in tailoring SFO for H-SOFCs, resulting in high fuel cell performance and good operational stability.
