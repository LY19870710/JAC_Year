## 1 Introduction

All-solid-state lithium metal batteries (ASSLMBs) have obtained significant attention due to their excellent safety and high energy density. Among various solid-state electrolytes, the ceramic Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO) is regarded as a promising candidate for ASSLMBs, owing to its high ionic conductivity, wide electrochemical window, and excellent electrochemical stability [1−3]. However, the growth of lithium dendrites within LLZO during cycling, leading to battery short-circuiting, poses a significant challenge [4,5].

Previous research attempted to solve this problem and found some factors contributing to lithium dendrite growth, such as the low relative density [6], large grain size of LLZO [7], and poor contact between lithium metal and LLZO [8]. Numerous solutions have been developed to address these problems [9]. The relative density of LLZO could reach 98% with a small average grain size (10,11] and the interfacial impedance decreases to lower than 1 Ω·cm2 [6]. Despite some improvement in battery performance, under moderate current densities, lithium dendrites still grow in LLZO during cycling [12,13]. In recent research, Han *et al*. [14] identified the high electronic conductivity of LLZO as the origin of lithium dendrite growth. Therefore, Tang *et al*. [15] deposited a nanoscale LiF layer on the surface of LLZTO to block electrons ingress, effectively inhibiting the growth of lithium dendrites, and increasing the value of critical current denisity (CCD) from 0.05 to 0.75 mA·cm−2. However, when electrons cross the interlayer, they will be reduced to form lithium metal at the grain boundaries [16]. Therefore, the construction of electron-blocking grain boundaries is crucial for improving the intrinsic properties of LLZO and preventing lithium dendrite growth. Zhang *et al*. [17] reported the *in-situ* Li*x*SiO*y* modification of the grain boundaries of LLZTO, efficiently decreasing its electronic conductivity, and the CCD value reaching 0.7 mA·cm−2. However, the symmetric cells only cycled at 0.3 mA·cm−2 for 100 h, and the voltage platform was unstable. This unsteady performance was due to the nonuniform distribution of the second phase. Figure 1(a) shows the schematic structure of grain boundary modifications [18−20], where LLZO grains are interconnected, and the additional second phase is unevenly distributed. Since the interconnected grains serve as electron pathways, the second phase is unable to completely impede electrons, resulting in the formation of lithium metal at unaltered grain boundaries.

In this work, we introduced LiF into LLZTO to prepare a series of x* wt% LiF–LLZTO (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 3) electrolytes. The grains of LLZTO were uniformly coated with a nanoscale insulating layer of LiF (Fig. 1(b)), forming a connecting electron-blocking network at the grain boundaries. The phase compositions, cross-sectional morphologies, relative densities, and ionic/electronic conductivity of the samples were characterized. We also explored the influence of LiF on the suppression mechanism of lithium dendrite in the electrolytes. Furthermore, the full cells could stably cycle at a relatively high rate in the long-term cycling and exhibited an outstanding capacity retention performance.

### 2.1 Preparation of LiF–LLZTO electrolyte

The LiF–LLZTO electrolytes were prepared by the traditional solid-state reaction method. LiOH·H2O (99.98%, excessive 10 wt%, Aladdin, China), ZrO2 (99.99%, Aladdin, China), La2O3 (99.99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), and Ta2O5 (99.5%, Aladdin, China) were weighed in a stoichiometric ratio of Li6.5La3Zr1.5Ta0.5O12. The original materials were wet ball-milled using zirconium oxide balls and isopropanol for 10 h at 400 r/min in polyurethane ball mill tanks. The ground slurry was dried at 80 ℃for 8 h and then sintered in an Al2O3 crucible for 6 h under an air atmosphere at 950 ℃. The obtained powder was wet ball-milled (with the same parameters) again to reduce its particle size. Then, different amounts of LiF (99.99%, Aladdin, China) were added, the amount of LiF is the *x* wt% of the LLZTO powder, where *x* = 0, 1, 2, 3. LiF and LLZTO powders were dry ball-milled in polyurethane ball mill tanks with zirconium oxide balls for 2 h at 175 r/min to obtain a uniform mixed powder. The mixture powder was squeezed at 250 MPa into pellets with a diameter of 10 mm. Besides, the powder was pressed with the same pressure into 35 mm × 5 mm × 4 mm strips for mechanical properties testing. Subsequently, they were calcined at 1320 ℃ for 20 min to obtain LiF–LLZTO pellets and strips. They were polished with different grit sandpapers (1000, 2000, and 3000) to about 0.7 mm thickness and 29 mm × 4 mm × 3 mm, respectively.

### 2.2 Characterization of LiF–LLZTO electrolyte

The three-point bending experiment (CMT4204; MTS, China) was used to determine the fracture strength of *x* wt% LiF–LLZTO (*x* = 0, 1, 2, 3) specimens. At a loading rate of 1 mm·min−1, the bottom span was 20 mm. The Vickers hardness of the specimens was measured with a microhardness tester (VMH-104; Walter Uhl, Germany). The indentations on the polished surface at loads of 5 N with a loading duration of 10 s were used to calculate the hardness of the samples. Each sample was performed with five sites, and adjacent indentation sites were kept at least 500 μm apart from one another.

The phases of LiF–LLZTO particles were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Empyrean; Malvern Panalytical, the Netherlands) with a scan rate of 10 (°)·min−1 (2*θ* = 10°–75°). The Archimedes method was adopted to measure the relative density of LiF–LLZTO pellets. The morphology and elemental distribution of the samples were characterized by the scanning electron microscope (SEM, MIRA3; TESCAN, Czech Republic) with an energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The microstructures of the LiF–LLZTO electrolytes were observed by the scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM, Talos F200s; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The LiF–LLZTO electrolytes were ground in an agate mortar for 30 min in the glove box to obtain the powders. Then, they were dispersed in isopropanol and dropped onto a carbon film supported by a Cu grid for TEM observation. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS, K-Alpha; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was adopted to test the chemical state of representative elements of the electrolytes. The C 1s peak at 284.5 eV corresponding to C–C bonding was used for charge compensation.

### 2.3 Electrochemical measurements

The electrical impedance spectroscope (EIS, CHI660e; CH Instruments, China) was measured with a temperature range of 25–100 ℃ (1 Hz−1 MHz) to calculate the ionic conductivity and activation energy of the pellets. To investigate the electronic conductivity of the samples, the direct current (DC) polarization was performed under a constant voltage of 1 V for 3600 s at 25 ℃.

The lithium symmetric cell was assembled by coating the molten lithium method to improve the interface contact [21]. The EIS of cells was measured at a frequency range from 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz at 25 ℃ to obtain the interface resistance (CS2350M; CORRTEST, China). The CCD of lithium symmetric cells was evaluated in time-constant mode (30 min stripping and 30 min plating, from 0.1 to 1.2 mA·cm−2) and capacity-constant mode (0.2 mAh·cm−2 for lithium stripping/ plating, from 0.1 to 1.4 mA·cm−2). The long-term cycling performance was measured at a current density of 0.3 mA·cm−2 in time-constant mode. The full cells were charged and discharged at 0.5 C in the voltage range of 2.5–4 V. The loading of cathode active material LiFePO4 (LFP) was ~3 mA·cm−2, and 5 µL ionic liquid (2 M LiFSI in Pyr13FSI) was adopted to wet the cathode interface. The cell measurements were performed with the LAND battery test system (CT3002A; LANHE, China) without any external pressure.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 2 displays the XRD patterns of the *x* wt% LiF–LLZTO powders (*x* = 0, 1, 2, 3). For each specimen, the positions and intensities of diffraction peaks are consistent with the cubic LLZO (JCPDS No. 45-0109). This result indicates the addition of LiF did not affect the cubic phase of LLZO. The absence of LiF detection in XRD data could be attributed to its low amount.

The cross-sectional morphology of the *x* wt% LiF–LLZTO (*x* = 0, 1, 2, 3) sintered pellets was observed by SEM (Fig. 3). For the cases of 0 wt% and 1 wt% LiF–LLZTO (abbreviated as 1LiF–LLZTO), the grains exhibit a bright and clean appearance. In comparison, nearly all grains of 2LiF–LLZTO are coated with an amorphous phase, while the 3LiF–LLZTO specimen shows a crystallized second phase at the grain boundaries, with grains partially covered with the amorphous phase. The specimen with *x* = 0 (Fig. 3(a)) and 1LiF–LLZTO (Fig. 3(b)) both exhibit small grains (~5 μm) with clearly defined grain boundaries, devoid of conspicuous pores defects. This is in line with the high relative density results presented in Table 1. Furthermore, the corresponding EDS mapping of the 1LiF–LLZTO specimen reveals a uniform distribution and faint intensity of the F element. The weak intensity might be attributed to a thin LiF layer coating the grains or F element doped within LLZTO. A partially enlarged view of 1LiF–LLZTO is displayed in Fig. 3(e), depicting a coating with a thickness of less than 100 nm on the grains. With the content of LiF increase to 2 or 3 wt%, the grain size significantly increases (~10 µm) and the distribution of F element shows distinct enrichment (Figs. 3(c) and 3(d)). The enlargement of the LLZTO grain size can be attributed to the lower melting temperature of LiF (848.2 ℃) compared to the sintering temperature (1320 ℃), which promotes accelerated grain growth through the liquid phase of LiF [22]. The EDS line scan of the 3LiF–LLZTO sample is shown in Fig. 3(f). The F element intensity obviously increases at the grain boundary while the intensities of other elements decrease. This means that excess LiF addition results in enrichment at the grain boundary. Furthermore, the LiF at this grain boundary is in a crystalline state, which means the enrichment might be owing to the crystallisation of LiF during the cooling down process of LLZTO sintering.

When applying LLZTO solid electrolyte in ASSLMBs, its mechanical properties are also crucial [23]. To explore the effect of LiF on the mechanical characteristics of LLZTO ceramics, the Vickers hardness and fracture strength were tested. Figure 4(a) shows the imprint of the indentation of the 1LiF–LLZTO sample which is clearly visible. The actual image of *x* wt% LiF–LLZTO (*x* = 0, 1, 2, 3) ceramic strips after sintering are shown in Fig. 4(b). Moreover, Fig. 4(c) and Table 1 present the Vickers hardness and fracture strength values in connection with the addition of LiF. As the LiF content grew, the Vickers hardness of *x* wt% LiF–LLZTO (*x* = 0, 1, 2, 3) ceramics increased. It eventually achieved the highest value of 8.00 GPa at *x* = 3, which is relatively high [24]. Combined with the SEM results in Fig. 3(e), Vickers hardness upward trend may be attributed to the molten LiF that could promote intergranular bonding. With the content of LiF increased from 0 to 2 wt%, the value of fracture strength gradually increased. However, as the content increased from 2 to 3 wt%, it decreased from 80 to 53.4 MPa (it still has higher strength than the unmodified one). In conjunction with the microstructure observed in Figs. 3(c) and 3(d), this may be due to the increase in grain size and extremely non-uniform distribution of LiF. Overall, the addition of LiF could efficiently improve the mechanical properties of LLZTO.

Figure 5(a) displays the EIS curves of the distinct specimens. Due to the high response frequency of the grains (> 1 MHz) and the low grain boundary impedance, the EIS curves of 0 and 1LiF–LLZTO specimens only exhibit a straight line of 45°, corresponding to a blocking electrode. The point of the intersection between the real part and the linear segment provides the total resistance of the ceramics. Upon increasing the LiF addition to 2 and 3 wt%, the grain boundary resistance escalates, manifesting as a semicircular feature in the middle response frequency range. This phenomenon might be ascribed to the relatively low ionic conductivity of LiF (10−8–10−6 S·cm−1) [15]. On the whole, with the increase of the addition of LiF, the total resistance gradually increases. As shown in Fig. 5(b) (the Arrhenius plots), the data points fit well with the fitting line, enabling the calculation of the activation energy for each specimen based on the slope of the line. In particular, the 1LiF–LZZTO specimen exhibited the lowest activation energy of 0.35 eV, possibly attributed to the LiF filling pores at the grain boundary, thereby promoting lithium ions migration (this sample has a slightly higher relative density than 0LiF–LZZTO, as shown in Table 1). Utilizing the steady-state current magnitude from the DC polarization curves in Fig. 5(c), the electronic conductivity of the samples is calculated. With the increased addition of LiF, the electronic conductivity progressively diminishes, primarily due to the low electronic conductivity of LiF (10−31 S·cm−1) [25]. The concrete values of ionic conductivity, activation energy, and electronic conductivity of the ceramics are presented in Table 1.

To explore the effect of LiF on the LLZTO’s capability to suppress dendrite formation, symmetric cells containing *x* wt% LiF–LLZTO (*x* = 0, 1, 2, 3) electrolytes were assembled to measure CCD values. Additionally, assessment of the resistance of the symmetric cells is a prerequisite before CCD testing. The EIS curves of symmetric cells are illustrated in Figs. 6(a)–6(d). The semicircle observed in the high-frequency region reflects the electrolyte resistance, while the semicircle in the mid-frequency range corresponds to the total interface resistance between the electrolyte and lithium anodes. The tail in the low-frequency region characterizes the electrode response. Half of the semicircle’s diameter in the mid-frequency region equates to the area-specific resistance (ASR). Although the ASR experiences a slight rise post LiF incorporation, it remains low. This elevation in the ASR could be attributed to the presence of LiF on the surface of LLZTO. In comparison, the ASR value of all samples is close to each other, suggesting that differences in interface resistance have a relatively low impact on CCD. As shown in Figs. 6(e)–6(h), the voltage increased linearly with the current density rise. In the time-constant mode (30 min), the Li/1LiF–LLZTO/Li symmetric cell shows the highest value of 0.8 mA·cm−2 at 25 ℃ (Fig. 6(f)) and 1.4 mA·cm−2 at 60 ℃ (Fig. 6(k)). In comparison, the CCD value of Li/0LiF–LLZTO/Li is only 0.4 mA·cm−2. This outcome shows the outstanding capability of LiF addition in suppressing lithium dendrite, with an optimal addition amount at 1 wt% in LLZTO. When at a high current density, the batteries exhibited a soft breakdown [26] and the voltage platform suddenly dropped which may be due to the low ionic conductivity of the LiF [15]. Furthermore, the CCD measurement of 0LiF–LLZTO and 1LiF–LLZTO specimens was conducted under capacity-constant mode (0.2 mAh·cm−2). The CCD value of 1LiF–LLZTO is as high as 1.1 mA·cm−2 at 25 ℃ (Fig. 6(j)). As for the 0LiF–LLZTO sample, its polarization voltage exhibits an obvious increase at a current density of 0.4 mA·cm−2 and experiences a short-circuit at 0.5 mA·cm−2 (Fig. 6(i)).

The variation of the ionic conductivity, electronic conductivity, and CCD with varying amounts of LiF addition is shown in Fig. 6(l). Both ionic and electronic conductivity experience a gradual reduction, while CCD increases firstly and then decreases with the amount of LiF elevating. In particular, with the amount of LiF increased from 0 to 1 wt%, the CCD value appears a sharp rise. When the additional amount of LiF is 1 wt%, a nanoscale LiF layer uniformly coated the grains, efficiently blocking the transmission of electrons (the electronic conductivity drops from 5.17×10−8 to 2.30×10−8 S·cm−1). Moreover, this sample shows the lowest activation energy, which could facilitate lithium ions transfer. These findings collectively elucidate the notable enhancement in lithium dendrite suppression. Upon raising LiF content from 1 to 2 or 3 wt%, electronic conductivity further decreases, accompanied by substantial declines in ionic conductivity. EDS mapping confirms the enrichment of F element in the samples with 2 or 3 wt% LiF. Therefore, the 1LiF–LLZTO specimen shows the highest CCD value. Compared to the 2LiF–LLZTO specimen, the electronic conductivity of the 3LiF–LLZTO specimen exhibits a decline (from 1.33×10−8 to 0.75×10−8 S·cm−1), while ionic conductivity also decreases, and crystallization of LiF occurs, which could explain for the same CCD value of them. In general, the suppression of lithium dendrite in LLZO is influenced by its ionic conductivity, electronic conductivity, and microstructure.

TEM was used to further observe the microstructure of the 1LiF–LLZTO. As shown in Figs. 7(a) and 7(b), the high-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images of 1LiF–LLZTO powders exhibit two brightness features, indicating different atomic numbers of elements contained in the corresponding areas. A thickness of about 10–100 nm layer was uniformly coated on the powders, notably, the brightness of the coated area appears lower, reflecting a smaller atomic number (Fig. 7(a)). The uniform distribution of LiF can be mainly attributed to the low molten temperature of LiF. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) analysis was performed on the powder in Fig. 7(b) (Fig. 7(c)). Figure 7(e) displays the zoom-in view around the tangerine dash line region in Fig. 7(c). The coated layer exhibits an amorphous state, while the spacing of the crystal plane of the powder measures 5.29 Å, corresponding to the (2* 1¯1) crystal plane of LLZTO. Combined with the results of the EDS mapping (Fig. 7(h)), its identity as LLZTO is confirmed. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) in Fig. 7(d), corresponding to the purple square area in Fig. 7(c), indicates that the LLZTO is a single crystal with a [ 1¯13] zone axis in the field of view. Moreover, selected area detail EDS data were recorded to analyze the chemical composition of the interlayer (Table 2). Combined with the brightness contrast change in Fig. 7(a) and the result in Area 1, the coated layer should be the LiF. However, in Area 2, there was no observed amorphous LiF, but the F element was detected, and this result suggests the possibility of the F element doping into the LLZTO.

To further identify whether the F element is doped into LLZTO, the XPS analyses are performed on the surface of the LLZTO pellets [27], as shown in Fig. 8. For the x* wt% LiF–LLZTO (*x* = 0, 1, 2, 3) specimens, aside from the comprehensive O 2s core-like electronic-states of various atomic oxygen at 23.5 eV [28], both observed peaks located at 25.7 and 28.5 eV in Ta 4f spectrum respectively stand for the Ta 4f7/2 and Ta 4f5/2 of the Ta–O bond in LLZTO [29,30]. With the LiF content increased, the relative intensities of the Ta–O and O 2s peaks gradually decreased. This may be due to the increased thickness of LiF, which affects the signal. To get more information about the chemical status of LLZTO, the 1LiF–LLZTO specimen was etched with argon ion for 100 nm [31]. The Ta–O bond peaks were also observed. Moreover, higher binding energy peaks at 29.4 eV (Ta 4f7/2) and 31.6 eV (Ta 4f5/2) were detected, which may originate from the Ta–F bond [32−34]. Figure 8(b) shows the F 1s XPS spectrums of the *x* wt% LiF–LLZTO (*x* = 0, 1, 2, 3) specimens. In the F 1s spectrum of the 1LiF–LLZTO after argon ion etching 100 nm, the peaks at 685.6 and 688.7 eV are assigned to LiF [35] and the Ta–F bond [32], respectively. The detected Li−F and Ta−F bonds imply that during the sintering process at 1320 ℃ for 1LiF–LLZTO, the added LiF not only exists at the grain boundary of LLZTO but also reacts with LLZTO. According to the previous research, the Ta–F bond may be owing to the F− substituting the O2− site during the sintering process [36−39]. This made us suppose that the strong chemical reaction between LiF and LLZTO may also play a role in the uniform distribution of LiF.

To clarify the interfacial contact between the lithium metal and electrolytes, the interfacial morphology of the Li/LLZTO and Li/1LiF–LLZTO were investigated by SEM. The corresponding EDS mapping images are presented in Figs. 9(a) and 9(b). Both specimens exhibit good interfacial contact, aligning with the interface resistance results in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b). This result excludes the influence of the interfacial contact between the lithium metal and electrolytes on the suppression of lithium dendrite. Moreover, the XPS data of the C 1s spectrum of the *x* wt% LiF–LLZTO (*x* = 0, 1, 2, 3) specimens is shown in Fig. 8(c). The peaks located at 284.5 and 289.5 eV correspond to the adventitious carbon and Li2CO3 [21,40]. After the LiF addition, the ratio of Li2CO3 to C–C peaks obviously decreases. It suggests that the coated LiF layer could efficiently inhibit the reaction of LLZTO with H2O and CO2 in air [41], which is attributed to the LiF’s stability in air. The less impurity of Li2CO3 on the LLZTO surface may benefit the CCD performance and improve stability during cycling. In summary, the significant improvement in lithium dendrite suppression is primarily attributed to the presence of a nanoscale insulating LiF layer coated on the grains.

To further probe the cycling stability of the symmetric Li/1LiF–LLZTO/Li cell, prolonged galvanostatic cycling was measured. It shows outstanding long-term cycling stability for over 2000 h at 0.3 mA·cm−2 (Fig. 9(d)). The cycling performance surpasses that of previously reported symmetric cells, as listed in Fig. 9(e) [15,17,19,20,42−52]. Additionally, the inset enlarged curves in Fig. 9(d) exhibit the voltage profiles of 0–5 cycles, 1000–1005 cycles, and 2000–2005 cycles. Throughout these cycles, a small voltage polarization ranging from 37 to 41 mV is consistently maintained. Compared to recent research reported, the change of the voltage polarization is extremely low and the voltage platform profiles are more stable in this work [29,50]. As the inset image in Fig. 9(d) shows, the interface between 1LiF–LLZTO and lithium metal maintains intimate contact after cycling. EIS results confirm that there is no short-circuit, and total resistance only increases by 32 Ω after 2000 h cycling (Fig. 9(c)). These results show that the 1LiF–LLZTO electrolyte possesses outstanding lithium dendrite suppression capability and stability during long-term cycling.

Figures 10(b) and 10(c) exhibit the rate performance of Li/1LiF–LLZTO/LFP cell (its schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 10(a)), with the discharge capacities of 151.2, 145.7, 134.4, and 119.0 mAh·g–1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, and 1 C, respectively. When the rate returns to 0.1 C, its discharge capacity recovers to 146 mAh·g–1, demonstrating the excellent reversible ability. The polarization voltage of the 0.5 C and 1 C curves is a little high, and this may be due to a relatively high impedance between the cathode and LLZTO (Fig. 10(d)). Furthermore, the long-term cycling stability of Li/1LiF–LLZTO/LFP was tested at 0.5 C. In particular, it shows superior capacity retention of 97.1% after 500 cycles, with a reversible capacity of 111.4 mAh·g–1, and an averaged Coulombic efficiency was 99.9% (Fig. 10(g)). In comparison, in this work, the Li/1LiF–LLZTO/LFP full cell exhibits an extended lifespan and higher capacity retention at a high current density than other reported full cells utilizing LLZO electrolyte, lithium metal anode, and LFP cathode (Fig. 10(f)) [17,20,42–46,52–61]. Additionally, the charge/discharge profiles for the 1st, 100th, 200th, 300th, 400th, and 500th cycles are shown in Fig. 10(e). Notably, the voltage traces do not exhibit significant changes during cycling, suggesting outstanding cycle stability of the cell. The EIS curves of Li/1LiF–LLZTO/LFP full cell before and after cycling for 500 cycles indicate a small increase in resistance without short-circuit occurrence (Fig. 10(d)). On the whole, the excellent electrochemical performance mainly benefited from the uniform LiF coated layer suppressing lithium dendrite formation, and the 1LiF–LLZTO electrolytes maintain good stability during the lithium stripping/plating procedure.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, we have successfully synthesized LiF–LLZTO composite electrolytes using the solid-state reaction method, wherein LiF was incorporated into LLZTO. The optimal LiF content in LLZTO was determined to be 1 wt%. This addition results in the uniform coating of nano-scale LiF layer onto LLZTO grains, leading to a reduction in electronic conductivity and the efficiently suppression of lithium dendrite growth. Consequently, the 1LiF–LLZTO exhibited a high CCD of 1.1 mA·cm−2 (capacity-constant mode) and demonstrated excellent cycling performance with a lifespan of over 2000 h at 0.3 mA·cm−2. In addition, full cells with 1LiF–LLZTO and LiFePO4 show outstanding capacity retention, retaining up to 97.1% after 500 cycles at 0.5 C, along with excellent rate performance. The results confirm the effectiveness of introducing a secondary phase with low electronic conductivity into LLZTO, creating a nanoscale coating on its grains, and establishing a continuous electron-blocking network at grain boundaries as a highly efficient strategy for suppressing lithium dendrite growth.
