## 1 Introduction

Currently, with the commercialization of 5G technology, research on 6-generation (6G) has gradually increased in popularity. On the basis of microwave communication, the signal transmission rate (reaching terabytes/s) and Internet of Things (IOT) requirements of 6G technology have increased. Thus, it is necessary to expand the applicability of this technology to the terahertz band next [1,2]. The development of high-performance passive devices that can function in this high-frequency band has become an important issue.

In the classical electromagnetic theory, Mie theory critically contributes to the interactions between electromagnetic waves and matter, and the occurrence of the first magnetic Mie resonance in the dielectric sphere can be approximately explained as Eq. (1) [3]:

(1) f≈c2nr

where *f* is the first magnetic resonance frequency; *c* is the speed of light; *n* is the refractive index of the sphere; *r* is its radius. Correspondingly, due to the decrease in wavelength from the microwave to the terahertz range, scattering and reflection have become the main signal transmission modes in nonline-of-sight (NLOS) transmission links [4,5]. Recently, dielectric metamaterials, which are conventionally used in many fields, have been considered for effective application in the terahertz band. The Mie resonance results from the displacement current rather than the conduction current, and the performance of the dielectric materials can determine the total loss [6−9]. Hence, research on the loss mechanism of dielectric materials covering 6G technology is of critical importance and can increase the number of different ceramic-based metamaterials.

The quantum, magnetic, optical, catalytic, and sensor characteristics of ANb2O6 (A = Zn, Co, Mn, Ni, etc.) columbite niobates, which are classic rutile-like systems, have been widely studied due to the similar structures of ABBABB-type (B means the ion in B-site) connections and the electronic structures of different metal ions in the A-site, especially transition metal ions with different 3d orbitals. B means the ions in B-site [10−14]. Moreover, in the field of wireless communication, this system is appealing for application as dielectric ceramics. Typically, ZnNb2O6 and MgNb2O6 exhibit low loss in the microwave range (*Q*×*f* > 80,000 GHz) and are candidates for use in the fabrication of high-performance dielectric resonators, and the microwave dielectric properties of ANb2O6 columbite niobates are considered to be very sensitive to the preparation process [15,16]. However, why ANb2O6 columbite niobates with similar α-PbO2 structures but different transition metal ions at the A-site always exhibit large differences in the *Q*×*f* value is unknown. For instance, NiNb2O6 and ZnNb2O6, which have similar conductivities and the same *Pbcn* space group, have similar dielectric constants but ﬁvefold differences in their *Q*×*f* values [17]. Moreover, there are few studies on the terahertz response of this system. Therefore, we chose ZnNb2O6, CoNb2O6, MnNb2O6, and NiNb2O6, which have different three-dimensional (3D)-layer electronic structures, to further explore the loss mechanism of this system covering the 6G band, extending its application area to the terahertz region. Herein, low-loss dielectric ceramics and metamaterials are effectively combined in the terahertz range. First, we fabricated ZnNb2O6, CoNb2O6, MnNb2O6, and NiNb2O6 ceramics using a solid-state method and a sol–gel method for verification. By combining the data from the terahertz time domain spectrum, the terahertz response of the ANb2O6 columbite niobates was initially investigated. Moreover, due to the limitations of current magnetic loss measurement methods, we compare different preparation processes and corresponding structural analyses, and the extrinsic factors that affect the microwave loss in this system are screened; thus, the microwave magnetic loss characteristics are evaluated. Then, according to Mie theory, a prototypical metamaterial reflector based on MnNb2O6 ceramics is designed to verify the progressiveness of these ceramics in the terahertz range. Low-loss microwave dielectric ceramics will likely become important in future terahertz passive devices.

### 2.1 Preparation by solid-state route

During solid-state preparation, the raw materials used were high-purity powders: ZnO, CoO, MnCO3, NiO, and Nb2O5 (> 99.9%, Aladdin, China). The powders were weighed to create ANb2O6 and ball-milled in deionized water for 6 h. The composition of the grinding balls was yttria-stabilized zirconia. After the drying process, the mixed powders were presintered at 950 °C for 5 h to preliminarily synthesize the main crystal phase. To obtain different resonant frequencies in the microwave range, the granulated powders were pressed into pellets with 10 mm in diameter and 4–5 mm in thickness, 15 mm in diameter and 7–8 mm in thickness, and 6 mm in diameter and 2–3 mm in thickness. These pellets were sintered at 1125–1250 °C for 6 h at a heating rate of 5 °C/min in air.

### 2.2 Preparation by sol–gel method

To study the influence of the preparation process, MnNb2O6 and NiNb2O6 samples were also prepared by the sol–gel method. The metal ions in the A-site originated from high-purity acetates (> 99.9%, Aladdin, China), and C6H4NNbO12 (> 99.9%, Aladdin, China) was used to provide the Nb5+ in the B-site. Moreover, slightly excess (molar ratio of metal ions of approximately 2 : 1) citric acid was used as a chelating agent to support the complexation reaction, and magnetic stirring was used for mixing at 80 °C. Moreover, ammonia (> 99.9%, Aladdin, China) was selected to modify the pH until clear, and the optimal pH was approximately 6–7 to maintain the uniformity of the crystal size. After drying and aging for 24 h, the sols became gels. Then, the gels were calcined at 700 and 950 °C to remove the organics and synthesize the main crystal phase, resulting in the ANb2O6 powders. Through ball milling, the granulated powders were pressed into pellets via the solid-state method described above. These pellets were sintered at 1000–1150 °C for 6 h at a heating rate of 5 °C/min in air.

### 2.3 Simulation of ANb<sub>2</sub>O<sub>6</sub> ceramic-based metamaterial reflector

In the simulation, the reflection spectra of the ANb2O6 all-ceramic metamaterial reflector were obtained by the commercial software COMSOL Multiphysics 6.0 based on the finite element method (FEM), and the *Q*×*f* value was used to characterize the loss characteristics. Then, the numerical complex permittivity of the ANb2O6 ceramic blocks was set to *ε'*[1 − (*f*/*Q*)i] [18], where *ε*′ is the dielectric constant; *Q* is the 1/loss at the desired frequency; i is the imaginary unit. Moreover, the polarization direction of the incident electric field was on the *x*-axis, and periodic boundary conditions were applied on the *x* and *y* boundaries to simultaneously form a metamaterial array in free space.

At the terahertz frequency, the dimensions of the blocks were 0.1 mm × 0.1 mm × 0.1 mm. These dimensions were approximately 1/5 of the operating wavelength. Assuming a nonmagnetic sphere with relative permeability *μ*b = 1, relative permittivity *ε* = *n**2*, and radius *r*s in free space, the magnetic dipole (MD) portion of the scattered field of the dielectric sphere was proportional to Eq. (2) [19]:

(2) bm=ψm(nx)ψm′(x)−nψm(x)ψm′(nx)ψm(nx)ξm′(x)−nξm(x)ψm′(nx)

where *x* = *k*0*r*0; *k*0 is the free-space wavenumber; *r*0 is the displacement vector; *ψ**m*(*x*) and *ξ**m*(*x*) are the Riccati–Bessel functions; *ψ*'*m*(*x*) and *ξ'**m*(*x*) are the first derivatives of *ψ**m*(*x*) and *ξ'**m*(*x*), respectively. The electric dipole portion of the scattered field was proportional to Eq. (3) [19]:

(3) am=nψm(nx)ψm′(x)−ψm(x)ψm′(nx)nψm(nx)ξm′(x)−ξm(x)ψm′(nx)

where *am* is the *m*-order electric multipole scattering coefficient. Based on effective medium theory, for *μ*b = 1, only by adjusting the period could the position of the electric resonance move in the low-frequency direction to form a reflection passband coupled with magnetic resonance, which guided the simulations [20].

### 2.4 Measurements

The crystal structure of the bulk samples was determined by a powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/MAX-2500, Rigaku, Japan) with a range of 10°–70° and a step size of 0.02°. Rietveld refinement by general structure analysis system (GSAS-II) software was used to analyze the structural characteristics of the ANb2O6 samples [21]. The starting models were ZnNb2O6 (ICSD#36290), CoNb2O6 (ICSD#15854), MnNb2O6 (ICSD#15856), and NiNb2O6 (ICSD#15853). Herein, the bulk densities were obtained with a Mettler–Toledo analytical balancer (XS64, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). The microstructures of the crystal morphology were observed by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; NanoSEM 430, FEI, USA). The valence of the transition ions was analyzed by an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Escalab 250Xi, Thermo Fisher, USA) using an Al Kα X-ray source. The test samples were composed of ceramic powder ground after sintering and passed through an 80 mesh sieve.

The bulk resistivities of the samples were measured by a high resistance tester (4339B, Agilent, USA). The dielectric properties of the bulk samples in the microwave range were measured by a network analyzer (8720ES, Agilent, USA). The dielectric constant was measured by the Hakki–Coleman method modified by Courtney, and the unloaded *Q* values were measured by the resonant cavity method with the TE01δ mode [22,23]. Here, the acquisition of resonant frequencies under the TE01δ mode depended on the diameter, height, and dielectric constant of the sample. Correspondingly, to achieve a reasonable diameter/height ratio during measurement, samples of different sizes require resonant cavities of different sizes. To achieve distinct resonant frequencies, we also designed three cavities of different sizes [24]. The temperature coefficient of the resonant frequency (*τ*f) was determined from the resonant frequencies in the temperature range of 25–85 °C and can be defined by Eq. (4):

(4) τf(ppm/°C)=f85−f25f25×60×106

where *f*25 and *f*85 are the resonant frequencies at 25 and 85 °C, respectively.

Similar to our previous work, the terahertz dielectric properties of bulk samples at room temperature were obtained by a terahertz time-domain spectroscope (TAS7500TS, Advantest, Japan) in dry air (the system generates terahertz pulses from a photoconductive antenna, with a frequency range of 0.1–4 THz and a time resolution of 2 fs). When the spectral resolution is 3.8 GHz, the time-domain scanning range is 262 ps. The humidity is approximately 6%, which is determined by Eqs. (5)–(8) [25]:

(5) Esam(ω)Eref(ω)=t12t21eiL(k−k0)e−α(ω)L/2

(6) k=2πn(ω)λ0,k0=2πλ0

(7) ϵ′=n2(ω)−(α(ω)λ04π)2

(8) tanδ=α(ω)n(ω)λ0/(2π)n2(ω)−(α(ω)λ04π)2

where *E*sam(*ω*) and *E*ref(*ω*) are the *E*-field amplitudes of the sample and the reference, respectively; *t*12 and *t*21 are the frequency-dependent complex Fresnel transmission coefficients; *L* is the thickness of the ceramic sample; *k* and *k*0 are the propagation wave vectors in the sample and in the vacuum, respectively; *λ*0 is the wave length in vacuum. *ε′* is the dielectric constant, and tan*δ* is the tangent of the loss angle. The refractive index *n*(*ω*) and frequency-dependent power absorption *α*(*ω*) of the samples could be obtained from the measured data. The bulk samples measured here were pressed into pellets 15 mm in diameter and 0.8–1 mm in thickness.

### 3.1 Analysis of the ANb<sub>2</sub>O<sub>6</sub> crystal structure

Figure S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) shows the relative densities and SEM images of the ANb2O6 (A = Zn, Co, Mn, and Ni) ceramics obtained by the solid-state method. Generally, the dielectric response of ANb2O6 (A = Zn, Co, Mn, and Ni) ceramics is very sensitive to the sintering temperature. Thus, the selection of an appropriate densification temperature is considered very important for eliminating extrinsic factors to generate precise dielectric properties. Herein, due to the 1 : 2 proportion of A2+ and Nb5+ in the ANb2O6 ceramics, the columbite phase had to be stable. The relative density (*ρ*relative) was an obvious feature that could characterize the best sintering temperature by the bulk density (*ρ*bulk) and theoretical density (*ρ*theory) (Eq. (9)):

(9) ρrelative=ρbulkρtheory

Herein, ZnNb2O6, CoNb2O6, and MnNb2O6 exhibited similar trends in terms of variations in their bulk densities, while the densification temperature of NiNb2O6 slightly increased. The optimal sintering temperatures were 1175 °C for ZnNb2O6, CoNb2O6, and MnNb2O6 and 1225 °C for NiNb2O6, which are reasonable values according to previous studies [15,26]. Moreover, dense grains with high crystallinity levels were clearly observed.

### 3.2 Terahertz responses of ANb<sub>2</sub>O<sub>6</sub> ceramics

Figure 2(a) represents the microwave responses of the ANb2O6 (A = Zn, Co, Mn, and Ni) ceramics prepared by the solid-state method, which exhibited the greatest densification, obviously good crystallinity, and good surface morphology. In the microwave range, the relative dielectric constant (*ε*r) and *τ*f of the four columbites were similar, while the *Q*×*f* values showed significant differences. These trends were basically in accordance with the results of previous studies [16,27,28]. Generally, the microwave dielectric loss of dielectric ceramics is mainly determined by extrinsic and intrinsic factors. The extrinsic factors come from a wide variety of sources, including pores, defects, and microstructure [29−31]. The intrinsic factors mainly come from the lattice vibration of the crystal structure of the sample, which is affected by its damping factor. Therefore, the loss mechanism of ANb2O6 dielectric ceramics should be comprehensively considered from the two aforementioned aspects.

In terms of extrinsic factors, the leakage loss of dielectric ceramics in the microwave band is an important factor affecting the microwave loss, which is often attributed to changes in defects or valences of elements [32−34]. Obviously, the extrinsic factors from the phase composition and microstructure were excluded as much as possible in the analysis above. The grain size was not directly related to microwave loss, as the largest and smallest grain sizes corresponded to the second to last smallest and largest Q*×*f* values, respectively, in the ANb2O6 samples. Thus, defects were suppressed to the greatest extent, and the XPS spectra of Zn 2p, Co 2p, Mn 2p, and Ni 2p were used to determine the valence changes in the ANb2O6 samples, as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. Here, the fitting of the XPS data was based on the Gaussian Lorentzian function, and reference information on the element binding energies was obtained from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) XPS database [35]. In theory, the binding energies of transition metal ions with the same element and different valence states of the same element are not distinct. The differences needed to be identified from the peak shape and peak positions. For Zn2+ with the 3d10 configuration, there was basically no valence change. Compared with that of Co3+, the binding energy of Co2+ exhibited a larger energy difference between 2p3/2 and 2p1/2. Mn2+ presented a satellite oscillation peak of 2p3/2 and a large binding energy at the tail of the oscillation peak. The Ni 2p results basically confirmed the characteristics of Ni2+ because its binding energy was lower than that of Ni3+ [36−41]. In addition, to compensate for the error caused by the ligand field, the insulation properties of each ANb2O6 sample were further tested and calculated, as shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. The corresponding results showed that all the ANb2O6 dielectric ceramics were good insulators, and the resistivities were on the same order of magnitude, which also proved the high stability of the crystal structure and valence state here. In ligand field theory, the valences of ions in compounds are not absolutely invariable due to charge transfer occurring when bonding with other ions. Even in extreme cases, the cationic valence changes slightly in this system, and the corresponding anions provide compensating effects without the presence of excess carriers and exhibit high resistivities. Specifically, there was no significant change in the *Q*×*f* value based on leakage loss.

Subsequently, the intrinsic factors were further studied by terahertz time domain spectroscopy. In Figs. 2(b)–2(d), the terahertz responses of the ANb2O6 (A = Zn, Co, Mn, and Ni) ceramics were determined, and the frequency range was close to the intrinsic vibrational region of the molecules. Obviously, there were minimal differences in the dielectric constants of both the microwave band and the terahertz band. Moreover, the ANb2O6 dielectric ceramics also exhibited a low loss and was approximately transparent to terahertz waves in a certain frequency range, especially CoNb2O6, MnNb2O6, and NiNb2O6, not ZnNb2O6, which had ultrahigh *Q*×*f* values in the microwave range. Here, the variation in the dielectric constant demonstrated that the polarization mechanism of the ANb2O6 dielectric ceramics was almost the same in both the microwave and terahertz frequency bands, and the contribution of the dielectric constant in the terahertz frequency band was mainly attributed to electron displacement polarization and ion displacement polarization. Moreover, the difference in the dielectric constant did not depend only on the ionic polarizability, and additional factors, such as crystal structural fluctuations during polarization, should be considered [42]. Furthermore, the variation in loss proved that intrinsic factors contributed most to the entire loss process of ANb2O6 ceramics in the terahertz band, and it could also be inferred that the large difference in microwave loss was not clearly determined by intrinsic loss [25].

To verify the above results, the crystal structure should be further analyzed. In Table 2, based on the XRD results, the bond lengths in the ANb2O6 ceramics were calculated, which could be used to further clarify the effects of A-site transition metal ions on the crystal structure. In columbites, ions in the A-site are connected to two O2− ions with different occupations to form an oxygen octahedron structure. Since A2+ ions were affected by the crystal field formed by the surrounding Nb5+ ions and other A2+ ions in the oxygen ligand field, they exhibited three different bond lengths in the oxygen octahedron, in which A–O1 shared an O1 atom with the adjacent Nb–O1, due to the distorted tetragonal crystal field formed by four adjacent Nb5+ ions. A–O2 shares O2 atoms with adjacent A–O2 and Nb–O2, which is affected by the distorted triangular crystal field formed by A2+ adjacent to two Nb5+ molecules. Due to the different distortion angles of the oxygen octahedron, the A–O2 bonds had two different lengths. Correspondingly, in the oxygen octahedron in the B-site, six bond lengths were obtained, in which O3 only appeared in the connection of the Nb–O octahedron. Moreover, in the Raman spectra in Fig. S4 in the ESM, the Raman shift of the Nb–O symmetric stretching mode also indicated the average variation in the bond length of the Nb–O bonds [29]. Finally, the deformation characteristics determined by the four different transition metal ions themselves (the oxygen ligand field was a medium weak field, resulting in the transition metal ions having a weak field and high spin; i.e., Zn2+:3d10, second-order Jahn–Teller effect; Co2+:3d7, distortion in the *XZ* plane with high spin; Mn2+:3d5, distortion in all directions with high spin; Ni2+:3d8, distortion in the *Z*-axis and *XY* plane) were suppressed, and the oxygen octahedron in the A-site was distorted [43]. This structural fluctuation was also applicable for describing the intrinsic loss, and the loss mechanism of the ANb2O6 ceramics in the terahertz band was relatively clear.

### 3.3 Prediction of magnetic loss and terahertz applications

Undoubtedly, the factors above could not determine the upper limit of the *Q*×*f* value of ANb2O6 ceramics in the microwave range, and we assumed a common but often neglected loss mechanism in low-loss microwave dielectric ceramics and magnetic loss. The complex refractive index should be used to express the response of matter to electromagnetic waves (Eq. (11)) [48]:

(11) n=ϵμ=(ϵ′+iϵ′′)(μ′+iμ′′)

where *ε* and *μ* are the permittivity (*ε'* is the real part of permittivity, i.e., dielectric constant; *ε''* is the the imaginary part of permittivity) and permeability (*μ'* is the real part of permeability; *μ''* is the imaginary part of permeability), respectively. In microwave dielectric ceramics, the magnetic part is often ignored, and dielectric loss is considered the dominant factor of the whole microwave loss. With the continuous development of this field, a variety of magnetic ions have been used to prepare microwave dielectric ceramics. In the microwave range, if the real part of the permittivity is multiplied by the imaginary part of the permeability in Eq. (11), this assumption might not be reasonable, especially in a system with low loss.

In ANb2O6 ceramics, the oxygen ligand field in the crystal structure of ANb2O6 is a medium weak field, and its oxygen octahedral central ion has a large high spin component. Thus, it is relatively easy to form outer orbital complexes, as shown in Fig. 3(a) [43]. The outer electrons of the oxygen ions entered the 4s empty orbital of the transition metal ions and formed a sp3 hybrid orbital to retain the unpaired electrons in the transition metal ions. The density of states (DOS) diagrams in Fig. S5 in the ESM also indicate that the electronic states mainly arose from metal ions d and O p. Moreover, there was a clear hybridization of electron orbitals between the metal ions and O2− ions. Moreover, the high density of states near the Fermi level could also indicate the existence of high spin states. At the same time, in the common edge connection mode of the AO6 octahedron, the bond angle of the distorted octahedron was not 90°. Thus, super exchange could occur between adjacent A-site ions, which is a necessary condition for magnetic loss. Nevertheless, even if resonance occurred, the magnitude of the loss was less than 10−4–10−3, which was difficult to measure directly. Thus, this impact was often ignored.

*
Fig. 3(a) Crystal structure and hybrid orbitals in ANb2O6 (A = Zn, Co, Mn, and Ni) ceramics. (b) Metal resonant cavities of different sizes, and (c) Q*×*f* values and bulk densities of MnNb2O6 ceramics obtained by different methods at different resonant frequencies.

As shown in Fig. 3(b), to obtain additional evidence, we utilized metal resonant cavities of different sizes to evaluate the microwave loss of the ANb2O6 ceramics at different resonant frequencies and considered factors related to preparation. For instance, Fig. 3(c) further shows the bulk density and *Q*×*f* values of MnNb2O6 ceramics of different sizes prepared by the sol–gel method and solid-state method. Here, the acquisition of different resonant frequencies relied on changing the size (diameter and height) of the samples, and the smaller the size was, the greater the resonant frequency. On the basis of ceramic densification by both methods, the corresponding resonant frequency of the sample with a diameter of 6 mm was approximately 14.3 GHz, that of the 10 mm sample was approximately 9.5 GHz, and that of the 15 mm sample was approximately 5.7 GHz. Figure S6 in the ESM shows that the surface morphologies of the samples obtained by the sol–gel method were dense and complete, no pores were observed, and there was no significant difference between them. Compared with the results from the solid-state method, the grain size was relatively larger and more irregular, while there was no significant difference in the corresponding *Q*×*f* value. Thus, it could be inferred that the loss was not mainly affected by grain size or morphology. Moreover, the *Q*×*f* value was mainly determined by the resonance frequency, and it doubled to more than 40,000 GHz at 14.3 GHz, which was obviously contrary to the condition in which the *Q*×*f* value of microwave dielectric ceramics was approximately constant in the microwave frequency band [16,49]. Similar phenomena were also obtained by the solid-state method except for ZnNb2O6, and the relative change in the CoNb2O6 samples was small (the *Q*×*f* value of the 6 mm sample was 92,317 GHz). For ZnNb2O6, the *Q*×*f* value obtained by the solid-state method decreased slightly to approximately 85,000 GHz for the 15 mm sample, and there was no significant change between the other samples (10 mm sample: 110,275 GHz, 6 mm sample: 108,313 GHz), which could be caused by the decrease in the consistency of the large sample. Thus, it could be inferred that there were other loss mechanisms in the microwave frequency band, which violates the traditional definition of the *Q*×*f* value. Combined with the terahertz test results and the crystal structure analysis, magnetic loss is the only possible cause of this phenomenon, which is often ignored in conventional testing. As the frequency increased, the impact of magnetic loss decreased. Through this frequency conversion testing method, the effect of magnetic loss on low-loss microwave dielectric ceramics was experimentally proven. Notably, the *Q*×*f* value of CoNb2O6 did not severely deteriorate when Co2+ ions were present in the A-site. Whether its structure has a certain inhibitory effect on the generation of magnetic loss needs to be further studied.

On the basis of this prediction, the influence of magnetic field on the microwave loss of the ANb2O6 system might not be negligible under these conditions, and it needed to be limited to the establishment of such low-loss ceramic systems in the microwave range. Furthermore, it might also exist in other ceramic systems, such as those containing rare earth ions, but was not necessarily the decisive factor due to the complex coordination environments, structural symmetry, and electronic structure. In the terahertz band, the ANb2O6 ceramics showed low loss and no obvious magnetic absorption, so their use could be extended to related applications in passive devices in the terahertz band, especially for MnNb2O6 and NiNb2O6.

Without the interference of magnetic loss, the dispersion characteristics of microwave dielectric ceramics could be used in the terahertz range, and MnNb2O6 or NiNb2O6 should have a *Q*×*f* value not lower than that of ZnNb2O6. Here, we designed and simulated a broadband terahertz reflector using ANb2O6 ceramic blocks to demonstrate the effect of loss on terahertz passive devices. The appropriate dielectric constant of ANb2O6 ceramics could ensure Mie resonance while achieving a larger linewidth under a single mode (electrical dipole (ED) or MD), and the lower loss determined the lower absorption loss of electromagnetic waves, thus resulting in greater reflectivity. Moreover, the design of metamaterial structures determines the effective coupling between ED and MD, further expanding the working bandwidth. Figure 4 shows the simulation results of the ANb2O6 ceramic-based metamaterial reflector with different *Q*×*f* values. According to the results above in Fig. 2, the real part of the permittivity we selected was 21 (as MnNb2O6), and the *Q*×*f* values for comparison were set in the range from 10,000 to 110,000 GHz. To adjust the position of the electric resonance, the period (*p*) was 0.16 mm, and the reflection results and electromagnetic field distributions are shown in Figs. 4(b) and 4(d), respectively. The reflector based on the ceramics with a high *Q*×*f* could achieve a relatively high reflectivity (> 90%) in a wide terahertz band (> 150 GHz), and if the *Q*×*f* value decreased to 10,000 GHz, the highest reflectivity was only 70%, which could not meet the application requirements. From the electromagnetic field distribution, due to the permeability near 1, the magnetic resonance was hardly affected by the period. The electric resonance can be modified by the period. The coupling and superposition effects between EDs and MDs can be intuitively analyzed through multipole decomposition, as shown in Fig. 4(c). Obviously, without the influence of magnetic loss, a low-loss ANb2O6 system would have broad application prospects in the terahertz range. Specifically, based on a comprehensive understanding of the material properties, the corresponding terahertz devices should be further developed.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, we predicted and demonstrated the loss mechanism of ANb2O6 (A = Zn, Co, Mn, and Ni) columbite niobates in the microwave and terahertz frequency ranges. Combined with the results of our tests and analysis, the magnetic loss from transition metal ions and amplification by resonance in low-loss ANb2O6 ceramics were proven to the greatest extent, which could be the reason for the large differences in the *Q*×*f* values for similar crystal structures in the current microwave range. In the terahertz frequency band, these ceramics all exhibited relatively low losses due to the depletion of the magnetic response.

Then, by designing a broadband metamaterial reflector as a prototype, the application advantages of ANb2O6 ceramics in the terahertz band were also demonstrated through simulations. In addition to broadband terahertz devices, large *τ*f values of ANb2O6 could be further optimized, with the potential to develop temperature-stable terahertz devices. Extending to ceramic-based dielectric metamaterials, the continuous development of ceramics could create additional high-performance metamaterial devices in the 6G era.
