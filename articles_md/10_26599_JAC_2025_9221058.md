## 1 Introduction

The gradual popularization of novel wireless communication technologies, especially fifth generation (5G) networks, and brilliant terminal generalization, has significantly transformed the way people interact with technology, enabling a more connected and intelligent lifestyle. These advancements not only push the boundaries of communication but also catalyze the proliferation of electronic devices across various sectors, increase intelligent living, and accelerate the prosperity of electronic technology [1,2]. However, unwanted and inevitable electromagnetic (EM) wave radiation inevitably interferes with the regular operation of electronic instruments and simultaneously threatens the health of humans [3]. This problem is especially pronounced with the rise of 5G, where the frequency bands in use can pose greater risks of interference. EM wave absorbing materials have been proven to be effective countermeasures for eliminating EM wave pollution. To satisfy the demands of extensive applications, EM wave absorbing materials should possess strong absorption intensity, broadband absorption bandwidth, and lightweight characteristics [4]. These properties are critical for the widespread adoption of EM wave absorbers in various fields, ranging from communication devices to medical equipment. Therefore, many strategies have been proposed to construct lightweight and superior EM wave absorbing materials, and the synergetic effect has been considered a classical theory for promoting EM wave attenuation [5,6].

Recently, the utilization of defects and unbalanced sites in the AB2X4 structure to induce polarization losses and enhance EM wave absorption performance has been extensively investigated [7,8]. These structural modifications aim to exploit the intrinsic properties of materials to increase the efficiency of EM wave attenuation at various frequencies. However, a high density of the structure usually results in an excessive filling ratio to pursue satisfactory absorption performance, which is contrary to its lightweight characteristics [9,10]. In recent years, substantial efforts have focused on designing and constructing lightweight and high-performance EM wave absorbing materials with hollow structures, such as self-assembly strategies, cation-exchange reactions, or template methods [11−13]. These methods leverage the unique properties of hollow structures, such as increased surface area and internal porosity, to improve the absorption performance without adding significant weight. From the viewpoint of structural design, hollow engineering is an effective strategy for adjusting the relative permittivity and the impedance band gap, thus optimizing the impedance matching and realizing strong EM wave absorption intensity with a low filler ratio [14−16]. However, the narrow absorption bandwidth still restricts their practical application. On the basis of previous investigations, heterogeneous interfaces can effectively tune the electronic bands between multiphase heterojunctions, resulting in broadband absorption bandwidth, which provides indispensable insight for the construction of EM wave absorbing materials with ultrabroadband absorption [17,18]. However, the absorption bandwidth has difficulty surpassing 10 GHz owing to the limited interface polarization. In addition, the challenge of achieving both high absorption intensity and a wide bandwidth remains a major hurdle in the development of next-generation absorptive materials.

Quantum dots (QDs), as an emerging focus of research in many fields, have been extensively investigated because of their unique electronic structure, quantum size effect, and a range of remarkable properties [19−21]. DQs, ranging from 2 to 10 nm, constitute a vital category of low-dimensional semiconductor materials. Their small size allows them to exhibit distinctive quantum properties that are not observed in bulk materials, making them highly attractive for a variety of applications, including EM wave absorption. At the nanoscale, electronic energy levels near the Fermi level undergo a transition from continuous states to discrete energy levels, and the confinement of electronic motion causes continuous energy bands to break down into discrete energy levels; thus, the quantization effect becomes increasingly prominent as the size diminishes due to spatial motion constraints [22]. As a result, these quantum dots can provide a more effective means of controlling the electromagnetic properties of materials. In this state, the ground-state energy level undergoes a blueshift, with electron movement constrained at the quantum scale, thereby imparting semiconductor-like properties [23−25]. Therefore, it is important to explore the interaction mechanism and electric field coordination between DQs and macroscopic composites. For example, the high density of elemental sites in AB2X4 facilitates size reduction, effectively confining electron movement, whereas the cooperative effects of multisite defects, lattice distortions, charge mismatches, and energy-level transitions contribute to the enhancement of polarization losses [26,27]. However, the single-phase structure of AB2X4 simplifies loss mechanisms, thereby restricting impedance matching and multilevel loss pathways, ultimately leading to a narrow absorption bandwidth [28−30]. This highlights the necessity of combining multiple strategies to address both the absorption intensity and bandwidth simultaneously. Thus, broadening the effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) and simultaneously improving the absorption intensity by constructing multiple heterogeneous interfaces and minimizing the particle size of magnetic domains is imperative.

Inspired by the aforementioned investigations, we successfully fabricated hollow carbon nanocages with CoFe2Se4 quantum dots (HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs) that simultaneously possess strong absorption intensity and ultrabroadband absorption bandwidth by using tightly arranged SiO2 spheres as hard templates. During the pyrolysis process, the Co–Fe–oleate complex reacts with the selenium powder to form CoFe2Se4-QDs, and the subsequent removal of SiO2 spheres results in the formation of a hollow cavity. Owing to the combined advantages of a unique electronic structure, abundant heterogeneous interfaces, and promoted impedance matching, a minimum reflection loss (*R*L) of −67.6 dB and an ultra broadband absorption bandwidth of 11.4 GHz are achieved for HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs with a thickness of 4 mm. This study provides critical insight into the construction of ultra broadband EM wave absorbing materials.

## 2 Experimental

**Synthesis of the Co-Fe-oleate complex**: Briefly, 1 mmol of CoCl2·6H2O, 1 mmol of FeCl3·6H2O, and 5 mmol of sodium oleate were dissolved in a mixed solution containing 5 mL of ethanol, 5 mL of deionized water, and 7 mL of hexane. The mixture was refluxed at 90 °C for 4 h. After that, the obtained products were cooled to room temperature and washed with deionized water several times by gently shaking. Aged for a period, the aqueous solution at the bottom was removed, yielding a black Co–Fe–oleate complex.

**Synthesis of tightly arranged SiO****2**: First, 1 mL of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) and 0.3 mL of NH3·H2O were dissolved in 20 mL of ethanol/water mixture (*v* : *v* = 1 : 1). Subsequently, the mixed solution was stirred for 40 min, and the white products were centrifuged three times to obtain tightly arranged SiO2.

**Synthesis of HCNs@CoFe****2****Se****4****-QDs**: 2 g of tightly arranged SiO2, 2.05 g of selenium powder, and 4 g of Co**–**Fe**–**oleate complex were ground in 4 mL of oleylamine, and then the mixed slurry was placed into a tube furnace and annealed at 650 °C for 2 h under an atmosphere of Ar. The obtained products were washed with 5 M NaOH for 24 h to remove the SiO2 template, resulting in the formation of HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs.

**Synthesis of amorphous carbon with CoFe****2****Se****4** **particles (AC@CoFe****2****Se****4****) and hollow carbon nanocages with CoFe particles (HCNs@CoFe)**: In comparison, AC@CoFe2Se4 was obtained by directly annealing the Co**–**Fe**–**oleate complex and selenium powder at 650 °C for 2 h under an atmosphere of Ar, and HCN@CoFe was obtained by annealing the mixture of tightly arranged SiO2 and Co**–**Fe**–**oleate complexes at 650 °C for 2 h under an atmosphere of Ar and subsequently removing the SiO2 template.

**Characterizations:** All composite materials are pyrolyzed using a tube furnace (OTF-1200X, Hefei Kejing Material Technology Co., Ltd., China). The microstructures were characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Verios G4, FEI, USA) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200X, FEI, USA). The crystal structure was determined via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 DISCOVER A25, Bruker, Germany). The surface composition and valence state of the elements were analyzed by an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Phoibos 100 spectrometer, SPECS, Germany). The hysteresis loops were analyzed by a multifunctional physical property measurement system (PPMS; CFMS-14T, Cryogenic, UK). The reflection loss, impedance match degree, radar cross section simulation, and computational analysis are presented in the ESM.

## 3 Results and discussion

The detailed synthetic process of HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs is clearly depicted in Fig. 1. Initially, tightly arranged SiO2 spheres with uniform diameters were obtained via high-speed centrifugation (Fig. 2(a) and Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)) and employed as hard templates to realize hollow engineering. In addition, with the assistance of oleylamine grinding, the as-synthesized tightly arranged SiO2 microspheres with abundant hydroxyl groups can absorb the CoFe–oleate complex on their surface via van der Waals interactions, and the gaps between the densely packed SiO2 microspheres are completely filled, resulting in the formation of the SiO2@Co–Fe–oleate complex. After pyrolysis mixing with the selenium powder, the CoFe–oleate complex was transformed into an ultrathin carbon shell with CoFe2Se4-QDs wrapped on the surface of the SiO2 microspheres (Fig. 2(b) and Fig. S2 in the ESM). The successful formation of SiO2@CoFe2Se4-QDs was further characterized by the TEM images in Fig. S3 in the ESM. Clearly, the SiO2 microspheres are completely encapsulated and connected by a carbon shell with CoFe2Se4-QDs. After removing the SiO2 templates, the SiO2@CoFe2Se4-QDs are transformed to HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs, and the results are verified by the obvious hollow cavity, as shown in Fig. 2(c) and Figs. S4 and S5 in the ESM. To illustrate the cooperative effects of CoFe2Se4-QDs and hollow engineering on the EM wave absorption capacity, AC@CoFe2Se4 was synthesized by directly annealing the Co–Fe–oleate complex, and HCNs@CoFe was synthesized by annealing a mixture of tightly arranged SiO2 and Co–Fe–oleate complexes and subsequently removing the SiO2 template. Specifically, AC@CoFe2Se4 has a casual morphology, as shown in Figs. 2(d) and 2(g) and Fig. S6 in the ESM, and the interplanar spacing of 0.235 nm is attributed to the (112) plane of CoFe2Se4 (Fig. S7 in the ESM). For HCN@CoFe, as shown in Figs. 2(e), 2(f), and 2(h), the obvious cavity demonstrates the presence of a hollow structure, and the particles with a diameter in the range of 50–200 nm match well with the elemental maps of Co and Fe (Fig. S8 in the ESM), confirming the formation of CoFe alloys. Interestingly, the catalytic effect of CoFe alloys is favorable for the growth of graphite carbon during the pyrolysis process (Fig. S9 in the ESM), favoring the increase in electron transfer and conduction loss [31,32]. Compared with HCNs@CoFe, the presence of selenium powder during the pyrolysis process effectively reduced the particle size, resulting in the formation of CoFe2Se4-QDs dispersed in the carbon matrix, as presented in Fig. 2(i).

To emphasize the presence of selenium powder in decreasing the particle size, HRTEM images of HCNs@CoFe and HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs are presented in Fig. 3. For HCN@CoFe, as shown in Fig. 3(a), these CoFe alloys embedded in the carbon matrix tend to aggregate owing to their intrinsic magnetic characteristics, and the corresponding diameters are in the range of 50–150 nm. In Fig. 3(b), the lattice fringe of 0.201 nm is ascribed to the (110) plane of CoFe. In addition, abundant defects and heterogeneous interfaces can also be observed in Fig. 3(c), which could act as dipole centers to induce defects and interface polarization for the enhancement of EM wave absorption. For HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs in Fig. 3(d), large CoFe particles completely disappear, and CoFe2Se4-QDs with Moiré fringes (Fig. 3(e)) and lattice hybridization (Fig. 3(f)) among different grain boundaries are clearly observed. The existence of defects induced by lattice strain verifies the stacking of CoFe2Se4-QDs with different crystal planes or orientations, and the lattice fringes at 0.235 and 0.225 nm correspond to the (112) and (211) crystal planes of CoFe2Se4, respectively. On the basis of polarization resonance, different crystal planes or orientations, accompanied by abundant heterogeneous interfaces (Fig. 3(j)), could lead to modified electronic bands or intercrystalline interface coupling, thus inducing discrepant band realignment to enhance dipole relaxation and interfacial polarization [33,34]. The element distribution maps in Fig. 3(g) match well with the HRTEM image of HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs, illustrating the successful formation of a hollow cavity. As depicted in Fig. 3(h), these indistinct crystal planes (green and brown regions) confirm the presence of lattice hybridization in CoFe2Se4-QDs. On the one hand, the decrease in size of the CoFe2Se4-QDs constrains their resistance to electron beam exposure. On the other hand, CoFe2Se4-QDs are completely wrapped by graphitic carbon generated by the catalytic effect of magnetic particles and amorphous carbon layers originating from the pyrolysis of precursors; thus, it is difficult to clearly observe the lattice fringes. In Fig. 3(i), two pairs of symmetric spots in the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern are connected into a line segment, indicating the transformation of exposed crystal planes, which is consistent with the results of the HRTEM images. Moreover, as shown in the STEM mode in Fig. 3(k), most CoFe2Se4 is observed in the form of white spots, and the corresponding size is smaller than 5 nm, further demonstrating the formation of CoFe2Se4-QDs.

Figure 4(a) shows the XRD patterns of AC@CoFe2Se4, HCNs@CoFe, and HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs. As expected, the three diffraction peaks at 44.9°, 65.4°, and 82.8° for HCNs@CoFe correspond to the (110), (200), and (211) planes of CoFe (PDF#49-1567), respectively [35,36]. For AC@CoFe2Se4 and HCN@CoFe2Se4-QD, the diffraction peaks of CoFe disappear, along with the appearance of obvious diffraction peaks at 32.9°, 43.7°, and 50.6°, suggesting the successful formation of CoFe2Se4. Raman spectroscopy was employed to evaluate the graphitization degree of the carbon skeleton, as shown in Fig. 4(b). The D peak (1348 cm−1) and the G peak (1589 cm−1) are typically utilized to describe the degree of graphitization or the level of order in a material. The D peak can be attributed to amorphous carbon and lattice dislocations, while the G peak is attributed to the in-plane vibration of sp2 C–C bonds. Therefore, the Raman spectrum of the material can be fitted to determine the degree of graphitization by using the ratio of the D peak to the G peak. Compared with AC@CoFe2Se4 (I*D/*I*G = 0.91) and HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs (*I*D/*I*G = 0.78), HCN@CoFe2Se4 (*I*D/*I*G = 0.75) has a lower *I*D/*I*G value, which is attributed to the presence of abundant graphitic carbon owing to the catalytic effect of magnetic CoFe alloys. In addition, as shown inFig. 4(c), AC@CoFe2Se4 and HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs display three characteristic peaks at approximately 171, 472.69, and 670 cm−1, and these peaks match well with the F2g, E2g, and A1g modes of CoFe2Se4. The porous properties were characterized by N2 adsorption‒desorption isotherms, as shown in Fig. 4(d). The hysteresis loops of all the samples exhibit typical mesoporous characteristics, and the conspicuous adsorption behavior at high relative pressure indicates the presence of macropores. These results are consistent with the pore size distribution (Fig. 4(e)), and the porous characteristics are favorable for increasing lightweight EM wave absorption [37−39]. As calculated in Fig. 4(f), the specific surface area and pore volume are 59.6 m²·g−1 and 0.1384 cm³·g−1 for HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs and 55.9 m²·g−1 and 0.1236 cm³·g−1 for HCN@CoFe, which are greater than those of AC@CoFe2Se4 (6.5 m²·g−1 and 0.0188 cm³·g−1) owing to the formation of hollow cavities. XPS was performed to analyze the surface chemical composition. As depicted in Fig. S10 in the ESM, the XPS full spectrum displays the signals of Se, C, O, Fe, and Co for AC@CoFe2Se4 and HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs, which are similar to those of HCN@CoFe in the absence of Se 3d. The Co 2p spectrum of HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs is shown in Fig. 4(g). The two binding peaks at 780.8 and 782.3 eV correspond to Co3+ and Co2+, and the higher characteristic peaks of Co at site A are attributed to the high atomic density on the crystal lattice nodes [40−42]. In contrast, the Fe element at site B is located within the intrinsic node of the unit cell, leading to weaker characteristic peaks in Fig. S11 in the ESM. The Se 3d spectrum in Fig. 4(h) shows that Se is uniformly and densely arranged inside the spinel structure. In Fig. 4(i), the O 1s spectra of HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs can be deconvoluted into two peaks: the lattice oxygen of the spinel structure at approximately 531.5 eV and the surface physically adsorbed or residual water molecules at 533.1 eV. Notably, the characteristic peaks of AC@CoFe2Se4 are similar to that of HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs in Fig. S12 in the ESM, whereas the significant changes in HCN@CoFe are the corresponding Co 2p spectrum and Fe 2p spectrum in which the characteristic peaks of Co0 and Fe0 species can be observed in Fig. S13 in the ESM, further demonstrating the formation of magnetic CoFe alloys.

R*L and EAB values of AC@CoFe2Se4, HCNs@CoFe, and HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs are calculated in Fig. 5 with a 20 wt% filling ratio. Clearly, as shown in Figs. 5(a)–5(c), AC@CoFe2Se4 has an inferior *R*L value and a narrow absorption bandwidth; the minimum *R*L is only −19 dB at 3 mm, and the EAB below −10 dB reaches 4.05 GHz when the thickness is 3.5 mm. As expected, hollow engineering can effectively increase the volume of absorbents, resulting in enhanced EM wave attenuation. Specifically, HCN@CoFe with a hollow cavity displays a minimum *R*L value of −57.9 dB, and the EAB increases to 8.36 GHz with a thickness of 2.3 mm (Figs. 5(d)–5(f)). In contrast, regarding HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs in Figs. 5(g)–5(i), the minimum *R*L value reaches as high as −67.6 dB at 4 mm, with an impressive EAB of 11.40 GHz. Both of the above conclusions demonstrate that hollow engineering is an efficient strategy for improving EM wave absorption. The mechanism of enhanced EM wave absorption can be explained by impedance matching, as shown in Fig. S14 in the ESM, where a |*Z*in/*Z*0| value equal to 1 means a higher *R*L value and a |*Z*in/*Z*0| value near 1 results in a broad EAB [43−45], where *Z*in is the input impedance of the microwave absorbers and *Z*0 is the impedance of free space. Notably, the strip area of HCNs@CoFe and HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs between 0.8 and 1.2 is larger than that of AC@CoFe2Se4, indicating a broad EAB and the results match well with those in Fig. 5.

The EM wave absorption performance depends on the dielectric loss, magnetic loss, and synergistic effects. Thus, the EM parameters are investigated in Fig. 6 and Fig. S15 in the ESM. The relatively complex permittivity (*ε′* and *ε′′*) values clearly decrease with increasing frequency (Figs. S15(a) and S15(b) in the ESM), and the tan*δ**ε* values (Fig. 6(a)) of HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs are greater than those of AC@CoFe2Se4 and HCN@CoFe, suggesting a greater dielectric loss capability. In addition, the *ε′′* values of HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs exhibit multiple resonance peaks, confirming the occurrence of multi polarization relaxation [46]. As anticipated, CoFe alloys possess strong ferromagnetic resonance compared with CoFe2Se4. Thus, the relatively complex permeability (Figs. S15(c) and S15(d) in the ESM) and magnetic loss (Fig. 6(b)) of HCNs@CoFe are greater than those of AC@CoFe2Se4 and HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs. Combining the advantages of a hollow cavity, abundant heterogeneous interfaces, negligible magnetic loss, and matched impedance, HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs display superior EM wave absorption intensity and broad EAB, as shown in Fig. 6(c). Figure 6(d) shows the attenuation constant (*α*) values of AC@CoFe2Se4, HCNs@CoFe, and HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs, which can be employed to measure the final EM wave attenuation [47−49]. HCNs@CoFe and HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs clearly exhibit higher *α* values than AC@CoFe2Se4 owing to their strong magnetic and superior dielectric loss capabilities, resulting in increased EM wave absorption ability. Moreover, it is clear that the *C*0 (*μ*''(*μ*')−2*f*−1) values of all samples (Fig. 6(e)) are not constant at 2–18 GHz, demonstrating that ferromagnetic resonance and eddy current loss simultaneously contribute to magnetic loss [50,51]. To further illustrate the EM wave absorption performance in the far-field domain, the radar cross-section (RCS) values of the absorbents on pure Al plates are simulated in the range of −90° Figs. 6(g)–6(i), the simulation results indicate that the RCS values of the HCN@CoFe and HCN@CoFe2Se4-QD coatings are lower than those of the Al plates, illustrating that the HCN@CoFe and HCN@CoFe2Se4-QD coatings could reduce the radar scattering intensities of the pure Al plates. Specifically, as shown in Fig. 6(f), the reduction RCS values of HCNs@CoFe and HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs at 0° are 16.86 and 22.93 dB·m2, respectively, which are one order of magnitude greater than those of AC@CoFe2Se4 (1.11 dB·m2) and match well with the *R*L values.

As shown in Fig. 7, the aforementioned results demonstrate that the EM wave absorption performance of HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs can be effectively manipulated by the combined merits of a hollow cavity, abundant heterogeneous interfaces, and matched impedances. First, compared with HCNs@CoFe, which possesses large CoFe alloys, the incorporation of selenium powder inhibits the aggregation of Co and Fe during the pyrolysis process, thus decreasing the particle size. The small size of CoFe2Se4-QDs greatly increases the number of heterogeneous interfaces in which defect-induced dipolar polarization and strengthened interfacial polarization (Fig. S16 in the ESM) significantly increase dielectric loss [52], resulting in superior *R*L values and broad EAB. Second, the rational design of hollow engineering can more effectively optimize impedance matching than a solid structure can. The presence of a hollow cavity dramatically decreases the relative permittivity and the impedance band gap between the absorbents and air; in this case, the incident EM wave can easily penetrate into the interior of the absorbents and can be caused by a synergistic effect [53,54]. Third, HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs with hollow cavities and tightly arranged structures prolong the path of EM waves and induce multiple scatter events, which can promote the trapping of EM waves in the internal structure and consume EM energy via interfacial polarization or intrinsic polarization [55,56].

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs with hollow cavities and superior EM wave absorption performance were successfully synthesized via a hard-template wet mixing strategy. The results demonstrated that the incorporation of selenium powder effectively decreases the particle size of magnetic domains and increases the number of heterogeneous interfaces, and hollow engineering dramatically decreases the relative permittivity and the impedance band gap, both of which favor the enhancement of dipolar/interfacial polarization and impedance matching. As a result, the minimum *R*L value of HCNs@CoFe2Se4-QDs reaches −67.6 dB, and the EAB is as broad as 11.40 GHz with a thickness of 4 mm. Furthermore, this strategy offers valuable guidance for regulating the particle size of magnetic domains and the porous structure, which are pivotal in adjusting the EM wave absorption attenuation.
