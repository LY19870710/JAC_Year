## 1 Introduction

Multilayer piezoelectric ceramic actuators are the core components of micro-displacement systems in precision equipment, such as lithography machine, transmission electron microscope and computed tomography [1,2]. Due to their excellent piezoelectric properties and wide operating frequency range, lead zirconate titanate (PZT) ceramics have been applied in multilayer piezoelectric actuators [3,4]. Constrained by the operating voltage ( 50 μm), the actual operating electric fields of multilayer piezoelectric ceramic actuators should be less than 40 kV/cm. However, currently, commercial lead-based materials such as PZT-5, PZT-8, and most reported lead-based materials exhibit lower strains (5−9]. Although some lead-based materials may have higher strains (> 0.35%), the applied electric fields are greater than 40 kV/cm [10−12]. Both the low strains and high applied electric fields restrict the practical application of these materials in devices, such as piezoelectric deformable mirrors and piezoelectric micro jetting valves [13,14]. Extensive research suggests that non-intrinsic contributions, specifically those related to non-180° ferroelectric domain switching, serve as the primary source of strain in PZT piezoelectric ceramics [15−18]. According to the Landau–Khalatnikov model and the nucleation limited switching (NLS) model, the switching of ferroelectric domains under external electric fields is generally caused by the migration of ferroelectric domain walls [19−21].

The ferroelectric domain wall migration mechanism and the corresponding time-dependent response to strain can be described by the multi-step stochastic mechanism (MSM) model and the inhomogeneous field mechanism (IFM) model, as represented by the blue curve in Fig. 1 [21−24]. Since a triangular wave test was employed, the variation in strain with time *t* can also be regarded as the variation in the electric field (*E*). It can be found that the ferroelectric domain walls migrate under the synergistic effect of the external electric field and mechanical stress, thereby driving ferroelectric domain switching to complete the entire strain response process. In this process, owing to the different mechanisms of mechanical stress sources in different stages as well as the different effects on the barrier heights, the mechanical stress promotes the migration of ferroelectric domain walls in stage I, while it impedes the migration of ferroelectric domain walls in stages II and III [24−26]. The main stage of ferroelectric domain switching (stage II) determines the magnitude of strain in ceramics. Therefore, reducing the mechanical stress in this stage is crucial for promoting the migration of ferroelectric domain walls to achieve large strains under low electric fields.

The mechanical stress during the ferroelectric domain wall migration process mainly originates from the ferroelectric domain wall stress, which is closely related to lattice distortion [25,27]. In highly distorted tetragonal PZT, it is an effective approach to reduce lattice distortion by creating the morphotropic phase boundary (MPB) with the phase transition to the R phase [28]. However, reducing the lattice distortion can make the cell parameters close to those of the cubic parent phase, which notably weakens the piezoelectric activity [29−33]. In addition to lattice distortion, grain size and ferroelectric domain morphology are also key factors influencing domain wall migration. For large grains, interlaced “banded domains” can form, where the complex stress sources associated with these domains hinder the migration of domain walls. Conversely, small grains facilitate the formation of simple “lamellar domains”, which are conducive to the migration of domain walls [34,35]. Nevertheless, once the grain size is below a critical threshold, grain boundary stress and non-uniform stress throughout the grain almost completely suppress domain wall motion [35,36]. For domain structures, the responses of different domain structures to an external electric field exhibit significant differences [37,38]. Studies have shown that decreasing the size of domains could effectively reduce the activation energy of domain walls, so the short and broad domain morphology delivers greater domain wall mobility than the slender domain morphology [20,39]. Additionally, 90° domain walls and their variants demonstrate more abundant three-dimensional piezoelectric responses than 180° domain walls [40]. Therefore, controlling the grain size slightly above the critical size (> 2 μm) to ensure simple domain morphology and regulating the domain size by disrupting the long-range ferroelectric ordering of the A-site may be effective for promoting domain wall motion [36]. As a typical perovskite-structured material, BaZrO3 (BZ) has the advantages of high insulation and low dielectric loss. The incorporation of BZ into PZT weakens the Pb–O bond strength via the occupation of Ba2+ cations with large radii on the A-site, thus disrupting the A-site ferroelectric long-range order and forming smaller ferroelectric domains. Additionally, the introduction of Ba2+ suppresses the generation of oxygen vacancies that could pin domain walls, contributing to d*33 [41,42]. The incorporation of Zr4+ cations in tetragonal PZT is also advantageous for the formation of MPB, facilitating the domain wall migration.

In our previous work, PbTiO3–PbZrO3–(Bi0.5Na0.5)TiO3 (PZT–BNT) piezoelectric ceramics with large strains were realized by defect engineering and domain engineering, but the applied electric field exceeded 40 kV/cm to achieve a large strain, which could not fully meet the requirements for device application. To address this issue, 0.95Pb(Zr0.56Ti0.44)O3–0.05(Bi0.5Na0.5)TiO3–*x*BaZrO3 (PZT–BNT–*x*BZ) ceramics were designed and prepared in this work. The schematic of corresponding modification is shown in Fig. 1. The results indicate that the addition of BZ effectively modulates the lattice distortion and reduces the grain size, leading to a decrease in the domain wall stress and the formation of a simplified domain structure; thus, a reduced saturation electric field (*E*v) is achieved. Moreover, short and broad striped domains with high amplitude characteristics are constructed by regulating the BZ content. Consequently, a large strain of 0.39% is achieved at an electric field of 40 kV/cm, with excellent temperature stability over the temperature range of 30–210 °C. The mechanism for improving the strain characteristics is discussed in detail.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

PZT–BNT–*x*BZ (*x* = 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, and 0.04) were prepared with high-purity PbO, TiO2, ZrO2, Bi2O3, Na2CO3, and BaCO3 (99.0% purity, Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) by the solid-state sintering technology. The above raw powders were first mixed according to the design ratio and ball-milled for 12 h in absolute ethanol. The mixed slurry was dried and calcined at 800 °C for 4 h. Subsequently, 5% polyvinyl alcohol was added to the resultant powders for granulation, and the powders were then pressed and molded at a pressure of 6 MPa (13 mm in diameter and 0.8 mm in thickness). Finally, the green body was heated at 600 °C for 4 h and sintered at 1200 °C for 2 h to complete the sintering process.

### 2.2 Performance testing and structure characterization

The phase structure was determined by an X-ray diffraction instrument (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany). The micromorphology was observed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Apero Hivoc, FEI, USA). The microstructure and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) data were observed by a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM; Helios5CX, Thermo Fisher, USA), in which the samples were treated by a precision ion thinning instrument (PIPS II 695, GATAN, USA) before testing. The morphologies of the ferroelectric domains were observed by a piezoresponse force microscope (PFM; Cypher ES, OXFORD, UK). A ferroelectric analyzer (TF ANALYZER 3000, AixACCT, Germany) was used to test the hysteresis loops and strain curves of the samples and their variation with temperature at a frequency of 1 Hz. The piezoelectric coefficient (*d*33) of the samples was measured using a quasi-static *d*33 measurement instrument (ZJ-3A, Institute of Acoustics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China). An impedance analyzer (Agilent, 4294A, USA) was used to determine the mechanical quality factor (*Q*m) and planar electromechanical coupling coefficient (*k*p) of the samples. The characteristic peak shifts of the ceramics at different incident angles were measured via the XRD cos*α* method (Smartlab, Rigaku, Japan) to calculate the internal stress of the ceramics. The variation in the dielectric constant with temperature was tested by a dielectric temperature spectrum test system (FZ-IV, Taidao, China). The *in situ* temperature dependence of the quasi-static *d*33 was measured by a piezoelectric temperature spectrum measurement system (PMA1000, BALAB, China).

### 3.1 Phase and microstructure analysis

Figures 2(a) and 2(b) display the XRD patterns of samples with different BZ contents. All the samples exhibit a distinct perovskite structure without any impurity phase, indicating that BZ has been successfully solubilized into the PZT. As the BZ content increases, the (200) and (002) diffraction peaks at approximately 2*θ* = 45° gradually approach each other, which indicates a transition from the tetragonal (T) phase to the rhombohedral (R) phase for the sample [43]. As the content of the R phase increases, the phase composition of the sample moves toward the MPB region, leading to an improvement in the strain performance [28]. Figure S1(a) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) shows the HRTEM image of the PZT–BNT–0.03BZ sample. The sample exhibited two different orientations of lattice fringes, corresponding to the (* 1¯10) crystal plane of the R phase and the (200) crystal plane of the T phase. Figure S1(b) in the ESM shows the SAED patterns at the junction of two different lattice fringes, exhibiting a clear polycrystalline diffraction ring. This phase transformation can also be supported by the analysis of Raman spectra for the samples in Fig. S2 in the ESM. By performing Gaussian fitting on the Raman spectra, 12 groups of optical vibration moduli were obtained. The Raman mode, vibration mode, and phase structure corresponding to each modulus are listed in Table S1 in the ESM. With the increase of BZ content, the peak intensities of the optical moduli E*(2TO1), *E*(3TO), *A*1(3TO), and *A*1(3LO) corresponding to the T phase decrease continuously, while the peak intensities of the optical moduli *E*(2TO2) and vibration moduli *R*l and *R*h corresponding to the R phase increase continuously [44,45]. When the perovskite ceramic is in the cubic parent phase, an ideal 90° domain will form a strain-free structure. However, as the lattice undergoes distortion, the ideal 90° domain structure will experience angle deviation, but due to constraints from the surrounding domains, the domain is forced to revert to a 90° state [50,51]. In this case, internal stresses occur near the domain walls. For PZT piezoelectric ceramics near the MPB, the domain wall stress can reach the magnitude of GPa, which is the primary factor influencing domain wall migration [52]. The magnitude of the stress can be reflected by the deviation angle (*ξ*) of the ferroelectric domain walls in Eq. (2) [4,53]. According to Eq. (2), as the *c*/*a* ratio decreases, *ξ* decreases, indicating lower internal stresses within the domain walls. This reduction in internal stresses is advantageous for the migration of domain walls under an external electric field. In this study, calculations of domain wall stresses were also carried out in the subsequent electric domain analysis section to further evaluate their effect on domain wall migration. In addition, the transition from the T phase, which has only 6 equivalent polarization axes, to the R phase, which has 8 equivalent polarization axes, contributes to a decrease in the polarization barrier. This phase transition increases the sensitivity of the sample to an external electric field. Furthermore, maintaining the phase of all samples within the T-rich phase region of the R–T phase boundary allows for excellent temperature stability while preserving the strain contribution from ferroelectric domains in PZT materials [54,55]. These factors together ensure the ability of the samples to achieve high strain even under low voltage conditions.

Figures 3(a)–3(d) present natural surface SEM images of the PZT–BNT–*x*BZ samples, with the insets displaying the grain size distribution obtained by counting over 200 grains and the corresponding results of Gaussian fitting. With increasing BZ content, the average grain size (AGS) decreases significantly from 8.64 to 2.53 μm. The introduction of Ba2+ cations induces the disorder of A-sites in the crystal lattice, disrupting the ordered phase within the material [56−59] and thus leading to a decrease in the grain size. In addition, Randall *et al*. [60] found a significant correlation between the grain size of PZT ceramics and the tetragonal phase lattice distortion. When the *c*/*a* ratio of the tetragonal phase is greater than 1.025, the variation in the *c*/*a* ratio has no obvious effect on the grain size. However, when the *c*/*a* ratio decreases below 1.025, the grain size decreases dramatically. This is also consistent with the conclusion in this study; that is, the grain size of the sample rapidly decreases from 8.64 to 2.53 μm as the tetragonal phase *c*/*a* ratio decreases from 1.0251 to 1.0207. The grain size distribution can be reflected by the full width at half maximum (FWHM) obtained from Gaussian fitting. The smaller the FWHM value, the more concentrated the grain size distribution. The sample reveals the narrowest distribution of the grain size with *x* = 0.03, indicating that the sample has the most uniform grain size.

### 3.2 Ferroelectricity and domain structure analysis

Figure 4(a) shows the polarization (*P*)–electric field (*E*) curves of the PZT–BNT–*x*BZ samples. With the increase of BZ content, the maximum polarization intensity (*P*max) of the samples increases, which is attributed to the transition from the T phase to the coexistence of the R–T phase in the samples. It is worth noting that when the BZ content increases to 0.03 and 0.04, the coercive field (*E*c) of the samples appears to increase significantly rather than decrease at MPB, as observed in conventional PZT ceramics. This phenomenon is closely related to the grain size. When the grain size is significantly larger than the critical size, *E*c of the samples will exhibit minimal change under a large signal electric field. Conversely, when the grain size decreases to approach the critical size, a distinct increase in *E*c becomes evident for the samples [20].

Figures 4(b) and 4(c) illustrate the bipolar strain (*S*)‒*E* butterfly curves and unipolar *S‒E* curves of the PZT–BNT–*x*BZ samples. Figure 4(d) shows the variation in *E*c and bipolar and unipolar strains of the samples with different BZ contents. The strain increases as the BZ content increases from 0.01 to 0.03. This can be attributed to the decrease in the *c*/*a* ratio of the T phase in the samples upon the introduction of the BZ, thus decreasing the internal stress within the ferroelectric domain walls. The reduction in internal stress facilitates the migration of these domain walls under an external electric field. Therefore, reaching strain saturation becomes more achievable for the samples. However, as the BZ content further increases to 0.04, the strain of the sample decreases, contrary to the aforementioned rule.

To explain the pattern of strain variation, the dependence of the applied electric field on the unipolar *S‒E* and *P‒E* curves is shown in Figs. 5(a)–5(d) and Fig. S3(a)–S3(d) in the ESM, respectively. A saturated electric field (*E*v) exists in all the samples, corresponding to the saturated strain (*S*v). Namely, when the applied electric field is less than *E*v, both the strain and *P*max of the samples change significantly with the increase of the external electric field. As the applied electric field reaches *E*v and continues to increase, the strain and *P*max gradually increase with a low slope, indicating that the samples are in a state of strain saturation.

In addition, the variation trend of the strain can also be represented by domain switching. For the change in domain switching under an external electric field, a two-phase exponential association equation derived from the KAI model can be used for fitting as Eqs. (3) and (4) [21,24,25]:

(3) ΔP(t)=P0+2P90(1−e−t/τ90)+2P180(1−e−t/τ180)

(4) S(t)∝ΔP(t)

where Δ*P*(*t*) and *S*(*t*) are the volume fractions of all domain switching and the strain values at different time for the applied voltage, respectively; *P*0 is the volume fraction of domain switching after initial polarization; *P*90 and *P*180 denote the volume fractions of 90° and 180° domain switching respectively; *τ*90 and *τ*180 represent the unique time constants for 90° and 180° domain switching, respectively; *t* denotes the time of applied voltage. In this study, a triangular wave voltage is employed, which increases linearly with time. Therefore, *τ*90, *τ*180, and *t* can also be expressed as functions of the electric field, denoted as *E*90, *E*180, and *E*t, respectively. Consequently, Eq. (3) can be expressed as Eq. (5):

(5) S(E)∝P0+2P90(1−e−Et/E90)+2P180(1−e−Et/E180)

The variation curves of the strain with the applied electric field fitted by Eq. (5) are shown in the insets of Fig. 5. Obviously, the variation in the strain with the electric field for all the samples is in accordance with the law of the KAI model. That is, it is in accordance with the variation rule as shown in Fig. 1. This also verifies the viewpoint that the increase in the strain slows as the voltage increases, eventually approaching a limiting value. In other words, a saturated strain electric field (*E*v) and saturated strain (*S*v) exist in all samples from the polarization switching to the electric creep stage. *E*v of the samples decreases with the increase in the BZ content, which indicates that the introduction of BZ promotes the migration of domain walls, thus making it easier for the samples to reach the strain saturation state. However, *S*v of the samples decreases from 0.45% to 0.21% as the BZ content increases, meaning that the strain is not solely governed by the domain wall migration.

According to the Landau–Devonshire thermodynamic phenomenological theory and the Ginzburg–Landau model, the ferroelectric domain morphology plays a crucial role in determining the strain magnitude [62−65]. Therefore, the PFM images of the PZT–BNT–*x*BZ samples were characterized, as shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b) and Fig. S4 in the ESM, to further analyze the reasons for the strain variation. When *x* = 0.01 and 0.02, numerous striped ferroelectric domains are present in the samples, which is a typical characteristic of T-phase enrichment. With the increase in the BZ content, the ferroelectric domains change from stripe morphology to stripe-island coexisting morphology. This transformation is due to the shift from the T-rich phase to the coexistence of the R–T phase in the samples. Specifically, the sample exhibits short and broad ferroelectric domains when *x* = 0.03.

To further determine whether the effect of islanded domains on strain is different from that of short and broad domains, switching spectroscopy (SS)-PFM testing was conducted on different ferroelectric domain structures. The results are shown in Figs. 6(c) and 6(d), and the analysis positions at the spots 1 and 2 are shown in Fig. 6(a). The maximum phase differences of different domains are around 180°, indicating normal ferroelectric properties within the tested regions [66]. As shown in Fig. 6(c), the amplitude curve of the spot 1 exhibits significant asymmetry, which is caused by the bias electric field induced by defect dipoles. In our previous work, Bi3+ and Na+ in BNT replaced Pb2+ in the A-site to form defect dipoles, thereby generating a bias electric field [55]. When the electric domains are reoriented under an external electric field, the bias electric field hinders the reorientation due to the inverse field. This phenomenon is manifested by a noticeable center shift and asymmetry in the *S‒E* butterfly curve, which is particularly pronounced in the samples with a higher content of the T phase. Obviously, compared with the striped domains of the spot 1 corresponding to the T phase, the amplitude butterfly curve obtained from the irregular island-like domains of the spot 2 corresponding to the R phase exhibits better symmetry. Moreover, Fig. 6(c) shows that striped ferroelectric domains exhibit higher amplitudes than island ferroelectric domains under the same testing conditions, indicating greater macroscopic strain. Accordingly, although the samples exhibit higher *S*v at *x* = 0.01 and *x* = 0.02, the strain values remain relatively low because *E*v is much greater than 40 kV/cm. At *x* = 0.04, the easier migration of the ferroelectric domain walls results in lower *E*v for the sample. However, constrained by the numerous irregular island-like domains with low amplitudes, the sample exhibits lower *S*v. Additionally, the smaller grain size also reduces the intrinsic contribution to the piezoelectric response, further explaining the significant strain reduction in the sample with *x* = 0.04 [60]. In the case of *x* = 0.03, the sample reaches the strain saturation state at 40 kV/cm and displays a domain morphology with high intrinsic strain, thereby achieving a large strain of 0.39%.

Figures 7(a)–7(d) show the distribution of ferroelectric domains in the grains of different samples at a wider range. When *x* = 0.01, the distribution of ferroelectric domains is complex, leading to the generation of complex mechanical stress [35,36]. Additionally, there exists a relationship *σ* = *g**m* between the grain size and the ferroelectric domain size of the samples, where *σ* is the ferroelectric domain size; *g* is the grain size; *m* is a constant between 0 and 1 [67]. The samples with larger grain sizes exhibit slender ferroelectric domains. Nevertheless, due to the extension of the stress field along with the ferroelectric domains, the morphology results in a wider distribution of the stress field, which is unfavorable for the migration of ferroelectric domain walls [68]. A typical 90° herringbone domain can be observed in Fig. 7(a), which has a significant deviation angle (*ξ*, where the blue solid line is the ideal 90° domain angle and the yellow dashed line is the actual domain angle). After measurement, it was found that the offset angle was approximately 1.5°, which was in accordance with the calculation of Eq. (2). Moreover, when ferroelectric domains are subjected to stresses, a small bending elastic deformation occurs near the domain walls. If the domains are regarded as parallel rod-like structures, then the domain wall stresses can be expressed as elastic bending stresses [52]. During the bending process, the bending angle of the deformed region is *α*/2, where *α* is linearly related to *ξ* (*α*/*ξ* ≈ 1.75). In PZT, whether it is a kink structure at the junction of lamellar domains or an angular mismatch structure between herringbone domains, the region where bending deformation occurs is generally within 100 nm [52,53,69]. The values of *E*, *v*, and *θ*0 are 60 GPa, 0.35, and 38.397°, respectively. The calculated *σ*ϕ values for different samples are shown in Fig. S5(b) in the ESM. With the increase of the BZ content, owing to the decrease in grain size, the samples exhibit a gradual increase in *σ*ϕ. However, since the average grain size of all the samples is above the critical value of 2 μm, *σ*ϕ does not dramatically increase. In this study, *σ*ϕ at the MPa level has a smaller influence on domain wall migration than *σ*m at the GPa level.

To further investigate the mechanism about the ferroelectric domain wall migration of the PZT–BNT–*x*BZ samples, the ferroelectric domains of the out-of-plane polarization reversal were observed, as shown in Fig. 8 and Fig. S6 in the ESM. A positive voltage was applied within the green frame line region at the out-of-plane center, and a reverse voltage of the same magnitude was applied within the white frame line region. The distribution of ferroelectric domains is almost unchanged at applied voltages of 5 and 10 V when the BZ content is 0.01 and 0.02, indicating that there is no significant migration of the ferroelectric domain walls, as shown in Figs. S6(a) and S6(b) in the ESM. As the BZ content increases to 0.03, irregular island-like domain switching is observed in the center region of the white frame when the applied voltage is 5 V, whereas the striped domains on the left side have almost no phase variation, indicating no domain switching. As the applied voltage further increases to 10 V, only the irregular island-like domains in the central region still show phase variations, which indicates that the irregular island-like domains are more sensitive to the external electric field compared to the stripe-like domains, as shown in Fig. 8(a). Figure 8(b) shows that when the BZ content reaches 0.04, a large number of irregular island-like domains appear. Thus, varying degrees of domain switching are observed in the central and surrounding regions at an applied voltage of 5 V, which also indicates that the sample has a higher domain wall mobility. With the increase in the applied voltage to 10 V, no further significant ferroelectric domain switching is observed. This indicates that a 5 V voltage is sufficient to achieve complete ferroelectric domain switching, which is attributed to the easier migration of ferroelectric domain walls facilitated by the synergistic effect of lower lattice distortion and a simpler ferroelectric domain distribution. The easily migrating ferroelectric domain walls imply that the material becomes “softer”, which can also be observed from the variation of *Q*m (Fig. S7 in the ESM). As the BZ content increases, a greater amount of migrating domain walls leads to increased heat dissipation during each vibration cycle, thus resulting in a gradual decrease in *Q*m [74]. Additionally, *d*33 partly originates from the external contribution of domain wall motion, and the increased domain wall migration increases *d*33 of the sample as the BZ content increases [59]. When *x* = 0.03, the sample is in the MPB state with the highest *d*33 and *k*p. As the BZ content continues to increase, the grain size of the samples decreases. Although a higher migration rate increases the external contribution to the piezoelectric properties, a decrease in the grain size to near the critical value also suppresses the intrinsic contribution to the piezoelectric properties, leading to a slight decrease in *d*33 and *k*p [60].

### 3.3 Temperature characteristics

Figure 9(a) shows the temperature dependence of the dielectric constant (*ε*r) and dielectric loss (tan*δ*) for the PZT–BNT–*x*BZ samples at 1 kHz, where the inset shows the variations in the Curie temperature (*T*c) and tan*δ* of the samples. The introduction of BZ reduces *T*c and increases tan*δ* of the material. This is because of the phase transition from the T phase with high *T*c (~490 °C) to the coexistence of the R–T phases as the BZ content increases, resulting in a decrease in *T*c. Additionally, the increased migration rate of domain walls leads to more thermal loss under an alternating electric field, causing an increase in tan*δ* [74]. Figures S8(a)–S8(d) show the temperature dependence of *ε*r and tan*δ* of the PZT–BNT–*x*BZ samples at different frequencies. All the samples show no frequency dispersion, indicating that the introduction of BZ does not alter the relaxation characteristics of the samples. This is because the relaxation behavior of PZT ceramics is related to the substantial doping of A-site ions and B-site disorder [10]. The addition of a small amount of Zr does not introduce a large number of A-site ions or new B-site ions. As shown in Fig. 9(b), a comparison is presented, showing the strain, *T*c, and applied electric field of the samples in this work alongside some typical PZT-based piezoelectric systems, and the direction of the arrows indicates the direction of the desired change in performance [8,11,28,31,75,76]. Apparently, the piezoelectric ceramics in this work demonstrate excellent comprehensive performance compared to other systems.

In addition to the strain characteristics, temperature stability is also a critical parameter for assessing the practicality of piezoelectric ceramics. Figures S9(a)–S9(d) in the ESM show the *P*–*E* hysteresis loops of the PZT–BNT–*x*BZ samples at 30–210 °C. The remanent polarization (*P*r) and *E*c of all samples exhibit a decreasing trend with increasing temperature, which is attributed to the promotion of ferroelectric domain wall motion induced by the thermal activation effect. Notably, the temperature characteristics of the unipolar *S‒E* under a 40 kV/cm electric field are different from those of the *P*–*E* hysteresis loops, as shown in Figs. 10(a)–10(d).

Fig. 10Temperature dependence of unipolar S‒E* curves of PZT–BNT–*x*BZ samples.

As depicted in Fig. 10, the strain values of the samples fluctuate within a small range as the temperature increases when the BZ contents are 0.01, 0.02, and 0.03. This is due to the relatively few spontaneous polarization directions, and the unipolar *S‒E* curves are not susceptible to temperature for the prepared PZT ceramics with tetragonal structures. Additionally, PZT ceramics in the Ti-rich region have higher phase transition temperatures, thus exhibiting good temperature stability [77]. In contrast, as the BZ content increases, the R-phase proportion in the ceramic with *x* = 0.04 increases, resulting in reduced temperature stability. A significant increase in the strain of the sample is observed above 140 °C when the BZ content reaches 0.04. This phenomenon is related to the phase structure of the sample. In the PZT ceramics, there exists an R–T phase transformation temperature (*T*t, approximately 150 °C) when the ratio of the R-phase to the T-phase is close to 1 : 1. As the temperature increases, the material undergoes a phase transition, resulting in a greater strain [77].

To further evaluate the temperature behavior of the PZT–BNT–*x*BZ ceramics, the *in situ* temperature dependence of the quasi-static *d*33 was tested, as shown in Figs. S10(a)–S10(d) in the ESM. When the BZ content ranges from 0.01 to 0.03, *d*33 of the samples generally slowly increases with rising temperature. When the temperature increases to slightly above *T*c, the samples exhibit depolarization, resulting in a sharp decrease in *d*33. This is because *d*33 is directly proportional to *ε*r; when the temperature exceeds *T*c, *ε*r decreases sharply, which also leads to a reduction in *d*33 [54]. When the BZ content is increased to 0.04, the R phase in the sample increases, and the increase in temperature leads to a transition from the R phase to the T phase, which results in a significant increase in *d*33. As the temperature continues to rise, the piezoelectric performance of samples with more R-phases generally deteriorates, but due to the increase in *ε*r with temperature, *d*33 does not decrease directly but rather decreases slowly over a small temperature range [29,77]. When the temperature rises above *T*c, *d*33 suddenly drops. Therefore, it is imperative to ensure that the PZT-based piezoelectric ceramics in the T-rich phase region simultaneously exhibit excellent temperature stability and large strains.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, PZT–BNT–*x*BZ ceramics were prepared using the solid-state method, and their strain characteristics were systematically studied. The results showed that the strain characteristics were strongly correlated with the lattice distortion and the distribution and morphology of the ferroelectric domains of the samples. Reducing the lattice distortion and simplifying the ferroelectric domain distribution could effectively promote the migration of ferroelectric domain walls in the PZT–BNT–*x*BZ ceramics, thereby lowering the applied electric field intensity to achieve a saturated strain. In addition, the short and broad ferroelectric domains exhibited high strain amplitude characteristics, macroscopically contributing to strain. Typically, the PZT–BNT–0.03BZ sample delivered a large strain of 0.39% at an electric field as low as 40 kV/cm, with a strain fluctuation of less than 14% over the temperature range of 30–210 °C. This work provides a new strategy for achieving large strains at low electric fields in PZT-based piezoelectric ceramics.
