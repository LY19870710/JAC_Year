## 1 Introduction

The advent of fifth-generation (5G) communication has revolutionized information transfer and accelerated the growth of the modern electronics industry [1−4]. However, the rapid development trends toward high integration, miniaturization, and high power make electronic devices face new challenges. Electromagnetic wave (EMW) pollution poses serious risks to device stability and national security, whereas excessive heat accumulation threatens the performance and lifespan of compact and integrated components [5−8]. Consequently, designing multifunctional flexible packaging materials that combine remarkable EMW absorption with efficient thermal conductivity is imperative.

Hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) is a two-dimensional material that is electrically insulating yet thermally conductive, attracting considerable attention for applications in thermal management [9,10]. Its unique properties of high thermal conductivity, chemical stability, and low dielectric constant make it a promising filler for polymer-based composites [11]. However, achieving simultaneous EMW absorption and thermal functionalities in a single composite remains challenging [12]. On the one hand, conductive fillers can increase dielectric loss because of their high electrical conductivity, but their excessive conductivity often leads to severe impedance mismatching, resulting in pronounced EMW reflection and thus weakening the absorption capability [13−15]. On the other hand, h-BN provides outstanding thermal conductivity but has low dielectric loss, poor interfacial compatibility, and weak dispersion due to its low dielectric performance, electrical insulation, and chemical inertness [16−18]. Thus, effectively modifying h-BN without damaging its structure while enhancing the interfacial polarization and dielectric response remains a major bottleneck in designing multifunctional BN-based composites. In our previous work, we reported the *in situ* deposition of Co at high temperature to induce the growth of pompon chrysanthemum-like BN microspheres (PC-BNSs) on carbon fibers (CFs) to improve the EMW absorption and thermally conductive performance of the final composites [19]. This strategy leverages the synergistic magnetoelectric effect of Co and CFs to optimize impedance matching, whereas the three-dimensional (3D) PC-BNS architecture enhances EMW scattering. Simultaneously, the PC-BNS structures formed interconnected thermal conduction networks with the CFs, significantly improving the heat transfer efficiency.

Conventional h-BN modification methods such as chemical oxidation, plasma treatment, or high-temperature sintering often require complex processes, harsh environments, and high solvent consumption, which may limit the scalability [20,21]. In contrast, liquid metal (LM) offer a simple, green, and scalable alternative for surface modification. Owing to their high fluidity, electrical conductivity, and thermal conductivity, LM offer new opportunities for designing multifunctional composites [22]. For example, Che *et al*. [23] demonstrated that LM-assisted galvanic reactions under sonication yielded indium/oxide core–shell nanoparticles with outstanding EMW absorption, achieving a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −40.25 dB and a maximum effective absorption bandwidth (EABmax) of 6.12 GHz. Li *et al*. [24] developed polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)-encapsulated LM-MXene aerogels with an RLmin of −63.10 dB, further highlighting the potential of LM-based materials in achieving strong EMW absorption. Inspired by these advances, the integration of LM with h-BN is expected to simultaneously enhance the interfacial polarization and thermal transport capabilities, thereby enabling the fabrication of composites with high EMW absorption and thermal management capabilities.

Herein, we report a facile and scalable mechanochemical strategy to modify boron nitride flakes (BNFs) with LMs to regulate their EMW absorption and thermally conductive performance. During the mechanochemical process, LM exhibit unique fluidic deformability, enabling effective interactions with the inert surfaces of BNFs. Specifically, LM form coordination interactions with the N atoms of BNFs, generating abundant interfacial dipoles and defect sites. These interactions not only enhance dielectric polarization and impedance matching but also promote phonon transport through tightly bridged BNF@LM interfaces, effectively overcoming the weak interfacial coupling of pristine BNFs. Compared with conventional chemical functionalization, LM modification avoids the need for corrosive solvents and multistep reactions, providing a clean, efficient, and scalable route to tailor both the EMW and thermal properties of BNFs. As expected, the optimized BNF@LM composites exhibit an RLmin of −48.4 dB and an EABmax of 5.76 GHz. Furthermore, when coupled with the aramid nanofiber (ANF) matrix, the H-BNF@LM composites can establish continuous thermal conduction pathways, markedly increasing the thermal conductivity of the H-BNF@LM/ANF films. Compared with that of pure ANF films, the thermal conductivity is enhanced by approximately 5 times, demonstrating excellent heat dissipation capability. The synergistic optimization of EMW absorption and thermal management capabilities opens new avenues for developing BN-based multifunctional EMW absorption materials.

### 2.1 Materials

Ga block, wire, and Sn wire (purity of 99.99%) were purchased from Beijing Goodwill Metal Co., Ltd., China. BNFs were obtained from Shandong Jonye New Materials Co., Ltd., China. Poly-p-phenylene terephthamide (PPTA) fibers were supplied by Tayho Advanced Materials Co., Ltd., China. Potassium hydroxide (KOH) powder was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd., China. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was purchased from Macklin, China. All chemical reagents were used without further purification.

### 2.2 Preparation of LM

Ga block, In wire, and Sn wire with a mass ratio of 68.5 : 21.5 : 10 were placed in an alumina crucible. The mixture was subsequently heated at 300 °C for 1 h under nitrogen protection, and the LM was obtained after furnace cooling.

### 2.3 Fabrication of BNF@LM composites

LM and BNFs were weighed at fixed ratios and placed into an agate mortar. The mixture was mechanically ground at room temperature for approximately 30 min until a uniform color was observed, ensuring maximal mechanochemical interaction between the LM and BNFs. A series of composites with different mass ratios of LM to BNF (1 : 2, 1 : 1, and 2 : 1) was prepared and designated L-BNF@LM, M-BNF@LM, and H-BNF@LM, respectively.

### 2.4 Preparation of the H-BNF@LM/ANF composite film

First, 0.6 g of PPTA fibers and 0.8 g of KOH were added to 300 mL of DMSO and magnetically stirred for 48 h. The resulting solution was then slowly dropped into deionized water to induce protonation, forming ANF flocculates. These flocculates were collected by vacuum filtration and alternately washed with deionized water and ethanol to completely remove residual solvent. The H-BNF@LM was subsequently dispersed in 20 mL of water by ultrasonication in an ice‒water bath for 20 min. The resulting H-BNF@LM dispersion was then mixed with an ANF dispersion at a mass ratio of 40:60, followed by ultrasonication and stirring for 30 min. Finally, the mixture was vacuum-filtered and hot-pressed at 100 °C under 2 MPa for 2 h to obtain the H-BNF@LM/ANF composite film. For comparison, a pure ANF film was fabricated under the same conditions but without H-BNF@LM fillers.

### 2.5 Characterizations

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the samples were acquired via an XRD (Cu-Kα, D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany). To further determine the elemental composition and surface chemical bonds of the final products, the composition and bonding states of H-BNF@LM were characterized by a Fourier transform infrared spectroscope (FTIR; Trace 100, Japan) and an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB Xi+, USA). The morphology and element distribution of BNF@LM were observed via a scanning electron microscope (SEM; TESCAN CLARA, Czech) equipped with an energy dispersion spectrometer (EDS, Ultim Extreme) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM F200, Japan). The morphological size of the ANFs was measured via an atomic force microscope (AFM; Bruker icon, Germany) in tapping mode. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed via a simultaneous thermal analyzer (STA 449F3, Netzsch, Japan) from room temperature to 800 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere. The thermal conductivity of each sample was measured via a transient hot-wire method thermal conductivity meter (TC3000E, XIATECH, China). Infrared thermal images were taken with a Yoseen X640D thermal imager.

### 2.6 Electromagnetic parameters measurement

The electromagnetic parameters of the BNF@LM-based absorbers were measured via a vector network analyzer (E5063A, Agilent, USA). The BNF@LM composites were mixed with paraffin, and then, the mixtures were pressed into ring-shaped samples (outer diameter: 7.00 mm; inner diameter: 3.04 mm). Typically, the ratios of the BNF@LM (L-BNF@LM, M-BNF@LM, and H-BNF@LM) composite paraffin were fixed to 40/60 to evaluate the influence of filler type on the electromagnetic properties. For comparison, H-BNF@LM-based absorbers with different ratios of H-BNF@LM to paraffin of 30/70, 40/60, 50/50, and 60/40 were prepared.

According to transmission line theory, the RL values can be calculated from Eqs. (1)–(3) [25−27]:

(1) RL=20log⁡|Zin−Z0Zin+Z0|

(2) Zin=Z0μrεrtan⁡h(j2πfdcεrμr)

(3) α=2πfc×(μ″ε″−μ′ε′)+(μ″ε″−μ′ε′)2+(μ″ε′+μ′ε″)2

where *Z*in is the normalized input impedance of the absorber, *Z*0 is the impedance of the free space, *f* is the absorption frequency, *d* is the thickness, *c* is the velocity of light, and *μ*r and *ε*r are the complex permittivity and complex permeability, respectively.

Through the relationship between real part of permittivity (*ε') *and imaginary part of permittivity (*ε")*, which is calculated according to Debye’s theory, the mechanism of dielectric loss can be estimated via Eq. (4) [28,29]:

(4) (ε″)2+(ε′−εs+ε∞2)2=(εs−ε∞2)2

where *ε*s and *ε*∞ are the permittivity of the absorbers at static permittivity and relative permittivity at high frequency, respectively. Theoretically, each Cole‒Cole semicircle corresponds to a polarization relaxation process.

### 2.7 Radar cross section (RCS) simulation

To evaluate the practical application of H-BNF@LM, the simulated structure consisted of an upper absorbing layer of 40 wt% H-BNF@LM with a thickness of 2.0 mm and a bottom photoelectrochemical (PEC) substrate with a thickness of 0.01 mm, designed as a square plate with lateral dimensions of 200 mm × 200 mm. A plane wave was used as the excitation source, propagating along the negative z-axis, while the sample was positioned on the *x*‒*y* plane. The experimentally measured electromagnetic parameters of the 40 wt% H-BNF@LM composites were employed in the simulation. The RCS is calculated as Eq. (5) [30]:

(5) RCS(dB⋅m2)=10log⁡(4πSλ2|EsEi|2)

where *S* is the area of the simulated plane, *λ* is the wavelength of the incident EMW, and *E*s and *E*i are the electric field intensities of the scattered wave and incident wave, respectively. The calculated RCS values quantitatively reflect the scattering strength, where a weaker scattering intensity indicates superior EMW absorption and stealth capability [31].

### 3.1 Design and application strategy for BNF@LM composites and structural properties

The fabrication and applications of the BNF@LM composites are shown in Fig. 1. First, as shown in Fig. 1(a), the LM was prepared through a facile thermal treatment process under ambient nitrogen with a Ga block, an In wire, and a Sn wire as the raw material (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). The LM was subsequently attached onto the surfaces of the BNFs via a facile mechanochemical grinding process. During this step, the collision energy facilitated partial oxidation of the LM and enabled strong anchoring of the LM onto the surfaces of the BNFs, thereby generating BNF@LM composites with abundant interfacial sites, as demonstrated in Fig. 1(b) [32]. Owing to the existence of LM, the as-obtained BNF@LM composites could be used in two main fields. (I) BNF@LM composites can be used as EMW absorption materials, where the coexistence of conductive LM domains and interfacial polarization centers promotes strong dielectric loss and efficient EMW attenuation (Fig. 1(c)). (II) BNF@LM composites can be further coupled with polymers to improve the thermal conductivity of polymer-based materials. For example, H-BNF@LM/ANF composite films could be prepared by blending ANFs obtained by the deprotonation of PPTA fibers and H-BNF@LM composites followed by vacuum filtration and a hot-pressing process (Fig. 1(d)). Through hydrogen bonding and interfacial interactions, a homogeneous composite suspension was formed, in which ANFs acted as “flocculants” to induce H-BNF@LM aggregation, effectively preventing the sedimentation of heavy LM [33]. The resulting suspension was then subjected to vacuum-assisted filtration followed by hot pressing, yielding H-BNF@LM/ANF composite films with a dense yet flexible structure. In this architecture, ANFs serve as a robust and flexible matrix, whereas BNF@LM composites provide dielectric regulation and continuous thermal conduction pathways.

The morphology and structure of the BNF@LM composites at various LM loadings were subsequently examined to gain deeper insight into their interfacial architectures. BNF@LM mixtures were obtained through mechanical grinding with gradually increasing LM loadings. Moreover, the digital photographs of all the as-obtained BNF@LM composites display consistent coloration, indicating the homogeneous mixing of LM with BNFs (Fig. S2 in the ESM). SEM images together with corresponding EDS elemental mappings provide direct evidence of the morphological evolution and elemental distribution of BNF@LM composites at different LM loadings. At low LM loading (Fig. 2(a)), LM droplets can only partially cover the BNFs, leading to blurred sheet boundaries, whereas the sparse distribution of Ga, In, and Sn signals suggests insufficient coverage. At higher LM loadings, the BNF boundaries in L-BNF@LM become much less distinct, and BNFs are nearly encapsulated by an interconnected LM phase, implying that LM acts as the matrix in the M-BNF@LM and H-BNF@LM composites. With increasing LM loading (Figs. 2(b) and 2(c)), a continuous LM framework gradually formed on the BNF surface, as confirmed by the denser and more uniform Ga, In and Sn signals in the elemental mapping results. In both the M-BNF@LM and H-BNF@LM composites, Ga, In, Sn, and B are distributed continuously throughout the mixtures, confirming the formation of a continuous LM network and homogeneous BN dispersion. This gradual transition from isolated LM droplets to an interconnected LM framework not only strengthens the interfacial contact between the LM and BNFs but also promotes the construction of electrically conductive and thermally conductive pathways. The high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images reveal local distortions and defect regions within the BN lattice, suggesting that LM infiltration induces partial lattice deformation and the formation of aperiodic regions at the interface. These microstructural changes support the existence of strong interfacial interactions between LMs and BNFs. Moreover, high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images further reveal the atomic-level dispersion of LM on BNFs, even after ultrasonication.

The structural characteristics of the samples were further elucidated. The XRD pattern reveals that the composites display the typical diffraction peaks of h-BN (PDF#34-0421), along with additional peaks assigned to gallium oxide hydroxide (PDF#54-0910) and Ga–In oxide (PDF#21-0334) (Fig. 3(a)) [33,34]. These results indicate partial oxidation of the Ga-In layered phase, which generated Ga-based hydroxides and Ga–In composite oxides during the mechanochemical process, confirming surface oxidation of the LM and the formation of LM oxide/BNF heterointerfaces. The FTIR bands at 1371 and 817 cm−1 correspond to the characteristic vibrations of BNFs (Fig. S3 in the ESM) [35]. Furthermore, the XPS analysis (Figs. 3(b) and 3(c)) provides direct evidence of coordination interactions between the metallic atoms of the LM and the N atoms of BN. Specifically, an additional peak appears at 396.7 eV in the N 1s spectrum of H-BNF@LM, which can be attributed to metal‒nitrogen coordination bonding. The Ga 2p and In 3d spectra reveal the coexistence of metallic states (Ga0 and In0) and oxidized states (Ga3+ and In3+), suggesting partial oxidation within the LM regions (Figs. 3(d)–3(f) and Fig. S4 in the ESM) [23]. This multiphase heterostructure, composed of BNFs, LMs, and oxides, generates abundant polarization centers and local conduction pathways, thereby increasing dielectric loss and optimizing impedance matching. To further clarify the influence of LM oxidation on the polarization behavior, the O 1s spectra of the BNF@LM composites with different LM loadings were analyzed (Fig. 3(f)). The O 1s peaks can be deconvoluted into three components: surface-adsorbed oxygen (533.0 eV), defect-associated oxygen (531.3 eV), and lattice oxygen (529.9 eV) [36]. The lattice oxygen content remains nearly constant (~31%), suggesting that variations in the LM content have little effect on the intrinsic oxygen coordination environment. In contrast, the proportion of defect oxygen increases with increasing LM loading, implying that more LM promotes the generation of oxygen-related defect sites during the mechanochemical process. These defective oxygen species serve as polarization centers and charge trapping sites, thereby reinforcing interfacial polarization and facilitating efficient EMW energy dissipation [37].

### 3.2 EMW absorption performance of the BNF@LM composites

The electromagnetic parameters of the BNF@LM composites with different LM loadings were investigated (Figs. 4(a)–4(c)). Both the *ε′* and *ε″* increase progressively with increasing LM loading, indicating an increased ability to store and dissipate EMW energy (Figs. 4(a)–4(c)) [38]. This enhancement originates from the introduction of conductive LM droplets, which generate additional interfacial polarization and electrically conductive pathways [39]. Moreover, for all BNF@LM composites, the nonmagnetic nature of the fillers indicates that magnetic effects make negligible contributions to the EMW absorption performance (Fig. S5 in the ESM) [40]. To further clarify these contributions, dielectric loss is separated into conduction loss and polarization loss, as shown in Figs. 4(d) and 4(e) [41], respectively. At low LM loading, both contributions are weak, which can be attributed to the limited conductive network and insufficient interfacial polarization. With increasing LM loading, more LMs can simultaneously increase conduction loss and polarization loss, thereby enabling more efficient energy dissipation [42]. In particular, the H-BNF@LM composites exhibit markedly stronger interfacial polarization than the other samples do, confirming that polarization loss dominates the overall attenuation mechanism. This conclusion is further supported by the calculated attenuation constant (Fig. 4(f)), which reaches the highest value for H-BNF@LM. Correspondingly, the RL curves (Figs. 4(g)–4(i)) demonstrate that the EMW absorption performance can be further improved by increasing the LM loading, with H-BNF@LM showing the best EMW absorption performance.

The effects of different H-BNF@LM loadings on the electromagnetic properties of the absorber were further investigated, as demonstrated in Figs. 5(a)–5(d), and all the composites exhibited excellent EMW absorption performance, as shown in Figs. 5(e)–5(l). When the H-BNF@LM content was 30 wt%, the absorber exhibited moderate EMW absorption with an EAB of 3.20 GHz (4.72–7.92 GHz) at 4.9 mm. When the H-BNF@LM content increases to 40 wt%, the optimized absorber achieves the strongest EMW absorption capability, with an RLmin of −48.4 dB at 2.0 mm and a broad EABmax of 5.76 GHz (12.16–17.92 GHz) at 1.9 mm. A further increase in the H-BNF@LM loading leads to a decrease in the EMW absorption capability and an RLmin of −31.2 dB at 2.0 mm, and an EABmax of 4.32 GHz (12.16–16.48 GHz) at 1.4 mm is obtained for the absorber with 50 wt% H-BNF@LM, whereas an RLmin of −19.3 dB at 2.3 mm and an EABmax of 4.48 GHz (13.20–17.68 GHz) at 1.2 mm are achieved for the absorber with 60 wt% H-BNF@LM. These results confirm that 40 wt% H-BNF@LM provides the optimal balance with impedance matching, enabling effective penetration and dissipation of EMW. In contrast, excessive H-BNF@LM loading deteriorates impedance matching with free space, causing stronger reflection despite higher attenuation constants and thereby reducing the overall EMW absorption efficiency.

To further assess the stealth potential of the H-BNF@LM composite, RCS simulations were shown in Figs. 6(a)–6(c). The PEC substrate displays strong scattering signals with a maximum RCS of 15.49 dB·m2 at 13.04 GHz. In contrast, the 40 wt% H-BNF@LM absorber-coated PEC markedly suppressed scattering, reaching a minimum RCS of −6.5 dB·m2 at the same frequency. The 3D far-field maps clearly illustrate the pronounced attenuation effect of the composite relative to the bare PEC, confirming that the optimized absorber significantly enhances the stealth performance. These results underscore the promising potential of the H-BNF@LM composite for practical EMW protection and radar stealth applications. Furthermore, the EMW absorption characteristics of the BNF@LM composites were thoroughly investigated, as demonstrated in Fig. 6(d). The incident EMW undergoes multiple interactions within the heterogeneous structure, including reflection, scattering, and transmission across BNF@LM interfaces. The presence of abundant heterogeneous boundaries and LM inclusions generates strong interfacial polarization [43]. Additionally, defects and dipolar groups induced by mechanical collisions contribute to Debye relaxation [44]. Both mechanisms significantly increase dielectric loss. Moreover, conductive LM domains establish localized current pathways that strengthen conduction loss, collectively promoting efficient dissipation of EMW energy [45]. The RL curves displayed in Fig. 6(e) confirm that the optimized H-BNF@LM achieves the strongest EMW absorption capability. These values surpass those of many composite absorbers reported in previous work (Fig. 6(g) and Table S1 in the ESM), highlighting the superiority of the BNF@LM composites [37,43,46−53]. Consistent with the *λ*/4 theory [54], tuning the absorber thickness allows the EAB to shift toward lower frequencies, and at 4.5 mm, the EAB reaches 1.60 GHz (4.24–5.84 GHz), fully covering the n79 band (4.4–5.0 GHz) relevant to 5G communication (Fig. 6(h)). Notably, the impedance matching performance was also analyzed to clarify the absorption mechanism. When the absolute value of the ratio between the input impedance of the absorber and the impedance of free space (|*Z*in/*Z*0|) equals 1, reflection at the air-absorber interface is minimized, indicating ideal impedance matching conditions [55]. Therefore, the closer |*Z*in/*Z*0| is to 1, the better the incident EMW can enter and be attenuated within the absorber [56]. The calculated impedance matching values (|*Z*in/*Z*0|) of H-BNF@LM (Fig. 6(f)) remain close to 1, verifying that LM introduction optimizes the balance between attenuation capability and impedance matching, thus enabling incident EMW to penetrate the absorber effectively [57]. Moreover, Cole–Cole plots, as shown in Fig. 6(i), reveal multiple semicircles, indicating the coexistence of interfacial polarization and Debye relaxation processes, whereas the linear tail at low frequency suggests conduction loss [58]. Overall, the synergy of interfacial polarization, Debye relaxation, and conduction loss, along with well-optimized impedance matching, underpins the strong attenuation capability and exceptional EMW absorption of the H-BNF@LM composite.

### 3.3 Multifunctional performance of H-BNF@LM/ANF films

To clearly demonstrate the morphological evolution from PPTA fibers to ANFs, SEM, and AFM analyses were employed. As shown in Fig. 7(a), the pristine PPTA fibers possess a smooth surface with a diameter of approximately 10 μm. When the PPTA fibers were fully exfoliated in a DMSO/KOH mixture, deprotonation of the amide N–H groups occurred, breaking the intramolecular hydrogen bonds and generating negatively charged polymer chains. Owing to the balance among π‒π stacking, electrostatic repulsion, and van der Waals forces, the ANFs were successfully dispersed, forming a homogeneous dark-red ANF/DMSO solution [59]. The SEM and AFM images shown in Figs. 7(b) and 7(c) reveal that slender nanoscale fibers are obtained after exfoliation, confirming the successful fibrillation of the PPTA fibers into ANFs. After coupling with H-BNF@LM, the ANF-based films exhibit excellent flexibility, as shown in Fig. 7(d) and Fig. S5 in the ESM, withstanding rolling, folding, and recovery without damage, making them suitable for flexible electronic applications. In addition, the SEM image of the ANF film (Fig. 7(e)) shows a distinct multilayered structure in the cross section. Similarly, the cross-sectional SEM image of the H-BNF@LM/ANF film (Figs. 7(f) and 7(g)) also reveals a well-integrated layered structure with H-BNF@LM fillers homogeneously embedded within the ANF matrix. The corresponding elemental mappings of B and Ga confirm that LM is uniformly distributed inside the H-BNF@LM/ANF composite film and tightly bridges beneath the BNFs, which facilitates the formation of efficient thermal conduction pathways.

All the films have an approximate thickness of 0.2 mm, and the H-BNF@LM/ANF composite film has a thermal conductivity of 0.54 W·m−1·K−1, which is nearly five times greater than that of the pure ANF film (0.10 W·m−1·K−1) (Fig. 8(a)). Under continuous heating at 100 °C, the H-BNF@LM/ANF films maintained higher surface temperatures than did pure ANF (Fig. S7 in the ESM), confirming the presence of reliable thermal transport pathways. Infrared thermography further demonstrated their practical heat dissipation capability. When employed as a thermal interface material (TIM) on a heat sink, the H-BNF@LM/ANF films effectively suppressed hotspot formation and maintained lower surface temperatures than did pure ANF or systems without TIMs, as demonstrated in Figs. 8(b)–8(d), highlighting their superior thermal management performance. These enhanced thermally conductive properties can be attributed to the continuous heat transport channels established by the BNF@LM networks within the ANF matrix [60]. To further verify their practical applicability, a Tesla coil wireless transmission experiment was performed, as shown in Fig. 8(f). When the pure ANF film was placed in the gap between a lit LED bulb and a Tesla coil, the LED bulb remained lit, as shown in Fig. 8(f2). In contrast, when the H-BNF@LM/ANF film is inserted into the same gap, the LED bulb is immediately extinguished, as demonstrated in Fig. 8(f3). These results confirm the strong EMW absorption and attenuation performances of the H-BNF@LM composites. In addition, the TGA curves of the pure ANF film exhibit a residue of 43.8 wt% at 800 °C, whereas the H-BNF@LM/ANF composite film retains 67.9 wt% under the same conditions (Fig. 8(e)). The much higher char yield of the composite demonstrates its enhanced thermal stability and carbonization resistance. This improvement can be attributed to the strong interfacial interaction and thermal barrier effect of the H-BNF@LM fillers, which together suppress polymer chain decomposition. Flame-retardancy tests further verified the superior thermal protection capability of the composite. The pure ANF film ignites rapidly within 3 s and undergoes severe structural damage after 10 s of direct combustion, whereas the H-BNF@LM/ANF film only shows surface charring, without any violent combustion, and maintains overall integrity (Fig. 8(g)). This remarkable flame resistance originates from the synergistic effects of the thermally stable BNF network and the dense LM layer, which effectively impedes oxygen diffusion and delays thermal degradation during combustion. This excellent flame-retardant behavior ensures the reliable performance of the H-BNF@LM/ANF films even under extreme conditions, highlighting their potential for use as next-generation flexible materials with integrated EMW absorption and thermal management capabilities.

## 4 Conclusions

A simple and scalable mechanochemical method has been developed to fabricate BNF@LM composites with enhanced EMW absorption and thermally conductive performance. The introduction of LM effectively activates the inert BN surface and generates multiphase heterostructures, introducing abundant interfacial polarization centers. As a result, the H-BNF@LM composites exhibit outstanding EMW absorption. In addition, the thermal conductivity of the H-BNF@LM/ANF film is efficiently enhanced and is approximately 5 times greater than that of the ANF film, indicating superior heat dissipation performance. In addition to strong EMW absorption and thermal management performance, the films also demonstrated excellent flexibility and remarkable flame retardancy, ensuring reliable operation even under harsh conditions. This strategy is a promising way to design next-generation multifunctional composites for EMW absorption.
