## 1 Introduction

The implementation of advanced lighting technologies in contemporary agriculture has revolutionized crop cultivation, especially in harsh environments or during natural disasters [1,2]. Generally, blue‒violet light (400–490 nm) plays an essential role in the growth and development of plants by promoting the growth of roots and stems, whereas red (≈ 660 nm) and far-red (≈ 730 nm) light are vital for regulating the entire lifecycle of plants, from seed germination to flowering and fruiting [3−5]. Light intensity is also a crucial factor influencing plant growth and development across a wide range of species, as high-intensity far-red light can improve the ability of plants to capture light, accelerate the electron transfer process, and increase photophosphorylation activity, leading to greater photosynthetic efficiency and crop yield [6−8]. However, despite the availability of blue InGaN light-emitting diodes (LEDs), plant lighting technology for precision agriculture is still in its infancy due to the lack of low-cost, high-brightness red and far-red light sources, spectrally matching the distinct absorption bands of two photoconvertible phytochrome states (*P*r and *P*fr).

Phosphor-converted light-emitting diodes (pc-LEDs) show great advantages in radiating long-wavelength visible light owing to their ability to customize the emission spectra of light sources without requiring complex drive circuits [9−12]. Furthermore, high-brightness pc-LEDs can be obtained by using ultrahigh-power-density blue LEDs (≈ 5 W·mm−2) [13] or even laser diodes (LDs) [14] as excitation sources; however, traditional “phosphor-in-silicone (PiS)” color converters are inapplicable to these cases, as a large amount of heat inevitably generated in the light conversion process cannot be rapidly dissipated through transparent organic resins with very low thermal conductivity (≈ 0.2 W·m−1·K−1) [15−17], resulting in strong thermal quenching of phosphors and carbonization of organic resins. In particular, the quantum defect (i.e., thermal relaxation) of downconverting a blue photon into a far-red photon is as large as approximately 40% [18]. To address the above problems, all-inorganic bulk materials such as phosphor-in-glass (PiG) composites [19−21] and luminescence ceramics [22−24] have been proposed as color converters. Among them, luminescence ceramics are generally regarded as the most promising candidates for high-brightness pc-LEDs and laser-driven light sources because of their significantly higher thermal conductivity than glass and organic resins as well as excellent physical and chemical stabilities. However, efficient and stable far-red-emitting ceramics are lacking, notwithstanding considerable developments in far-red phosphors and PiG composites. In addition, densifying techniques based on high-pressure and high-vacuum powder sintering for preparing ceramics face the drawbacks of high cost and low production efficiency [25,26].

In this work, we developed a high-performance Cr3+-activated far-red-emitting silicate ceramic whose emission spectrum well overlapped with the *P*fr absorption band by making full use of the glass crystallization process. Unlike traditional powder sintering, full crystallization from the designed mother glass is a unique approach for preparing bulk ceramics without the need for high pressure, high vacuum, or lengthy heat treatment, which has proven to be effective in developing transparent noncubic ceramics [27], nanocrystalline multiphase ceramics [28], and, recently, highly nonstoichiometric ceramics [29,30]. This inspired us to propose a simple modified annealing method for glass-crystallized Cr3+-activated ceramics, where a high temperature was adopted to improve single-phase formation and subsequent grain growth concurrently. In addition, a weakly reducing atmosphere was introduced to prevent the occurrence of Cr4+ and oxygen vacancies, which are both common luminescence killers for Cr3+ [18,24]. Furthermore, the silicon-containing Y2CaAl4SiO12 (YCAS) garnet, which possesses a high glass-forming ability compared with the well-known Y3Al5O12 (YAG) was selected as the host, and a series of Y2Ca1−*x*Ba*x*Al4SiO12:Cr3+ solid solutions were designed via the cation substitution of Ba2+ for Ca2+ so that the emission spectrum was largely regulated from the previously reported [24] broad near-infrared (NIR) band to the desired narrow far-red band due to the strengthened crystal field of the octahedral Cr3+ sites. Finally, owing to the near-unity internal quantum efficiency (IQE) and near-zero thermal quenching of the optimal Y2Ca0.3Ba0.7Al4SiO12:Cr3+ (YCBAS:Cr3+) ceramic, a maximum wall-plug efficiency of 27% for the far-red pc-LED and a record output power for the laser-driven far-red light source exceeding 2.1 W were obtained, highlighting its great potential as an all-inorganic color converter for high-power plant-growth light sources.

### 2.1 Materials and synthesis

The ceramics of Y2Ca1−*x*Ba*x*Al4−*y*Cr*y*SiO12 (*x* = 0.1–1.0 and *y* = 0.005–0.08) were synthesized via a pressureless glass crystallization method. The high-purity raw materials Y2O3 (99.999%), CaCO3 (99.999%), BaCO3 (99.999%), Al2O3 (99.999%), SiO2 (99.999%), and Cr2O3 (99.99%) were weighed stoichiometrically and mixed in an agate mortar for 30 min. Subsequently, glass beads with diameters ranging from 3 to 4 mm were prepared via the aerodynamic levitation method. A detailed description of the process is available in previous works [18,24]. Polished ceramic plates, with thicknesses varying between 0.2 and 0.6 mm, were obtained by heat-treating glass samples in a tube furnace for 7 hours at temperatures ranging from 1050 to 1350 °C. A 95% N2 + 5% H2 atmosphere was employed to suppress the oxidation of Cr3+ ions. The phosphor powder with a composition of Y2Ca0.3Ba0.7Al3.96Cr0.04O12 was synthesized at 1600 °C for 10 h in a 95% N2 + 5% H2 atmosphere.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The thermodynamic analysis of the glass samples was performed via a TG-DSC instrument (STA 449 F3, Netzsch) under an air atmosphere at a heating rate of 10 K**·**min−1, with *α*-Al2O3 as the reference material. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired via a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.54 Å), covering a scanning range of 2*θ* = 10°–80° at a scan rate of 5° per minute. The Rietveld refinement analysis was performed via the FullProf program. The EPR measurements were performed at an X-band frequency via a spectrometer (EPR200-340 Plus, CIQTEK). The surface morphology and composition of the samples were characterized via transmission electron microscopy (Talos F200s, Thermo Scientific) at 200 kV and equipped with an energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS) in conjunction with a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; GeminiSEM 360, ZEISS) operating at 30.0 kV. Luminescence properties, including excitation and emission spectra, decay curves, and temperature-dependent emission spectra, were measured via a fluorescence spectrometer (FLS1000, Edinburgh Instrument), with the temperature regulated precisely by a high-temperature controller (TAP-02, Orient KOJI). The IQE and absorption efficiency (AE) were recorded via a UV–NIR absolute photoluminescence quantum yield spectrometer (Quantaurus-QY Plus C13534-12, Hamamatsu Photonics), with an error margin of approximately 1% over three independent measurements.

### 2.3 Device fabrication and performance measurements

Far-red pc-LEDs were fabricated by directly coating LED chips (1 W, 450 nm) with YCBAS:Cr3+ ceramics that had been annealed at 1350 °C for 7 h and then sealed with highly transparent silicone. The optical properties of the pc-LED devices, including EL spectra and photoelectric efficiency, were characterized via an integrated photoelectric measurement system (LHS-1000, EVERFINE), which was equipped with an array spectrophotometer (350–1100 nm, HAAS-2000) and an integrating sphere (SPEKTRON R98, *φ*50 cm). An adjustable 450 nm LD (LSR450CP-45 W-FC, LASEVER, China) served as the excitation source to evaluate the optical performance of devices under high-density radiation. The spot area was maintained at 0.714 cm2. All the photographs were captured via a smartphone (iPhone 15, Apple).

### 3.1 Glass crystallization and structural characterizations

An aerodynamic levitation system equipped with two high-power CO2 lasers was employed to synthesize transparent glass precursors. This method enables containerless melting at high temperatures (≈ 2000 °C) and rapid cooling (≈ 300 oC·s−1), allowing glass with the composition of Y2Ca1−*x*Ba*x*Al4-*y*Cr*y*SiO12 (*x* = 0.0–1.0 and *y* = 0.00–0.08) to be obtained without contamination (Fig. 1(a)) [24,28,30]. The glass samples were fully crystallized into ceramics after a 7-h heat treatment in a reducing atmosphere above the crystallization temperature (Fig. 1(b)). The crystal structure was confirmed by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Fig. 1(c)), where all the observed diffraction peaks were accurately indexed to the cubic garnet structure (PDF#75-0554), indicating their high phase purity. The diffraction peak shifts toward smaller angles, suggesting that Ca2+ (1.12 Å) ions are replaced by larger Ba2+ (1.42 Å) ions. Furthermore, the XRD patterns depicted in Fig. 1(d) confirm that the YCBAS:Cr3+ glass first crystallizes into a multiphase composite and then transforms into a single-phase garnet ceramic. To confirm the local structure, Rietveld refinement of the XRD patterns of the as-prepared ceramics was performed, and the results are presented in Fig. 1(e) and Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The calculated curves closely align with the experimental data, as evidenced by the final refinement factors (Table S1 in the ESM). These refinement parameters suggest a high degree of confidence in the reliability of the results and their reference values. The gradual increase in the lattice volume with increasing Ba2+ content signifies the successful substitution of Ca2+ with Ba2+ (Fig. 1(f)). Previous studies [24,31] have demonstrated that Ca2+–Si4+ cosubstitution for Y3+–Al3+ in the YAG structure can induce the formation of [AlO6] octahedra with distinct crystal field strengths. Consequently, multiple octahedral sites with high crystal field stabilization energy remain available for Cr3+ occupation after Ba2+ is incorporated into the YCBAS structure (Fig. 1(g)) [32−34].

### 3.2 Luminescent properties of YBCAS:Cr<sup>3+</sup> ceramics

The YCAS:Cr3+ ceramic emits broadband NIR light originating from the 4T2 →4A2 transition of the Cr3+ ion in a weak crystal field [24]. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the emission peak of Cr3+ gradually blueshifted from 760 to 730 nm with the cation substitution of Ca2+ by Ba2+ in the YCAS host. Although Ba2+ has a larger ionic radius than Ca2+, its substitution expands the dodecahedral site but leads to a contraction of the octahedral AlO6 sites due to polyhedral tilting. Specifically, the expansion of dodecahedral sites is more significant than the shrinkage of tetrahedral sites. This alteration causes the oxygen atoms at the shared apex of the AlO6 octahedra to move closer to the central Al3+ ions, thereby forming a small octahedral Al3+ site for Cr3+. This effect reduces the Cr–O bond length at the octahedral sites, thereby increasing the strength of the crystal field acting on Cr3+ ions [31,42,43]. The crystal-field parameters can be calculated according to the Tanabe‒Sugano formulas (Eqs. (1)–(3)) [35]:

where *D*q represents the crystal field strength parameter, and *B* denotes the Racah parameter. The *D*q/B ratio increased from 2.1 (*x* = 0.0) to 2.6 (*x* = 1.0), confirming an increase in the crystal field strength (Fig. 3(b)). Generally, Cr3+ undergoes a millisecond-scale 2E→4A2 radiative transition under a strong crystal field, whereas a microsecond-scale 4T2→4A2 transition occurs under a weak crystal field. As Ba2+ substitution increases, the room-temperature decay lifetimes of YCBAS:Cr3+ under 420 nm excitation substantially increase from the microsecond scale to the millisecond scale (Fig. 3(b)), indicating an increase in the crystal field strength, as expected. The typical excitation and emission spectra of the optimal YCBAS:0.04Cr3+ ceramic are depicted in Fig. 3(c). The interconfiguration spin-allowed 4A2 → 4T2 transition of Cr3+ features a broad absorption band that matches that of commercial blue LEDs. The emission spectrum reveals a sharp emission band at 689 nm from the 2E → 4A2 transition, as well as broadband NIR light (*λ*em > 750 nm) from the 4T2 → 4A2 transition. The distinct excitation spectra (Fig. 3(d)), along with the notable deviations from single-exponential decay curves (Fig. S2 in the ESM) monitored at 689 and 800 nm, provide strong evidence for the presence of multiple Cr3+ emission centers. This interpretation is further corroborated by the time-resolved emission spectra (TRES) presented in Fig. 3(e). The broadband emission attributed to the 4T2→4A2 transition of Cr3+ ions in a weak crystal field is predominantly observed at short decay times (~50 μs), whereas at longer decay times, the sharp emission band centered at 689 nm, corresponding to the 2E → 4A2 transition, becomes increasingly intense and spectrally distinct. The local coordination environment of Cr3+ in YCBAS:0.04Cr3+ was investigated via EPR spectroscopy. Cr3+ ions possess a 3d3 electronic configuration with a total spin of *S* = 3/2. Following Hund’s rule, the ground state of Cr3+ is 4A2g, which undergoes further splitting in an octahedral crystal field into one singlet (4A2g) and two triplet states (4T1g and 4T2g), thereby enabling detectable EPR transitions [36,37]. As shown in Fig. 3(f), the EPR spectrum features a low-field broad resonance near *g* = 4.86 and a broad high-field resonance at *g* = 1.99. The low-field signal is typically attributed to isolated Cr3+ ions, whereas the high-field signal is indicative of exchange-coupled Cr3+ pairs in a weak ligand field [38,39]. Generally, the broad band at approximately *g* = 4.86 suggests the presence of multiple types of Cr3+ centers, whereas the weak signal at *g* = 1.99 reflects a disturbed environment associated with Cr3+–Cr3+ interactions. These findings further confirm that Cr3+ ions occupy multiple [CrO6] octahedral sites.

As shown in Fig. 4(a), the emission spectrum of the YCBAS:*y*Cr3+ ceramics gradually broadens from a narrow band to a wider profile with increasing Cr3+ concentration. This broadening is attributed to the reduced distance between Cr3+ ions occupying sites with different crystal field strengths at higher doping levels, which facilitates nonradiative energy transfer from high-energy Cr3+ sites (2E → 4A2) to low-energy sites (4T2 → 4A2). Moreover, the accelerated decay of strong-field Cr3+ centers, with the average lifetime decreasing from 1.0 ms (*y* = 0.005) to 0.3 ms (*y* = 0.08) (Fig. 4(b)), further confirms the occurrence of Cr3+ → Cr3+ energy transfer at higher doping concentrations [40,41].

The higher temperature converted the multiphase ceramic into a highly crystalline single-phase ceramic with larger grain sizes. In contrast, the traditional high-temperature solid-state method failed to obtain single-phase YCBAS:Cr3+ phosphor powders (Fig. S3 in the ESM), as with other silicate garnets [31,44,45]. As a result, fewer defects in the YCBAS ceramic lead to fewer nonradiative recombination pathways [18,24], thereby significantly enhancing the IQE from 22% to 97% (Fig. 4(c), Fig. S4 in the ESM). The average lifetime of the excited 2E level of Cr3+ (Fig. 4(d)) notably increases from 0.4 to 0.9 ms because of the suppressed nonradiative transitions. Additionally, the YCBAS:0.04Cr3+ ceramic has a high AE of 46%, approximately twice that of its powder-type counterpart with the same Cr3+ concentration. In a specified area of the sample irradiated by incident light, there is a relatively larger quantity of Cr3+ ions in the densified bulk ceramic than in the dispersed phosphor powder. In addition, unlike phosphor powders, which are strongly scattering materials with short optical path lengths, highly densified ceramics allow a much longer path length for incident light due to the much weaker light scattering effect, resulting in a higher absorption probability at a given Cr3+ concentration [16,24]. The EQE of 45% highlights the superiority of far-red ceramics over other Cr3+-activated luminescent materials (Table S3 in the ESM), suggesting their potential for high-performance applications in plant lighting sources.

The operating temperature of high-power pc-LEDs and pc-LDs can reach 200 °C or higher. The resulting thermal quenching effect leads to the IQE loss of color converters, which increase the operating temperature and significantly degrade the optical performance of devices [46−48]. The development of all-inorganic bulk materials such as YCBAS:Cr3+ luminescence ceramics with high thermal conductivity (≈ 3.5 W·m–1·K–1)[49] is considered an effective strategy for circumventing this problem, as they can rapidly dissipate heat to avoid the heat accumulation associated with organic encapsulation. As shown in the temperature-dependent emission spectra of the YCBAS:0.04Cr3+ ceramics (Fig. 5(a)), the emission intensity decreases as the temperature increases from 30 to 150 °C owing to the thermally enhanced nonradiative relaxation. The temperature-dependent luminescence decay curves (Fig. S5 in the ESM) further support this observation, revealing a shortened lifetime with increasing temperature. This behavior arises from the thermal population of the 4T2 state, which has a faster decay rate than the 2E state does. Moreover, the thermally activated concentration quenching effect becomes more pronounced at higher Cr3+ concentrations, further deteriorating the thermal stability of the ceramics, as shown in Fig. 5(b). The YCBAS:0.04Cr3+ ceramic heat-treated at lower temperatures faces stronger thermal quenching (Fig. S6 in the ESM), whereas the ceramic heat-treated at 1350 °C demonstrates superior thermal quenching resistance (*I*150 ºC = 99%). ### 3.3 High-performance far-red light sources

To evaluate the light conversion capability of the YCBAS:0.04Cr3+ ceramic, we fabricated a far-red-emitting pc-LED based on a blue chip (450 nm, 1 W). The far-red light photoelectric efficiency, defined as the ratio of the far-red light power to the input electric power, reached 27% at a drive current of 20 mA (2.67 V), demonstrating a 2.3-fold improvement over the corresponding PiS device (12%@20 mA) (Fig. 6(a)). The electroluminescent (EL) spectrum displays dual-band emission peaks at 450 nm and 730 nm (Figs 6(b) and 6(c)), which overlap with the absorption bands of chlorophyll-a and phytochrome (*P*fr), respectively. The calculated spectral matching degree of 71% indicates great application potential for precise plant growth lighting (Fig. 6(d)). Although the far-red output power of the all-inorganic pc-LED monotonically increases within the rated input current of the blue LED (350 mA), reaching a maximum of 183 mW, such a pc-LED device is still limited in generating high-power far-red light owing to the “efficiency droop” effect of chips (Fig. S7 in the ESM) and thermal accumulation within the organic binders.

YCBAS:Cr3+ ceramics with high thermal conductivity can effectively dissipate heat, making them preferable for high-power applications over traditional PiS devices. As a proof of concept, we assembled laser-driven far-red light sources by combining a power-tunable 450 nm LD with YCBAS:Cr3+ ceramics having different thicknesses in reflection mode (Fig. 7(a)), whose EL spectra are displayed in Fig. 7(b) and Fig. S8 in the ESM. As shown in Fig. 7(c), the YCBAS:0.04Cr3+ ceramic with a thickness of 0.4 mm exhibits no luminescence saturation until the laser power density reaches 204 W∙cm−2, thereby obtaining a maximum far-red output power of up to 2.1 W. In contrast, the corresponding phosphor-organic composite sample will be carbonized at a power density of just 1 W∙mm−2 [18]. Additionally, the maximum blue-to-far-red conversion efficiency—the ratio of the far-red output power to the incident blue laser power—reaches 36% (Fig. 7(d)), indicating excellent light conversion capability. Furthermore, a smartphone camera successfully captured images of a logo from distances of 5–30 m (Fig. S9 in the ESM), illuminated by a prototype far-red LD flashlight with an output power of 20 W (Figs 7(e) and 7(f)), further demonstrating the great potential of these ceramics for laser-driven, high-power far-red light sources.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a far-red-emitting Cr3+-activated silicate ceramic via full glass crystallization. A high annealing temperature ensures a single garnet phase, large grain size, and thus high crystallinity, whereas a reducing atmosphere is beneficial for the formation of Cr3+ centers. With these advantages of the modified annealing method, we obtained efficient and stable far-red emission of Cr3+ with near-unity IQE and near-zero thermal quenching in a solid solution with a garnet structure, whose emission band was regulated to match well with the absorption band of the phytochrome (*P*fr) state by crystal field engineering via rational cation substitution. Furthermore, using the optimal YCBAS:0.04Cr3+ ceramic as the all-inorganic color converter, we achieved a high wall-plug efficiency of 27% for the far-red pc-LED and a record output power for the laser-driven far-red light source exceeding 2.1 W, confirming its excellent light conversion capability for high-power far-red light sources that have important applications in precision agriculture, as well as deep-tissue optogenetics [53,54], and photobiomodulation therapy, etc. [55,56].
