## 1 Introduction

As the threat of energy and environmental crises continues to escalate, there is a growing global focus on regulating fluorinated greenhouse gases, making the elimination of inefficient vapor-compression refrigeration technology an imperative priority [1,2]. In recent decades, considerable attention has been given to the exploration of high-efficiency, zero-emission, and solid-state refrigeration technologies [3,4]. Among these, ferroelectric (FE) refrigeration, which is based on the electrocaloric (EC) effect, holds exceptional promise [5−8]. The EC effect describes a physical phenomenon wherein reversible adiabatic temperature change (Δ*T*) and/or isothermal entropy change (Δ*S*) are induced by a change in the electric field (Δ*E*) [9−11]. Since the groundbreaking discovery of a giant Δ*T* of 12 K in the PbZr0.95Ti0.05O3 film in 2006 [12], extensive efforts have been undertaken over the past fifteen years to explore various high-performance EC materials, including thin films [13−15], thick polymer films [16−19], and bulk ceramics [20−22].

Compared with lead-free FE ceramics, lead-based FE ceramics always demonstrate superior EC performance owing to the large polarizability contributed by the 6s2 lone pairs of Pb2+ [23,24]. Nair *et al.* [25] and Crossley *et al.* [26] observed significant EC effects of Δ*T* = 2.2 K (@26 kV/cm) and Δ*T* = 5.5 K (@290 kV/cm) near room temperature in PbSc0.5Ta0.5O3 bulk ceramics and multilayer capacitors, respectively. However, the expensive raw material Sc2O3 and the challenges associated with the sintering process impede mass production. In our previous research, we achieved a remarkable EC response of Δ*T* = 1.8 K (@120 kV/cm) at 36 °C in PbMg0.5W0.5O3 ceramics [27], but the necessity for a large applied electric field also complicates practical applications. Compared with the PbB'0.5* B0.5″O3 ceramics mentioned above, the Pb(ZrTi)O3-based FE ceramic system boasts a lengthy research history and well-established industrial applications in the piezoelectric field, indicating its capability for mass production and ease of electrical manipulation. This solves worries regarding practical applications of EC technology in Pb(ZrTi)O3-based FE ceramics, thereby enabling them to attract the attention of a wider audience of EC researchers [28,29].

As widely recognized, an excellent EC effect can be achieved near the first-order FE‒paraelectric (PE) phase transition, which is attributed to drastic polarization changes and significant field-induced lattice structure changes [30−32]. Consequently, significant efforts have been directed towards shifting the high Curie temperature (T*C) of over 200 °C in Pb(ZrTi)O3 FE ceramics to room temperature, catering to routine refrigeration applications. The predominant strategy involves appropriate ion substitution modifications. For example, Ge *et al.* [33] introduced 70 mol% Sr2+ ions into PbTiO3 crystal lattice, shifting the *T*C to 11 °C, thereby achieving a substantial EC effect of Δ*T* = 1.02 K (@40 kV/cm) near room temperature. Similarly, substituting 11 mol% Ba2+ ions for Pb2+ ions in (Pb0.97La0.02)(Zr0.58Sn0.29Ti0.13)O3 ceramics resulted in *T*C of 37 °C [34], yielding a significant Δ*T* of 1.58 K (@70 kV/cm) near room temperature. In addition to room-temperature refrigeration applications, refrigeration technology is indispensable in both low-temperature and high-temperature environments. Therefore, there is a growing focus on achieving a broad operating temperature range in EC studies, with relaxor ferroelectric (RE) ceramics featuring diffuse phase transitions seen as promising options. However, this often comes at the expense of reducing the peak EC value. For example, although RE multilayer ceramics of 0.92Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3–0.08PbTiO3 exhibit a wide temperature range of 0–80 °C, EC Δ*T* was below 0.4 K (@20 kV/cm) [35]. Similarly, a maximum EC response of only Δ*T* = 0.25 K (@40 kV/cm) was observed in Pb0.865La0.09Zr0.8Ti0.2O3 RE ceramics, despite exhibiting a wide EC operation temperature range of 0–140 °C [36]. Hence, it appears challenging to simultaneously achieve a large Δ*T* value and excellent temperature stability of the EC effect in a single material.

In fact, the significance of attaining a manipulable single working temperature point in one EC material system outweighs the importance of a broad operation temperature range. This stems from the one-to-one correspondence between actual refrigeration application scenarios and specific refrigeration devices. In detail, it does not seem appropriate to amalgamate the functions of a refrigerator and an air conditioner within the same refrigeration device. To address this need for EC materials with multiple target operation temperatures, the composition phase diagram has emerged as a powerful design tool. Figure 1 depicts the room-temperature composition phase diagram of (PbLa)(ZrTi)O3 solutions with varying La contents and Zr/Ti ratios [37]. For all undoped Pb(Zr,Ti)O3 compositions, *T*C is considerably higher than room temperature, making it challenging to meet the diverse refrigeration requirements; thus, a relatively high La content is necessary to appropriately lower *T*C. Moreover, Zr4+ and Ti4+ have different ionic radii, electronegativities, and ionic polarizabilities; thus, further manipulation of the Zr/Ti ratio enables the achievement of multiple target EC operation temperatures. Therefore, charge-balance Pb0.775La0.15Zr*x*Ti1−*x*O3 (*x* = 0.3, 0.35, 0.4, 0.45, and 0.5) compositions across the FE and RE phase regions are selected, where the composition near the phase boundary indicates a near-room-temperature *T*C. As the composition point deviates from the phase boundary, *T*C gradually increases to higher temperatures within the FE region but decreases toward lower temperatures in the RE region. Finally, manipulable EC operation temperatures ranging from 150 to −45 °C are successfully achieved, satisfying the diverse requirements of various refrigeration application scenarios, ranging from industrial equipment to freezer cabinets.

## 2 Experimental

Pb0.775La0.15Zr*x*Ti1−*x*O3 (*x* = 0.3, 0.35, 0.4, 0.45, and 0.5) ceramics were synthesized via a conventional solid-state reaction method. Analytical reagent-grade PbO (≥ 99%), La2O3 (≥ 99%), ZrO2 (≥ 99%), and TiO2 (≥ 98%) powders were procured from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China and employed as the initial materials. To compensate for lead loss due to high-temperature volatilization, an excess of 5 mol% PbO was added during the weighing process. The powders were ball-milled and thoroughly mixed for 4 h with ZrO2 balls and ethyl alcohol as the milling medium. The slurries were subsequently dried overnight and calcined twice at 900 °C for 3 h to synthesize a homogeneous compound. The powders were then subjected to another round of ball milling, followed by granulation using polyvinyl alcohol as a binder. After sizing, the particles were pressed into green pellets with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 1 mm under 6 MPa pressure. Following the removal of the binder at 600 °C for 2 h, the pellets were sintered at 1300 °C in a lead-rich atmosphere for 3 h. The sintered samples were subsequently polished to a thickness of 0.2 mm using sandpaper and coated with a 5 mm diameter Au electrode (area: 19.63 mm2) to prevent breakdown during high-voltage measurements.

The phase structures of the samples were analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; SmartLab, Rigaku, Japan) operating at 40 kV and 150 mA with Cu Kα1/2 radiation. The surface morphologies were observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-6700F, JEOL, Japan). The temperature dependence of the dielectric properties was measured using an LCR meter (a type of electronic test equipment used to measure the inductance (L), capacitance (C), and resistance (R) of an electronic component; Keysight E4980A, USA) at a heating rate of 3 °C/min controlled by a temperature chamber. The temperature-dependent specific heat capacities were determined using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC; Q2000, TA Instruments, USA) in the modulated DSC (MDSC) mode (heating rate 2 °C/min, modulation period 100 s, and modulation amplitude 0.8 °C). The polarization–electric field (*P*–*E*) hysteresis loops were acquired using a FE analyzer (TF2000E, aixACCT, Germany) outfitted with a high-voltage amplifier (610, Trek, USA) operating at a frequency of 1 Hz at different temperatures. The EC effect was determined by recording the isothermal heat flow curve via a modified DSC (Q2000, TA Instruments, USA), during which a square wave high voltage was provided by a high-voltage amplifier (677B, Trek, USA) [36,38].

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 2(a) presents SEM images of the fresh surfaces of PLZT ceramics with varying Zr/Ti ratios, revealing a relatively dense, compact, and pore-free microstructure. All the ceramics exhibit polygonal grains with non-uniform sizes. Employing the line intercept method, we assessed the grain size distributions for different compositions. As illustrated in Fig. 2(a), the average grain sizes (*G*a) for *x* = 0.3, 0.35, 0.4, 0.45, and 0.5 are 2.25, 2.58, 2.68, 2.87, and 3.31 µm, respectively. The observed increase in grain size may be attributed to the presence of increased chemical inhomogeneities at the B sites, which signifies a heightened chemical concentration gradient [39,40]. This facilitates diffusion during the sintering process, thereby promoting grain growth. However, overall, there is no significant difference in the microstructure of the ceramic system, suggesting a minimal contribution to the regulation of electrical properties.

Compared with the microstructure, the phase structure plays a pivotal role in optimizing the electrical performance of FE materials. As depicted in Fig. 2(b), the XRD patterns reveal a pure perovskite structure without any second phase for all the samples. To highlight the phase structure variations with composition, locally magnified (110)PC and (200)PC diffraction peaks are displayed. An increased Zr content causes a shift in peaks to lower angles, indicating an expanded lattice due to the larger ionic radii of Zr4+ (0.72 Å) substituting for smaller Ti4+ (0.605 Å) [41]. In the (200)PC region, significant peak splitting is observed for all samples, with a clear distinction between the *x* = 0.30–0.40 and *x* = 0.45–0.50 samples, which is attributed to different lattice distortions. Referring to Inorganic Crystal Structure Database (ICSD) 13964 (space group: *P*4*mm*), it can be observed that there is a strong tetragonal distortion present in *x* = 0.30–0.40 samples, characterized by two splitting peaks of (002)T and (200)T [42,43]. The observation of a higher (002)T peak than the (200)T peak is believed to be related to the domain orientation [44,45]. The near-phase-boundary composition as well as A-site vacancies caused by La3+ substitution for Pb2+ facilitate domain wall motion, thereby rendering domain orientation susceptible to external disturbances, such as stress induced by the preparation and processing of bulk ceramic samples. For *x* = 0.45, an additional peak of (200)PC emerges between the (002)T and (200)T peaks, as indicated by ICSD 13964 (space group: *P*4*mm*) and ICSD 13968 (space group: *Pm** 3¯m*), suggesting the distinct coexistence of tetragonal and pseudocubic phases. With the *x* content rising to 0.50, the intensity of the (200)PC peak slightly increases, indicating a rise in the pseudocubic phase content. Furthermore, the tetragonal distortion characterized by the *c*/*a* ratio can be quantitatively derived from the Bragg equation (2*d*sin*θ* = *nλ*) and is expressed as Eq. (1):

(1) c/a=sinθ(200)Tsinθ(002)T

where * θ(002)T and  θ(200)Trepresent the diffraction angles corresponding to the (002)T and (200)T peaks, respectively. As the Zr content increases from x* = 0.35 to *x* = 0.40, the *c*/*a* ratio declines from 1.0126 to 1.0095. For the tetragonal phases at *x* = 0.45 and *x* = 0.50, the *c*/*a* ratio decreases further to 1.0069 and 1.0059, respectively. This evolution in the lattice structure is attributed to a synergistic effect involving different factors, such as the tolerance factor (*t*), average electronegativity (*χ*) difference, and ionic polarizability (*α*) [46,47]. The tolerance factor for the perovskite lattice is defined as Eq. (2) [48]:

(2) t=RA¯+RO¯2(RB¯+RO¯)

where * RA¯,  RB¯, and  RO¯ represent the (average) radii of the A-site, the B-site, and the oxygen ions, respectively. The increase in the Zr4+ content, which corresponds to a larger  RB¯, is accompanied by a notable reduction in the tolerance factor, from 0.9749 for x* = 0.30 to 0.9632 for *x* = 0.50. As the stability of the tetragonal phase is positively correlated with the tolerance factor, a weaker ferroelectric and decreased *T*C are obtained with increasing *x*. More specifically, the tetragonal distortion is attributed to the displacement of the B-site ions to the apex angle of the oxygen octahedron, whereby a larger B-site ion indicates a smaller displacement length, resulting in a reduction in the tetragonal distortion and *c*/*a* ratio [49]. In addition, the Pauling electronegativities for Zr, Ti, and O are 1.33, 1.54, and 3.44, respectively [50]. Consequently, the substitution of Zr4+ for Ti4+ results in a more pronounced electronegativity difference between the B-site ion and the O ion. The stronger ionic bond results in compressed oxygen octahedrons, which also accounts for the decreased relative displacement length of the B-site ion away from the center of the oxygen octahedron [46]. The smaller ion polarizability of Zr4+ (*α* = 2.08 Å3) than that of Ti4+ (*α* = 2.50 Å3) also contributes to the weakened FE characteristics of PLZT ceramics [51]. These observations are consistent with the phase diagram, which depicts a transition from FET to RE phases with increasing Zr4+ content, as shown in Fig. 1 [37].

In addition to the global phase structure, the local short-range order structure is also crucial in perovskite ferroelectrics. Therefore, the room-temperature Raman spectra in the frequency range of 200–1000 cm−1 for PLZT ceramics are depicted in Fig. 2(c). Overall, all the compositions exhibit similar Raman spectra, but with increasing *x* content, the spectra become broader and more diffuse. To further analyze the variations in the Raman vibrations, the spectra are fitted using ten Voigt Amp G/L peak shape functions, based on the studies of Buixaderas *et al.* [52,53]. The *E*(TO2), *B*1, *E*(TOs), *A*1(TO2) and *E*(LO2) modes, associated with the Zr/Ti atoms against the oxygen octahedra (Slater band), are located between 200 and 450 cm−1. For Raman frequencies above 500 cm−1, the *A*1(LO2), *E*(TO3), *A*1(TO3), *E*(LO3), and *A*1(LO3) modes are identified, corresponding to the bending of the oxygen octahedra (axe band). As the Raman resonance peak position shifts reflect the change in the lattice structure, Fig. 2(d) displays the Raman resonance peak position for the ten modes as a function of the *x* content. A consistent trend of shifting to lower wavenumbers with increasing *x* content is observed for all modes, indicating a reduction in lattice distortion [54,55]. In particular, the displacement of B-site ions decreases, and the bending of the oxygen octahedra becomes weaker. This results in a reduction in tetragonality and the *c*/*a* ratio, which in turn leads to a decrease in unit cell polarity. In particular, the *B*1, *E*(TOs), *E*(LO2), A1(LO2), *E*(TO3), and *A*1(TO3) modes exhibit distinct changes between *x* = 0.40 and *x* = 0.45, which is attributed to the emergence of the pseudocubic phase. To describe the breadth of the Raman resonance peak, we introduce the ratio of the intensity to the full width at half maximum (FWHM) for the peak, as shown in Fig. 2(f). A higher ratio indicates a less broad and diffuse Raman resonance peak. As the *x* content increases, reflecting enhanced structural disorder and reduced polarity of the unit cell, the ratios also decrease, with a noticeable change occurring between *x* = 0.40 and *x* = 0.45 [56]. This further supports the phase transition from the tetragonal phase to the coexistence of tetragonal and pseudocubic phases. The deviations from the overall trend for the *E*(LO2), *A*1(LO2), *E*(TO3), and *A*1(TO3) modes can be attributed to their low intensities, which lead to inevitable fitting errors.

Figure 3(a) illustrates the heat flow curve of PLZT ceramics during the heating process. For *x* = 0.3, a sharp heat flow peak is observed near 125 °C, indicating a first-order FE-PE phase transition [57]. With increasing *x* content, the heat flow peak shifts towards lower temperatures, reaching 87 °C for *x* = 0.35 and 47 °C for *x* = 0.40. However, for samples with higher *x* content (*x* = 0.45 and *x* = 0.50), the heat flow peak diminishes, resulting in smooth heat flow curves. This behavior is characteristic of a diffuse phase transition in RE materials [36]. Figure 3(b) compares the heat flow curves of poled and unpoled *x* = 0.45 ceramics obtained by modified DSC. Notably, a heat flow peak is detected near 7 °C in *x* = 0.45 ceramics after being poled at a low temperature of −60 °C. The difference in temperature-induced phase transition behaviors between unpoled and poled samples can be attributed to electric-field-induced irreversible phase transition from RE to FE states. Figure 3(c) depicts the temperature dependencies of heat capacity data for different PLZT ceramics, where the obtained peak locations being entirely consistent with those shown in Fig. 3(a). Moreover, the heat capacity values of all compositions near room temperature approximate 0.33 J/(g·°C), aligning with the theoretical value [12].

Compared with pure thermal property curves, dielectric temperature spectra offer more detailed information about FE phase transitions, particularly those lacking a thermal response. Figures 4(c)–4(e) display the temperature-dependent relative dielectric constant (*ε*r) and loss tangent (tan*δ*) at various frequencies ranging from 0.1 to 1000 kHz. The tan*δ* for all samples within the testing temperature range remains below 0.2, potentially aiding in the ability to withstand high electric fields during EC applications. The *ε*r–*T* curves of the *x* = 0.30 ceramics exhibit a sharp increase at approximately 123 °C, corresponding to the Curie temperature (*T*C), which is consistent with the position of the thermal phase transition peak in Figs. 3(a) and 3(c). Upon slightly exceeding *T*C, the *ε**r*–*T* curves display visible frequency dispersion. The temperature (*T*m) for the maximum relative dielectric constant (*ε*m) gradually shifts away from *T*C and towards higher temperatures with increasing frequency, indicating a weak relaxor ferroelectric characteristic in the *x* = 0.30 sample. As the *x* value increases to *x* = 0.35 and *x* = 0.40, *T*C drops to 85 and 47 °C, respectively. Concurrently, the frequency dispersion behaviors are significantly intensified, suggesting that increasing the *x* content effectively reduces the ferroelectric stability and enhances the relaxor characteristics. Moreover, *T*C is undetectable in the dielectric temperature spectra for *x* = 0.45, accompanied by the appearance of *T*s and *T*f. *T*s, corresponding to a shoulder in the dielectric temperature spectra, is related to the relaxation of polar nanoregions (PNRs). *T*f denotes the freezing temperature of polar nanoregions, where the *ε*r–*T* curves exhibit an obvious inflection point, as shown in the inset of Fig. 4(e) [58]. For *x* = 0.50, *T*f is not directly discernible from the dielectric temperature spectra but can be inferred from the Vogel‒Fulcher law (Eq. (3)) [59]:

To quantitatively assess the diffuseness of the *ε*r–*T* curves near *T*m, we employ an empirical Gaussian-type relation, as depicted in Eq. (4) [60,61]:

(4) εmεr=1+(T−Tm)22δg2

where *δ*g, which measures the degree of diffuseness of the phase transition, is determined through a quadratic fit between *ε*m/*ε*r and *T*−*T*m, as depicted in Fig. 4(g). Furthermore, a modified Curie‒Weiss law is also employed to elucidate the degree of diffusiveness, as expressed in Eq. (5) [62,63]:

(5) 1εr−1εm=(T−Tm)γC

where the diffusive factor (*γ*) is one of the pivotal parameters for relaxor ferroelectrics, ranging from 1 to 2, corresponding to normal ferroelectric and fully relaxor states, respectively, while parameter *C* represents the Curie‒Weiss constant. As shown in Fig. 4(h), by plotting and linearly fitting the function ln(1/*ε*r−1/*ε*m) against ln(*T*−*T*m), the slope yields a value of *γ*. The obtained *δ*g and *γ* values are presented in Fig. 4(i) and show a nearly linear increase with increasing *x* content, indicative of an enhanced relaxor behavior. These dielectric findings closely align with the structural evolution depicted in Fig. 2.

In addition to the temperature-dependent phase transition behavior, polarization properties play a pivotal role in EC materials, as the EC effect is regarded as an external manifestation of variations in the inner polarization state of FE materials [64]. Figures 5(a)–5(e) illustrate the polarization and current density responses of different PLZT ceramics to alternating electric fields. A square polarization hysteresis loop is observed for the *x* = 0.30 composition, characterized by a large remanent polarization (*P*r) of up to 21.9 µC/cm2 and a large coercive electric field (*E*C) reaching 17.8 kV/cm, suggesting strong FE features. The current density curves further corroborate this observation, exhibiting two distinct peaks corresponding to the 180° reversal of the FE domains. As the *x* content increases, a notable decline in the squareness of the polarization hysteresis loop is evident in the *x* = 0.35 and *x* = 0.40 ceramics. Subsequently, slim polarization hysteresis loops emerge for the *x* = 0.45 and *x* = 0.50 compositions, accompanied by the absence of peaks in the current density curves. This progression further implies a gradual weakening of ferroelectricity and a reinforcement of relaxor behavior with increasing *x* content. Furthermore, Fig. 5(f) depicts the variations in the maximum polarization (*P*m), *P*r, and *E*C with respect to the *x* content. All the FE characteristic parameters exhibit transitive changes between the *x* = 0.40 and *x* = 0.45 ceramics, indicating a phase transition from FE to RE behavior.

For the *x* = 0.30–0.40 compositions, the polarization hysteresis loops gradually transition from square to slim shapes with increasing temperature, indicating an FE‒PE phase transition. This observation aligns with the trends observed in the thermal response curves (Fig. 3) and dielectric temperature spectra (Fig. 4). Notably, pinched polarization hysteresis loops are observed when the temperature slightly exceeds *T*C, as exemplified by *x* = 0.30 ceramics at 135 °C, *x* = 0.35 ceramics at 90 °C, and *x* = 0.40 ceramics at 60 °C. This is a commonly observed phenomenon in FEs with first-order phase transitions, stemming from the low barrier between the FE and PE phases, which induces a reversible electric-field-induced phase transition [65]. However, only a slim ferroelectric hysteresis loop is consistently observed across the entire test temperature range in the *x* = 0.45 and *x* = 0.50 compositions because of their nonpolar characteristics. Although the poled *x* = 0.45 composition exhibits a temperature-induced phase transition, the corresponding transition temperature of 7 °C falls outside the tested temperature range. Based on the polarization hysteresis loops obtained at different temperatures, Fig. 6(f) shows the temperature dependence of *P*r and corresponding pyroelectric coefficient (* ∂P*/* ∂T*). A notable decrease in *P*r is observed in *x* = 0.30–0.40 ceramics near 127, 88, and 56 °C, respectively, as a response to the FE‒PE phase transition. Moreover, the * ∂P*/* ∂T*–*T* curves exhibit sharp negative peaks around these temperatures. Conversely, both *P*r and * ∂P*/* ∂T* remain close to zero for *x* = 0.45 and *x* = 0.50 ceramics because of their stabilized nonpolar characteristics with increasing temperature.

*
Fig. 6Ferroelectric properties of PLZT ceramics at different temperatures: (a–e) polarization–electric field loops at 40 kV/cm; (f) temperature dependence of remanent polarization (P*r) and pyroelectric coefficient (∂*P*/∂*T*); (g–k) contour plots of ∂*P*/∂*T* value as a function of temperature and electric field.

To further elucidate the phase transition behavior under the combined influence of the electric field and temperature, Figs. 6(g)–6(k) depict contour plots of the * ∂P*/* ∂T* values as a function of temperature and electric field, derived from the upper branch of the polarization hysteresis loops (Figs. 6(a)–6(e)). A sloping light band, representing the sets of peaks in the * ∂P*/* ∂T*–*T* curves under various electric fields, is observed slightly above the *T*C for the *x* = 0.30–0.40 ceramics. This band serves as a boundary between the PE and FE phases. The positive slope of the phase boundary indicates the generation of a low-temperature phase under an external electric field, suggesting a significant positive EC effect [66]. However, only a faintly discernible bright spot is observable at 30 °C and 20 kV/cm in the *x* = 0.45 ceramics, indicating that the phase boundary may manifest more prominently at lower temperatures. Owing to their similar nonpolar behavior, the contour plots of the * ∂P*/* ∂T* value for the *x* = 0.50 composition remain consistently dark across the entire region. The varying characteristics or positions of the phase boundaries in different PLZT ceramics afford them a diverse range of EC application temperatures, ranging from above 120 °C to below room temperature.

To evaluate the EC effect in PLZT ceramics, direct heat measurements were conducted under isothermal conditions via modified DSC, and the obtained typical real-time heat flow curve under a DC electric field is depicted in Fig. 7(a). The heat flow curve sharply increases upon application of the electric field, followed by a return to baseline after sufficient time and a subsequent abrupt decline upon removal of the electric field. The constant baseline indicates minimal Joule heating, thereby corroborating the veracity of the EC measurement results. Furthermore, to minimize potential errors, each data point is measured four times for averaging. The released heat is 5% greater than the absorbed heat in the given case because of the electric energy loss caused by polarization hysteresis, which is a common phenomenon induced by the electric-field phase transition [30,67]. Therefore, we choose the absorbed heat to deduce the changes in the EC temperature. As illustrated in Fig. 7(b), all the PLZT ceramics exhibit positive EC effects across the entire test temperature range. For the *x* = 0.30 ceramics, a significant maximum EC effect of Δ*T*m = 1.1 K (@40 kV/cm) is observed at 130 °C, which is slightly above *T*C. As the *x* content increases, the location of Δ*T*m shifts to lower temperatures in correspondence with the change in *T*C, accompanied by a decrease in the Δ*T*m value. Ultimately, the *x* = 0.50 ceramics exhibit Δ*T*m of 0.59 K (@40 kV/cm) at −30 °C. It is known that the EC effect is a strong electric field-related property, so the EC effect reflects the phase transition behavior for the poled *x* = 0.45 ceramics, rather than the unpoled ones, based on the electric-field-induced irreversible phase transition in Fig. 3(b). Therefore, the temperature-related first-order phase transitions in the *x* = 0.30–0.40 and poled *x* = 0.45 ceramics result in sharp EC peaks, whereas a broad EC peak is observed in the *x* = 0.50 RE composition with a diffuse phase transition.

*
Fig. 7EC effects of PLZT ceramics via direct heat measurement: (a) typical isothermal heat flow curve under a periodic DC electric field; (b) EC temperature changes as a function of temperature under different applied electric fields; (c) evolution of maximum EC temperature changes with increasing x* content under different applied electric fields, along with underlying physical mechanism; (d) variations of operation temperatures for different settling conditions with increasing *x* content and associated application scenarios.

To delve into the physical mechanism governing the evolution of the EC peak values in the PLZT ceramics, Fig. 7(c) illustrates Δ*T*m with increasing *x* content under different excited electric fields. In the *x* = 0.30–0.45 ceramics, a mere 10 kV/cm electric field yields a substantial EC effect, attributed to the electric-field-induced PE‒FE phase transition [31]. Beyond this threshold, the EC effect slightly increases, with a relative increase rate lower than that observed in the 0–10 kV/cm range. This increase in the EC effect originates from microstructural changes in the FE phase, including domain growth and dipole stretching [38]. However, the *x* = 0.50 ceramics display a distinct evolutionary trend in terms of the EC effect. An electric field of 10 kV/cm induces a small EC effect of only 0.1 K, while the change of 10–20 kV/cm triggers a large EC effect. The former is ascribed to the orientation or growth of PNRs, while the latter results from an electric-field-induced RE‒FE phase transition [63]. The further increase in the electric field induces a slight additional increase in the EC effect, analogous to the microstructural changes observed in the *x* = 0.30–0.45 ceramics. The segmented evolutionary behavior of EC Δ*T* versus the electric field is demonstrated by Landau–Devonshire phenomenological theory [68]. Moreover, the EC effect originating from the RE‒FE phase transition in the *x* = 0.50 ceramics is significantly weaker than that resulting from the PE‒FE phase transition in the *x* = 0.30–0.45 ceramics. This can be attributed to the configurational entropies for the three phases, which follow a specific relationship of PE > RE > FE [63].

In addition to the magnitude of Δ*T*m, the EC operation temperature serves as a crucial parameter for practical refrigeration applications. For example, the normal operation of some large-scale industrial equipment may exceed 100 °C, while micro-scale electronic chips within integrated circuits typically cannot withstand temperatures above approximately 70 °C. In daily life, essential appliances such as air conditioners and freezer cabinets must maintain temperatures close to 25 and 0 °C, respectively. Figure 7(d) illustrates the operation temperature ranges corresponding to Δ*T*m and several fractional multiples of Δ*T*m in the PLZT ceramics. As the *x* content increases, there is a nearly linear decline in the operation temperature, ranging from up to 150 to −45 °C, fully meeting the requirements of the aforementioned refrigeration application scenarios. Furthermore, manipulation of the *x* content holds the potential to facilitate ultra-high and ultra-low temperature refrigeration applications in extreme environments. In addition to Δ*T*m and operation temperature, the EC strength (Δ*T/*Δ*E*) is also an important parameter for EC materials. While the obtained EC strength corresponding to Δ*T*m in our study is only 0.0275 (K·cm)/kV, the composition and process can be optimized to enhance the dielectric peak and subsequently improve the EC strength, thus enabling further enhancement of the EC refrigeration capacity [69,70].

## 4 Conclusions

In conclusion, our research successfully demonstrates the successful manipulation of the Zr/Ti ratio in Pb0.775La0.15Zr*x*Ti1−*x*O3 bulk ceramics to achieve large electrocaloric effects with adjustable operation temperatures spanning. By utilizing the room-temperature (PbLa)(ZrTi)O3 phase diagram, we strategically design compositions spanning the ferroelectric and relaxor ferroelectric phase regions. The structural, thermometric, dielectric, and polarization analyses reveal a gradual decrease in the phase transition temperature from 125 °C to below 0 °C with increasing Zr/Ti ratio. Consequently, the ceramics exhibit an adjustable operation temperature with a large electrocaloric effect ranging from 150 to −45 °C, as validated by direct electrocaloric heat measurements. This meets the diverse refrigeration application requirements, ranging from industrial equipment to freezer cabinets, and presents the potential for refrigeration in more extreme temperature environments. Our work not only proposes an effective design solution for high-performance electrocaloric material design but also broadens their application scenarios.
