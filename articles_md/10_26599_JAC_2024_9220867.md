## 1 Introduction

The rapid advancement of the aerospace industry has necessitated increasingly higher demands for engine thermal efficiency, resulting in turbine blades being subjected to elevated temperatures [1−4]. In the 1950s, the NASA-Lewis Research Centre initially proposed thermal barrier coatings (TBCs). TBCs effectively insulate turbine blades, enabling reliable engine operation for extended duration in environments surpassing their melting points. Meanwhile, TBCs enhance corrosion resistance and prolong the service life of turbine blades [5−7]. Currently, conventional TBC materials are commonly composed of yttria partially stabilized zirconia (YSZ), which exhibit significant phase transition and failure when exposed to service environments exceeding 1250 ℃ [8−16]. The study of advanced TBC materials with enhanced high-temperature stability, including rare-earth pyrochlore, rare-earth magnetite, rare-earth phosphate, chalcocite, rare-earth tantalite, mullite, and others has emerged as a key factor in achieving higher thrust-to-weight ratio engines [17−24]. Among these materials, gadolinium zirconate (Gd2Zr2O7) has exceptionally low thermal conductivity, a suitable coefficient of thermal expansion, and a thermally stable high-temperature phase structure. It exhibits exceptional potential as new TBC materials, capable of withstanding temperatures exceeding 1300 ℃ [25−28].

However, Gd2Zr2O7 is also subject to chemical attack by environmental sediments (e.g., dust, sand, and volcanic ash). These sediments often carry abrasive particles (containing Fe and Ni) generated in the high-temperature gas path of the engine. They mainly consist of CaO, MgO, Al2O3, and SiO2, with minor amounts of Fe2O3, TiO2, K2O, etc. exhibiting distinct regional variation. Consequently, they are named CMAS based on their principal components [29]. CMAS becomes molten or semi-molten at high temperatures and is deposited onto TBC surfaces, penetrating the coating through its pores. Currently, the 33CaO–9MgO–13AlO1.5–45SiO2 composition model is widely accepted as a general representation of CMAS [30,31]. The simplified and unified composition increased comparability of results and facilitated systematic investigation of individual components. However, the chemical composition plays a pivotal role in governing melting, adhesion, and flow properties of environmental sediments, thereby significantly influencing their interaction with TBCs. The representation of the general composition may not fully capture complexities involved in the spreading and infiltration of chemically complex natural melts, where phase equilibria may involve completely different phases.

Recently, we conducted an elemental composition analysis of environmental sediments and found the presence of Fe. Additionally, the proportions of other oxides display significant deviations from those found in general CMAS. As it is known, the composition of CMAS exerts a significant influence on its melting point and viscosity. The estimation of the melt viscosity of a multicomponent silicate chemical model could be conducted based on Vogel–Fulcher–Tammann (VFT) [32]. However, the available literature on CMAS melts lacks sufficient data regarding their actual viscosity. The utility of viscosity models has almost no experimental basis. Additionally, Mechnich and Brace [33] investigated the melting behavior of CMAS by incorporating transition metal oxides (FeO, TiO2), which indicated that complex compositions tend to exhibit a broader softening/melting range. To elucidate the impact of CMAS composition on both melting point and viscosity, it is imperative to comprehend the role played by each oxide in the formation of the CMAS glass skeleton. Currently, the research in this field primarily focuses on the role of SiO2, CaO, MgO, and Al2O3 [34−36], while the role of Fe2O3 is often ignored. The influence mechanisms of Fe on the melting point and viscosity of glass melt are unclear.

Additionally, the composition of CMAS has a significant influence on the TBC–CMAS interaction. The ability of TBC materials to effectively resist CMAS relies on the rapid crystalline interaction with the CMAS melt, which is contingent upon the chemical composition of the local melt. Some studies found that the presence of Fe exerts an influence on the formation of both Fe-garnet phase and spinel, consequently impacting the formation of a compact crystalline layer [37,38]. Mechnich and Brace [33] also found an unreported Gd,Fe-zirconolite recession product (Gd0.95Zr1.15Fe0.8Ti0.9Al0.2O7) in the volcanic ash-induced decomposition of Gd2Zr2O7. Although Gd2Zr2O7 coatings are famous for their good favorable corrosion resistance to the general CMAS compositions [26,28,39−42], further investigation is warranted to elucidate what it performs when exposed to complex environmental sediment containing Fe.

In this study, based on our discovery of substantial quantities of Fe in environmental sediments, an expanded “CMAS-Fe” system was introduced. Four compositional models of CMAS-Fe were deployed to investigate their properties and the influence of Fe on the melt structure. Additionally, corrosion experiments of the Gd2Zr2O7 coatings under CMAS-Fe attack at 1350 ℃ were employed to elucidate the influence of Fe contents on the degradation behavior of the Gd2Zr2O7 coatings, and thereby unraveling the corrosion resistance Gd2Zr2O7 coating under CMAS-Fe attack.

Four types of CMAS-Fe composition were employed in this study, which were determined by EDS analyzing the environmental sediments on different zones of turbine blades, summarized in Table 1. Firstly, CaO, MgO, Fe2O3, and SiO2 were pretreated in a box furnace (SX-G08163, Tianjin Zhong-Huan Experimental Electric Furnace Co., Ltd., China) at 900 ℃ for 5 h to desiccate. Finally, the molten CMAS-Fe was immediately poured into a beaker filled with deionized water. The CMAS-Fe powders were obtained after drying, grinding, and sieving for subsequent analytical tests and corrosion experiments.

### 2.2 Corrosion experiments of Gd2Zr2O7 coatings with CMAS-Fe

The Gd2Zr2O7 coatings were produced onto a graphite substrate (10 cm × 5 cm × 0.2 cm) using air plasma spraying (APS) equipment (Metro 7M, Oerlikon, Switzerland). Relevant parameters are shown in Table 2. The free-standing Gd2Zr2O7 coatings were obtained by calcining at 900 ℃ for 1 h to separate it from the graphite substrate before corrosion experiments. The coverage density of CMAS-Fe powders is 20 mg/cm2 [43,44]. Four types of CMAS-Fe were applied onto the Gd2Zr2O7 coatings using the leaky sieve method [45]. Firstly, the coverage quality of the CMAS-Fe powders was calculated based on the surface area of Gd2Zr2O7 coatings and the coverage density of CMAS-Fe powders. Subsequently, the CMAS-Fe powders were sieved onto the Gd2Zr2O7 coating surface multiple times in small amounts using a 200-mesh sieve to ensure uniformity. The CMAS-Fe covered samples were repeatedly weighed throughout the entire process until the desired quality was achieved. Finally, the CMAS-Fe covered samples were respectively placed in the furnace (SX-G08163), and subjected to short-term corrosion for 0.1 h, as well as long-term corrosion for 1 and 10 h at 1350 ℃.

### 2.3 Analysis and test methods

The heat absorption and exotherm of the melting process of CMAS-Fe were determined using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC; STA-449C, NETZSCH, Germany). The temperature range extended from room temperature to 1500 ℃ at a heating rate of 20 ℃/min under an air atmosphere. A high-temperature viscometer (BCT1700, Huanao, China) was used to measure the viscosity of CMAS-Fe. The CMAS-Fe powders were heated to 1450 ℃ and subsequently cooled at a rate of 3 ℃/min, enabling the temperature-viscosity curve. Cross-sectional microstructural characterization was performed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Tdcls4800, Hitachi, Japan) and the crystalline phase composition was analyzed using an energy spectrometer (EDS; Genesis XM2, EDAX Inc., USA). The type of the crystalline phase was determined by an X-ray diffraction analyzer (XRD; D8 Advanced, Bruker, Germany) with a scanning range from 20° to 80°. The Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR; Nicolet IS-10, Thermo Fisher, USA) was employed to acquire interferometric light for the sample analysis, enabling the determination of molecular spatial configuration. A Vickers hardness tester (HVS-1000B, Laizhou Huayin test Instrument Co., Ltd, China) was employed to measure the sample hardness. The super depth-of-field microscopy (Smartzoom 5, Zeiss, Germany) is used to record the corrosion depth every 10 h during the cyclic corrosion experiments.

### 3.1 Melting behavior and viscosity of four types of CMAS-Fe

The four CMAS-Fe compositions are derived from distinct zones of the turbine blade. The sediment particles with varying sizes exhibit distinct inertia, which affects their ability to track the trajectory of airflow. Consequently, these particles deposit on different zones of the turbine blade surface, thereby causing variations in compositions [46]. In contrast to the widely used generic 33CaO–9MgO–13Al2O3–45SiO2, our CMAS-Fe models have substantial quantities of Fe, lower Ca content, and higher Al content. Fe originates from grinding debris generated through friction and high temperatures within engine components. Among them, CMAS-Fe 3 displays the highest Fe content.

To investigate the melting points of the four types of CMAS-Fe, DSC tests were conducted and the corresponding results are illustrated in Fig. 1(a). CMAS-Fe 1 displays a single crystallization peak at 1083 ℃, whereas CMAS-Fe 2, 3, and 4 exhibit two crystallization peaks with the first one occurring at 909, 868, and 910 ℃ respectively, followed by the second peak at 1049, 1088, and 1027 ℃. Furthermore, the DSC curves reveal that all four types of CMAS-Fe display double melting peaks. The first melting peak corresponds to the melting of partially crystallized crystals during the heating process, exhibiting narrow melting widths due to their quasi-isotropic melting behavior. The first crystalline peaks in CMAS-Fe 1 and 2 exhibit a relatively low intensity, suggesting a low degree of crystallinity. Conversely, CMAS-Fe 3 and 4 have enhanced crystallinity, resulting in pronounced melting peaks with high intensity. Four CMAS-Fe samples are not melted during the two melting peaks. The second melting peak corresponds to the melting of amorphous CMAS-Fe. CMAS-Fe 1 exhibits the broadest temperature range for melting, indicating its initial melting temperature (as shown by the arrow) is ~1250 ℃. Additionally, the melting point of CMAS-Fe slightly increases with the increase of the Fe content. CMAS-Fe 2, 3, and 4 exhibit a comparable melting behavior, with an initial melting temperature of ~1270 ℃.

The high-temperature viscosity of our four CMAS-Fe samples is presented in Fig. 1(b). The viscosity of CMAS-Fe 1 with a relatively low Fe content exhibits a gradual decrease with increasing the temperature. In contrast, CMAS-Fe 2, 3, and 4 with high Fe contents display a sharp drop in viscosity at a threshold temperature of ~1250, 1125, and 1225 ℃, respectively, all of which are below their respective melting points. This observation suggests that CMAS-Fe may induce a threat to TBC even at temperatures lower than their melting points.

The threshold temperature is evidently influenced by the melt composition. In general, the higher the Fe content in the melt, the lower its corresponding threshold temperature. Additionally, upon surpassing the threshold temperature, a higher Fe content in the melt leads to decreased viscosity, which substantiates the influence of Fe on the glass network structure. However, for CMAS-Fe with a marginal difference in the Fe content, such as CMAS-Fe 2 and 4, the threshold of CMAS-Fe 2 with a slightly higher Fe content slightly increases compared to that of CMAS-Fe 4. This observation implies that in addition to Fe being the primary determinant of viscosity, other compositions also contribute to its variation. The specific mechanism will be also explained in Section 4.

### 3.2 CMAS-Fe corrosion behavior of Gd2Zr2O7 coatings at 1350 <strong>℃</strong> for 0.1 h

XRD patterns of Gd2Zr2O7 coatings attacked by CMAS-Fe for 0.1 h at 1350 ℃ are shown in Fig. 2(a). Attacked by CMAS-Fe 1, 2, and 4, Gd2Zr2O7 coatings exhibit similar characteristics with diffraction peaks of anorthite (CaAl2Si2O8). However, in the case of CMAS-Fe 3 attack, diffraction peaks of garnet (Gd3Al5O12) and cubic phase zirconia (c-ZrO2) are detected.

The cross-sectional microstructure of Gd2Zr2O7 coatings attacked by CMAS-Fe 1 for 0.1 h at 1350 ℃ is shown in Fig. 3. As illustrated in Fig. 3(a) and the magnified image of the interface (Fig. 3(b)), the reaction layer exhibits dispersed spherical phases at the reaction front, which subsequently diffuse into the middle and upper part of the residual CMAS-Fe 1. The magnified image of the top zone 1 is presented in Fig. 3(c), revealing the precipitation of the rod-shaped phase A with a black contrast. The EDS analysis results of all crystalline phases are shown in Table 4. It can be confirmed that the phase A is CaAl2Si2O8 combined the XRD result. It is an intrinsic crystallization product characterized by a low formation enthalpy, which tends to preferentially precipitate during the initial stages of corrosion [42,55−57]. Furthermore, Fig. 3(d) displays a magnified image of the zone 2 within the reaction layer, where the spherical white contrasted phase B predominantly consists of Zr, Gd, and O elements. In conjunction with the XRD result, it can be ascertained as ZrO2, exhibiting a fully stable cubic phase with 85 mol% ZrO2 and 15 mol% Gd2O3. The spherical ZrO2 particles are interspersed with a small acicular crystalline phase C, which can be determined as Gd-oxyapatite (Ca2Gd8(SiO4)6O2) according to an approximate Ca : Gd : Si ratio of 1 : 4 : 3.

According to the EDS result, it is determined that the composition includes Ca, Mg, Al, Si, and Fe elements, with their proportions being basically consistent with those in CMAS-Fe 2. According to previous studies, when the Gd content in CMAS reaches 14.75 at%, the precipitation of Gd-oxyapatite occurs [58]. However, the Gd content in the phase D is 14.19 at%, which closely approaches the threshold for the Gd-oxyapatite precipitation. We named it CMAS-FGZ, which only exists at the moment of the contact between CMAS and Gd2Zr2O7 coating and is challenging to observe in experimental phenomena, followed by immediate precipitation of Gd-oxyapatite.

Based on the corrosion experiments of four types of molten CMAS-Fe to Gd2Zr2O7 coatings, a common characteristic observed in all SEM images is that a reaction layer consisting of acicular Gd-oxyapatite and spherical c-ZrO2 is formed at the reaction front of CMAS-Fe, while c-ZrO2 diffuses upward to the middle and upper part of residual CMAS-Fe. ### 4.1 Effects of Fe addition on melting point and viscosity of CMAS-Fe

The melting point and viscosity of CMAS-Fe are determined by various oxides in CMAS-Fe glass, which are categorized as network formers, network out-growths, and network intermediates. Network formers can form a glass network and serve as the primary structural units within the glass skeleton. For instance, Si4+, possessing a coordination number of 4, forms tetrahedral structures with oxygen atoms and interconnects through apex angles to generate amorphous glass [34,55]. Therefore, the larger the number of tetrahedral connections [SiO4], the higher the stability of glass. The network out-growths are unable to form a glass skeleton and exist independently outside the network, typically with a coordination number ≥ 6. Ca2+ and Mg2+ act as network out-growths, and O2− in oxide easily dissociates from its cationic bonds to become free oxygen, thereby promoting network disconnection [35,43,59]. Therefore, within a specific range, an increase in the contents of CaO and MgO tends to reduce the melting point of glass. Also, the ratio of Ca : Si is a crucial parameter that influences the viscosity of the melt. A lower ratio of Ca : Si leads to a higher melt viscosity [60,61]. The network intermediates are between the network formers and network outgrowths, such as Al2O3 [62]. When the coordination number is 6, the intermediates exist as network outgrowths. However, upon capturing free oxygen, the coordination number is altered to 4, thereby transforming network intermediates into network formers responsible for constructing the glass skeleton [63].

According to Mossbauer spectroscopy, Fe in glass can exist as both Fe2+ and Fe3+ [64,65]. The coordination of Fe3+ with oxygen ions can occur in either tetrahedral or octahedral configurations. [FeO4] tetrahedra connects with [SiO4] units to enhance glass polymerization, while independent [FeO6] octahedra decreases it as network outgrowth [66]. Therefore, Fe2O3 is also a network intermediate, but the relative proportion of these two coordination numbers varies significantly depending on the specific components, which requires specific analysis according to the cases. To investigate the influence mechanism of Fe on glass structures of our four CMAS-Fe melt, Fourier infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was employed to analyze the glass skeleton of CMAS-Fe samples, and the spectra results are shown in Fig. 13(a). The curves all exhibit prominent spectral peaks within the range of 800–1200 cm−1, corresponding to symmetrical stretching vibrations of Si and non-bridging oxygen in [SiO4] tetrahedra [67]. This indicates that CMAS-Fe still maintains [SiO4] tetrahedra as its fundamental glass skeleton structure.

The intensity of the [SiO4] tetrahedral absorption peak exhibits a positive correlation with the Fe content in glass. First, the augmentation of elements involved in skeleton formation, such as Si, Fe, and Al, results in a reduction of the Ca content. This reduction diminishes the depolymerization of network outgrowths to glass, which is evidenced by an increase in the melting point of the glass melt. Secondly, Fe reinforces interconnections between [SiO4] tetrahedra and enhances glass polymerization. Specifically, the absorption peak in the range of 600–800 cm–1 corresponds to the tensile vibration of [FeO4] tetrahedra in CMAS-Fe, while the absorption peak near 1400 cm–1 represents its frequency-doubled resonance [68,69]. It can be observed that an increase in the Fe content enhances the tensile vibration of [FeO4] tetrahedra, indicating a stronger formation ability of these tetrahedra within the glass structure and their increased participation ability in skeleton formation. The absorption peak observed at approximately 500 cm–1 corresponds to the bending vibration of the Si–O bond [70]. The connectivity of the Si–O bond is enhanced with an increase in the Fe content, indicating that the addition of Fe2O3 provides more bridging oxygen.

However, a high content of Fe in CMAS-Fe leads to a decreased proportion of [SiO4] tetrahedra within the glass network. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 13(b), [FeO4] tetrahedra exhibit relatively poor stability at high temperatures, which tends to break away from the glass structure and transform into [FeO6] octahedra that act as network outgrowths, depolymerizing the glass structure. Hence, the high-temperature viscosity of CAMS-Fe glass decreases rapidly at a specific temperature threshold, as shown in Fig. 1(b). Simultaneously, the Fe content in the melt significantly influences the temperature threshold. As the Fe content increases, a decreasing trend of the threshold is observed. Other oxides present in CMAS-Fe also contribute to a certain extent to the threshold. According to their respective roles in glass skeleton, SiO2 can elevate the threshold value, whereas CaO and MgO have the potential to decrease it. This conclusion is substantiated by the viscosity test results of CMAS-Fe 2 and 4. Although CMAS-Fe 2 has a slightly higher Fe content, its CaO content is lower and its SiO2 content is higher than that of CMAS-Fe 4, resulting in a slightly higher threshold.

Therefore, the Fe addition in environmental sediments is undesirable. Although a higher Fe content increases the melting point of glass melt, it concurrently reduces its viscosity at high temperatures. The CMAS-Fe poses a threat of infiltration and corrosion even at temperatures below its melting point, accelerating the corrosion to Gd2Zr2O7 coatings.

#### 4.2.1 Evolution of crystallization products with Fe contents

The evolution process of the crystallization products of Gd2Zr2O7 coatings under CMAS-Fe corrosion is shown in Fig. 14(a). During the initial stage, the Gd2Zr2O7 coating rapidly dissolves by CMAS-Fe attack. The leaching process of Gd2O3 from Gd2Zr2O7 occurs until the local concentration of Gd in CMAS-Fe surpasses the threshold value required for Gd-oxyapatite nucleation. Then, acicular Gd-oxyapatite is precipitated, and Gd2O3 fully stabilized spherical c-ZrO2 remains and constantly diffuses upward. The initial crystallization products in CMAS-Fe with different compositions vary, which significantly influences changes in melt viscosity and infiltration rate. As shown in Figs. 3, 4, and 6, large-sized anorthite is precipitated in the low Fe contents melt, with its quantity being directly proportional to the Ca:Si ratio, as shown in Table 1. The precipitation of anorthite consumes Ca and Si at a ratio of 1 : 2, thereby causing a reduction in the overall Ca : Si ratio in the melt. This change increases the melt viscosity leading to a slower infiltration to the Gd2Zr2O7 coating. Therefore, despite CMAS-Fe 4 having a lower high-temperature viscosity compared to CMAS-Fe 2, it exhibits slightly less corrosive to the Gd2Zr2O7 coating during the initial stage due to the precipitation of a large amount of anorthite.

However, no anorthite was detected in CMAS-Fe 3, which has the highest Fe content. The absence of anorthite blocking and a low melt viscosity contribute to the intensified diffusion of spherical c-ZrO2. Then the precipitation of Fe-garnet is facilitated through the provision of Gd and Zr essential elements. The initial small-size Fe-garnet cannot effectively block further upward diffusion of spherical c-ZrO2, ultimately resulting in its embedding within the growing feathery Fe-garnet crystals. Additionally, Fe-garnet results from atomic substitution involving Zr, Ca, Fe, and other elements within Gd3Al5O12. Its precipitation has minimal impact on both the Ca : Si ratio value and Fe content, which results in limited viscosity changes in the melt. Consequently, as shown in Fig. 5(a), the thickness of the reaction layer resulting from CMAS-Fe 3 corrosion is significantly larger than others during the initial stage, and the Gd2Zr2O7 coating exhibits the weakest resistance to CMAS-Fe 3 attack.

In summary, Gd2Zr2O7 coatings exhibit unsatisfactory corrosion resistance to CMAS-Fe attack in the initial stage, which results from the failed formation of a compact reaction layer. The low viscosity of the CMAS-Fe melt reduces resistance to the upward diffusion of spherical c-ZrO2 and creates a pathway for further infiltration of CMAS-Fe.

#### 4.2.2 Evolution of crystallization products with time

The extension of corrosion time also induces a transformation in crystallization products. After CMAS-Fe attack for 1 h, the reaction layer tends to stabilize, and the corrosion rate is greatly reduced. Anorthite and Fe-garnet, which have a small formation enthalpy, are transitional phases that only exist in the initial stage. They would disappear with the reaction time due to their high cohesion energy [42]. While spinel is precipitated, which has a lower cohesion energy and a higher stability among crystallization products [54]. Simultaneously, an interpenetrating network consisting of Gd-oxyapatite, ZrO2, and residual CMAS-Fe is formed. The solubility limit of elements in Gd-oxyapatite is dictated by the formula MI2MII8(SiO4)6X2, where Mg2+ and Ca2+ are assumed to occupy the MI site [71−73]. Zr4+ and Gd3+ occupy the MII site, and X represents the oxygen anion [74]. The precipitation ratio (MI : MII) can predict the consumption of the Gd2Zr2O7 coating on Gd-oxyapatite precipitation, i.e., the lower the MI : MII ratio, the more the coating consumption and the less CMAS-Fe consumption [75]. MI2MII8(SiO4)6X2 Gd-oxyapatite has a maximum MI:MII ratio of only 0.25, resulting in a low solubility towards CMAS-Fe elements and the presence of residual CMAS-Fe in the interpenetrating network. Additionally, the precipitation of Gd-oxyapatite requires more coating consumption, reducing the corrosion resistance of Gd2Zr2O7 coatings.

The diffusion of elements and the consumption of Fe during spinel precipitation induce compositional changes in CMAS-Fe, leading to an increase in the viscosity and the cessation of downward infiltration. Consequently, the residual CMAS-Fe becomes stably immobilized within the gap between Gd-oxyapatite and ZrO2. However, CMAS-Fe 3 causes severe degradation of crystallization products and enhances diffusion of elements in the interpenetrating network. Consequently, CMAS-Fe with a higher Fe content may pose a greater threat to Gd2Zr2O7 coatings.

### 4.3 Mechanisms for Gd2Zr2O7 coatings resistant to CMAS-Fe corrosion under growth of reaction layers

When the Gd2Zr2O7 coating is attacked once by CMAS-Fe for 10 h, the thickness of the reaction layer does not further increase after a period of time. As shown in Fig. 9, it is evident that the sum of the thicknesses of the reaction layer and the remaining Gd2Zr2O7 coating is equal to the thickness of the original Gd2Zr2O7 coating, which indicates that the Gd2Zr2O7 coating prevents CMAS-Fe infiltration by consuming itself to react with it. In addition, the mechanical property test shows that the fracture toughness and hardness of the formed reaction layer are comparable to the Gd2Zr2O7 coating. The turbine blades rotate at high speeds during operation, and the high hardness of the reaction layer contributes to better resistance to erosion and friction caused by sediments in the high-temperature gas path [76]. The fracture toughness is an important parameter for measuring crack propagation ability. Higher toughness indicates that the reaction layers have a greater capacity to tolerate strain and resist cracks [77].

In particular, the fracture toughness and hardness of the reaction layer formed by CMAS-Fe 3 are significantly superior to those of the Gd2Zr2O7 coating. The higher hardness of the reaction layer may be attributed to its reduced porosity and increased compactness. Meanwhile, the diffusion of elements is enhanced in the high Fe content melt, thereby facilitating intergranular doping. This increases lattice distortion and other defects, leading to higher disorder and fracture energy, which ultimately improves the fracture toughness. Although there is a favorable mechanical compatibility between the reaction layer and the Gd2Zr2O7 coating, it remains imperative to further discuss the impact of the growth of the reactive layer on the corrosion resistance of the Gd2Zr2O7 coatings under continuous CMAS-Fe attack in practical service conditions.

When the Gd2Zr2O7 coating is exposed to cyclic attack by CMAS-Fe, the reaction layer continuously grows. The subsequently deposited CMAS-Fe first reacts with the top reaction layer, where the dissolved Gd continues to react with molten CMAS-Fe, and the melt infiltrates downwards. Although the reaction layer exhibits a relatively compact structure, the redeposition of CMAS-Fe changes its chemical component. The residual CMAS-Fe within the interpenetrating network becomes a low-viscosity melt due to the enrichment of Fe, which provides a pathway for the infiltration of redeposited CMAS-Fe, thereby reducing the corrosion resistance of Gd2Zr2O7 coatings, as illustrated in Fig. 14(b). Following multiple infiltration of molten CMAS-Fe, the Gd in ZrO2 layer is completely depleted, resulting in the presence of porous regions in the top zone.

Subsequently, during the downward infiltration process of molten CMAS-Fe, the solution of Fe, Mg, and Al into other phases is limited, leading to the element aggregation and eventual precipitation of spinel within the middle zone of the Gd2Zr2O7 coating. The formation of spinel is a time-consuming process, and the continuous infiltration of molten CMAS-Fe only allows it to remain at the coating surface temporarily. Meanwhile, the zone in close proximity to the Gd2Zr2O7 coating continues to be predominantly composed of Gd-oxyapatite and ZrO2. Consequently, spinel is precipitated exclusively in regular rows within the middle zone, which consumes a large amount of Fe in molten CMAS-Fe. Prolonged and continuous corrosion leads to variation in melt composition, thereby influencing melt viscosity. Although CMAS-Fe 3 exhibits the highest corrosion threat to the Gd2Zr2O7 coating during the initial stage, its high Fe content facilitates significant spinel precipitation. Then, the large consumption of Fe in CMAS-Fe 3 results in an increase in viscosity and a decrease in the corrosion rate of the Gd2Zr2O7 coating. Hence, the precipitation of crystallization products is a significant factor affecting the corrosion resistance of the coating.

Under the cyclic CMAS-Fe attack, the Gd2Zr2O7 coating alleviates the corrosion through its self-consumption resulting in continuous growth of the reaction layer. As a result, the Gd2Zr2O7 coatings had a large consumption in thickness. Although the reaction layer exhibits matched mechanical properties, it cannot resist the continuous infiltration of CMAS-Fe, which degrades the coating performance and even poses a potential risk of complete depletion of the Gd2Zr2O7 coating. First, the porosity of the reaction layer is significantly reduced, thereby greatly reducing the strain tolerance. Secondly, the thermal conductivity of the CMAS-Fe melt is greater than that of the Gd2Zr2O7 coating [78,79], so the presence of residual CMAS-Fe in the interpenetrating network further increases the thermal conductivity. The thermal insulation performance of the Gd2Zr2O7 coating would gradually diminish with the continuous growth of the reaction layer. Therefore, the Gd2Zr2O7 coating exhibits unsatisfactory corrosion resistance in long-term applications.

In the context of developing strategies for new TBC materials against environmental sediment corrosion, it is imperative to comprehensively consider the impact of diverse components. The measures of melting point and viscosity of environmental sediments with varying compositions contribute to the analysis of their permeability dynamics. Also, the investigation of the influence mechanism of various components on TBC corrosion resistance is conducive to the development of TBC modification strategies tailored to specific local conditions. Additionally, the corrosion resistance of Gd2Zr2O7 to CMAS with various components needs to be further improved. First, the coating composition can be modified. It is essential to form compact crystalline layers on the Gd2Zr2O7 coating at the initial attack stage by adding rare earth elements. The stability of the crystal lattice can be also enhanced by doping rare earth atoms, thereby improving the capability of Gd2Zr2O7 coatings to maintain structural integrity. Secondly, the structural modification is also effective. The top layer laser glazing treatment could enhance the microstructure compactness of the coating, or design a composite coating structure to effectively prevent melt infiltration.

## 5 Conclusions

In this study, four types of CMAS-Fe were employed to investigate the influence of the Fe content on the CMAS-Fe structure. Furthermore, the mechanism underlying microstructure evolution and degradation of Gd2Zr2O7 coatings when attacked by CMAS-Fe was elucidated, yielding the following conclusions.

(i) Fe acts as a network intermediate in CMAS-Fe glass, which could form tetrahedrons [FeO4] that participate in the formation of a glass skeleton, thereby increasing the melting point of CMAS-Fe. However, at high temperatures, the transformation of [FeO4] tetrahedrons into octahedral structures [FeO6] disrupts the polymerization of glass. Hence, the higher the Fe content, the lower the high-temperature viscosity of CMAS-Fe.

(ii) The precipitation of crystallization products influences the corrosion resistance of the Gd2Zr2O7 coating to CMAS-Fe, which varies with Fe contents and corrosion time. During the initial corrosion, Gd-oxyapatite and c-ZrO2 are precipitated from Gd2Zr2O7 coatings. A high ratio of Ca : Si facilitates the precipitation of anorthite, thereby increasing the melt viscosity. While a high Fe content promotes Fe-garnet precipitation. Prolonged corrosion time results in an interpenetrating network composed of Gd-oxyapatite, ZrO2, and residual CMAS-Fe. Meanwhile, anorthite and Fe-garnet disappear, while spinel is precipitated with its amount positively correlated to the Fe content. In addition, the attack of CMAS-Fe with higher Fe contents results in severer degradation of crystallization products.

(iii) The reaction layer exhibits comparable hardness and fracture toughness to the Gd2Zr2O7 coating, thereby achieving favorable mechanical compatibility. However, under the cyclic CMAS-Fe attack, the residual CMAS-Fe in the interpenetrating network provides a pathway for the redeposited CMAS-Fe infiltration. The precipitation of crystallization products significantly influences the melt viscosity and infiltration rate by affecting the melt composition. The Gd2Zr2O7 coatings alleviate the detrimental effects of melt through its self-consumption, exhibiting unsatisfactory corrosion resistance under continuous CMAS-Fe attack.
