## 1 Introduction

The demand for novel high-performance materials has become increasingly important to meet the increasing requirements for applications in extreme environments. High-entropy (HE) ceramics are solid solutions of inorganic compounds with at least five elements in equal or nearly equal atomic ratios [1]. HE ceramics have become a research hotspot in recent years, and many research papers have been published on their preparation and property evaluation. The reported ceramic systems include high-entropy oxides [2], borides [3−7], carbides [8−11], silicides [12,13], nitrides [14], carbonitrides [15], and fluorides [16]. HE ceramics have excellent mechanical properties [17], thermophysical properties [18,19], catalytic performance [20], and energy storage capabilities [21,22].

The diborides and carbides of the transition metal elements of IVB, VB, and VIB groups generally demonstrate high melting temperatures and good oxidation resistance. They are usually classified as ultra-high temperature ceramics (UHTCs) [23]. In comparison, diboride ceramics show better oxidation resistance, and carbide ceramics show higher melting temperatures or heat resistance. Making composites of diborides and carbides is a straightforward approach to maximize the features of the two phases [24−26]. High-entropy composition design is believed to provide a much wider possibility for adjusting the properties of both diborides and carbides. UHTCs consisting of dual-phase high-entropy diborides and carbides have recently been proposed and reported [27−31]. In previous work, two preparation routes were used to fabricate the above-mentioned dual-phase high-entropy diboride and carbide ceramics. The 1st route: Qin *et al*. [27] prepared a series of dual-phase (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2–(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C UHTCs by spark plasma sintering (SPS) at 2200 °C using commercial single-component diboride and carbide powders as raw materials. They found that the metal element compositions in the diboride and carbide phases were different owing to their different formation energies. Although compositional segregation was observed in the final ceramics, the reported work did not demonstrate the metal element exchange process. It is still unclear whether the metal element exchange occurred among single-component diborides and carbides before the formation of high-entropy phases or started between the high-entropy diboride and high-entropy carbide phases after their formation. The 2nd route: Luo *et al*. [31] synthesized dual-phase high-entropy diboride and carbide powders through boro/carbothermal reduction (BCTR) using metal oxides, B4C, and C as starting material. Then they fabricated dual-phase (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)B2–(Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C ceramics by SPS at 2000 °C. Compositional segregation was also found in the prepared dual-phase high-entropy diboride and carbide ceramics. However, the reported work did not study whether the metal element exchange directly occurred during the boro/carbothermal reduction process of the dual-phase high-entropy diboride and carbide powders or whether it occurred during the sintering process of the final dual-phase ceramics.

Medium-entropy ceramics with material properties no less than those of HE ceramics have been developed [32−34]. Demirskyi *et al*. [35] produced medium-entropy (Zr1/3Hf1/3Ta1/3)B2 (ZHTB) ceramics by SPS using commercial diboride powders. The flexural strength was 318±14 MPa at room temperature (RT). Zhang *et al*. [36] prepared a series of Hf-based ternary diboride ceramics based on boro/carbothermal-derived diboride powders, namely, (Ti1/3Zr1/3Hf1/3)B2, (Zr1/3Hf1/3Ta1/3)B2, and (Ti1/3Hf1/3Ta1/3)B2. They pointed out that no obvious oxide impurities or refined microstructures were found in the (Zr1/3Hf1/3Ta1/3)B2 system. On the other hand, equimolar compositions of carbide solid solutions of Zr, Hf, and Ta were prepared using arc melting. (Hf,Ta)C had the best oxidation resistance, and the Vickers hardness of (Zr,Hf,Ta)C was 27.1±2.3 GPa at a load of 100 gf [37]. Although various single-phase ternary medium-entropy diboride and carbide ceramics have been reported, investigations of the phase composition and material properties of dual-phase medium-entropy diboride–carbide ceramics, especially on the chemical compatibility and phase equilibrium relationship between the medium-entropy diboride and carbide, have been scarce until now.

In this study, (Zr,Hf,Ta)B2–(Zr,Hf,Ta)C dual-phase medium-entropy diboride–carbide ceramics were prepared by SPS using synthesized medium-entropy diboride and carbide powders. The possible exchange of existing metal elements between the medium-entropy diboride and carbide phases during the sintering of the samples was examined. The densification, microstructure, mechanical properties, and thermal properties of the dual-phase ceramics were investigated.

### 2.1 Synthesis of single-phase medium-entropy diboride and carbide powders

(Zr1/3Hf1/3Ta1/3)B2 (ZHTB) powders were synthesized by boro/carbothermal process reduction between transition metal oxides and B4C. (Zr1/3Hf1/3Ta1/3)C (ZHTC) powders were synthesized by carbothermal reduction of oxides and C. The raw materials used were commercially available B4C (~1.5 μm, Suzhou Topo New Materials Co., Ltd., China), carbon black (purity ≥ 99%, ~0.04 μm, Alfa Aesar, China), and oxide powders. The monoclinic-ZrO2 (purity ≥ 99.95%, ~0.2 μm) was provided by Dongguan Surpass Structural Ceramics Co., Ltd., China. Ta2O5 (purity ≥ 99.5%, ~1.5 μm) was purchased from Zhuzhou Cemented Carbide Group Co., Ltd., China. The monoclinic-HfO2 (purity > 99.5%, ~100 nm) was provided by Found Star Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China. The synthesis routes were reported in our previous publication, and the synthesis temperature was 1700 °C [7,10].

### 2.2 Preparation of dual-phase medium-entropy ceramics

The as-synthesized ZHTB and ZHTC powders were weighed according to the designed compositions: *x*ZHTB–*y*ZHTC, where *x* = 5, 25, 50, 75, and 95 vol%, and *x* + *y* =100. The corresponding samples were denoted as 05B/95C, 25B/75C, 50B/50C, 75B/25C, and 95B/05C, respectively. The starting materials were homogenized in ethanol for 24 h using ZrO2 ball mixing media in a roller jar mill and subsequently dried using a rotary evaporator. The dried mixtures were passed through a 200-mesh sieve and then sintered via the SPS process (HP D 25, FCT, Germany). The first stage involved heating at a rate of 100 °C/min to 1700 °C and maintaining an applied pressure of 30 MPa for 5 min for the removal of absorbed gases and volatile oxide impurities. In the second stage, the temperature was increased to 2100 °C and held for 10 min under an applied pressure of 50 MPa to achieve final densification.

### 2.3 Characterizations

The bulk density of the obtained ceramics was measured by using the Archimedes principle. The theoretical densities of dual-phase ceramics were calculated from the theoretical densities of single-phase medium-entropy diborides and carbides with the rule of mixture (ROM) method. The theoretical densities of single-phase medium-entropy diborides and carbides were calculated based on their cell masses and lattice parameters. Phase composition analysis of the synthesized powders and sintered bulk ceramics was carried out using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8-Advance, Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation at 40 kV and 40 mA in the range of 2*θ* from 10° to 90°. Si was employed as an internal standard for calibrating the XRD peak positions of the samples. The lattice parameters were subsequently calculated from the XRD data via JADE 6.0 software. Rietveld structure refinement of the XRD data for the 50B/50C sample (crushed into powder and passed through a 200-mesh sieve) was performed using FULLPROF software. The microstructure was characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Maia3, Tescan, Czech Republic) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDS; SU8220, Hitachi, Japan) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-2100 F, JEOL, Japan). A high accelerating voltage of 10 kV and a working distance of 10 mm were used under the secondary (SE) and back-scattered (BSE) electron modes. The average grain sizes of the medium-entropy diboride and carbide phases were measured using the linear intercept method based on BSE images, with more than 200 grains counted for each phase.

The Young’s modulus was tested using an advanced ultrasonic material characterization system (UMS-100, TECLAB, France) based on the density and thickness of the sintered ceramics. A Vickers hardness tester (HXD-1000TM/LCD, Taiming, China) was used to measure the hardness (HV1.0 and HV5.0) of the polished samples with a load of 9.8 and 49 N for 15 s. The fracture toughness of the sintered ceramic samples was calculated according to the Anstis equation [38]. The thermal conductivities were measured and calculated using an instrument (LFA467, Netzsch, Germany) from 50 to 500 °C, the details of which can be found in our previous work [7].

### 3.1 Phase composition of ZHTB and ZHTC samples

Figure 1 compares the XRD patterns of medium-entropy ZHTB and ZHTC powders with those of ZrB2, HfB2, TaB2, ZrC, HfC, and TaC powders obtained from standard PDF cards. XRD confirms that the synthesized powders have good crystallinity due to the high heat-treatment temperature. Two sets of diffraction peaks corresponding to the AlB2-type hexagonal lattice (space group *P*6/*mmm*) appear in the XRD pattern of ZHTB powders (Fig. 1(a)), indicating that the synthesized ZHTB powder is a phase mixture of two solid solutions with the same hexagonal crystal structure. The designed single-phase medium-entropy diboride with a nominal composition (Zr1/3Hf1/3Ta1/3)B2 cannot be formed at 1700 °C via the current synthesis route. Moreover, the synthesized ZHTC powder is also a dual-phase solid solution with the same face-centered crystal structure, as shown in Fig. 1(b). In previous studies, a similar phenomenon was found in the synthesis of (Ti0.2Zr0.2Hf0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2)C powders by the carbothermal reduction process [39].

### 3.2 Phase evolution of dual-phase medium-entropy ceramics

The XRD patterns of the sintered dual-phase medium-entropy ceramics are shown in Fig. 3(a). All the samples exhibit diffraction peaks corresponding to a hexagonal AlB2 phase (nominally HfB2: PDF#75-1049) and a cubic rock salt phase (nominally HfC: PDF#73-0475), indicating that the sintered ceramics consist of diboride and carbide dual phases. In addition, small peaks for oxide impurities can be observed only in the 05B/95C ceramics. Figure 3(b) illustrates that the (100) diffraction peak of the diboride phase shifts to high angles owing to a decrease in the lattice parameters: from *a* = *b* = 3.156 Å and *c* = 3.494 Å in 05B/95C to *a* = *b* = 3.132 Å and *c* = 3.436 Å in 95B/05C. The (111) diffraction peak of the carbide phase shifts to lower angles due to an increase in the lattice parameters: from *a* = *b* = *c* = 4.527 Å in 95B/05C to *a* = *b* = *c* = 4.581 Å in 05B/95C (Table 1).

The composition of  each phase in the sample was analyzed using point-type EDS (Table 2). As the medium-entropy diboride phase content in the dual-phase ceramics increases, the Zr content decreases, and the Ta content in the medium-entropy diboride phase increases. ### 3.3 Metal element exchange analysis

EDS analysis of 50B/50C samples reveals a clear difference in element distribution (Fig. 4). Zr element tends to combine with B, while Ta prefers to combine with C. The Hf metal element concentration seems to be uniformly distributed in both phases. The difference in the formation energies of single-component metal diborides (* EfMB2) and carbides ( EfMC) could explain the preferential solid solution of transition metals in diborides vs. carbides [27]. In this experiment, as  EfZrB2 (−0.994 eV·atom−1) is much lower than  EfZrC(−0.948 eV·atom−1) [29], the Zr element is more stable in the medium-entropy diboride phase. In contrast,  EfTaB2 (−0.649 eV·atom−1) is much greater than  EfTaC (−0.740 eV·atom−1) [29], and Ta element is more stable in the medium-entropy carbide phase. Thus, Zr and Ta elements exchange occurred during sintering (Zr1/3Hf1/3Ta1/3)B2–(Zr1/3Hf1/3Ta1/3)C ceramics, resulting in a Zr-riched medium-entropy diboride phase and Ta-riched medium-entropy carbide phase. The ratio of the percentage of element M in the diboride phase to that in the carbide phase ( XMZHTB/XMZHTC) can reflect the Zr-rich and Ta-rich degrees samples. ### 3.4 Microstructure and densification

The BSE images for the polished surfaces of the sintered dual-phase samples are shown in Fig. 7. Refined grain sizes were found in the dual-phase diboride‒carbide ceramics. For a given volume of dispersed particles, a dual-phase structure increases the distance between phases or decreases the frequency of grain boundaries between the same phases, which could inhibit grain growth. An increase in the number of dispersed particles also enhances the inhibition of grain growth [41]. The 50B/50C sample (diboride phase ~1.3 μm and carbide phase ~1.4 μm) exhibits smaller mean grain sizes than the single-phase diboride (~3.3 μm) and carbide (~5.2 μm) ceramics. The carbide phase grain size (~10.8 μm) in 05B/95C was larger than that of the ZHTC ceramics. It can be assumed that a small amount of the added diboride acted as a sintering aid and promoted the growth of carbide grains.

All the dual-phase diboride–carbide samples have no obvious pores and are fully dense, which benefits from metal element exchange between the diboride and carbide phases. In general, sintering has three driving forces: the surface free energy of the starting powder particles, the applied pressure, and the chemical reaction [42]. Metal element exchange is a type of chemical reaction that provides a driving force to promote the densification process. To verify the effect of metal exchange on the densification process, dual-phase ceramics with a nominal composition of 50 vol% (Zr0.51Hf0.20Ta0.19)B2–50 vol% (Zr0.25Hf0.28Ta0.47)C (named 50B'/50C'), namely, the final composition of 50B/50C sample after SPS (Table 2), were prepared by the same process as that used for 50B/50C. It is assumed that there is no metal element exchange in 50B'/50C' during sintering. The relative density changes with sintering time during the sintering process of 50B/50C and 50B'/50C' are compared, as shown in Fig. 8. The 50B/50C sample demonstrates a better sintering behavior than the 50B'/50C' sample, and this phenomenon is attributed mainly to metal element exchange during the sintering process, as discussed above. A similar phenomenon promoting the densification of diboride–carbide ceramics has also been reported in the (Ti,Zr)B2–(Zr,Ti)C system [43,44]. Owing to the metal element exchange between the medium-entropy diboride and carbide phases, the theoretical densities of the medium-entropy diboride and carbide phases in the sintered dual-phase ceramics deviate somewhat from those of the equimolar phases. This is considered to be the reason that the calculated relative densities of the sintered dual-phase ceramics exceed 100% (Table 1).

### 3.5 Mechanical properties

The measured Vickers hardness, Young’s modulus, and fracture toughness values of the samples are presented in Table 1. In general, the Vickers hardness of high-entropy ceramics is affected by two main factors. (1) Grain size. The increased hardness observed in the dual-phase medium-entropy diboride–carbide ceramic samples can be attributed to the refined grain size compared with those of the ZHTB and ZHTC samples. The average grain sizes of diboride and carbide phases in the 50B/50C sample were smaller than those in the single-phase ZHTB and ZHTC samples. The Vickers hardness of the dual-phase 50B/50C sample was approximately 24% greater than that of the ZHTB sample and 15% greater than that of the ZHTC sample. (2) Lattice distortion. The average atomic radius difference, denoted by * δ(r), is often used to describe the lattice distortion in high-entropy materials [4]. The average atomic radius differences of dual-phase medium-entropy diboride and carbide ceramics can be calculated by Eqs. (4) and (5):

(4) δ(rdual−phasemolarratio)=X∑i=1nXi(1−ri∑i=1nXiri)2+Y∑i=1nci(1−ri∑i=1nciri)2

(5) δ(rdual−phasevolumeratio)=x∑i=1nXi(1−ri∑i=1nXiri)2+y∑i=1nci(1−ri∑i=1nciri)2

where  Xi and  Ci are the molar contents of the medium-entropy diboride and carbide phases in dual-phase ceramics, respectively;  ri is the atomic radius of metal element;  X and  Y are the molar contents of medium-entropy diboride and carbide phases in the dual-phase ceramics, respectively; x* and *y* are the nominal volume contents of the designed equimolar (Zr1/3Hf1/3Ta1/3)B2 and (Zr1/3Hf1/3Ta1/3)C phases, respectively. In this study, the average atomic radius differences of dual-phase medium-entropy ceramics (~4.00%) are lower than those of single-phase medium-entropy diborides and carbides (~4.11%). This indicates that the lattice distortion of the dual-phase system is reduced after metal element exchange, which can theoretically decrease the intrinsic hardness of each medium-entropy phase. However, compared with ZHTB and ZHTC ceramics without any metal element exchange, the dual-phase medium-entropy diboride and carbide ceramics formed after metal element exchange have higher hardness values. The experimental results indicate that the hardness increase can be attributed mainly to the refined grain size in the dual-phase medium-entropy diboride and carbide ceramics rather than to the change in lattice distortion.

On one hand, the Young’s moduli of the medium-entropy diboride and carbide phases are strongly affected by their element composition, respectively. Theoretically, a high Young’s modulus of the diboride or carbide resulted from a strong chemical bond between the metal and non-metal elements. The Young’s moduli of ZrB2, HfB2, and TaB2 are 523, 535, and 497 GPa, respectively [45], and those of ZrC, HfC, and TaC are 393, 445, and 558 GPa, respectively [46−48]. Thus, the chemical bond of Zr–B is stronger than that of Ta–B in the medium-entropy diboride phase, and the chemical bond of Ta–C is stronger than that of Zr–C in the medium-entropy carbide phase. The metal element exchange of Zr and Ta that happened in the dual-phase ceramics not only resulted in the replacement of the relatively weaker Ta–B bond by the stronger Zr–B bond in the medium-entropy diboride phase but also caused the replacement of the relatively weaker Zr–C bond by the stronger Ta–C bond in the medium-entropy carbide phase, which leads to the increase in Young’s moduli of the medium-entropy diboride and carbide phases. As a result, the Young’s moduli of the dual-phase ceramics (red line in Fig. 9) were greater than the calculated ones (blue dash-dot line in Fig. 9). Furthermore, more metal element exchange should result in a more significant increase in the measured Young’s modulus, as shown by the gap between the red line and blue dash-dot line in Fig. 9. On the other hand, the diborides and carbides with different element contents have different theoretical densities. The metal element exchange process affects the real volume content of the two phases in the dual-phase ceramics. A comparison of the lattice parameters of the equimolar medium-entropy phases in ZHTB and carbide in ZHTC to those of the non-equimolar phases in the dual-phase ceramics reveals that metal element exchange can increase the cell volume of the diboride phase and decrease the cell volume of the carbide phase, which increases the volume content of the diboride phase by approximately 2% and decreases the volume content of the carbide phase by approximately 2% in the final non-equimolar dual-phase ceramics. Therefore, as the Young’s modulus of the medium-entropy diboride phase is greater than that of the medium-entropy carbide phase, a slight increase in the Young’s modulus of the dual-phase ceramics through the phase content change should exist.

### 3.6 Thermal conductivity

Figure 11 shows the thermal conductivities of the single-phase ZHTB ceramics, dual-phase medium-entropy diboride–carbide ceramics, and single-phase ZHTC ceramics. At room temperature, the thermal conductivities of ZHTB, 95B/05C, 75B/25C, 50B/50C, 25B/75C, 05B/95C, and ZHTC are 24.2, 31.7, 30.2, 19.5, 21.4, 16.8, and 13.1 W·m−1K−1, respectively. In general, the thermal conductivity of the dual-phase ceramics is affected by several factors, such as phase composition, grain size, grain boundary phase, solid solution formation, impurity phase, and porosity. Consequently, different factors are considered to control the thermal conductivity of different samples. The carbide-matrix samples 25B/75C and 05B/95C have relatively low thermal conductivities owing to the carbide matrix having low thermal conductivity. The diboride-matrix samples 95B/05C and 25B/75C have high thermal conductivities even greater than those of the single-phase medium-entropy diboride ceramics. The possible reason is the removal of oxide impurities by the existing carbide phase. It is often unavoidable that oxide impurities from raw powders and oxide contamination from the sintering process are introduced into non-oxide ceramics, including borides, carbides and nitrides. According to the EDS results, the oxygen contents of ZHTB, 95B/05C, 75B/25C, 50B/50C, 25B/75C, 05B/95C, and ZHTC samples are 0.90, 0.63, 0.62, 0.58, 0.50, 0.48 and 0.49 wt%, respectively. The oxygen content decreases with increasing carbide content, which indicates that the existing carbide phase could decrease the oxide impurity content.

On the other hand, it is well known that phase boundaries can cause phonon scattering, decreasing the thermal conductivity of dual-phase materials. Here, 50B/50C has the finest grain size and the largest amount of phase boundaries. However, its thermal conductivity (19.5 W·m−1K−1 at RT) is not the lowest but is located almost in the middle among the prepared dual-phase ceramics, as shown in Fig. 11. This may indicate that the intrinsic thermal conductivities of the medium-entropy diboride and carbide phases rather than their phase boundaries dominate the thermal conductivity of the dual-phase ceramics. Figure 12 shows the phase interface between the two phases in the 50B/50C sample. No aggregation of impurities was observed at their phase interfaces. The clear and direct interface between the medium-entropy diboride and carbide phases is considered to have mitigated the phonon scattering by a phase boundary. Therefore, the design of phase content and metal element composition of diborides and carbides in dual-phase ceramics can be tailored to adjust the thermal conductivity of the material.

## 4 Conclusions

Dense dual-phase medium-entropy (Zr,Hf,Ta)B2–(Zr,Hf,Ta)C ceramics were fabricated, and the preferential occupancies of elements Zr, Hf, and Ta in the two phases were discussed based on the analysis of chemical composition and thermodynamic calculations. The mechanical and thermal properties of dual-phase medium-entropy diboride–carbide ceramics were investigated. Several conclusions can be drawn.

1) Owing to the pinning effect, refined grain sizes of diboride and carbide phases were obtained in the dual-phase medium-entropy ceramics.

2) Preferential occupancies of metal elements (Zr, Hf, and Ta) in diboride vs. carbide are justified based on the EDS analysis, Rietveld structure refinement, and thermodynamic calculations. Metal element exchange can promote densification during sintering of (Zr,Hf,Ta)B2–(Zr,Hf,Ta)C ceramics.

3) The dual-phase 50B/50C sample exhibited a higher hardness owing to the refined microstructure. The dual-phase ceramics demonstrated a higher Young’s modulus because of the metal element exchange between the medium-entropy diboride and carbide phases. The toughness of the dual-phase ceramic samples increased with increasing carbide phase content. The thermal conductivity showed a complex relationship with the phase composition, indicating that multiple factors existed to control the thermal conductivity.

4) The design of phase content and metal element composition of diborides and carbides can be employed to tune the comprehensive properties of dual-phase ceramics of diborides and carbides.
