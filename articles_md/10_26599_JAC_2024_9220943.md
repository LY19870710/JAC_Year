## 1 Introduction

As the inevitable choice for capacitors to develop towards miniaturization, high capacitance, low power consumption, high frequency, and high reliability, multilayer ceramic capacitors (MLCCs) are widely used in various fields, such as mobile phones, computer displays, electric vehicles, and satellite base stations [1,2]. The marketization of MLCCs has led to a focus on low-cost development while maintaining high performance and integration [3,4]. Currently, the three most common metal internal electrode material systems are Ag–Pd, Ni, and Cu [5−7]. By using relatively inexpensive Cu and Ni as base metal electrodes (BMEs) and co-firing with ceramics under a neutral or reducing atmosphere (to prevent oxidation of electrode materials), it is possible to produce base metal electrode MLCCs (BME-MLCCs) at a greatly reduced cost. This also helps to avoid Ag migration issues in Ag–Pd electrodes, thereby improving the reliability and stability of MLCCs [8].

MLCCs are classified according to the Electronic Industry Alliance code, which specifies the operating window and temperature stability. Class II MLCCs are based on modified BaTiO3 [9] (dielectric constant (*ε*r) > 2000) with temperature coefficient of capacitance (*T*cc) ≤ ±15% (@−55–125 °C). Class I MLCCs, called C0G MLCCs, are fabricated from dielectric materials, such as CaZrO3 [10], MgTiO3–ZnTiO3 [11], BiVO4 [12], and MgNb2O6 [13], with lower *ε*r (20–40), low dielectric loss, and high temperature stability (*T*cc ≤ ±30 ppm/°C, @−55–125 °C). The C0G MLCCs now sell more than 100 billion parts per year [9]. With the rapid development of technology, devices are increasingly required to withstand extreme environments, such as oil exploration and automotive/avionics engine compartment circuitry, where temperatures may exceed 200 °C [7]. Thus, explorations of dielectric materials with excellent stability, high insulation resistance, and low dielectric loss over a wide temperature range of MLCCs have attracted considerable attention. Kimura *et al*. [14] reported that the inhibition of the antiferroelectric phase transition of CaTiSiO5 by Zr4+ substitution can improve temperature stability and resistivity. Li *et al*. [15] reported that codoping Zn2+ and Ti4+ can result in an ultrabroad temperature range (−100–700 °C) of stability (−27 ppm/°C ≤ *T*cc ≤ 10 ppm/°C) and a high resistivity of *ρ* > 1014 Ω·cm in BaAl2O4 ceramics. (Bi0.95Li0.05)(V0.9Mo0.1)O4 and Na2Mo2O7 ceramics with opposite *T*cc values were selected as the end elements to produce C0G MLCCs with excellent temperature stability (12].

Zirconate-based dielectric ceramics with perovskite structures are becoming increasingly popular due to their exceptional chemical and thermal stability, as well as their excellent dielectric properties [16,17], and are used as dielectric materials for BME-MLCCs. Pollet *et al*. [10,18] reduced the sintering temperature of CaZrO3 ceramics by adding lithium–calcium fluoride flux and prepared MLCCs in a reducing atmosphere. *T*cc of 13.4 ppm/°C from −55 to 200 °C was obtained for CaZrO3-based BME Class I MLCCs [19]. Temperature dependence of dielectric constant (*τ*ε) of the CaZrO3 ceramics is positive, and *τ*ε (~40 ppm/°C) can be adjusted by doping Ti4+. For instance, Ca(Zr0.8Ti0.2)O3 single-layer capacitors with 10 μm thick exhibited excellent temperature stability over a wide temperature range above 150 to 250 °C [20]. Both SrZrO3 and CaZrO3 have similar structures and can be completely solubilized. Yamaguchi *et al*. [21] indicated that Ca- and Sr-rich regions have lower dielectric losses than SrZrO3 and CaZrO3 endmembers. Therefore, researchers have investigated (Sr,Ca)(Zr,Ti)O3 ceramics, but the ceramic component remains CaZrO3 as the main component. Chen *et al*. [22,23] added Li–B–Si–Ca–Mn glass to (Ca0.7Sr0.3)(Zr0.97Ti0.03)O3 ceramics to reduce the sintering temperature and prepared BME-MLCCs. However, the dielectric loss of CaZrO3 ceramics and (Sr,Ca)(Zr,Ti)O3 ceramics with CaZrO3 as the main component is still high. Our previous studies [24−26] constructed a series of (Sr,Ca)(Zr,Ti)O3 multicomponent perovskite solid solution compounds through structural regulation and component design. Researchers have identified three (*Pm** 3¯m* ↔ *I*4/*mcm* ↔ *Pnma*) [27] or even four (*Pm** 3¯m* ↔ *I*4/*mcm* ↔ *Imma* ↔ *Pnma*) [28,29] phase transitions in the SrZrO3–SrTiO3 system due to octahedral tilting. These phase transitions may have a significant impact on the dielectric properties. Based on this finding, a series of (Sr*x*Ca1−*x*)(Zr0.95Ti0.05)O3 (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 1) ceramics were prepared, and short-range ordered superlattice structures were found in Sr-rich components, which can reduce the intrinsic loss of the ceramics [24]. Furthermore, the nonstoichiometric design eliminated the second phase of the (Sr,Ca)(Zr,Ti)O3 ceramics, reducing the nonintrinsic loss and increasing the resistivity [25]. Consequently, (Sr,Ca)(Zr,Ti)O3 ceramics with excellent dielectric properties were obtained. However, the sintering temperature of the (Sr,Ca)(Zr,Ti)O3 ceramics mentioned above exceeds 1400 °C, making it unsuitable for low-temperature co-firing with the BME. Furthermore, the anti-reduction mechanism of the ceramics requires further investigation.

Doping low-melting-point compounds is a common method for reducing the sintering temperature of ceramics [30−32]. The principle of selecting low melting point compounds is to reduce the sintering temperature of the powders without deteriorating the performance [33,34]. Mn compounds, which are typical low-melting dopants, have been widely used for this purpose [35]. Additionally, studies have shown that the resistance to reduction of BaTiO3 ceramics can be enhanced by doping Mn2+ as the acceptor [36]. However, the effect of Mn doping on (Sr,Ca)(Zr,Ti)O3 ceramics has not been analyzed in detail. Therefore, we plan to reduce the sintering temperature and improve the electrical performance of (Sr,Ca)(Zr,Ti)O3 ceramics in a reducing atmosphere by doping with Mn, making them suitable for co-firing with BME.

In this work, (Sr0.7Ca0.3)1.02(Zr0.95−*x*Ti0.05Mn*x*)O3+*δ* (SCZTM, *x* = 0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, and 0.05) ceramics were prepared in a reducing atmosphere (H2–N2). The effects of Mn doping on the sintering temperature, phase composition, microstructure, and dielectric properties and their influence mechanisms were systematically investigated. Subsequently, high-performance BME-MLCCs were successfully fabricated using SCZTM (*x* = 0.01) ceramic powders.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

Using the commercially available powders SrCO3 (99.9%, Sinocera, China), CaCO3 (99.6%, sinocera), ZrO2 (99.0%, Sinocera, China), TiO2 (99.85%, rutile phase, Klamar, China), and MnCO3 (99.9%, Alfa Aesar, China) as the raw materials, (Sr0.7Ca0.3)1.02(Zr0.95−*x*Ti0.05Mn*x*)O3+*δ* (*x* = 0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, and 0.05) ceramics were synthesized via a high-temperature solid-state reaction method. Stoichiometric powders were weighed and milled for 4 h using a planetary ball milling machine (KEQ-4L, HongChun Machinery Equipment, China) with zirconia media in deionized water. After drying, the mixtures were synthesized at 1150 °C in air. A stirring burnisher was used for 10 h to make the synthetic powder particle size *D*50 less than 250 nm. Dried powders were added to polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, 7 wt%) as a binder and then pressed into discs (13 mm in diameter and 2 mm in thickness) or cylinders (13 mm in diameter and 8 mm in thickness) under a uniaxial pressure of 160 MPa. Finally, these samples were sintered in a reducing atmosphere (H2 : N2 = 2 : 98) for 4 h. The sintering temperatures were 1400–1500 °C for SCZTM (*x* = 0) and 1250–1350 °C for SCZTM (*x* = 0.01–0.05).

### 2.2 Characterizations

The bulk density of the sintered samples was measured using the Archimedean drainage method. The phase purity was analyzed by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; CuKα1, *λ* = 1.54056 Å, 40 mA, 40 kV, D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany). 2*θ* range was from 10° to 90°, the step size was 0.02°, and the step time was 1.5 s. TOPAS software (version 3.0) was used to perform the Rietveld refinement. The microstructure of the sintered samples was observed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; Magellan 400, FEI, USA). Raman spectra of the ceramics were measured by a confocal Raman spectrometer (inVia, Renishaw, UK) with an incident wavelength of 532 nm. Elements and chemical valence distributions of the samples were analyzed by an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific, USA).

Thermally stimulated depolarization current (TSDC) measurements were conducted with a pA meter (Keithley 6517B, China), and temperature measurements were performed with a temperature controller (Quatro, Novocontrol Technologies, Germany). Volume resistance measurements were carried out on a high-temperature resistance measurement system (HRMS-1000I, Partulab, China). Volume resistance data were measured in the temperature range from 25 to 300 °C under a 100 V DC voltage. The resistivity (*ρ*) is defined as Eq. (1):

(1) ρ=Rv×Ah

where *R*v is the volume resistance of the samples; *h* and *A* are the thickness and electrode area of the samples, respectively. The microwave dielectric properties of the samples were measured using a vector network analyzer (E8362B, Agilent, China) and a temperature chamber. The temperature coefficient of resonance frequency (*τ*f) was measured in the temperature range from 25 to 85 °C. A precision impedance analyzer (E4990A, Keysight, China) and a wideband dielectric spectrometer (Novocontrol Alpha, Novocontrol Technologies, Germany) were used to measure *τ*ε and tan*δ* at 1 MHz. The value of *τ*ε is defined as Eq. (2):

(2) τε=ε2−ε1Δt×ε0×10−6(ppm/°C)

where *ε*0, *ε*1, and *ε*2 are the dielectric constants of the sample measured at 25, −55, and 200 °C, respectively. Dielectric withstand voltages of MLCCs were tested using a high-voltage power supply (Acal BFi). A radio frequency impedance material analyzer (E4991B, Keysight, China) was used to test the equivalent series resistance (ESR) and self-resonance frequency (SRF) of the MLCCs.

### 3.1 Density and micromorphology studies

The bulk density of the SCZTM ceramics varied with respect to the sintering temperature, as shown in Fig. 1(a). With increasing sintering temperature, the bulk density first increases and then remains stable. Based on the trend of density changes, the SCZTM (*x* = 0) sample in this article was selected with a sintering temperature of 1450 °C, while the other samples were selected with a sintering temperature of 1300 °C. This study utilized samples prepared at the optimal sintering temperature for follow-up testing and analysis. Figure 1(b) illustrates the relative density of SCZTM ceramics at the optimal sintering temperatures. With increasing *x*, the relative density of the samples generally increases. The relative density of all the samples is greater than 97.6%. Figure 2(a) illustrates that the undoped Mn sample contains more intracrystalline pores. This suggests that Mn doping can not only lower the sintering temperature but also facilitate the venting of gas and increase the density of the samples.

### 3.2 Microstructure analysis

Figure 3(a) displays the XRD patterns of the SCZTM ceramics. All the samples exhibit no discernible secondary phase, which is consistent with the results of our previous nonstoichiometric design [25]. The main phase is an orthorhombic (*Pnma*) perovskite structure, corresponding to JCPDS Card No. 44-0161 (SrZrO3). However, it is important to note that the diffraction angles of the orthorhombic phase and the cubic phase of SrZrO3 are very similar. Therefore, it is possible that the samples may contain the cubic phase [24,25]. The partially enlarged drawings of the XRD patterns in Figs. 3(b) and 3(c) show that the diffraction peaks shift to higher angles as *x* increases, indicating that Mn with a small ionic radius enters the lattice and forms a continuous solid solution. Figure 3(c) shows that with increasing *x*, the diffraction peaks corresponding to (321), (240), and (123) tend to gradually integrate, which suggests a decrease in the degree of orthogonal distortion of the ceramics.

Rietveld refinement was used to determine the crystal structure [37,38]. To further determine the occupancy of Mn ions, we applied this method to analyze the crystal structure of the samples. The results are presented in Figs. 4(a)–4(e). We assumed that the *Pnma* orthorhombic phase and *Pm** 3¯m* cubic phase coexist in the ceramics and that Mn ions occupy the B sites in the perovskite. The goodness of fit values (*R*p, *R*wp, and *R*exp) indicate that the finishing results have high reliability, confirming the validity of our hypothesis. The fitting results show that with increasing *x*, the cubic phase content increases from 5.51 wt% (*x* = 0) to 9.92 wt% (*x* = 0.05). In comparison to XRD, Raman spectroscopy provides more detailed and accurate bonding characteristics of the sample, making it an effective method for studying the crystal structure of ceramics [39]. The Raman spectra of the SCZTM ceramics after normalization and fitting of the Raman scattering peak are shown in Fig. 5. The fitting effect is good, with a goodness of fit above 99.8%. Compared with the *x* = 0 sample, no new vibration modes appear in the other samples, and the position and relative strength of the Raman vibration peak do not change significantly. Moreover, the Ag mode (near 420 cm−1) has the strongest vibration peak among all the samples. Therefore, there is no obvious change in the crystal structure, including the phonon vibration mode, after Mn doping. The design of our experimental component is that Mn replaces Zr4+ equally, and if more Mn (5%) enters the A-site, the crystal structure changes significantly. These results indicate that Mn enters the B-site lattice to replace Zr4+ and form a solid solution.

Figures 5(b) and 5(c) demonstrate the redshift of both the B–O bending vibration peak and B–O torsional vibration peak with increasing *x*. This suggests a decrease in the energy of both the B–O bending and torsional vibrations. This phenomenon is typically caused by an increase in crystal cell volume. However, Figs. 4(f) and 4(g) show that as *x* increases, the volume of the crystal unit cell decreases. Zhang *et al*. [37] reported that the Ti–O vibrations of the monoclinic structure are stronger than those of the orthorhombic and cubic structures in TiO6 octahedra. Therefore, we believe that this is due to a decrease in the degree of lattice distortion, which is consistent with the XRD results. The enhanced B–O tensile vibration mode (Fig. 5(d)) may contribute to the reduction in cell volume [40]. Previous studies have focused on the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the 550 cm−1 vibration mode (Fig. 5(e)), which is related to the anharmonic lattice vibration [24]. In this study, with an increase in *x*, there is little change in the FWHM, and no clear pattern emerges. This suggests that Mn doping has little influence on the intrinsic lattice loss of ceramics.

Therefore, it is necessary to understand the concentration and valence state of Mn ions in different samples. The XPS analysis in Figs. 6(a)–6(c) shows the chemical compositions and valence states of the SCZTM (x* = 0.02, 0.03, and 0.05) ceramics. It is difficult to detect the peak when the Mn content is too low, so the SCZTM (*x* = 0.01) sample was not tested. The valence states of Mn are Mn4+, Mn3+, and Mn2+, with corresponding peaks at 645.3, 642.4, and 640.9 eV, respectively. The ratios of Mn4+, Mn3+, and Mn2+ ions in the SCZTM ceramics are shown in Fig. 6(d). For all ceramics, the proportion of Mn4+ ions increases with increasing Mn doping amount, while the proportions of Mn2+ and Mn3+ ions decrease. In general, it is expected that an increase in the amount of Mn doping will result in an increase in the concentration of Mn2+ and Mn3+ ions in the sample, as well as an increase in the concentration of * VO⋅⋅.

TSDC technique can not only provide information about the types of defects but also quantitatively calculate the activation energy and concentration of defects, which can easily establish the relationship between the microscopic defect structure and the macroscopic electrical properties. Therefore, it is a reliable method for defect analysis [41]. Figures 7(a)–7(d) display TSDC spectra of SCZTM (x* = 0, 0.01, and 0.05) ceramics under different polarization conditions. The defects observed include trapped charges, defect dipoles, and space charges (mainly * VO⋅⋅). These can be identified from the TSDC spectra by the change in peak intensity (the maximum value of the absolute current density (J*m)) and peak position (the temperature at which the absolute value of the current density reaches its maximum (*T*m)) with respect to the polarization temperature (*T*p) and polarized electric field (*E*p) [42,43]. It is clear that the variations in the TSDC peaks in the ranges of 105 °C ≤ *T*m ≤ 140 °C (called peaks B1 and B2) and 185 °C ≤ *T*m ≤ 235 °C (called peaks C1, C2, and C3) are similar, indicating a potential common physical origin. Additionally, doped Mn samples (*x* = 0.01 and 0.05) exhibit a fuzzy peak (called peaks A2 and A3) at low temperatures of −30 °C ≤ *T*m ≤ −20 °C.

Figures 7(a) and 7(b) show *T*m and *J*m of the relaxation peak in relation to *T*p and *E*p. *T*m of B1 peak remains constant despite changes in *T*p and *E*p, while *J*m increases with increasing *E*p. This may be attributed to the relaxation of the defect dipole. The increase in *T*p and *E*p results in an increase in both *T*m and *J*m of C1 peak, indicating a possible relationship between the peak C1 and the relaxation of * VO⋅⋅. The changes in B2, C2, and C3 peaks in Figs. 7(c) and 7(d) are similar to those of B1 and C1 peaks, respectively, except for slightly different T*m and *J*m values. Therefore, it is assumed that the physical fundamentals of the peaks B2, C2, and C3 are the same as those of the peaks B1 and C1. *T*m and *J*m values of the A2 and A3 peaks hardly change with respect to *T*p, suggesting that the A2 and A3 peaks are preliminarily associated with the relaxation of defective dipoles.

To further determine the types of defects, the activation energy of the defects was calculated using the initial rise method (Eq. (7)) [44]:

(7) lnJ(T)≈lnA−EakBT

where *E*a is the activation energy; *k*B is the Boltzmann’s constant; *J* is the current density; *A* is a constant; *T* is the temperature. Figure 7(e) shows *E*a of the peak B1 at different *E*p values calculated using the initial rise method. The peak B1 calculated by this method is 0.67–0.71 eV. Lee *et al*. [45] reported that the activation energy of the * TiTi′–VO⋅⋅ defect dipoles in BaTiO3 is 0.72 eV, so it is inferred that the peak B1 corresponds to the  TiTi′–VO⋅⋅ defect dipoles. Formation mechanism of  TiTi′–VO⋅⋅ defect dipoles is shown in Eq. (8):

(8) TiTi×+OO×→TiTi′+VO⋅⋅+0.5O2(g)

C1, C2, and C3 peaked at 0.16–0.22, 0.14–0.58, and 0.35–0.45 eV, respectively. This peak is close to the activation energy of  VO⋅⋅ at 0.33 eV [46], suggesting that peak C corresponds to the  VO⋅⋅ defect. Peaks A2 and A3 have lower activation energies (0.18–0.21 and 0.15–0.24 eV) and are present only in the Mn-doped samples. Therefore, we believe that the relaxation of this defect dipole is closely related to Mn doping, and its defect reaction is shown in Eqs. (9) and (10):

(9) MnO→TiO2MnTi′+VO⋅⋅+OO×

(10) Mn2O3→TiO22MnTi′+VO⋅⋅+3OO×

In this case, when  MnTi′ and  VO⋅⋅ interact with each other, a  MnTi′−VO⋅⋅ defect dipole may form. Therefore, it is inferred that peak A is related to the relaxation of the  MnTi′−VO⋅⋅ defect dipoles.

The changes in the relaxation of various defects after Mn doping were further analyzed. Figure 7(f) shows a comparison of TSDC spectra of SCZTM (0 ≤ x* ≤ 0.05) ceramics under the same polarization conditions. After Mn doping, peak A is generated, while peak B gradually decreases in intensity and eventually disappears, possibly because * MnTi′ is more likely to combine with  VO⋅⋅ than  TiTi′. When a small amount of Mn is doped (0 ≤ x* ≤ 0.01), although the need for an electric neutral balance will lead to the formation of * VO⋅⋅,  MnTi′ is easy to attract  VO⋅⋅, forming  MnTi′−VO⋅⋅ defect dipoles, which will reduce the concentration of  VO⋅⋅. It is challenging for  MnTi′−VO⋅⋅ defect dipoles to move and respond to changes in high-frequency electric fields, as shown in Fig. 8. This defect dipole formation mechanism can reduce ionic conductivity and lead to a reduction in extrinsic loss. However, with increasing Mn doping (0.01 ≤ x* ≤ 0.05), the formation of a large * VO⋅⋅ increases the concentration of  VO⋅⋅. This explains the change in J*m of the peak C in Fig. 7 (f).

### 3.3 Resistivity and dielectric properties

Figure 9 shows the resistivity (*ρ*) of the SCZTM ceramics. The resistivity of all the samples at room temperature is high, above 1014 Ω·cm. However, as the testing temperature increases, the resistivity of the ceramics decreases. At high temperature (taking 300 °C as an example), the undoped Mn (*x* = 0.01) sample has a low resistivity of approximately 1011 Ω·cm, and the SCZTM (*x* = 0.01 and 0.02) samples exhibit the highest resistivity. With the continuous increase in Mn doping, the resistivity of the ceramics decreases. The variation in resistivity is consistent with the variation in the concentration of * VO⋅⋅, indicating that the ionic conductance is dominant and controlled by the defect dipole formation mechanism.

where *V*m is the volume of the molecule, and *α*D is the molecular polarizability. According to Eq. (11), *ε*r-cal is related to the unit volume polarizability of the molecule. Given that the molecular volume difference between the two phases is minimal (approximately 0.05%) and that the cubic phase content difference is relatively modest (5.51% to 9.92%), it can be reasonably assumed that the influence of two-phase coexistence on the dielectric constant can be disregarded. Although the ionic polarizability of Mn2+ (2.64 Å3) is smaller than that of Zr4+ (3.25 Å3) [47], the introduction of Mn ions also results in a smaller cell size. Moreover, due to the small change in *ε*r (*ε*r of the sample doped with 5 mol% Mn is less than 2% of that of the undoped sample), the influence of the density on *ε*r cannot be ignored [48], so it is necessary to consider many factors to explore the change in *ε*r after Mn doping.

*Q×f* values of SCZTM ceramics as a function of *x* are displayed in Fig. 10(b). As *x* increases from 0 to 0.05, *Q×f* values of the ceramics initially increase from 24,150 GHz (*x* = 0, @6.15 GHz) to 30,257 GHz (*x* = 0.01, @6.12 GHz) and then decrease to 15,250 GHz (*x* = 0.05, @6.13 GHz). *Q×f* values are influenced by intrinsic lattice loss caused by phonon vibration, internal strain, crystal structure, and other internal factors. It has been found that the crystal structure of ceramics has no obvious change in Raman spectra, and Mn doping has no significant effect on the anharmonicity of lattice vibrations. Therefore, the nonintrinsic loss is likely the main reason for the change in *Q×f* values. The response of the grain boundary is difficult to maintain with the frequency change in the microwave frequency band, so the influence of grain boundary loss is small. Under the premise that the sintering is relatively dense and there is no secondary phase, the nonintrinsic loss caused by * VO⋅⋅ will have an impact on Q×f* values. The defect dipole formation mechanism causes an initial increase and then a decrease in the concentration of * VO⋅⋅, which is consistent with the change in Q×f* values.

The dielectric constant and dielectric loss with the change in frequency are tested, and the results are shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. tan*δ* of all the samples is less than 0.001 in the test frequency range of 1 kHz to 1 MHz, indicating a low dielectric loss characteristic. Moreover, Table 2 shows that *ε*r of the ceramics at 1 MHz increases with *x*. This trend is similar to that of *ε**r* measured at the resonance frequency. SCZTM (*x* = 0) ceramics have the largest grain size and the lowest tan*δ*, so we suspect that the grain size has a greater impact on the dielectric loss of the samples at low frequencies.

In summary, SCZTM (*x* = 0.01) ceramics sintered in a reducing atmosphere has a low sintering temperature, low dielectric loss, high insulation resistance, and high temperature stability over a wide temperature range. This makes it a promising candidate for use as a dielectric layer in BME-MLCC technology.

### 3.4 Fabrication of MLCCs

The as-prepared SCZTM (*x* = 0.01) ceramic powders were mixed with plasticizer, dispersing reagent, resin, and solvent in a planetary ball milling machine (KEQ-4L, HongChun Machinery Equipment, China). A tape-casting machine (CAM-C25, KEKO, Slovenia) was used to process the mixtures. Nickel pastes were printed onto the green sheets to produce internal electrodes. Next, the printed sheets were stacked and pressed at 65 °C under 70 MPa isostatic pressure. Then, the sheets were cut into chips using a cutting machine. Finally, the chips were sintered at a peak temperature of 1280 °C for 2 h to form MLCCs in a reducing atmosphere. The MLCCs comprise several active layers, each approximately 10 μm thick, with a designed size, as shown in Fig. 11(a).

*
Fig. 11(a) Optical image, (b) cross-sectional SEM image, and (c–i) SEM-EDS images of elemental (Sr, O, Ti, Ca, Zr, Mn, and Ni) distributions of SCZTM MLCCs (x* = 0.01).

Figure 11(b) displays the SEM micrograph of the cross-section of the SCZTM MLCCs. The ceramic layers exhibit high densification and low porosity, which are crucial for the dielectric properties of MLCCs [51,52]. The Ni electrode is approximately 1 μm thick, and it is continuous without any noticeable cracks. There is no stratification between the ceramics and Ni electrodes. To investigate the compatibility and reliability of the ceramics and Ni electrode, scanning electron microscopy-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) analysis was conducted on the cross section. The results are presented in Figs. 11(c)–11(i). The analysis revealed that the Ni electrode does not diffuse into the dielectric layer, and a distinct boundary is observed between the ceramics and the inner electrodes. Furthermore, Sr, O, Ti, Ca, and Zr are uniformly distributed in the MLCCs, with no signs of agglomeration. The above results show that SCZTM ceramics can be well matched with Ni electrodes. Thus, SCZTM (*x* = 0.01) ceramics can be selected for application in BME-MLCCs.

SRF is the frequency of acceptor resonance that arises from capacitance and spurious inductance [39]. If the working frequency exceeds SRF, the parasitic inductor takes over the circuit operation, causing the MLCCs to overheat and potentially fail. ESR is the resistance or impedance of a capacitor in series at SRF. In an ideal scenario, ESR is zero, indicating that the capacitor has zero energy loss when operating at this frequency. In reality, ESR can approach zero only due to various defects. For MLCCs, smaller ESR values are preferable under the same conditions. As shown in Table S1 in the ESM, the testing revealed that SCZTM MLCCs (*x* = 0.01) had a higher SRF (≥ 155.1 MHz) and lower ESR (≤ 239.9 mΩ). Additionally, ESR and SRF of MLCCs with the same specifications decrease as the capacity increases. Therefore, by further designing SCZTM MLCCs (*x* = 0.01) with various dielectric layers, SCZTM MLCCs (*x* = 0.01) can be obtained to meet the specific needs of this work.

The capacitance of the SCZTM MLCCs remained stable over a wide temperature range, as shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. A comparison of the electrical and dielectric properties between this work and other BME-MLCCs is summarized in Table 3 [10,22,53]. SCZTM MLCCs (*x* = 0.01) achieved the lowest tan*δ* of 0.2×10−4 and the highest *IR* of 39.6 TΩ with a withstand voltage ≥ 1150 V. More importantly, *T*cc of SCZTM MLCCs (*x* = 0.01) is within ±15 ppm/°C in the temperature range from −55 to 200 °C, which is a wider temperature range than that of the other BME-MLCCs. To summarize, SCZTM MLCCs exhibit excellent electrical and dielectric properties and are well-suited for high-frequency applications with wide operating temperature ranges and ultralow losses.

## 4 Conclusions

SCZTM (0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.05) ceramics were prepared by a solid-state reaction method. Mn doping causes grain refinement in the ceramics and decreases the sintering temperature of the ceramics from 1450 to 1300 °C. Rietveld refinement and Raman spectra indicate that Mn ions are fully solubilized into B-site lattice. XPS shows that the doped Mn ions have various valence states of Mn2+, Mn3+, and Mn4+ and that the concentration of * VO⋅⋅ increases with increasing Mn doping. As measured by the TSDC,  MnTi′ formed by Mn doping is more likely to be associated with  VO⋅⋅ to form  MnTi′–VO⋅⋅ defect dipoles, while the free  VO⋅⋅ seems difficult to excite. Therefore, this behavior can increase the insulation resistance, reduce the extrinsic loss, and increase the Q×f* values of the ceramics. The SCZTM (*x* = 0.01) ceramics are the least free * VO⋅⋅ and exhibit excellent dielectric properties: moderate dielectric constant (ε*r = 36.16), high resistivity (*ρ* = 8.93×1014 Ω·cm), low dielectric loss (tan*δ* = 2.43×10−4), and high thermal stability (*τ**ε* = 15.44 ppm/°C (@−55–200 °C)). MLCCs with excellent performance have been successfully prepared. These materials meet the application requirements of a wide operating temperature range (*T*cc = 10.88 ppm/°C, @−55–200 °C) and ultralow dielectric loss (tan*δ* = 0.2×10−4). The ceramics with *x* = 0.01 also exhibit excellent performance in the microwave frequency band: *ε*r = 35.40, *Q×f* = 30,257 GHz (@6.12 GHz), and *τ*f = −9.9 ppm/°C. These materials are expected to be further applied to Cu-based RF microwave MLCCs to further reduce the sintering temperature.
