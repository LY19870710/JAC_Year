## 1 Introduction

With the rapid development of space technology, thermal structural materials play a crucial role in improving the performance of advanced aerospace vehicles [1−3]. Over the past years, graphite materials have been widely applied as hot-structured components in the aerospace industry owing to their excellent properties, such as low density, low thermal expansion coefficient, and high thermal stability [4−6]. However, their poor mechanical properties reduce the reliability of graphite materials as key structural components. In particular, in an ablation environment with oxygen and ultra-high temperature, the performance of graphite materials severely deteriorates because of oxidation erosion and mechanical denudation [7−9]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to enhance the mechanical and anti-ablative properties of graphite materials to improve their operational reliability in harsh aerospace environment.

Currently, the introduction of ultra-high temperature ceramics (UHTCs, such as WC, HfC, and TaC) with high melting points, high moduli and excellent ablation properties into a graphite/carbon matrix is considered an effective method for improving ablation performance [10−15]. Generally, coating technology and matrix modification are two common routes to increase the ablation resistance of graphite matrix. To date, many studies have shown that coating technology is an effective reinforcement method that can reduce the ablation rate by an order of magnitude compared with that of pure graphite [16,17]. For example, Wang *et al*. [18] prepared a TaC coating on a graphite substrate by chemical vapor deposition process, and the linear ablation rate of the sample was reduced to 0.17 μm·s−1 after ablation for 60 s. However, the large thermal stress at the interface and weak interfacial adhesion can lead to the risk of coating peeling and the reduction in reliability when UHTC-coated graphite materials serve as key structural components in aerospace vehicles.

Matrix modification by introducing UHTCs into graphite matrix has been proven to be a favorable route to improve the mechanical properties and ablation resistance of the graphite matrix [19−24]. Li *et al*. [25] fabricated HfC-modified C/C composites with excellent ablation resistance via combination of impregnation and chemical vapor infiltration, which resulted in a 55% lower average linear ablation rate and a 21% lower mass ablation rate than those of pure C/C composites. In our previous work [26], WC-reinforced graphite composites obtained by the surface coating and spark plasma sintering (SPS) method, whose bending strength was 14 times greater that of pure graphite, exhibited superior mechanical properties. However, the density of these UHTC-reinforced graphite composites was relatively high, which conflicted with the lightweight pursuit of aerospace materials.

Compared with most UHTCs, ZrC has the advantage of a much lower density (6.73 g·cm−3); thus, it is considered a potential reinforcement for thermal structural components in the aerospace industry and is also suitable for modifying the mechanical properties and ablation performance of graphite and carbon matrix [27,28]. In addition, ZrO2, the oxidation product of ZrC, possesses a high melting point (~2700 °C), low thermal conductivity, and good thermal stability at ultra-high temperature [29], which could establish an anti-oxidation layer for the graphite substrates.

In this work, three-dimensional (3D) ZrC skeleton-enhanced graphite composites were fabricated via combination of molten salt coating and SPS. The 3D ZrC skeleton is expected to provide an effective support for the graphite matrix to increase structural stability. Moreover, the 3D ZrC skeleton is designed to seal the graphite particles completely in the composites, and it is expected that the ZrC skeleton can provide long-lasting oxidation protection for each graphite particle. Moreover, during the ablation process, the 3D ZrC skeleton may be converted into the 3D porous ZrO2 skeleton, which can effectively isolate heat and oxygen, leading to improved ablation performance. The effects of ZrC content on the mechanical and ablation properties of the composites were investigated in detail, and the strengthening and ablation mechanisms were also analyzed systemically.

### 2.1 Materials

The graphitized MCMB powders (8–10 μm), which served as the graphite matrix, were supplied by Long Time Technology Co., Ltd., China. Zirconium hydride (ZrH2, 10 μm, 99.9%), which acted as the zirconium source, was supplied by Shanghai St-nano Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China. Potassium chloride (KCl, > 99.8%) and sodium chloride (NaCl, > 99.8%), which served as reaction media in the molten salt coating process, were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China.

### 2.2 Preparation

The preparation process of the ZrC skeleton-reinforced MCMB composites (MCMB@ZrC composites) is schematically illustrated in Fig. 1. The fabrication process can be divided into two steps: (1) preparation of MCMB@ZrC powders with a shell–core structure by molten salt coating and (2) sintering of the as-fabricated MCMB@ZrC powders by SPS route to obtain MCMB@ZrC composite bulks. The contents of ZrC in the composites can be tailored by adjusting the molar ratio of ZrH2/MCMB.

*
Fig. 1Schematic diagram of fabrication process of MCMB@ZrC composites.

First, NaCl and KCl powders were mixed at a molar ratio of 1 : 1. Then, ZrH2 and MCMBs with molar ratios ranging from 1/15 to 1/4 were added to the salt mixture at a weight ratio of 1/6. Afterwards, the mixtures of salts and reactants were transferred into the Al2O3 crucibles with lids in a vacuum furnace. The furnace temperature was increased to 1200 °C at a rate of 10 °C·min−1 under an Ar atmosphere. Then, the powder mixtures were heat-treated at 1200 °C for 60 min. Meanwhile, ZrC coatings with different thicknesses gradually formed on the surface of MCMBs in the liquid molten salts. After cooling down to room temperature, the products were repeatedly washed with boiled water to remove residual salts. The MCMB@ZrC powders were obtained after drying in an oven at 80 °C for 10 h.

Second, the as-obtained MCMB@ZrC powders were poured into a graphite die and then densified in an SPS furnace (Ed-PASIII, Elenix Ltd., Japan) under vacuum. The sintering temperature, axial pressure, and holding time were 1800 °C, 50 MPa, and 5 min, respectively. Finally, the MCMB@ZrC composites with high densities were obtained. The MCMB@ZrC composites were named as x*ZC, where *x* refers to the volume fraction of ZrC in the composites.

### 2.3 Testing

The true density of the obtained MCMB@ZrC powders was characterized by a densitometer (AccuPyc II 1340, V2.01, Micromeritics, USA). The bulk density of the MCMB@ZrC composites was evaluated using the Archimedes method, and the relative density was the ratio of the bulk density to the true density. An electronic universal testing machine (UTM2103, Suns Technology Stock Co., Ltd., China) was employed for testing the flexure strength using the three-point bending method. The dimensions of the samples, loading speed, and span were 3 mm × 4 mm × 20 mm, 0.5 mm·min−1, and 16 mm, respectively. The fracture toughness of the composites was measured by the single edge notched beam (SENB) method for five samples. ### 2.4 Characterizations

The phase composition of the composites was detected via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X'pert pro, PANalytical, the Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation (wavelength = 1.54 nm). The morphologies and element distributions of the samples were characterized via a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; SU6600, Hitachi, Japan) equipped with electron energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The interface details of MCMB/ZrC were analyzed via a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM; JM-2100F, Japan). The TEM samples were first polished to a thickness of 50 μm by using sandpaper and then thinned by using an ion beam thinner (691, Gatan, USA) until a thin area appeared. The 3D spatial morphologies of the MCMB@ZrC composites were analyzed using an X-ray micro-computed tomograph (μ-CT; Zeiss, 610Versa, Germany) with an optical magnification of 4, a voltage of 150 kV, and a current of 150 μA.

### 3.1 Phase composition and microstructure of MCMB@ZrC powders

Figure 2(a) exhibits the typical XRD pattern of the obtained MCMB@ZrC powders. The MCMB@ZrC powders are composed of only C (PDF No. 00-041-1487) and ZrC (PDF No. 03-065-4932). Figures 2(b)–2(g) display the typical morphology and cross-sections of MCMB@ZrC powders prepared with various ZrH2/MCMB molar ratios. As shown in the high-magnification SEM image (the inset of Fig. 2(a)), the ZrC grains on the ZrC surface are nanoscale, which is favorable for subsequent densification. As shown in Figs. 2(c)–2(g), the MCMB@ZrC powders exhibit a shell–core structure, where the bright gray shell is the ZrC coating and the inner dark core refers to the MCMB matrix. The thickness of the ZrC coating increases obviously with the ZrH2/MCMB molar ratio. Significant gaps were observed at the interface of ZrC and MCMB, especially in the powders fabricated with molar ratios of 1/10, 1/7, 1/5, and 1/4 (Figs. 2(d)–2(g)). This may be because the coating growth requires C atoms at the edge of MCMB particles to diffuse constantly outside the coating and react with Zr atoms dissolved in the molten salt at the interface between the ZrC coating and the molten salt. Thus, the thicker the coating, the larger the gap. By combination of true density (Table 2) and mixing rule, the ZrC content in the MCMB@ZrC powders were calculated to be 21, 24, 30, 37, and 45 vol%, corresponding to ZrH2/MCMB molar ratios of 1/15, 1/10, 1/7, 1/5, and 1/4, respectively. Here, the MCMB@ZrC composites are named as 21ZC, 24ZC, 30ZC, 37ZC, and 45ZC, respectively, according to the volume fraction of ZrC in the composites.

### 3.2 Phase composition and microstructure of MCMB@ZrC composites

Figure 3(a) exhibits the XRD patterns of the obtained MCMB@ZrC composites after SPS. With the variation in the ZrH2/MCMB molar ratio from 1/15 to 1/4, there is a significant decrease in the characteristic peaks of C. When the materials were densified by the SPS method, MCMB@ZrC composites with high density were obtained. The true density, bulk density, and relative density of the MCMB@ZrC composites are listed in Table 2. The densities of MCMB@ZrC composites with various ZrC contents are in the range of 3.16–4.23 g·cm−3. The relative density of the MCMB@ZrC composites tends to increase with increasing ZrC volume fraction. When the volume fraction of ZrC exceeds 30%, the MCMB@ZrC composites are almost completely densified without any sintering aid. In particular, 45ZC has a maximum relative density of 99.85%. However, the relative density of 24ZC is slightly lower than that of 21ZC, even if the ZrC content in 24ZC is higher than that in 21ZC, which could be attributed to the gaps between the MCMB and ZrC in the 24ZC powders.

Figures 3(b) and 3(c) present typical 3D μ-CT images of the MCMB@ZrC composite with the ZrC content of 45 vol%, where the blue skeleton refers to the 3D ZrC skeleton and the black holes refer to MCMB. The 3D ZrC skeleton can be distinguished clearly by 3D μ-CT analysis because of the different densities [30] of ZrC (6.73 g·cm−3) and MCMB (2.20 g·cm−3). The two-dimensional (2D) μ-CT images (Figs. 3(d) and 3(e)) in two directions perpendicular to each other also confirm the well-organized structure of the MCMB@ZrC composite.

The microstructures of the sintered MCMB@WC composites are shown in Figs. 3(f)–3(k). The dark areas represent MCMBs, and the bright areas represent ZrC. As shown in Fig. 3(f), the ZrC skeleton in 21ZC is uneven and discontinuous. Because the ZrC content in 21ZC is not enough to fill the triangular grain boundaries of MCMB particles, some pores appear at the triangular grain boundaries. During the sintering process, ZrC grains with high hardness are pressed into the surface of MCMB particles by axial pressure, which act as anchors to connect adjacent MCMB particles. In addition, the soft MCMB particles with discontinuous ZrC coatings deform under axial pressure in the SPS route and then stack with each other, squeezing out pores between the particles.

When the ZrC content exceeds 21 vol% in the composites, the ZrC skeleton begins to become continuous, and the anchor effect of the ZrC grains disappears. For ZC24, the ZrC content is relatively low, and the sintering driving force is insufficient, which may cause the formation of closed pores at the ZrC/MCMB interface. As shown in Fig. 3(g), some interface pores appear not only at the triangle grain boundaries of 24ZC but also in the ZrC skeleton. Therefore, the relative density of sample 24ZC is the lowest. Moreover, the thickness of the ZrC skeleton increases with increasing ZrC content. In particular, the ZrC skeleton with high uniformity and continuity is obtained when the ZrC content exceeds 30 vol% (Figs. 3(h)–3(j)). Under these circumstances, the sintering driving force is sufficient to cause the pores in the powders to be almost completely discharged, thus promoting the densification of the composites. No gap can be observed in the high-magnification image of the MCMB/ZrC interface, as displayed in Fig. 3(k), indicating that the gaps observed in the MCMB@ZrC powders have been effectively eliminated by densification during the SPS process.

### 3.3 Interface bonding

To clarify the binding interface between MCMB and ZrC, texture analysis was carried out quantitatively for each diffraction peak according to the Harris method [31] based on the XRD results (Fig. 3(a)). To evaluate the preferential orientation of the ZrC coating formed on the MCMB surface, the texture coefficient (*C**hkl*) for each (*hkl*) crystallographic plane was calculated by Eq. (3) [32,33]:

(3) Chkl=Ihkl/Ir,hkl1/1nn(∑Ihkl/Ir,hkl)

where *I**hkl* and *I*r,*hkl* refer to the intensities of the (*hkl*) Bragg reflection for the measured and reference card of the ZrC phase, respectively, and *n* is the number of diffraction peaks. According to the XRD patterns, six main diffraction peaks of the ZrC phase are identified in the MCMB@ZrC composite powders: (111), (200), (220), (311), (222), and (400). Figure 4 shows the texture coefficient values for different crystallographic planes of the ZrC phase in the MCMB@ZrC composites with different ZrC contents. The texture coefficients of MCMB@ZrC with different ZrC contents present a similar trend. The values of *C*200 are much larger than those of other crystallographic planes, indicating that the  crystallographic direction is the preferred orientation of the ZrC phase growing on the surface of MCMB.

To confirm the above conjecture, the MCMB/ZrC interface configurations were observed by HRTEM, and the results are shown in Fig. 5. The MCMB(002)//ZrC(200) interface is clearly observed in the HRTEM images. Figure 5(b) reveals that the lattice fringes of the (002) lattice plane in the MCMB and the (200) lattice plane in ZrC are 0.34 and 0.25 nm, respectively. However, an obvious amorphous layer appears between the ZrC and MCMB phases, as shown in Fig. 5(b). The elemental mapping images shown in Figs. 5(c) and 5(d) illustrate that the interfacial area contains only the elements Zr and C. These observations demonstrate that the MCMB and ZrC phases have poor interface bonding.

The poor interface bonding may be attributed to the lattice misfit of MCMB/ZrC. The lattice misfit between two dissimilar crystals is expressed as Eq. (4) [34]:

(4) f=d1−d2d2×100%

where f* is the theoretical lattice misfit degree; *d*1 and *d*2 are the interplanar spacings of ZrC and MCMB, respectively. Accordingly, the calculated lattice misfit for MCMB(002)//ZrC(200) is 36%, indicating that the MCMB/ZrC interface is an incoherent interface [35]. Generally, the interaction in the incoherent interface is weak, and the interface bonding strength is poor.

### 3.4 Mechanical properties of MCMB@ZrC composites

The effects of ZrC content on the bending strength, fracture toughness, and fracture work of the MCMB@ZrC composites are summarized in Fig. 6. As shown in Fig. 6(a), the bending strength of the MCMB@ZrC composites decreases from 66 MPa (21ZC) to 41 MPa (24ZC), then increases with increasing ZrC content, and finally increases to 112 MPa (45ZC). The fracture toughness first decreases from 1.21 MPa·m1/2 (21ZC) to 0.99 MPa·m1/2 (24ZC) and then increases with increasing ZrC content. 45ZC exhibits the maximum fracture toughness, whose value is 1.72 MPa·m1/2. Compared with pure graphite [36−38] (Table 3), the 3D skeleton enhances the mechanical properties of the MCMB@ZrC composites to some extent.

where γ*, *S*, *P*, and *x* denote the fracture work, the cross-sectional area of samples, the load, and the displacement, respectively. Fracture work is the energy required to form a new unit fracture area due to crack propagation in the process of fracture of a material [40], which is an important reference for measuring the thermal stress resistance of the material [41]. Interestingly, the fracture performance of MCMB@ZrC composites slightly differs (Fig. 6(d)), demonstrating that the crack propagation resistance of the MCMB@ZrC composites with varying ZrC contents is similar to that of the other samples.

The fracture morphologies of the MCMB@ZrC composites are shown in Fig. 7. Surprisingly, 21ZC shows a hybrid mode of intergranular and transgranular fractures. As shown in Fig. 7(a), some of the cracks propagate through the MCMB particles to split the MCMB particles (the arrows marked in Fig. 7(a)), whereas the other cracks propagate along the MCMB/ZrC interface to make the MCMB particles be pulled out intactly. Figure 7(b) shows a high-magnification image of the MCMB/ZrC interface in 21ZC after fracture. Some ZrC grains are anchored into the MCMB particles by SPS (the arrows marked in Fig. 7(b)), which remarkably strengthens the interface bonding between MCMB and ZrC. Therefore, 21ZC presents better mechanical properties than 24ZC even if the ZrC content in 21ZC is the lowest. However, as the ZrC grains contact each other to form a continuous skeleton among the MCMB particles, the pinning effect of the ZrC grains disappears, and the interface bonding is weakened. As shown in Fig. 7(c), sample 24ZC has a typical intergranular fracture morphology. In particular, a significant crack appears at the MCMB/ZrC interface (yellow arrow in Fig. 7(c)), indicating poor interface bonding between MCMB and ZrC. The residual pores at the MCMB/ZrC interface further weaken the interfacial strength. In addition, a continuous and thin ZrC skeleton is formed at 24ZC, which is not strong enough to withstand the load. Thus, 24ZC has the worst mechanical properties. As displayed in Figs. 7(c)–7(e), samples 30ZC, 37ZC, and 45ZC, with continuous and thick ZrC skeletons, also present typical intergranular fracture morphologies. When a continuous and thick ZrC skeleton is formed, it becomes the main load-bearing phase in the composites during fracture. The strength of the ZrC ceramic is an order of magnitude greater than that of MCMB [38,42]. Despite the poor interface bonding, the mechanical properties can still be improved by increasing the skeleton thickness. Therefore, the bending strength and toughness of the composites increase with increasing the ZrC content.

### 3.5 Ablation performance of MCMB@ZrC composites

The oxyacetylene torch ablation test at the heat flux of 2.4 MW·m−2 for 60 s was performed to evaluate the ablation performance of MCMB@ZrC composites. The micrographs of MCMB@ZrC composites before and after the ablation tests are displayed in Fig. 8. As shown in Fig. 8(b), a white coating is peeled off from the surface of 21ZC after ablation. In addition, there is no obvious ablation pit on the ablated surface of 21ZC. The whole top layer seems to have been stripped away by the oxyacetylene torch flame with high scour. When the ZrC content is varied from 24 to 37 vol%, the integrity of the MCMB@ZrC composites is maintained after ablation. However, a significant crack cuts across the ablated surface of 37ZC. When the ZrC content increases to 45 vol%, the MCMB@ZrC composite fractures directly during the ablation test. These findings indicate that the thermal shock resistance of the MCMB@ZrC composite decreases with increasing the ZrC content.

To analyze the ablation behavior of the MCMB@ZrC composites, 21ZC with the most obvious ablative marks was investigated in detail. The morphologies of the peeling coating, loose powders on the surviving composite, and different regions on the ablated surface of 21ZC are observed, as shown in Fig. 11. As shown in Figs. 11(a)–11(c), the exfoliation layer is mainly composed of a ZrO2 skeleton rather than a dense coating. The porous ZrO2 skeleton is attributed to the low volume fraction of original ZrC in 21ZC, the low sintering degree of ZrO2, and the escape of the CO/CO2 gases formed by the oxidation reactions. This porous ZrO2 skeleton is unable to exert a sealing function to insulate oxygen and heat from the oxyacetylene torch flame. As a result, the ablation rates of 21ZC are much higher. Below the exfoliation layer, some loose powders are scattered on the surface of the surviving composite. The morphologies of these loose powders are shown in Figs. 11(d)–11(f). The loose powders are mainly composed of MCMB particles and some ZrO2 fragments. Owing to the oxidative damage, the graphite lamellae on the surface of MCMB become more obvious. In the high-magnification image (Fig. 11(e)), some MCMB particles with oxidative damage are covered by burst ZrO2 coatings. The oxidation of ZrC to ZrO2 is accompanied by a volume expansion of 28%, and ZrO2 has a high coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE, ~12×10−6 K−1) [49,50]. The thermal expansion coefficient increases by an order of magnitude, and the volume changes dramatically; thus, the ceramic shell bursts. When the flame has a high degree of scour, the burst ZrO2 shell is finally crumbled into fine powders. These loose powders are most likely the evidence that composites are subjected to ablation and oxidation by the oxyacetylene torch flame.

The surface temperature variations of the MCMB@ZrC composites with respect to the ablation time are recorded, as shown in Fig. 13. The final temperatures of 21ZC–45ZC are 2350, 2307, 2290, 2273, and 2243 °C, respectively. The surface temperature decreases with increasing ZrC content in the MCMB@ZrC composites. There are two reasons for this. First, more ZrO2 with high thermal resistance is produced by the ZrC skeleton with a high relative content in the composite during the ablation process, which prevents heat exchange for the ablated surface. Second, carbon combustion is exothermic reaction (Reaction (6): ΔH* = −227 kJ·mol−1; Reaction (7): Δ*H* = −395 kJ·mol−1), which was further verified by thermogravimetric–differential scanning calorimetric (TG‒DSC) analyses of the MCMB@ZrC composites and pure MCMBs (TG‒DSC curves and detailed analyses are presented in the ESM). Owing to the loose structure of the ZrO2 layer on the ablated surface, it is easily peeled off, and then the fresh surfaces consisting of MCMB and ZrC are constantly exposed. The greater the C content in the MCMB@ZrC composite, the more heat released by the carbon combustion reaction, and the higher the surface temperature. The above analysis reveals that the 3D ZrC skeleton can effectively reduce the surface temperature.

The schematic diagram of ablation process for the MCMB@ZrC composites is shown in Fig. 14. When the oxyacetylene torch flame is sprayed onto the MCMB@ZrC composites, the MCMB particles on the surface are burn up, and the ZrC skeleton on the surface begins to be oxidized into the ZrO2 skeleton. Here, the ZrO2 skeleton presents a loose structure rather than a stable sealing layer (Fig. 14(c)). There are four reasons for this. First, owing to the large difference in CTE between ZrO2 (~12×10−6 K−1) and ZrC (6.7×10−6 K−1) [51], the conversion from ZrC to ZrO2 involves a great volume change (~28% volume expansion), which results in a loose skeleton structure. Second, the gas products CO2 and CO escape from the composites, which produces gas channels in the ablated layer. Third, when the surface temperature reaches approximately 1170 °C [52], volume shrinkage occurs in the oxidized skeleton for the phase transition from m-ZrO2 to t-ZrO2, leading to a loose structure in the ZrO2 skeleton. As the surface temperature increases to 1300 °C, the sintering behavior starts to take place in the ZrO2 skeleton [53]. However, ZrO2 is difficult to be densified due to its high melting point and poor sinterability in situations with flame scour, which is another reason for the loose structure.

Owing to the high degree of scour caused by the oxyacetylene torch flame, the loose structure in the ablated ZrO2 layer gives rise to the delamination and spallation of the coating. In particular, 21ZC, with a discontinuous ZrC skeleton, is susceptible to mechanical erosion. The ZrO2 layer peels from the ablated surface by the oxyacetylene torch flame. Subsequently, a new surface consisting of MCMB and ZrC is exposed (Fig. 14(d)). Therefore, the surface after ablation is mainly composed of ZrC and C and contains a small amount of ZrO2, as detected in the XRD patterns in Fig. 10(a).

As soon as the flame is removed from the ablated surface, the surface temperature immediately drops from 2200 to 1300 °C. This large temperature change can lead to considerable thermal stress in the MCMB@ZrC composites, which may result in cracking of the composites. Owing to the CTE mismatch, the maximum thermal stress is located at the interface of MCMB and ZrC in the composites, regardless of the content of ZrC. The thermal stress can be calculated by Eq. (10) [54]:

(10) σ0=α2−α11+υ12E1+1−2υ2E2×ΔT

where  σ0 is the residual stress;  α1 and  α2 are the CTE values of MCMB and ZrC;  υ1 and  υ2 refer to the Poisson’s ratios of MCMB and ZrC, respectively;  E1 and  E2 are the elastic moduli of MCMB and ZrC, respectively.

For the composite with a core‒shell structure, the radial stress and shear stress are generated at the interface of the MCMB and ZrC according to Eq. (11) [54]:

(11) σR=−σ0×(Rr)3

and Eq. (12):

(12) σT=12σ0×(Rr)3

where σ*R, *σ*T, *R*, and *r* are the radial stress, the shear stress, the radius of the composite powders, and the radius of the MCMB powders, respectively.

During the cooling process of the ablation test, the ZrC skeleton is subjected to tensile stress, while the MCMB particle is subjected to compressive stress (Fig. 14(d)). Obviously, the tensile stress at the interface increases with increasing ZrC content. Here, the graphite matrix improves the thermal shock resistance. However, according to the calculation of fracture work, the load-bearing capacities of the MCMB@ZrC composites with various ZrC contents are nearly the same (Fig. 6(d)). Thus, the composites with high ZrC contents tend to crack more easily than those with low contents of ZrC. Based on Eq. (12), the tensile stress in the ZrC skeleton of 45ZC (* σ0 = 435 MPa, R*/*r* = 1.22) is calculated to be 395 MPa, which is much higher than its load-bearing capacity. Therefore, the ablated 45ZC cracks during the cooling process.

Owing to the oxyacetylene torch flame, the ablation behavior of the MCMB@ZrC composites can be described to be denudation layer by layer. At the early stage, the MCMB and ZrC skeletons on the surface are burnt up and transferred into the ZrO2 skeleton. Owing to the poor sinterability, gaseous escape, phase change, and volume change of ZrO2, the superficial ZrO2 layer exhibits a loose structure, which is easy to be blown away by the oxyacetylene torch flame with high scour. Subsequently, a new surface consisting of MCMB and ZrC is exposed to the flame, which will be oxidized into ZrO2 and then blown away again with continuous erosion of the flame. Thus, the ablation behavior of the MCMB@ZrC composites is layer-by-layer denudation.

Compared with pure graphite materials [47,55], the 3D ZrC skeleton-reinforced MCMB-based composites reduce the ablation rate by an order of magnitude. The 3D ZrC skeleton provides effective support for the graphite matrix in the ablation process. Simultaneously, the surficial ZrO2 skeleton, which is converted from the original ZrC skeleton and has a low thermal conductivity, effectively impedes the transfer of heat to the interior of the surviving samples. Unfortunately, the ZrO2 layer cannot provide stable protection for the surviving composites because of its loose structure and high mechanical denudation of the flame. Moreover, the higher the ZrC content, the greater the thermal stress inside the composite. Therefore, to achieve synergistic improvement of mechanical and ablative properties, introducing an appropriate amount of ZrC skeleton reinforcement into the MCMB matrix is necessary.

## 4 Conclusions

Continuous 3D ZrC skeleton-reinforced graphite (MCMB@ZrC) composites were successfully fabricated by combination of molten salt coating and SPS. With the introduction of a continuous ZrC skeleton, the densification of the MCMB@ZrC composites was remarkably increased, and the maximal relative density reached 99%. The mechanical properties of the graphite matrix were improved remarkably by the support of a continuous ZrC skeleton. When the ZrC content reached 45 vol%, the bending strength and fracture toughness of the MCMB@ZrC composite increased to 112 MPa and 1.72 MPa·m1/2, respectively. When subjected to an oxyacetylene torch flame, the MCMB@ZrC composites exhibited a layer-by-layer denudation ablation behavior. Owing to the increased thermal stress, the thermal shock resistance of the MCMB@ZrC composites during ablation decreased with increasing ZrC content. The MCMB@ZrC composite with 30 vol% ZrC exhibited the best ablation performance and remained intact after ablation, with linear and mass ablation rates of 2.13 μm·s−1 and 4.24 mg·s−1, respectively.
