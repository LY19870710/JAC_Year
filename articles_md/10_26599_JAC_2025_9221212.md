## 1 Introduction

The proliferation of 5G networks and artificial intelligence systems has significantly increased the complexity of the electromagnetic (EM) environment [1,2]. This growing complexity has raised serious concerns over EM interference and pollution. These issues impair the reliability of high-precision electronic devices (e.g., high-heat-flux chips in autonomous vehicles and data servers), pose potential health risks from prolonged exposure, and compromise military security by reducing the stealth capabilities of defense equipment [3,4]. EM wave absorbing materials (EWAMs) are widely regarded as environmentally friendly solutions to this issue, as they dissipate incident EM energy through conversion into thermal energy, avoiding the secondary pollution caused by traditional EM shielding materials [5,6]. Ideal EWAMs should simultaneously possess a thin matching thickness, broad effective absorption bandwidth (EAB), strong reflection loss (RL), and low filler loading to meet the requirements of practical applications [7,8]. For this purpose, researchers have explored a wide range of EWAMs, including metal-based materials (e.g., alloys, ferrites, and metal oxides), carbon-based materials (e.g., carbon nanoparticles, carbon nanofibers, and graphene oxide), and other types of absorbers (e.g., conductive polymers, carbides, and MXenes), all of which aim to enhance EM absorption performance [9−12]. Among them, carbon materials stand out due to their notable advantages, including excellent electrical conductivity, low density, tunable dielectric properties, diverse morphological structures, and high chemical stability [13,14]. These attributes render them highly promising candidates for high-efficiency EM wave absorption.

A central challenge in the research of EWAMs lies in the trade-off relationship between impedance matching and EM attenuation capabilities [13,15]. A high relative complex permittivity enhances dielectric loss but often causes mismatched impedance with free space, leading to strong EM wave reflection rather than absorption. Conversely, overprioritizing impedance matching typically compromises loss capability, hindering efficient EM energy conversion and attenuation [16]. To synergistically optimize these two properties, researchers often adopt a multilayer (usually involving an impedance layer, a loss layer, or a reflective layer) gradient strategy in the design of macroscopic structures [17,18]. For example, Ji *et al*. [19] designed a multicomponent gradient‐stacked structure comprising Ti3C2T*x* MXene/polymer films via tape-casting and lamination techniques. In this architecture, an outer layer with low MXene content served as an impedance matching layer to reduce reflection, intermediate layers with gradually increasing MXene content acted as loss layers for EM energy dissipation, and a bottom layer with high MXene content functioned as a reflective layer, redirecting unabsorbed EM waves back into the absorption zone for further attenuation. Through high-frequency structure simulator (HFSS) simulations and experimental validation, this multilayer gradient structure effectively mitigated the inherent conflict between high EM loss and poor impedance matching observed in single-layer MXene composites, achieving full-band coverage in the X-band (8.2–12.4 GHz) and a minimum RL (RLmin) below −40 dB. Although carbon materials are widely used as EWAMs because their conductive loss characteristics originate from conjugated π-electron systems, their excessively high relative complex permittivity also leads to severe impedance mismatch, limiting their practical applicability [7,20]. Inspired by macroscopic multilayer EWAMs, researchers have recently constructed micro-nanoscale composites with an impedance-loss gradient layer to further optimize EM absorption performance. For example, Ban *et al*. [21] utilized different polymer precursors (e.g., polyimide and polyaniline) to construct amorphous/graphitic carbon heterogeneous structures on carbon nanotubes through thermal treatment. In this system, the outer amorphous carbon layer significantly improved the impedance matching, whereas the inner graphitic carbon component enhanced the EM loss performance. The impedance–loss gradient layer interface further promoted multiple reflections and polarization relaxations, ultimately achieving an excellent RLmin of −45.7 dB and a maximum effective absorption bandwidth (EABmax) of 3.9 GHz with a filler loading of only 5.0 wt%. Our group also made relevant progress in enhancing the RL intensity and EAB value by designing impedance-loss layer structures, such as reduced graphene oxide@N-doped porous carbon nanocubes (rGO@NPCNs) and carbon nanotubes@porous carbon nanoparticles (CNT@PCNs) composites [7,22]. These findings demonstrate that constructing an impedance–loss gradient at the microscopic scale may effectively optimize the inherent trade-off relationship between impedance matching and loss capability, thereby guiding the development of high-performance EWAMs.

Apart from regulating the inherent EM properties of EWAMs, the deliberate design of their microstructures is equally critical for enhancing EM absorption performance [23,24]. According to Maxwell–Garnett theory, the presence of pores in a composite can significantly modulate the effective permittivity, thereby creating favorable conditions for optimal impedance matching and EM dissipation [14]. Guided by this theory, research efforts have progressively shifted toward the design of carbon materials with precisely controlled microstructures, including hierarchical porosity, hollow cavities, and yolk–shell heterostructures, to compensate for limitations in intrinsic EM properties [25,26]. Among these, hollow structures exhibit remarkable advantages due to their internal cavities. They not only significantly reduce the overall density but also introduce numerous solid–gas interfaces, enhancing interfacial polarization. Moreover, the multiple reflections within the cavities may prolong the propagation path of the EM wave, thereby improving the energy dissipation efficiency [16]. For example, Zhou *et al*. [27] synthesized hollow carbon nanospheres (HCNs) through the pyrolysis of hollow copolymer nanospheres. They revealed that, compared with solid carbon nanospheres, the resulting HCNs with hollow structures markedly improved the EM absorption performance, with an RLmin of −50.8 dB and an EABmax of 4.8 GHz. This enhancement is attributed primarily to improved impedance matching and multiple reflections induced by the hollow architecture. Furthermore, our recent research on SiC/C composites has further revealed the dual role of hollow structures in the EM wave absorption mechanism [14]. At the same volume fraction, hollow structures remarkably improve impedance matching because of their high porosity, whereas at the same mass fraction, they enhance the overall dielectric loss capability because of their higher specific volume. Contemporary research on EWAMs often proceeds along two distinct trajectories: one stream focuses on modulating intrinsic EM properties, whereas the other emphasizes the optimization of microstructural design [28,29]. To date, there have been few reports on the systematic integration of these two strategies. It is thus anticipated that a synergistic combination of intrinsic EM property regulation and microstructural engineering could substantially enhance the overall EM absorption performance.

In this work, hollow double-shell Co/C microspheres with gradient graphitization were successfully constructed. During pyrolysis, the inner carbon shell is catalyzed *in* *situ* by metallic Co nanoparticles into graphitic carbon, whereas the outer shell remains amorphous due to the absence of catalysts, resulting in a gradually decreased degree of relative graphitization from the interior to the exterior. This unique dual-shell structure effectively balances the trade-off relationship between dielectric loss and impedance matching by leveraging the synergistic effects of gradient graphitization and a hollow structure. EM analyses revealed that the hollow cavity combined with the outer amorphous carbon shell significantly improved the impedance matching, thereby facilitating the penetration of incident EM waves into the Co/C microspheres. Simultaneously, the gradient heterogeneous interface formed between the outer amorphous carbon and the inner graphitic carbon enhances interfacial polarization, contributing substantially to EM energy dissipation. The obtained composites exhibit good EM absorption performance with an RLmin of −62.9 dB, and the EABmax can be further significantly broadened to 11.3 GHz through a rationally designed multilayer structure. More importantly, computer simulation technology (CST) simulations demonstrate that the composite achieves a remarkable radar cross section (RCS) reduction value of 22.3 dB·m2, confirming its excellent radar stealth capability in practical applications.

### 2.1 Raw material

All chemicals, including absolute ethanol (EtOH), poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA; [CH2CHOH]*n*, > 99%), ammonium hydroxide solution (NH3·H2O, 25%–28%), resorcinol (C6H6O2, 99%), formaldehyde (CH2O, 37 wt%), melamine (C3H6N6, > 99%), acetic acid (CH3CO2H > 99.5%), cobalt acetate (CH3CO2)2Co, > 98%), tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane hydrochloride (tris HCl; C4H11NO3, ≥ 99%), and dopamine (DA; C8H11NO2, 98%) were purchased from Aladdin Technology Co., Ltd., China. All reagents were used as received without further purification. Deionized water was used throughout the experiments.

### 2.2 Synthesis of materials

Synthesis of melamine formaldehyde (MF) resin microspheres: PVA (0.54 g) was dissolved in 90 mL of deionized water at 90 °C to form a transparent solution. Specifically, melamine (2.8 g) was dissolved in formaldehyde (5.5 mL) at 60 °C and then quickly added to the PVA solution. Acetic acid (2.0 mL) was introduced under stirring, and the mixture was stirred at 5000 r/min (60 °C, 7 min). The product was collected by centrifugation, washed with deionized water, and dried at 60 °C.

Synthesis of MF/Co3O4 microspheres: MF microspheres (0.3 g) and cobalt acetate (0.5 g) were dispersed in 60 mL of ethanol by stirring and ultrasonication. The mixture was heated in a Teflon-lined autoclave at 150 °C for 3 h. The product was centrifuged, washed, and dried.

Synthesis of MF/Co3O4@polydopamine@phenolic resin (MF/Co3O4@PDA@PR) microspheres: MF/Co3O4 microspheres (0.1 g) were dispersed in 100 mL of deionized water containing tris HCl (0.12 g). DA (0.05 g) was added, and the mixture was reacted at 25 °C for 12 h to form the PDA shell. The resulting MF/Co3O4@PDA microspheres were washed and dried. To form the outermost PR shell, MF/Co3O4@PDA (0.18 g) was dispersed in a mixture of deionized water (18 mL), ethanol (36 mL), and NH3·H2O (0.84 mL). Resorcinol (0.18 g) and formaldehyde (0.16 mL) were added, and the reaction proceeded at 30 °C for 3 h. The product was collected by centrifugation and dried.

Synthesis of Co@N-doped graphitic carbon@amorphous carbon (Co@NGC@AC) microspheres: MF/Co3O4@PDA@PR microspheres were annealed at 800 °C (5 °C·min−1) for 2 h under Ar/H2 to form hollow Co@NGC@AC microspheres. For comparison, N-doped amorphous carbon@amorphous carbon (NAC@AC) microspheres were prepared similarly without the addition of cobalt salt.

Synthesis of Co@NGC@AC/Co and NAC@AC/Co microspheres: Based on thermogravimetric (TG) analysis, appropriate amounts of Co@NGC@AC or NAC@AC microspheres were mixed with a cobalt salt/ethanol solution. After solvent evaporation, the mixture underwent carbothermal reduction at 350 °C under Ar/H2 to obtain Co@NGC@AC/Co or NAC@AC/Co microspheres.

### 2.3 Material characterization

The morphology and elemental distribution were analyzed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-IT800, JEOL, Japan) and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Spectra 200, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Raman spectra were recorded with a 532 nm laser (LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba Jobin Yvon Co., Ltd., Japan). An X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB Xi+, Thermo Scientific, USA) and an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) were used to determine the phase constitution, chemical states, and crystal structures. The specific surface area and pore size distribution were measured via N2 adsorption‒desorption at 77 K (surface area pore size analyzer; ASAP 2020 system, Micromeritics Instrument Corp., USA). TG curves were obtained via an analyzer (STA 449 F3 Jupiter, NETZSCH, Germany) to determine the elemental content and thermal decomposition temperature by a thermal gravimetric analyzer (TGA; HCT-4, Beijing Hengjiu Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd., China). A four-probe resistivity meter (RTS-9, Guangzhou 4-probes Technology Co., Ltd., China) was used to measure the conductivity of the resulting composites. Magnetic hysteresis loops were recorded with a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM; JDAW-2000C&D, Changchun Yingpu Co., Ltd., China) at room temperature, with an applied field of up to 15 kOe.

### 2.4 EM parameter measurement

The samples were mixed with paraffin (1:3 w/w) and pressed into toroidal rings (inner diameter (*Φ*in) = 3.04 mm, outer diameter (*Φ*out) = 7.00 mm). The EM parameters (*ε*r and *μ*r) were measured via a vector network analyzer (N5234A, Keysight Technologies, USA) over 2.0‒18.0 GHz. The RL (dB) value was calculated as Eqs. (1) and (2):

(1) RL=20log|(Zin−Zo)/(Zin+Zo)|

(2) Zin=Zo(μr/εr)1/2tanh(j(2πfd)/c(μrεr)1/2)

where *Z*in and *Z*o are the normalized input impedance of the material and the characteristic impedance of free space, respectively. *f*, *c*, and *d* are the frequency and velocity of the incident EM wave and the thickness of the absorber, respectively.

### 2.5 RCS simulation

To quantitatively evaluate and predict the radar stealth capabilities of the as-synthesized materials in a realistic scenario, RCS simulations were performed via the CST Microwave Studio. The simulation model was designed to mimic a typical application where an absorption layer was coated on a metal substrate. It consisted of two square plates (180 mm × 180 mm), with a 2.0 mm thick EM wave absorption layer atop a 1.5 mm thick perfect electric conductor (PEC). The thickness of the absorption layer was selected on the basis of the experimental finding that the Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres exhibit optimal absorption at a matching thickness of 2.0 mm at approximately 16.6 GHz, allowing for a direct correlation between the transmission line measurements and the RCS simulation. The EM parameters of the absorption layer were assigned by fitting the experimentally measured data to a dispersion model within the CST software, ensuring that the simulation accurately reflected the real material properties. A linearly polarized plane wave was incident from the positive *Z*-axis toward the negative *Z*-axis, with the electric and magnetic fields polarized along the *X*-axis and *Y*-axis, respectively, to simulate a common far-field radar illumination condition. To replicate an unbounded free space environment, far-field open boundaries were set in all directions. An integral solver was employed for its computational efficiency and robustness in handling this type of EM problem. The choice of the simulation frequency, 16.6 GHz, was directly guided by the experimental RL results. According to transmission line theory, the Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres demonstrated the strongest EM wave absorption at this frequency. Therefore, simulating the RCS reduction at 16.6 GHz provided a critical assessment of the material’s performance, directly linking the macroscopic radar stealth characteristics to the intrinsic EM absorption behavior. The RCS values can be calculated via Eq. (3):

(3) σ(dB⋅m2)=10log(4πSλ2|EsEi|2)

where *S* and *λ* are the area of the target object simulation model and the wavelength of the EM wave, respectively, and *E*s and *E*i are the electric field intensity of the transmitted wave and the electric field intensity of the received wave, respectively.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(a) illustrates the preparation process of hollow double-shelled Co@NGC@AC microspheres, featuring a gradient structure where graphitization decreases gradually from the inner to the outer shells. In the initial step, melamine and formaldehyde undergo acid-catalyzed condensation, forming uniformly dispersed MF microspheres with a regular spherical morphology, rough surface, and average diameter (*D*A) of ~1.23 μm via a facile self-assembly process. Their structural characteristics were confirmed via SEM (Figs. 1(b) and 1(c)) and TEM (Fig. 1(d)). After Co2+ was adsorbed and hydrothermally converted into Co3O4 nanoparticles, the resulting MF/Co3O4 microspheres retained the spherical morphology of the MF precursors (Figs. 1(e) and 1(f)). The Co3O4 nanoparticles are uniformly dispersed and embedded within the microspheres (Fig. 1(g)), and statistical analysis revealed no significant change in their diameter distribution (Fig. 1(e)). Furthermore, the XRD peaks at 2*θ* = 19.1°, 31.4°, 37.3°, 45.1°, 59.5°, and 65.6° confirmed the successful conversion of adsorbed Co2+ into crystalline Co3O4 during the process. To prevent outward diffusion of Co particles during pyrolysis and thereby engineer a significant graphitization gradient across the shell, the MF/Co3O4 microspheres are coated with an *in situ*-formed PDA layer in a tris-HCl buffer solution. This process yields core–shell MF/Co3O4@PDA microspheres featuring a smooth surface and a slightly increased *D*A of ~1.29 μm (Fig. S2 in the electronic supplementary material (ESM)). TEM image analysis revealed that the PDA shell has an average thickness of approximately 119 nm (Fig. S3 in the ESM). The as-synthesized MF/Co3O4@PDA microspheres are then dispersed in an alkaline aqueous ethanol solution containing resorcinol and formaldehyde to form a PR layer, yielding MF/Co3O4@PDA@PR microspheres with a double-shell structure (Figs. 1(h) and 1(i)). Statistical analysis revealed that the resulting microspheres had an increased *D*A of ~1.35 μm compared with that of the single-shell MF/Co3O4@PDA precursor (~1.29 μm, Fig. S2 in the ESM), along with a smoother surface morphology. TEM characterization clearly demonstrated the successful formation of the desired double-shell structure (Fig. 1(j) and Fig. S4 in the ESM). Elemental mapping analysis (Fig. 1(k)) further confirmed this structure, showing distinct spatial distributions where N and Co were primarily localized in the inner shell, whereas C was distributed uniformly throughout the entire microsphere.

After high-temperature pyrolysis, the MF/Co3O4@PDA@PR microspheres are converted into the corresponding hollow double-shell Co@NGC@AC with a D*A range of ~1.29 μm and a shell thickness of approximately 221 nm (Figs. 2(a)–2(d)). The elemental mapping images also clearly indicate that Co is concentrated in the inner graphitic carbon layer, whereas C and N are sequentially and uniformly distributed in the interior and outer shells (Fig. S5 in the ESM). To demonstrate the significant role of constructing the amorphous carbon shell externally, hollow single-shell Co@NGC microspheres derived from the precursor MF/Co3O4@PDA microspheres were also prepared under the same experimental conditions, and their microstructure was characterized. As shown in Figs. 2(e)–2(g), the resulting Co@NGC microspheres exhibit a rough surface and uniform size distribution, with a *D*A of approximately 1.26 μm. The broken spheres in Fig. 2(f) also prove the hollow structure. Additionally, the TEM image reconfirmed the internal hollow structure, with a wall thickness of approximately 195 nm (Fig. 2(g)). The internal hollow structure formed from MF combustion reduces the density and may also increase the number of EM wave reflection paths, thereby enhancing the absorption performance [14,30]. To explore the relative graphitization degree of the carbon component of the Co@NGC microspheres, the carbon layers near the Co nanoparticles inside the microspheres (region I) and the outermost carbon layer far from the Co nanoparticles (region II) were selected for investigation, as shown in Figs. 2(h) and 2(i). The carbon layers from high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) in regions Ⅰ and Ⅱ show distinct lattice fringes of graphitic carbon (marked with a white line), with a stripe spacing of 0.34 nm corresponding to the (002) crystal plane of graphite carbon, as well as lattice fringes of 0.20 nm corresponding to the (111) crystal plane of face-centered cubic (FCC) configuration Co nanoparticles (marked with a yellow line) [7,31]. The high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) image further confirmed the formation of hollow Co@NGC microspheres (Fig. 2(j)), and the corresponding element mappings demonstrated the uniform distributions of C, N, and Co (Figs. 2(k)–2(m)). These results indicate that the prepared Co@NGC microspheres have been transformed into hollow graphitic carbon microspheres and that a carbon shell with a relatively high degree of graphitization has been constructed inside the double-shell Co@NGC@AC microspheres.

The crystal structure and phase evolution of the as-prepared samples were characterized by XRD patterns (Fig. 3(a)). As observed, both the Co@NGC and Co@NGC@AC microspheres exhibited three well-resolved characteristic peaks at approximately 44.2°, 51.5°, and 75.8°. These peaks are indexed to the (111), (200), and (220) planes of FCC metallic Co (JCPDS No. 89-4307), respectively [32]. Compared with those of the MF/Co3O4 precursor (Fig. S1 in the ESM), no characteristic peaks of Co oxides (e.g., CoO and Co3O4) are detected, suggesting that the adsorbed Co2+ salt in these samples is completely reduced to zerovalent metallic Co during the pyrolysis process [33]. In addition, they also have one diffraction peak at 26.2°, which is ascribed to the (002) plane of graphitic carbon (JCPDS No. 41-1487) [32]. However, the intensity of this peak in the Co@NGC@AC microspheres is relatively weaker than that in the Co@NGC microspheres, and its full width at half maximum (FWHM) has also become slightly wider. This suggests a decrease in crystallinity, likely resulting from the coating of the external amorphous carbon shell. To further confirm the above result, Raman spectroscopy was also adopted to determine the difference in the relative graphitization degree of the carbon component [34]. As shown in Fig. 3(b), each sample shows two characteristic D-band and G-band peaks at 1350 and 1586 cm−1, respectively, which are closely related to the bonding state of the carbon atoms. The D-band is derived from defects or disordered carbon, whereas the G-band is attributed to sp2 hybridized bonds in carbon-based materials. The intensity ratio of the D-band to the G-band (I*D/*I*G) reflects the relative graphitization degree of the carbon material [35]. In our case, the corresponding *I*D/*I*G value increases from 0.75 (Co@NGC microspheres) to 0.94 (Co@NGC@AC microspheres). According to the three-stage model proposed by Ferrari and Robertson, the *I*D/*I*G value does not vary monotonically with the structural order of carbon throughout all the transformation stages. In the initial stage, where amorphous carbon transforms into nanocrystalline graphite, the formation of small graphite-like domains can effectively passivate defect sites within the disordered carbon matrix. As a result, in such a predominantly amorphous system, an increase in the *I*D/*I*G value is generally associated with a relative increase in the D-band intensity. In the subsequent stage involving the transition from nanocrystalline graphite to perfect graphite, the continued improvement in structural crystallinity is reflected in the Raman spectrum by a dominant enhancement in the G-band. Consequently, a further increase in graphitic crystallinity leads to a decrease in the *I*D/*I*G value. Nevertheless, for our prepared composites containing both nanocrystalline graphite and amorphous carbon phases, the observed increase in the *I*D/*I*G value consistently indicates a greater degree of structural disorder. This result confirms that the introduction of the outer amorphous carbon layer reduces the overall degree of graphitization. In addition to the phase composition and degree of relative graphitization mentioned above, the component content also plays a crucial role in modulating the EM properties [36]; therefore, we used TG analysis to determine the exact amounts of Co particles and carbon components in the composites, as shown in Fig. 3(c). For the Co@NGC and Co@NGC@AC microspheres, the weight loss curves exhibit three stages. The first stage is associated with the evaporation of adsorbed water and remains stable below 200 °C, indicating a negligible content of physically adsorbed water. The second stage involves the pyrolysis of carbon and the oxidation of Co to Co3O4 between 200 and 340 °C. The increasing trend of the Co@NGC@AC microspheres is not as pronounced as that of the Co@NGC microspheres, mainly because the combustion of more carbon components during this process partially offsets some of the oxidation of metallic Co, as well as the lower Co content in the composites. As the temperature increases (the third stage), the weight on the curve decreases, and eventually, the product weight stabilizes at 500 °C, with the final product being Co3O4. On the basis of the residual weight percentage, the carbon content in the Co@NGC and Co@NGC@AC microspheres was calculated via Eq. (4) [32]:

*

(4) wt%Carbon=1−wt%water−wt%R3M(Co)M(Co3O4)

where wt% R* represents the residual weight percentage after combustion, *M* denotes the molecular weight, and wt% Carbon is the carbon mass percentage. The results indicate relative carbon contents of 70.8 and 83.7 wt% for the Co@NGC and Co@NGC@AC microspheres, respectively, whereas the corresponding Co contents are 29.2 and 16.3 wt%, respectively.

The surface chemical compositions and atomic states of the Co@NGC and Co@NGC@AC microspheres were examined via XPS. As shown in Fig. 3(d) and Fig. S6 in the ESM, the survey spectra confirmed the presence of C 1s, N 1s, and O 1s peaks in each sample. A weak signal for Co 2p is also detected, which can be attributed to the low content of Co nanoparticles and the limited penetration depth of XPS. The small amount of oxygen likely originates from surface oxygen-containing functional groups and defect sites in the amorphous carbon shells [14]. In the Co 2p spectra (Fig. 3(e)), the characteristic peaks at 779.2 and 796.1 eV represent the 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 peaks of Co0, respectively, whereas the characteristic peaks at 781.5 and 798.1 eV are attributed to the 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 peaks of Co2+, which are related to the partially oxidized states of the metallic Co nanoparticles on the surface [37]. In addition, two peaks at 786.0 and 803.8 eV corresponding to the satellite peak are also observed. The C 1s spectra of each sample can be fitted with four peaks, namely, C=C, C–C, C–N/C–O, and O–C–O, located at 284.6, 285.3, 286.5, and 289.3 eV, respectively (Fig. 3(f)) [7]. The inner MF microspheres and nitrogen-rich PDA shell introduce N atoms into the carbon matrix during high-temperature carbonization, and the corresponding N 1s spectra are shown in Fig. 3(g). Each sample can be deconvoluted into peaks located at approximately 398.4, 399.8, and 401.2 eV, which are ascribed to the bands of pyridine N, pyrrole N, and graphitized N, respectively [7]. In summary, the presence of N heteroatoms with electronegativity differences from those of carbon atoms and abundant oxygen-containing functional groups could induce a dipolar effect to increase the degree of polarization loss. Concurrently, graphitic N could act as an electron donor to provide electrons to C in graphene, thus promoting conductivity loss [31]. The porous characteristics are further illustrated by the N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms of the Co@NGC and Co@NGC@AC microspheres (Fig. 3(h)). Their absorption curves both show a gradual hysteresis adsorption loop at a relative pressure ranging from 0.40 to 0.99 and are classified as typical type-IV shapes according to the classification of the IUPAC, indicating the coexistence of micropores and mesopores [22]. The corresponding pore size distribution analysis (Fig. S7 in the ESM) reveals that the most likely pore sizes for both samples are centered in the mesoporous range of 3–10 nm. The formation of mesopores within the shell is attributed primarily to the release of volatile carbon-containing molecules during high-temperature pyrolysis. The Brunauer‒Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area and pore volume of the Co@NGC and Co@NGC@AC microspheres are 303.5 m2·g−1/0.25 cm3·g−1 and 361.5 m2·g−1/0.28 cm3·g−1, respectively (Fig. 3(i)). Compared with those of Co@NGC microspheres, the BET specific surface area and pore volume of Co@NGC@AC microspheres are greater because of the introduction of an amorphous carbon layer with abundant mesopores. According to Maxwell–Garnet theory, more air introduced by the porous structure can regulate the relative complex permittivity of EWAMs, which in turn improves the impedance matching in favor of EM wave absorption [14]. Moreover, the high specific surface area and large pore volume, combined with the hollow double-shell structure, contribute significantly to the low apparent density of the microspheres. This intrinsic lightweight characteristic ensures that the final composites (i.e., a 25 wt% loading in paraffin) readily meet the essential lightweight criterion for practical EWAMs.

Figure S8 in the ESM displays the magnetic hysteresis loops of the Co@NGC and Co@NGC@AC microspheres. Both samples reach magnetic saturation at an applied field of approximately 7 kOe, indicating typical ferromagnetic behavior originating from the Co nanoparticles [38−40]. Owing to the nonferromagnetic nature of the outermost amorphous carbon shell, the saturation magnetization (*M*s) values of Co@NGC and Co@NGC@AC decrease to 44.2 and 23.1 emu·g−1, respectively, under an external static magnetic field, which is consistent with the increase in the relative carbon content. Notably, these *M*s values are substantially lower than that of bulk Co (166 emu·g−1). The reduction in magnetization can be attributed to two main factors. First, the metal Co in these samples consists of nanoparticles; according to the surface effect, the increased specific surface area at the nanoscale can lead to noncollinearity of surface magnetic moments, thereby reducing the overall *M*s value. Second, the carbon matrix in the synthesized Co/C microspheres acts as a nonmagnetic “spacer”, dispersing among the Co nanoparticles and impeding effective magnetic coupling and synergy between adjacent particles, which further diminishes the macroscopic magnetic response. Coercivity (*H*c), another key parameter derived from the hysteresis loop, is illustrated in the inset of Fig. S8 in the ESM. The *H*c values of the two samples are nearly identical, approximately 370 Oe, within the restricted ranges of the applied field (−2.0 to 2.0 kOe) and magnetization (−5.0 to 5.0 emu·g−1). In general, *H*c is governed by the magnetocrystalline anisotropy constant, exchange stiffness constant, and grain size of magnetic nanoparticles [41,42]. In this case, the first two factors can be considered negligible, as they depend on the chemical composition of the magnetic material, and the magnetic component in all samples is metallic Co. Therefore, the nearly equivalent *H*c values suggest a consistent average grain size of the Co nanoparticles.

The as-synthesized composites with gradient graphitization show great potential as EWAMs. The RL intensity and EAB based on transmission line theory are used as direct and effective indicators of the EM wave absorption performance of materials [43]. An RL value below −10 dB (90% absorption) is generally required for practical applications. The greater RL intensity and wider EAB indicate that they have better EM wave absorption properties [44,45]. As seen from the results of the XRD patterns, Raman spectra, and TG curves (Figs. 3(a)–3(c)), although the Co@NGC and Co@NGC@AC microspheres are both composed of Co and C components, they differ in the degree of relative graphitization of the carbon component and in the Co content. The EM absorption properties of a composite are influenced by both its structure and composition. Therefore, to ensure that the constructed gradient graphitization is the sole factor affecting the differences in their EM absorption properties, the Co@NGC@AC microspheres are subjected to posttreatment to ensure that their Co content is consistent with that of the Co@NGC microspheres. The specific processing steps are described in Section 2.2. Figure S9 in the ESM shows SEM and TEM images of Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres. These results indicate that the Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres maintain their spherical morphology (Fig. S9(a) in the ESM) and hollow double-shell structure (Fig. S9(b) in the ESM). In addition to Co nanoparticles previously distributed in the inner shell, additional Co nanoparticles are observed on the outer shell, confirming the successful modification of Co nanoparticles on the surface of Co@NGC@AC microspheres after low-temperature carbothermal reduction treatment (Fig. S9(b) in the ESM). The XRD pattern of the Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres is similar to that of the Co@NGC@AC microspheres (Fig. S10(a) in the ESM). Both of them exhibit diffraction peaks at identical positions with comparable intensities. These similarities indicate that Co nanoparticles introduced during posttreatment do not catalyze further graphitization at low pyrolysis temperatures. On the basis of the TG curve (Fig. S10(b) in the ESM) and calculation via Eq. (4), the Co content of the Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres was determined to be 28.9 wt%, which is almost consistent with that of the Co@NGC microspheres. Furthermore, the identical *I*D/*I*G value (0.94) in the Raman spectra further confirms the similar degree of relative graphitization between the two samples (Fig. S10(c) in the ESM). These results collectively demonstrate that the difference in the degree of relative graphitization is the primary distinction between the Co@NGC and Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres. The EM wave absorption properties of the Co@NGC and Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres are shown in Fig. 4, and the specific measurement process and calculation methods are described in Section 2.4. Figures 4(a)–4(h) show the measured 3D RL maps and EAB values with variable thicknesses of 1.0–5.0 mm in the frequency range of 2.0–18.0 GHz. Each sample exhibits EM wave absorption properties, but their performance varies significantly. As shown in Figs. 4(a) and 4(d), the Co@NGC microspheres have relatively weak absorption, with an RLmin of −27.6 dB (thickness of the EWAMs (*d*) = 4.9 mm, *f* = 14.6 GHz) and an EABmax of 3.1 GHz at a thickness of 4.4 mm. Compared with Co@NGC microspheres, Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres demonstrate a significant improvement in EM wave absorption performance, with an RLmin value of −62.9 dB (*t* = 2.5 mm, *f* = 8.4 GHz) and an EABmax of 4.9 GHz (13.1–18.0 GHz, *t* = 2.0 mm) (Figs. 4(b) and 4(e)). Therefore, constructing an amorphous carbon shell as an impedance-matching layer on the exterior of Co@NGC microspheres can significantly enhance their EM wave absorption performance.

These findings confirm that the design of a gradient graphitization structure indeed enhances the EM wave absorption performance. In addition, the calculated coverage of qualified absorption (i.e., RLmin Figs. 4(g) and 4(h)). The weaker EM wave absorption performance of the Co@NGC and NAC@AC/Co microspheres can be attributed to poor impedance matching or low EM attenuation capabilities at the corresponding matched thicknesses. Thus, the rational design of hollow double-shell Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres via gradient graphitization is an effective strategy for enhancing EM absorption performance. To highlight the advantages more intuitively than EWAMs, a comparison of the EM wave absorption performance between Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres and several other typical carbon-based composites that have been published in recent years was conducted (Fig. 4(i)) [47−61]. Compared with their counterparts, the as-prepared Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres exhibit superior EM wave absorption performance in terms of both RLmin and EABmax, and this phenomenon firmly demonstrates the significance of gradient graphitization.

The differences in the EM wave absorption properties of these samples arise primarily from variations in their intrinsic *ε*r and *μ*r, where the real part (*ε*r*'* and *μ*r*'*) represents the EM energy storage capability and the imaginary part (*ε*r*"* and *μ*r*"*) signifies the EM energy attenuation capability [10]. As presented in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b), the *ε*r*'* and *ε*r*"* values of Co@NGC, Co@NGC@AC/Co, and NAC@AC/Co microspheres decrease with increasing frequency (i.e., frequency dispersion behavior), which can be attributed to their delayed response to the alternating EM field at higher frequencies [31]. Co@NGC microspheres have the highest *ε*r*'* and *ε*r*"* values across the entire frequency range, e.g., *ε*r*'* ranges from 17.1 at 2.0 GHz to 8.9 at 18.0 GHz, and *ε*r*"* varies from 16.9 at 2.0 GHz to 4.6 at 18.0 GHz, which are attributed to the superior dielectric property of the graphitic carbon matrix, thereby demonstrating the strongest storage and dissipation capabilities of incident EM waves. However, excessively high relative complex permittivity can impair impedance matching between EWAMs and free space [7]. Compared with those of the Co@NGC microspheres, the *ε*r*'* and *ε*r*"* values of the Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres clearly decrease, i.e., *ε*r*'* decreases from 11.7 at 2.0 GHz to 8.1 at 18.0 GHz, and *ε*r*"* decreases from 5.4 at 2.0 GHz to 2.5 at 18.0 GHz. Two distinct resonance peaks are observed in the *ε*ᵣ*"* curve (inset, Fig. 5(b)), indicating dielectric relaxation under the applied electric field [7]. Obviously, the relatively complex permittivity of Co@NGC microspheres can be regulated after an amorphous carbon layer is introduced by gradient graphitization. For the NAC@AC/Co microspheres, the *ε*r*'* and *ε*r*"* values are the lowest due to the poor electron transport capability (low relative graphitization degree) resulting from the absence of metal Co. In other words, the EM energy storage/attenuation capability of these materials is limited, which indicates that constructing hollow Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres with gradient graphitization from the interior to the exterior can effectively enhance the dielectric loss capability. The dielectric loss tangent (tan*δ**ε* = *ε*r*"*/*ε*r*'*) values reflect the total dielectric loss capability of all the samples shown in Fig. 5(c), and their tan*δ**ε* values follow the order of Co@NGC > Co@NGC@AC/Co > NAC@AC/Co microspheres, which is consistent with the change trend of the relative complex permittivity.

In general, the contribution of dielectric loss mainly arises from conductive loss and polarization loss [1]. According to free electron theory (*ε*r*"* = *σ*/2π*fε*0), conductive loss is closely related to conductivity (*σ*), and a high *σ* value can usually increase conductive loss to consume more EM waves [14]. Four-probe conductivity measurements revealed that under the same filler loading in wax (25 wt%), the *σ* values of the films with Co@NGC, Co@NGC@AC/Co, and NAC@AC/Co microspheres were 6.24×10−2, 2.54×10−2, and 0.71×10−2 S·cm−1, respectively (Fig. 5(d)). Although Co nanoparticles are present, their small size (32]. Thus, their conductivity is mainly dominated by the carbon content and the degree of relative graphitization. According to the TG results (Fig. 3(c) and Figs. S10(b) and S12(b) in the ESM), their carbon contents are almost the same. Therefore, the conductivity is mainly determined by the degree of relative graphitization, and this phenomenon again demonstrates the importance of gradient graphitization. For polarization loss, interfacial polarization and dipole orientation polarization are considered two important modes for EM energy attenuation in the frequency range of 2.0–18.0 GHz [62,63]. In general, interfacial polarization occurs at heterogeneous interfaces between different EM components due to differences in space charge distribution. The space charge moving back and forth between the heterogeneous interfaces can respond to the EM wave and thus realize efficient attenuation [7]. In our case, compared with Co@NGC, Co@NGC@AC/Co, and NAC@AC/Co microspheres have more semicircles in the Cole–Cole curves derived from * εr′ vs  εr″ (Fig. S13 in the ESM). According to the Debye relaxation model, these findings provide clear evidence of multiple polarization relaxation processes. These processes occur at heterogeneous interfaces, such as those between Co nanoparticles/graphitic carbon, graphitic carbon/amorphous carbon, graphitic carbon/air, and graphitic carbon/wax, and are attributed to enhanced interfacial polarization [14]. In addition, all samples display obvious frequency dispersion behavior, which is a typical signal of dipole orientation polarization. Dipole polarization likely arises from functional groups, defects, and N doping, which create dipolar centers [62]. When the change in these dipoles lags behind the electric field, they tend to acquire energy from this field to complete the rearrangement, resulting in the consumption of EM energy.

Figures S14(a) and S14(b) in the ESM display the μ*r*'* and *μ*r*''* curves of the Co@NGC, Co@NGC@AC/Co, and NAC@AC/Co microspheres. Although the *μ*r*'* and *μ*r*''* values of these samples fluctuate slightly in the frequency range of 2.0–18.0 GHz, their overall values exhibit small differences, with *μ*r*'* and *μ*r*''* varying within the ranges of 1.07–1.00 and 0.07–0.01, respectively, which is correlated with the same content of the internal magnetic component Co. Magnetic loss in the gigahertz frequency range is derived mainly from the eddy current effect and the ferromagnetic resonance and can be determined by the *C*0 value (*C*0 = (*μ*r*''*(*μ*r*'*)−2*f*−1) [45]. When the *C*0 value does not change with the measured frequency and remains constant, magnetic loss results only from eddy current loss [64]. For Co@NGC, Co@NGC@AC/Co, and NAC@AC/Co microspheres, in the low-frequency region (2.0–9.0 GHz), the *C*0 value shows a fluctuating trend, indicating the existence of natural resonance and eddy current loss, whereas the high-frequency region (9.0–18.0 GHz) is associated with eddy current loss because the *C*0 value always remains constant independent of frequency (Fig. S14(c) in the ESM). The magnetic loss tangent (tan*δ*m = *μ*r″/*μ*r′) can be used to evaluate their magnetic loss capability, as presented in Fig. S14(d) in the ESM [11]. While eddy current effects and ferromagnetic resonance contribute to magnetic loss, the measured tan*δ*m is still much smaller than tan*δ**ε*. This is due to the ineffective magnetic coupling between carbon-coated Co nanoparticles at low filler contents, indicating that dielectric loss dominates the attenuation of incident EM waves. Therefore, although the presence of Co nanoparticles can enrich heterogeneous interfaces, narrow the gap between the relative complex permittivity and relative complex permeability, and improve impedance matching, its primary function here is still to regulate the relative graphitization degree of composites.

Figure 5(e) shows the *α* vs. frequency curves for the Co@NGC, Co@NGC@AC/Co, and NAC@AC/Co microspheres, and their *α* values increase monotonically in the studied frequency range. In addition, the *α* value has a similar order as the tan*δ**ε* value at a certain frequency point, i.e., Co@NGC > Co@NGC@AC/Co > NAC@AC/Co microspheres, indicating that dielectric loss is the dominant contributor to EM wave attenuation. Although Co@NGC microspheres have the largest complex permittivity, tan*δ**ε*, and *α* values among them (Figs. 5(a)–5(c) and 5(e)), they are inconsistent with the EM wave absorption performance, which may be ascribed to discrepancies in their impedance matching characteristics. In fact, impedance matching is a primary consideration in the design of EWAMs, and it typically reflects the ability of EM waves to travel into the interior of EWAMs, which can be followed by taking into account the strong EM attenuation characteristics [19]. The optimal impedance matching characteristic is a prerequisite for achieving excellent EM wave absorption performance, which can be evaluated via the intrinsic wave impedance (*η*in) in Eq. (6), and a smaller gap between the *η*in value and 377 Ω (wave impedance of free space) indicates better impedance matching [7].

As shown in Fig. 5(f), the *η*in of the samples follows an increasing trend from Co@NGC to NAC@AC/Co, with average values of 108.3, 125.2, and 144.9 Ω, respectively. This trend directly correlates with the EM properties. The Co@NGC microspheres, despite having a high EM attenuation capability, suffer from the most severe impedance mismatch because of the large contrast between *ε*r and *μ*r, which is the intrinsic reason for their weak EM wave absorption. Therefore, compared with the Co@NGC and NAC@AC/Co microspheres, the Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres ultimately achieve the best EM absorption performance by enhancing the attenuation capability and impedance matching.

On the basis of the metal back model, a feasible EM loss mechanism for Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres is given in Fig. 5(g). Although Co nanoparticles more or less consume EM energy through magnetic loss, the corresponding magnetic coupling ability in the carbon matrix is very limited due to the low content of Co nanoparticles. Therefore, dielectric loss is regarded as the main way to attenuate the EM energy of Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres. First, benefiting from optimal impedance matching, EM waves can penetrate Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres in large quantities. Moreover, the establishment of micron-scale hollow cavities in these microspheres can also intensify their multiple reflection behavior, thus promoting the conversion of EM energy into thermal energy to achieve efficient attenuation. Second, the specific volume of Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres can be markedly increased due to the interior hollow structure, which makes it easy to construct conductive networks for electron migration in the wax matrix, resulting in the formation of a microcurrent amplifier to increase conductivity loss. Third, the residual functional groups, defect sites, and N doping in Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres create numerous active sites, both of which can serve as polarization centers (i.e., dipoles). These dipoles can induce many dipole polarization processes, thereby improving the attenuation of EM energy. Fourth, the tight combination of heterogeneous interfaces, including Co nanoparticles/graphitic carbon, graphitic carbon/amorphous carbon, graphitic carbon/air, and graphitic carbon/wax, causes charge accumulation and redistribution to increase interfacial polarization loss. In summary, the enhanced EM wave absorption properties of the Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres can be attributed to the synergistic effects of gradient graphitization and the hollow structure.

To verify the feasibility of practical application, the commercial software CST is used to simulate the RCS performance of EWAMs [2]. The detailed simulation parameter settings are provided in Section 2.5. Figures 6(a)–6(d) show 3D far-field radiation photos of a PEC plate and PEC plates coated with Co@NGC, Co@NGC@AC/Co, and NAC@AC/Co microspheres. The color and radiation flap structure reflect the intensity of the radar-scattered signal at different azimuthal angles. The radar scattering signal is the weakest when the PEC plate is coated with the Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres, demonstrating the strongest EM attenuation capability. In addition, the 2D RCS distributions and calculated RCS values are shown in Figs. 6(e)–6(g). The maximum RCS reduction values for PEC plates coated with Co@NGC, Co@NGC@AC/Co, and NAC@AC/Co microspheres are 12.3, 22.3, and 8.7 dB·m2, respectively, as the azimuth angle varies from −90° to 90°. These results indicate that the Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres, with the highest RCS reduction, possess the best EM attenuation capability, achieving excellent radar stealth performance, which is consistent with the analysis based on transmission line theory. In recent years, periodic designs with subwavelength structural units have been rapidly developed due to multiple responses to EM waves by combining both internal microscale and external macroscale structural effects, providing more opportunities to increase the EAB value of single-layer EWAMs. Therefore, a multilayer periodic structural unit is designed in this work to increase the EAB value of Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres. Figure 6(h) illustrates the detailed configuration of the proposed multilayer periodic structure, whose geometric parameters are carefully optimized through extensive preliminary simulations. Specifically, the lowest layer is designed as a continuous plate with dimensions of 12.0 mm (side length) × 1.0 mm (thickness), whereas the upper two layers feature discontinuous periodic step structures with side lengths × thicknesses of 10.0 mm × 1.5 mm and 8.0 mm × 2.0 mm from bottom to top, respectively. The RL curves of the simulated gradient multilayer (4.5 mm total thickness) and various single-layer thicknesses (1.0, 2.0, 2.5, and 4.5 mm) are shown in Fig. 6(i). By comparison, this optimized multilayer structure significantly enhances the EAB value of Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres, and the value of EABmax extends from 4.9 GHz (single layer 2.0 mm) to 11.3 GHz (multilayer 4.5 mm), fully covering the X and Ku bands, which is also obviously better than the EAB value of a single layer of 4.5 mm. According to Maxwell–Garnett theory, periodic designs with discontinuous structures reduce the equivalent permittivity because they have more air media than flat structures do, thereby optimizing impedance matching properties, and strong EM wave reflection between unit structures also contributes to improving EM attenuation [7,67]. Thus, this multilayer design of Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres clearly demonstrates the advantage of achieving a broad EAB at a small thickness, providing a valuable reference for the future design and preparation of broadband EWAMs.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, this work innovatively demonstrates that a double-shell gradient graphitization structure is a decisive design for reconciling the inherent conflict between impedance matching and attenuation in carbon-based materials. Unlike homogeneous or randomly structured carbons, our deliberately engineered microspheres feature an amorphous outer shell for optimal impedance matching and a graphitic inner shell for strong dielectric loss, with the gradient interface between them intensifying interfacial polarization. This synergistic design, combined with the hollow structure, enables the Co@NGC@AC/Co microspheres to achieve an excellent RLmin of −62.9 dB and a broad EABmax of 4.9 GHz. Moreover, a rationally designed multilayer structure further extends the EABmax value to 11.3 GHz. The significant RCS reduction of 22.3 dB·m2, as verified by CST simulations, underscores the superior radar stealth capability of this gradient structure. This study thus establishes a new approach for designing high-performance, lightweight, and broadband EWAMs by mastering the spatial distribution of graphitization.
