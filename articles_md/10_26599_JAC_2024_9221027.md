## 1 Introduction

Laser lighting is considered a next-generation high-brightness semiconductor light source in automotive headlight, projected display, medical endoscope, and optical communication [1−5]. Laser-excited phosphor (LEP) technology is a common method to produce laser-driven white lighting, in which a color converter is driven by a blue laser diode (LD) through a remote transmission-type or reflection-type packaging [6−10]. Considering the high energy density of laser excitation, inorganic color converters have been developed to replace traditional phosphor-in-resin converters, including single crystals (SCs) [11−14], phosphor ceramics (PCs) [15−19], and phosphor-in-glass (PiG) converters [20−22]. The PiG converter displays several competitive advantages, such as phosphor versatility, adjustable luminescence, simple processing, and low cost [23−28]. Unfortunately, the PiG bulk is confronted with severe luminescence saturation even under low laser power density (LPD) owing to the low thermal conductivity (TC) of glass matrix [29−31].

A thin-film converter of PiG film (PiGF) sintered on heat-conducting substrate (PiGF@HCS) has been developed to replace the PiG bulk [32−35]. PiGF@HCS not only maintains the outstanding merits of PiG but also bears laser irradiation with a high LPD, which originates from its efficient heat dissipation from the high TC of substrates and the excellent heat transfer of thin films [36,37]. Recently, transparent sapphire and non-transparent ceramics (Al2O3 and AlN) have become common heat-conducting substrates for PiGF@HCSs [38−41]. The corresponding PiGF@sapphire and PiGF@ceramic converters have been prepared as mainstream luminescent materials for transmissive and reflective laser lighting, respectively [42−44]. Zheng *et al*. [45] first developed PiGF@sapphire coated with one-dimensional photonic crystals, which realizes transmissive white light with a luminous flux (LF) of 1839 lm@11.2 W/mm2. Zhang *et al.* [46] designed novel sapphire@PiGF@sapphire with double-sided thermal channels, resulting in a tolerable LPD of up to 46.47 W/mm2 and a maximum LF of 7602 lm. Yang *et al.* [47] fabricated PiGF@Al2O3, which enables reflective white light with a desirable LF of 7169 lm@13.25 W/mm2 owing to its thermal management via a cool fan with heat sink. Yu *et al.* [48] prepared PiGF@AlN with a low working temperature for reflective laser lighting due to the high TC (~200 W/(m·K)) of AlN. Considering the low reflectivity of AlN, Mou *et al*. [49] designed a highly reflective BN-glass interface to achieve PiGF@BN–AlN, resulting in reflective white light with a high luminous efficacy (LE) of 214.2 lm/W. On the whole, the common PiGF converter for transmissive laser lighting is PiGF@sapphire, while those for reflective laser lighting are PiGF@ceramic (PiGF@Al2O3 and PiGF@AlN) and PiGF@BN–AlN, the optical–thermal performance of which should be investigated. Although many PiGF@HCS converters have been developed for transmissive or reflective laser lighting, the overall comparison of the optical–thermal performances of these PiGF@HCS converters has not been studied in laser lighting so far. Furthermore, different heat-conducting substrates have strong effects on heat transfer and light transport in PiGF@HCSs, which further affects the luminescence saturation and phosphor conversion efficiency. In this case, these PiGF@HCS converters cannot reach a balance point in performance, reliability, and cost, limiting their actual applications in high-brightness laser lighting.

Herein, Y3Al5O12:Ce3+ (YAG) PiGFs coated on different heat-conducting substrates (PiGF@HCSs) were proposed for transmissive and reflective high-brightness laser lighting. The PiGF@HCSs of PiGF@sapphire, PiGF@Al2O3, PiGF@AlN, and PiGF@BN–AlN were fabricated by a simple film printing and low-temperature sintering technology. The microstructures and luminescence properties of PiGF@HCSs were observed. The optical performance of PiGF@HCSs with different phosphor contents was studied. The luminescence saturation and working temperature of PiGF@HCSs under various LPDs were measured and compared. Comprehensive analyses of the optical–thermal performance, reliability, and cost of these PiGF@HCSs were carried out. The application prospects of PiGF@HCSs in high-brightness laser lighting were demonstrated.

### 2.1 Fabrication of PiGF@HCS converters

Commercial yellow-emitting YAG phosphor particles (Intematix, USA) were treated as raw luminescent materials. Low-temperature borosilicate glass powders (25B2O3–10SiO2–35ZnO–6Li2O–12La2O3–12WO3) were synthesized by a melting-quenching method. An organic solvent was obtained by dissolving ethyl cellulose in terpilenol (Aladdin, China) at 75 °C for 60 min. Single-crystal transparent sapphire, Al2O3 ceramic, and AlN ceramic plates (Feier Optics, China) with a size of 15 mm × 15 mm × 1 mm were used as heat-conducting substrates. A BN-glass paste was prepared by mixing 10 wt% BN particles (Sinopharm, China) with a grain size of 5–10 µm and borosilicate glass.

The PiGF@HCS converters with different heat-conducting substrates were fabricated by a simple film printing and low-temperature sintering technology, as shown in Fig. 1. First, a phosphor glass paste was obtained by adequately mixing YAG phosphor, borosilicate glass, and organic solvent. The phosphor concentration of phosphor glass paste was controlled at 40, 45, 50, 55, and 60 wt% to adjust the luminescent properties. Then, the phosphor glass pastes were uniformly printed on the sapphire, Al2O3, and AlN substrates by using an adjustable film applicator and dried at 120 °C for 30 min. The space distance between the scraper of the applicator and the upper surface of heat-conducting substrates can be easily adjusted by a thread chasing lever, which effectively controls the printing thickness of the paste. Finally, the paste-coated substrates were sintered in a muffle furnace at 680 °C for 60 min and annealed at 300 °C for 60 min. PiGF@sapphire, PiGF@Al2O3, and PiGF@AlN were fabricated as the transmissive and reflective PiGF@HCS converters. For comparison, the BN-glass paste was first printed on the AlN substrate, and the phosphor glass paste was printed on it and then sintered under the same process conditions. PiGF@BN–AlN with BN-glass reflective layer was fabricated as the reflective PiGF@HCS converter.

### 2.2 Characterizations

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of PiGF@HCS converters were characterized by an X-ray diffractometer (Empyrean, PANalytical B.V., the Netherland) with nickel-filtered Cu Kα radiation in the range of 10°–80° (2*θ*). The photoluminescence excitation (PLE), photoluminescence (PL) spectra, and fluorescence decay curves of PiGF@HCS converters were tested by a fluorescence spectrometer (QuantaMaster 8500, Horiba Instruments, Canada). The transmittance and reflectance spectra of PiGF@HCS converters and substrates were measured by a UV–visible spectrophotometer (SolidSpec-3800, Shimadzu, Japan) in the wavelength range of 400–780 nm. The optical performances of PiGF@HCS converters under various LPDs, including electroluminescence (EL) spectra, LF, LE, saturation threshold (ST), correlated color temperature (CCT), color rendering index (CRI), and CIE chromaticity coordinates, were measured by a self-made system. This system consists of a blue LD source (450 nm, 50 W, RAYCUS, China), a spectrometer detector (HAAS-2000, EVERFINE, China) and an integrating sphere with a diameter of 50 cm. The PiGF@HCS converters were placed on a test bench inside the integrating sphere, which ensures the consistency of measurement results. The blue laser light from the LD source was coupled by a fiber and a collimating lens. The laser light with a spot area of approximately 1 mm2 was irradiated on the surface of PiGF@HCS converters. The working temperatures of PiGF@HCS converters under various LPDs were measured by a thermal infrared imager (E63, FLIR, USA). The PiGF surface temperatures of PiGF@HCS converters were measured, and the reflective converters of PiGF@Al2O3, PiGF@AlN, and PiGF@BN–AlN were bonded on aluminum fins by using silicone thermal grease.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 2(a) shows the XRD patterns of PiGF@HCS converters. The diffraction peaks of the four PiGF@HCS converters are the same to each other, which is consistent with the YAG phosphor, indicating that the YAG phosphor maintains an intact crystal in the glass matrix of PiGF@HCSs. The PiGF@HCS converters have an excitation peak centered at approximately 468 nm originating from the Ce3+: 5d→4f electron transition, as presented in Fig. 2(b). The PL spectra of PiGF@HCS converters display broadband emission centered at approximately 535 nm under 450 nm light excitation. The PLE and PL spectra of PiGF@HCS converters, which are the same as those of raw YAG phosphor, are not obviously different. The fluorescence lifetimes of PiGF@sapphire, PiGF@Al2O3, PiGF@AlN, and PiGF@BN–AlN are 62.33, 63.51, 61.42, and 65.80 ns, respectively, resulting in negligible lifetime differences among the four PiGF@HCSs and improved luminescence saturation from the short decay time, as shown in Fig. 2(c). These results demonstrate that PiGF@HCSs maintain the luminescence properties of phosphor after the sintering process. Figure 2(d) shows the transmittance spectra of sapphire and PiGF@sapphire. The sapphire substrate has good transparency, and the total transmittance is approximately 87%. The transmittance of PiGF@sapphire drops to 55% owing to the scattering effect from phosphor particles and residual pores. The transmittance spectrum of PiGF@sapphire has an absorption band centered at approximately 468 nm, which corresponds to the excitation peak of YAG phosphor. The reflectance of Al2O3 substrate reaches 94%, while that of AlN is only 45% owing to the strong light absorption of the AlN material, as presented in Fig. 2(e). The reflectance of PiGF@Al2O3 and PiGF@AlN reduces to 62% and 37%, respectively, and still have an absorption band centered at approximately 468 nm. Combined with the BN-glass reflective layer, the BN–AlN substrate enables the reflectance higher than 98%, and the reflectance of the corresponding PiGF@BN–AlN declines to 70%. The high reflectivity of PiGF@BN–AlN is conducive to light reflection and phosphor conversion in reflective laser lighting.

The element distribution proves that YAG phosphor maintains the complete crystal structure in the PiGF@HCS converters.

The optical performance of PiGF@sapphire, PiGF@Al2O3, PiGF@AlN, and PiGF@BN–AlN converters with different phosphor concentrations excited by the blue LD with an LPD of 9 W/mm2 was measured, as shown in Figs. 4(a)–4(d). As the phosphor concentration increases from 40 to 60 wt%, the yellow emission intensities of these PiGF@HCS converters gradually increase, and their emitted blue light intensities reduce. The corresponding LF and LE of PiGF@HCS converters first rapidly increase and then slowly increase. At a phosphor concentration of 60 wt%, PiGF@BN–AlN achieves the highest LF and LE values of 1808 lm and 201 lm/W, respectively, which are similar to those of PiGF@sapphire and 1.3 and 1.6 times those of PiGF@Al2O3 and PiGF@AlN, respectively. Furthermore, the CCT of PiGF@HCS converters decreases with increasing phosphor concentration, and the CRI of PiGF@HCS converters has no obvious variation at around 65. Consequently, the PiGF@HCS converters with a phosphor concentration of 55 wt% enable cool white light with a suitable CCT of approximately 6309 K and chromaticity coordinates (0.3171, 0.3227) close to the Planck curve.

The luminescence saturation of PiGF@sapphire, PiGF@Al2O3, PiGF@AlN, and PiGF@BN–AlN converters with a phosphor concentration of 55 wt% was investigated under a blue LD with various LPDs, as shown in Figs. 5(a) and 5(b). The LFs of these PiGF@HCS converters gradually increase with increasing LPD before their LPD-STs. The LPD-STs of PiGF@AlN and PiGF@BN–AlN are 24 and 21 W/mm2, while those of PiGF@sapphire and PiGF@Al2O3 are only 12 and 15 W/mm2, respectively. The difference in LPD-STs is attributed mainly to the diverse heat conduction abilities of various substrate materials and the BN-glass layer, which affects the thermal quenching of PiGFs. PiGF@sapphire realizes a luminous flux (LF) of 2076 lm at 12 W/mm2, which is higher than those of PiGF@Al2O3 (1890 lm@15 W/mm2) and PiGF@AlN (1915 lm@24 W/mm2). The transmissive PiGF@sapphire achieves a higher LF than the reflective PiGF@Al2O3 and PiGF@AlN even under a lower LPD-ST, which is attributed to higher phosphor conversion and light extraction in all directions in PiGF@sapphire and lower light reflection of PiGF@Al2O3 and PiGF@AlN. Notably, PiGF@BN–AlN enables a maximum LF of 3058 lm@21 W/mm2 owing to the enhanced light extraction and phosphor conversion from the high reflectivity of the BN-glass layer. Furthermore, the LE of PiGF@BN–AlN reaches 194 lm/W, which is 1.6 times greater than that of PiGF@AlN, while those of PiGF@sapphire and PiGF@Al2O3 are 192 and 150 lm/W, respectively.

The thermal performance and luminescence stability of PiGF@sapphire, PiGF@Al2O3, PiGF@AlN, and PiGF@BN–AlN converters were studied under blue LDs with various LPDs and driven time. Benefited from the high TC of different substrates, the large amount of laser-induced heat of PiGF can be dissipated to the substrates and then to ambient air. As the LPD increases from 3 W/mm2 to each LPD-ST, the maximum working temperatures of PiGF@HCS converters rise rapidly, as presented in Fig. 6(a). PiGF@sapphire displays higher working temperatures under every LPD than other PiGF@HCSs owing to the low TC of sapphire. PiGF@AlN with a higher TC of AlN achieves the lowest working temperatures under every LPD. Since the BN-glass layer increases the interface thermal resistance of PiGF and AlN, PiGF@BN–AlN has higher working temperatures than PiGF@AlN. As shown in Fig. 6(b), the maximum working temperature of PiGF@AlN is only 93.3 °C under the LPD of 9 W/mm2, while those of PiGF@sapphire, PiGF@Al2O3, and PiGF@BN–AlN rise to 193.8, 133.6, and 117 °C, respectively. On the other hand, the LF decays and temperature changes of PiGF@HCS converters under each LPD-ST were measured at various laser driven time, as shown in Fig. 6(c). The working temperatures of PiGF@HCS converters increase sharply before 10 min laser time and then tend to be stable after 30 min laser time. PiGF@sapphire, PiGF@Al2O3, PiGF@AlN, and PiGF@BN–AlN converters display stable working temperatures of 302, 298, 277, and 310 °C, respectively, even after the laser driven time of 270 min. Furthermore, the PiGF@HCS converters have a small reduction in LF after the long-time driving. The LF maintenance of PiGF@sapphire, PiGF@Al2O3, PiGF@AlN, and PiGF@BN–AlN converters reaches 95.3%, 93%, 94.6%, and 95%, respectively. The results indicate that the fabricated PiGF@HCS converters achieve high luminescence stability even under their LPD-STs, making promising converters for high-brightness laser lighting.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, we developed unique heat-conducting architectures of PiGF@HCS converters enabling high-brightness laser lighting. PiGF@HCSs of PiGF@sapphire, PiGF@Al2O3, PiGF@AlN, and PiGF@BN–AlN were fabricated by a simple film printing and sintering technology. The optical performance of PiGF@HCSs was optimized by adjusting the phosphor concentration. The effects of heat-conducting substrates on the optical–thermal performance of PiGF@HCSs were investigated under various LPDs. The optical–thermal performance, reliability, and cost of PiGF@HCSs were comprehensively analyzed and compared. PiGF@sapphire possesses an LPD-ST of 12 W/mm2 and a corresponding LF of 2076 lm, which is higher than PiGF@Al2O3 of 1890 lm@15 W/mm2 and PiGF@AlN of 1915 lm@24 W/mm2. PiGF@BN–AlN achieves a maximum LF of 3058 lm@21 W/mm2. The maximum LE of PiGF@BN–AlN is 194 lm/W, which is 1.6 times that of PiGF@AlN, while those of PiGF@sapphire and PiGF@Al2O3 are 192 and 150 lm/W, respectively. At an LPD of 9 W/mm2, PiGF@AlN displays a working temperature of 93.3 °C, which is lower than those of PiGF@sapphire, PiGF@Al2O3, and PiGF@BN–AlN (193.8, 133.6, and 117 °C, respectively). The PiGF@HCS-based laser lighting source realizes high luminance and a long irradiation distance. This work provides guidance for the development of luminescent glass ceramics, which will promote their wide applications in high-brightness laser lighting and displays.
