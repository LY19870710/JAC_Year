## 1 Introduction

The advent of the 5G communication era has spurred exponential growth in wireless communication devices, smart terminals, and precision electronic instruments, revolutionizing modern connectivity. However, this technological advancement has also triggered increasingly serious electromagnetic wave (EMW) pollution, which poses significant threats to human health, the ecological environment, and the functionality of military equipment [1−3]. In response to this challenge, the development of advanced electromagnetic protection materials with the characteristics of “thin, light, wide, and strong” is highly important.

Carbon-based materials, including biomass-derived carbon [3], carbon aerogels [4], graphene [5], and carbon nanotubes [6], have emerged as promising candidates owing to their advantages of simple preparation, excellent electrical conductivity, low density, and large specific surface area. However, their practical application is hindered by the single dielectric loss mechanism and poor impedance matching [7,8]. Consequently, designing and developing new composites with the synergistic effects of magnetic loss and dielectric loss has become an important research direction for enhancing EMW absorption (EMWA) performance. Magnetic materials such as Fe/Co/Ni metals [9], transition metal oxides [10], and ferrites [11] exhibit excellent magnetic loss capability and high saturation magnetization, making them attractive for EMWA applications. Although these materials possess the advantages of good chemical stability, high magnetic crystalline anisotropy, and easy synthesis, their high density, tendency to agglomerate, and insufficient dielectric loss remain critical limitations [2]. To overcome these limitations, researchers have proposed a strategy of combining magnetic materials with carbon-based materials to enhance EMWA performance by tuning the impedance matching characteristics and attenuation capabilities of the material. In recent years, metal-organic framework (MOF) derivatives have become promising precursors for EMWA materials because of their unique structural tunability, large specific surface area, low cost, tunable physical properties, and easy processing [4,12]. By precisely controlling the pyrolysis conditions, MOF derivatives can achieve porous structures, low density, and good electrical conductivity. For example, the MOF/rGO derived magnetic and dielectric aerogels Fe3O4@C/reduced graphene oxide (rGO) and Ni-doped Fe3O4@C/rGO showed notable EMWA performance, simultaneously achieving strong absorption and broad bandwidth at low thickness of 2.5 mm (−58.1 dB and 6.48 GHz) and 2.8 mm (−46.2 dB and 7.92 GHz), respectively [4]. Tang* et al. *[13] developed Co@C/C composites through acetic acid etching and high-temperature pyrolysis, yielding a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) value of −49.80 dB at 4.77 mm, and an effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) value of 5.00 GHz. The excellent absorption performance was attributed to the synergistic effects of conductive loss, polarization loss, and magnetic loss, which endowed the sample with good impedance matching and EMWA capability. Yuan *et al*. [8] prepared ZrO2/C/rGO/Ni composites via a hydrothermal method, γ-ray irradiation, and high-temperature pyrolysis. The sample exhibited an RLmin value of −48.39 dB at a thickness of 2.70 mm, with an EAB value of 4.67 GHz. This outstanding absorption capability was attributed to the combined effects of conductive loss, polarization loss, eddy current loss, and natural resonance. However, these excellent EMWA performances present a large thickness, resulting in weight and volume challenges for practical applications.

To achieve thin materials, researchers have increasingly focused on 3d–4f rare earth metals. Owing to the unique 3d–4f electronic configuration and large ionic radius, the sample exhibited special electromagnetic characteristics, which effectively regulated and optimized the EMWA performance [14,15]. Recent studies have highlighted several successful approaches in this field. For example, Yan *et al*. [16] fabricated Bi-doped LaFeO3 via a solid-state reaction, achieving remarkable performance, with an RLmin value of −39.48 dB at 2.50 mm and an EAB value of 3.63 GHz at 2.18 mm. The excellent performance was attributed to the combined effects of conductive loss, polarization loss, and magnetic loss. Liu *et al*. [17] utilized Nd2O3@carbon nanofibers as a scaffold to construct layered porous EMWA foams. The sample exhibited an RLmin value of −52.33 dB and an EAB value of 6.64 GHz at a thickness of 2.76 mm. This architecture not only provided excellent EMWA capabilities but also demonstrated dual functionality for infrared stealth applications. Similarly, Liu *et al*. [18] prepared LaFeO3/C composites via a one-pot heating method. The results revealed that at a thickness of 2.94 mm, the RLmin value was −26.60 dB, and the EAB value was 4.00 GHz. By adjusting the La3+ defect concentration, the dielectric loss characteristics of the material could be effectively optimized, promoting EMW attenuation. Doping strategies have been proven particularly effective, as demonstrated by Wang *et al*. [19] in their sol-gel synthesis of LaCo1−*x*Fe*x*O3. In particular, when *x* was 0.1 and the thickness was 1.95 mm, LaCo0.9Fe0.1O3 achieved an RLmin value of −41.00 dB and an EAB value of 5.16 GHz. This success was attributed to the precise balance of the dielectric and magnetic loss mechanisms through controlled Fe3+ incorporation. Despite these advances, the field still faces the fundamental challenge of simultaneously achieving strong reflection loss and broad absorption bandwidth while maintaining the thinness of the sample.

In this study, Dy2O3/Fe3C/N-doped carbon (NC) (DFC) composites were synthesized via a two-step synthesis approach involving solvothermal processing followed by high-temperature carbonization of DyFe-MOF precursors. The electromagnetic properties were systematically tuned by varying the Dy3+/Fe3+ molar ratio. The RLmin value of the DFC reached −56.08 dB at a thickness of only 1.76 mm, and the EAB value reached 5.12 GHz (12.56–17.68 GHz). The superior EMWA performance originates from optimized impedance matching, multiple scattering and reflections, dielectric loss, and magnetic loss. Moreover, the unique high-coordination characteristics of Dy3+ created strong synergistic interactions within the MOF framework, generating rich heterogeneous interfaces during carbonization that promoted interfacial polarization. Simultaneously, the low electronegativity of Dy3+ facilitated efficient electron transfer to the Fe3+ sites, significantly increasing dielectric loss while maintaining optimized impedance matching. The introduction of Dy3+ and Fe3+ is a key strategy to achieve the synergistic effect between magnetic loss and dielectric loss. Dy3+ effectively enhanced the dielectric properties of the material, whereas Fe3+ increased its magnetic properties. This combination of the two components optimized the impedance matching between the material and free space, reducing the reflection of EMW on the material surface and promoting absorption. The prepared composites incorporated the dielectric loss of Dy2O3, the magnetic loss of Fe3C, and the conductive loss of NC. This synergistic action of multiple mechanisms enabled the composites to achieve efficient absorption in wide bands. As a dielectric material, NC not only contributes to conductive loss but also helps improve impedance matching, further reducing EMW reflection and enhancing the absorption efficiency. Radar cross-section (RCS) simulations indicated that at a detection angle of 0°, the DFC exhibited a maximum RCS reduction of 17.96 dB·m2 compared with that of a perfect conductive layer, further confirming its practical application value. Therefore, this work establishes rare-earth-based bimetallic MOF derivatives as promising platforms for developing advanced EMWA materials that simultaneously meet the demanding requirements of thinness, broad frequency absorption, and strong attenuation. The demonstrated approach provides a new perspective for designing novel high-performance EMW absorbents through rational coordination chemistry control and microstructure engineering.

### 2.1 Raw materials

Iron nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O) was purchased from Shanghai Shanpu Chemical Co., Ltd. Dysprosium(III) nitrate pentahydrate (Dy(NO3)3·5H2O) and trimesic acid (H3BTC) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Co., Ltd. N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), deionized water (DI), and absolute ethanol (EtOH) were provided by Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. All reagents were used without further purification.

### 2.2 Synthesis of DyFe-MOFs

DyFe-MOFs were synthesized via the solvent thermal method. The total metal molar ratio of Dy3+/Fe3+ was set as 2 mmol. Dy(NO3)3·5H2O and Fe(NO3)3·9H2O with different molar ratios, along with 2 mmol H3BTC, were dissolved in 45 mL of a mixed solution of DI : EtOH : DMF (1 : 1 : 1 v/v/v). The solution was ultrasonicated for 5 min and then magnetically stirred for 30 min. Subsequently, the solution was transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave at 90 °C for 12 h and then slowly cooled to room temperature. Finally, the resulting product was filtered, washed at least 5–6 times with DI and EtOH, and dried overnight in a vacuum drying oven at 60 °C. To investigate the effects of the Dy3+/Fe3+ molar ratio on the electromagnetic properties of the DyFe-MOFs, the molar ratios of Dy(NO3)3·5H2O and Fe(NO3)3·9H2O were set to 1 : 1, 1.2 : 0.8, and 1.4 : 0.6 to obtain samples DF-1, DF-2, and DF-3, respectively.

### 2.3 Fabrication of Dy<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>/Fe<sub>3</sub>C/NC composites

Dy2O3/Fe3C/NC composites were fabricated via a high-temperature carbonization method. A proper amount of the obtained DyFe-MOF precursors was placed in a tubular furnace. Under an N2 atmosphere, the precursors were heated to 800 °C for 3 h at a heating rate of 5 °C/min. After natural cooling, the Dy2O3/Fe3C/NC composites were collected and denoted DFC-1, DFC-2, and DFC-3 according to the molar ratio of Dy3+/Fe3+ (1 : 1, 1.2 : 0.8, and 1.4 : 0.6, respectively).

### 2.4 Characterizations

The crystal structure of the synthesized samples was characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; PANALYTICAL, X'Pert3 Powder) with a Cu Kα radiation source. The morphology and microstructure of the prepared samples were examined via a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; FEI, model Nova Nano SEM 450), a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Hitachi, SU-70), and a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Thermo Scientific, Talos F200X G2). Raman spectroscopy was performed via a Raman spectrometer (THERMO FISHER, model DXR) with a 488-nm excitation laser. The elemental composition and chemical state of the samples were analyzed via an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS; Shimadzu, AXIS SUPRA). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was conducted on an FT-IR spectrometer (NICOLET, model NEXUS-670) over the range of 4000–400 cm−1. The specific surface area and porosity of the samples were investigated via a N2 adsorption–desorption analyzer (MICROMERITICS, Model TriStar II 3020). The static magnetic properties were measured at room temperature via a Lake Shore 7404 vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM). To explore the EMWA properties of the materials, the fabricated Dy2O3/Fe3C/NC composites were mixed with paraffin at 60 °C. Since paraffin contributes little to the dielectric and magnetic performance, it was used as the matrix material. The samples (40 wt%) were compounded with paraffin and then pressed in a mold to form a coaxial ring with an inner diameter of 3.04 mm and an outer diameter of 7.00 mm. The relatively complex permittivity and permeability in the frequency range of 2.00–18.00 GHz were measured from a vector network analyzer (VNA; Agilent, model N5244A) via the coaxial-line method. For comparison, we tested weight ratios of 35, 45, and 50 wt% and found that the performance of the samples was not ideal at these ratios.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(a) illustrates the fabrication process of the DFC composites. DyFe-MOFs were synthesized via a hydrothermal method, and DFCs were obtained via high-temperature carbonization [20]. During pyrolysis, under a nitrogen atmosphere, the thermal decomposition of DyFe-MOFs begins, the organic ligands are gradually pyrolyzed, and volatile products are released, while the metal components are retained. As the calcination temperature continued to increase, in an oxygen-deficient environment, the metal components reacted with the oxygen in the MOF framework. Owing to the strong oxidizing properties of Dy3+, Dy2O3 was preferentially formed. Moreover, the carbon produced by the pyrolysis of the organic part of the MOFs reacts with Fe3+ to generate Fe3C under high-temperature conditions. After continuous heat treatment under a nitrogen atmosphere, a structurally stable DFC finally formed. Notably, owing to the lower electronegativity of Dy3+ than that of Fe3+, Dy3+ could donate electrons to the Fe3+ sites and increase the electron density [21]. The detailed compositional parameters are provided in Table S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). Through this series of processes, the DFC was successfully prepared.

XRD analysis, as shown in Fig. 1(b), confirmed the presence of crystalline phases in all the samples, which was in excellent agreement with the reference patterns [22]. Detailed examination (Fig. 1(c)) revealed that the diffraction peaks at 2θ* = 28.97°, 33.58°, 48.24°, and 57.24° for DFC-1, DFC-2, and DFC-3 corresponded to the (222), (400), (440), and (622) crystal planes of Dy2O3 (JCPDS No. 22-0612), respectively [23]. Moreover, the diffraction peaks of Fe3C (JCPDS No. 35-0772) were detected at 2*θ* = 37.63°, 42.87°, 43.74°, 44.56°, 44.99°, and 45.86° for all the samples, which were attributed to the (121), (211), (102), (220), (031), and (112) crystal planes, respectively [24]. The coexistence of these crystalline phases in all DFC samples confirmed successful composite formation through our synthetic approach.

The morphological evolution and structural features of the DFC composites were systematically investigated through SEM and TEM. As shown in Fig. 2(a), DF-2 exhibited an agglomerated morphology composed of irregular polygons. Notably, this distinctive morphology was preserved in the derived DFC-2 after high-temperature calcination treatment (Fig. 2(b)), demonstrating excellent structural stability during the carbonization process. To further reveal the microstructure of the composites, DFC-2 was analyzed in depth via TEM. As shown in Figs. 2(c) and 2(d), uniform decorations of Dy2O3 and Fe3C were observed together. The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images (Fig. 2(e)) revealed lattice fringes with spacings of 0.20, 0.30, and 0.34 nm, corresponding to the (031) plane of Fe3C, the (222) plane of Dy2O3, and the (002) plane of graphite carbon, respectively [23,24]. Selective area electron diffraction (SAED) (Fig. 2(f)) further confirmed the presence of the Fe3C (031) and Dy2O3(222), (440), and (622) crystal planes. Complementary elemental mapping images (Figs. 2(g)–2(k) and Fig. S1 in the ESM) revealed the uniform spatial distribution of all the constituent elements (Dy, Fe, C, O, and N), confirming the homogeneous composition of the composites at the nanoscale.

XPS was used to analyze the elemental valence states of DFC-2. The survey spectrum in Fig. 3(a) confirmed the coexistence of Dy, Fe, C, N, and O in the composite. Figure 3(b) shows the high-resolution Dy 4d spectrum. The characteristic doublet peaks at 152.86 and 156.10 eV corresponded to Dy 4d5/2 and Dy 4d3/2, respectively, demonstrating the formation of Dy2O3 [22]. In Fig. 3(c), the high-resolution Fe 2p spectrum displayed satellite peaks at 710.25 and 724.40 eV, along with main peaks at 707.30 eV (Fe 2p3/2) and 720.36 eV (Fe 2p1/2), indicating the presence of Fe3C [25]. The high-resolution C 1s spectrum is shown in Fig. 3(d). The four deconvoluted peaks at 284.80, 285.24, 285.96, and 286.68 eV were related to C=C/C–C, C–N, C–O, and O–C=O, respectively [7,24]. The high-resolution N 1s spectrum (Fig. 3(e)) exhibited characteristic peaks at 398.18, 400.53, and 401.61 eV, assigned to pyridinic N, pyrrolic N, and graphitic N, respectively [3,23]. The dipole polarization effect occurs owing to the electronegativity difference between N and C, which promotes electron migration, enhancing the dielectric loss capability of DFC-2 [25]. In the high-resolution O 1s spectrum (Fig. 3(f)), the characteristic peak at 529.10 eV was related to Dy–O, and the peak at 531.03 eV was attributed to adsorbed oxygen caused by surface hydroxyl groups [8,26]. This comprehensive XPS analysis verified the successful formation of the DFC composite, featuring multiple chemical environments that synergistically contribute to its EMWA properties.

Raman spectroscopy was employed to investigate the carbon structural characteristics of the DFC series. As shown in Fig. 3(g), all three composites presented characteristic peaks at the D-band (1363 cm−1) and G-band (1595 cm−1), where the D-band represents disordered carbon and the G-band represents graphitic carbon [2]. The graphitization index (I*D/*I*G ratio) was quantified to evaluate the relative abundance of disordered versus ordered carbon structures [27]. As a result, the *I*D/*I*G values for DFC-1, DFC-2, and DFC-3 were 0.89, 0.91, and 0.95, respectively. This progressive increase in *I*D/*I*G values with increasing Dy3+ content demonstrates a systematic increase in structural disorder within the carbon matrix. The increase in disordered carbon can act as dipole centers, inducing dipole polarization to improve the conductive loss of the material [3]. Therefore, the synergistic combination of ordered and disordered carbon phases in DFC composites establishes an ideal electronic structure for electromagnetic energy dissipation, contributing significantly to the excellent EMWA performance observed.

VSM measurements revealed distinct ferromagnetic behavior in all DFC samples (Fig. 3(h)), and all samples exhibited typical ferromagnetic effects. According to Table S2 in the ESM, the saturation magnetization (*M*S) values of DFC-1, DFC-2, and DFC-3 were 23.39, 18.39, and 13.76 emu·g−1, respectively, showing a clear decreasing trend, which was attributed mainly to the decrease in the content of magnetic components in the samples. Importantly, the coercivity (*H*C) values of the three samples were 266.61, 185.52, and 250.33 Oe, respectively, among which DFC-2 had the lowest *H*C value. Combined with microstructure analysis, it is believed that the uniform distribution of magnetic particles within the carbon matrix and the reduced grain size effectively reduce the magnetocrystalline anisotropy, thereby contributing to excellent soft magnetic properties [12]. Therefore, appropriate magnetic loss is beneficial for the storage and attenuation of EMW energy, further optimizing the impedance matching characteristics of DFC [28]. FT-IR spectroscopy (Fig. 3(i)) was used to identify key functional groups. The broad peak appearing at 3435 cm−1 for all samples was attributed to the stretching vibration of O–H bonds in adsorbed water molecules [26]. The characteristic peak at 1381 cm−1 was attributed to the stretching vibration of C–N bonds [29]. The peaks at 554 and 464 cm−1 were attributed to Dy–O stretching vibrations, suggesting the presence of Dy2O3 [30]. This spectroscopic evidence corroborates the successful integration of organic and inorganic components, with preserved functional groups contributing to interfacial polarization effects.

Nitrogen physisorption measurements were conducted to investigate the porous structure of DFC materials. The adsorption–desorption isotherms (Fig. S2(a) in the ESM) displayed characteristic type-IV curves with pronounced hysteresis loops in the relative pressure range (*P*/*P*0) of 0.4–1.0, indicating the abundant mesoporous properties of all the samples [14]. Table S3 in the ESM shows the detailed textural parameters; the specific surface areas (*S*BET) of DFC-1, DFC-2, and DFC-3 were 132.86, 138.57, and 138.29 cm2·g−1, respectively; the average pore diameters (*D*pore) were 7.28, 6.46, and 7.01 nm, respectively; and the pore volumes (*V*pore) were 0.28, 0.26, and 0.27 cm3·g−1, respectively. The comparable *S*BET values across all the samples suggest that the controlled incorporation of Dy3+ and Fe3+ effectively prevented the collapse of the mesoporous structure in DFC-2. The pore size distribution profiles in Fig. S2(b) in the ESM demonstrated continuous mesopore distributions (2–50 nm) for all the samples, further verifying the abundant mesoporous characteristics of the material. Therefore, the larger *S*BET and abundant porous structure of DFC-2 reduced the material density, promoted multiple reflections and scattering of EMW, optimized impedance matching, and its porous structure provided more active sites for EMW energy dissipation, enhancing the EMW attenuation capability [31].

The EMWA performance of DFC composites was quantitatively assessed via transmission line theory. The RL value of the material can be calculated via Eqs. (1) and (2) [7,9]:

(1) RL=20log|(Zin−Z0)/(Zin+Z0)|

(2) Zin=Z0μrεrtanh(j2πfdμrεrc)

where *Z*0, *Z*in, *f*, *d*, *c*, *ε*r, and *μ*r represent the free space impedance, input impedance of the absorber, frequency, thickness, speed of light, relative complex permittivity, and relative complex permeability, respectively. Generally, the frequency corresponding to an RL value less than −10 dB is defined as the EAB, indicating that at least 90% of the incident EMW can be effectively attenuated and absorbed [32]. Figure 4 shows the two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) RL diagrams of DFC at thicknesses of 1.00–5.50 mm and frequencies of 2.00–18.00 GHz. As shown in Figs. 4(a1)–4(a3), DFC-1 at a thickness of 2.00 mm had an RLmin value of −13.08 dB, indicating poor EMWA capability. With increasing Dy3+ content, DFC-2 (Figs. 4(b1)–4(b3)) demonstrated excellent EMWA capability. When the thickness was only 1.76 mm, the RLmin value reached −56.08 dB, and the EAB value was 5.12 GHz (12.56–17.68 GHz). As shown in Figs. 4(c1)–4(c3), as the Dy3+ content continued to increase, the performance of DFC-3 decreased significantly. At a thickness of 2.50 mm, the RLmin value was −19.45 dB. These results indicate that the EMWA performance of the material was clearly related to the molar ratio of Dy3+/Fe3+. Adjusting the molar ratio of Dy3+/Fe3+ aims to balance dielectric loss and magnetic loss, thereby improving impedance matching and enhancing defect polarization and interface polarization, which ultimately leads to a strong RL and a broad absorption bandwidth.

To understand the relationship between the RL and matching thickness in depth, the relationship was explained via the 1/4 wavelength theory. The specific equation was given in Eq. (3) [33,34]:

(3) tm=nc4fmRe(|μr||εr|)(n=1,3,5,…)

where Re refers to the real part of the complex value; n* is the mode number, which represents the harmonic number of the quarter-wavelength resonance; *t*m and *f*m represent the matching thickness and frequency, respectively. When the phase difference between the incident wave and the reflected wave was 180°, the interference cancellation effect of the EMW could be achieved [31]. As shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM, the RLmin values of all the samples gradually shifted to lower frequencies with increasing thickness. The experimental thickness values (green symbols) aligned well with the theoretical predictions (orange curves), validating the quarter-wavelength mechanism.

The EMWA characteristics are generally governed by the relative complex permittivity (*ε*r = *ε*′ − j*ε*″) and the relative complex permeability (*µ*r = *µ*′ − j*µ*″) of the material [2]. The real part of the permittivity (*ε*') and the real part of the permeability (*μ*′) represent the storage capacity of the EMW, whereas the imaginary part of the permittivity (*ε*″) and the imaginary part of the permeability (*μ*″) represent the dissipation capacity of the EMW [35]. Additionally, the dielectric loss tangent (tan*δ**ε* = *ε*″/*ε*′) and the magnetic loss tangent (tan*δ**μ* = *μ*″/*μ*′) are employed to characterize the types of loss within the composites [36]. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the *ε*′ values of DFC-1, DFC-2, and DFC-3 decreased from 21.70, 13.00, and 7.09 to 9.57, 8.35, and 5.50, respectively. The *ε*′ values of all the samples tended to decrease with increasing frequency, indicating typical frequency dispersion behavior [33]. In particular, all samples had several fluctuations in the high-frequency region, possibly caused by the inhomogeneity of the material itself during the carbonization process. In Fig. 5(b), the *ε*″ values of all the samples showed decreasing trends in the range of 2.00–10.00 GHz, whereas in the range of 10.00–18.00 GHz, DFC-1 and DFC-2 presented multiple resonance peaks. This phenomenon might be attributed to the small size effect, surface effect, and multiple polarization relaxations, which enhance the attenuation ability of the EMW [37]. Figure 5(c) shows the tan*δ*ε values of all the samples, which are similar to the *ε*″ values in Fig. 5(b), confirming the existence of multiple polarization relaxation mechanisms in the composites [38]. The higher tan*δ*ε value of DFC-1 might have been due to the greater amount of graphitized carbon, which facilitates electron migration and transport. However, the excessively high dielectric constant of DFC-1 led to impedance mismatch, resulting in poor EMWA performance. The lower dielectric constant of DFC-3 limits electron migration and polarization loss, resulting in poor EMWA performance [39]. Consequently, the appropriate dielectric constant of DFC-2 provided sufficient loss capability and maintained good impedance matching characteristics. As shown in Figs. 5(d) and 5(e), the *μ*′ and *μ*″ values were significantly lower than the *ε*′ and *ε*″ values, indicating that dielectric loss played a major role and that magnetic loss had a smaller effect on the EMW loss capacity. Notably, all samples exhibited distinct resonance peaks in the range of 2.00–18.00 GHz, effectively enhancing the EMW attenuation capability. Among them, the *μ*″ of DFC-2 was higher than those of DFC-1 and DFC-3 in the range of 2.00–11.00 GHz, which might have been due to moderate magnetocrystalline anisotropy. Moderate magnetic crystal anisotropy helps the material maintain a higher permeability over a broader frequency range. This not only enhances the ability of the material to attenuate EMW but also ensures that the sample has relatively high magnetic loss [31]. As shown in Figs. 5(e) and 5(f), *μ*″ and tan*δ*μ have negative values in the high-frequency region, which is attributed to the fact that eddy currents are generated inside the absorbing material under the action of the applied magnetic field, thus forming a new induction magnetic field [33]. When the intensity of the induction magnetic field is greater than the intensity of the applied magnetic field, negative values occur, which also contribute to the dissipation of the EMW to some extent [25]. The tan*δ**μ* values had the same trend as the *μ*″ values did, indicating that magnetic loss mainly depended on *μ*″ [40]. In the range of 2.00–18.00 GHz, the *μ*″ and tan*δ**μ* values fluctuated greatly, which was attributed to natural resonance at low frequencies, exchange resonance at medium–high frequencies, and eddy current loss [41]. Moreover, the tan*δ**μ* value was lower than the tan*δ**ε* value, suggesting that the loss type of the composites was mainly dielectric loss and that magnetic loss was supplemented.

The dielectric loss characteristics of DFC composites were systematically investigated via Debye relaxation theory. According to this model, the polarization relaxation process can be graphically represented by Cole–Cole plots, with the relationship between *ε*′ and *ε*″ expressed by Eq. (4) [29,42]:

(4) (ε′−εs+ε∞2)2+(ε″)2=(εs−ε∞2)2

where *ε*s and *ε*∞ represent the static dielectric constant and the high-frequency limit dielectric constant, respectively. In Figs. 5(g)–5(i), all the samples exhibited distorted semicircles, confirming multiple polarization mechanisms. Generally, the greater the number of Cole–Cole semicircles is, the more pronounced the polarization relaxation phenomenon of the material [4]. Among them, DFC-2 presented the most pronounced semicircular features, indicating the strongest polarization effects. Furthermore, DFC-1 had the longest slanted line at the tail, suggesting that the contribution of conductive loss to EMW attenuation was greater than that of polarization loss. The shorter tail lines of DFC-2 and DFC-3 implied a relatively smaller contribution of conductive loss, with the role of polarization relaxation being more prominent [43]. Further analysis revealed that as the content of Dy3+ increased, conductive loss gradually decreased; hence, polarization loss played a major role in the EMW attenuation process. To explore the contributions of the conductive loss *ε*c″ and polarization loss *ε*p″ to the dielectric loss in DFC, Eq. (5) was used for fitting [13,24]:

(5) ε″=εp″+εc″=2πfτ(εs−ε∞)1+(2πf)2τ2+σ2πfε0

where *σ* is the electrical conductivity, and *ε*0 is the vacuum permittivity. By equivalent circuit fitting, conductive loss and polarization loss were represented by parallel and series resistance–capacitance models, respectively [27]. The dielectric loss of the DFC was fitted via the Debye relaxation model and the Python least squares method. As shown in Fig. S4(a) in the ESM, the conductive loss intensity followed the order DFC-1 > DFC-2 > DFC-3. With increasing frequency, the conductive loss of all samples gradually decreased and then stabilized, and *ε*c″ exhibited an inverse relationship with frequency, indicating that the loss mechanism was primarily conductive loss at low frequencies. To further verify the intensity of conductive loss, the fitting results were summarized (Table S4 in the ESM). In the high-frequency region (Fig. S4(b) in the ESM), owing to the decrease in *ε*″ and the increase in the relaxation response, *ε*p″ increased with frequency, suggesting that polarization loss was the dominant mechanism at high frequencies.

To complement the dielectric loss analysis, we systematically investigated the magnetic loss mechanism of the samples, and the eddy current loss was evaluated. The eddy current coefficient (*C*0) can be calculated via Eq. (6) [31,44]:

(6) C0=μ″(μ′)−2f−1

The variation in *C*0 effectively reflects the contributions of different loss mechanisms. As shown in Fig. 5(j), *C*0 fluctuated for all samples in the range of 2.00–6.00 GHz, which was attributed primarily to the natural resonance at low frequencies [33]. In the 6.00–16.00 GHz range, the samples exhibited smaller fluctuations, which can be linked to the exchange resonance at high frequencies [35]. When the frequency increased to 16.00–18.00 GHz, the *C*0 value tended to stabilize, verifying the occurrence of eddy current loss. Therefore, the coexistence of natural resonance, exchange resonance, and eddy current loss collectively contributed to EMWA. The attenuation constant (*α*) is a key parameter for measuring the EMWA performance of a material and reflects the dielectric loss and magnetic loss of the material [8]. The larger the *α* value is, the stronger the attenuation capability of the material to EMW and the higher the absorption efficiency [35]. To quantitatively assess the overall attenuation capability, we calculate *α* via Eq. (7) [42,45]:

(7) α=2πfc×(μr′′εr′′−μr′εr′)+(μr′′εr′′−μr′εr′)2+(μr′εr′′+μr″εr′)2

The frequency-dependent *α* values revealed significant differences among the samples (Fig. 5(k)). The *α* values of DFC-1, DFC-2, and DFC-3 were 89.83–399.54, 69.56–206.83, and 14.90–111.06 m−1, respectively. As the frequency increased, the *α* values gradually increased, and a higher *α* value indicated a stronger attenuation capability. An analysis of the electromagnetic parameters revealed that DFC-1 possessed a larger dielectric constant and hence exhibited the highest *α* value, but its EMWA performance was poor due to poor impedance matching. DFC-3 had the smallest *α* value, demonstrating the worst absorption performance. Owing to the appropriate *α* value, DFC-2 showed exceptional EMWA performance. Typically, ideal EMWA performance requires an appropriate *α* value, and excessively high or too low *α* values are not conducive to EMWA [38]. To highlight the superior EMWA performance of this work, DFC-2 was compared with similar samples reported in recent years. As shown in Fig. 5(l) and Table S5 in the ESM, DFC-2 had a broader EAB value and a stronger RLmin value at a thinner thickness.

To further evaluate the impedance matching characteristics of the samples, the delta (|Δ| function) can be used for calculation, as shown in Eqs. (8)–(10)[19,46]:

(8) |Δ|=|sinh2(Kfd)−M|

(9) K=(4πε′μ′sinδe+δm2)/(cosδecosδm)

(10) M=4μ′cosδecosδm(μ′cosδe−ε′cosδm)+(tan(δm2−δe2))2(μ′cosδe+ε′cosδm)2

where *δ*e (*δ*e = *ε*″/*ε*′) and *δ*m (*δ*m = *μ*″/*μ*′) represent the tangent values of the dielectric constant and magnetic permeability, respectively. When |Δ| ≤ 0.4, the material has good impedance matching characteristics, allowing more EMW to enter the absorber interior without being reflected, thereby achieving excellent EMWA performance [47]. The impedance matching results explain the observed EMWA performance, as shown in Figs. 6(a)–6(c). The impedance matching absorption areas of DFC-1, DFC-2, and DFC-3 were 15.33%, 39.96%, and 18.08%, respectively. Among them, DFC-1 and DFC-3 had poor absorption performance, mainly due to excessively high and low *α* values, leading to impedance mismatch. Conversely, DFC-2 achieved good matching because of its appropriate *α* value, promoting effective EMW attenuation. Therefore, achieving appropriate impedance matching and *α* values is key to efficient EMWA.

RCS simulations were used to research the EMWA performance of materials in actual far fields. The simulation model featured a 1.76-mm-thick absorbing layer on the surface and a 2.00-mm-thick layer of perfect electrical conductor at the base. The model was designed as a square plate with each side measuring 200 mm. Positioned within the *X*–O–*Y* plane, the plate was exposed to a linearly polarized electromagnetic wave approaching the *Z*-axis, with the electric field polarization aligned along the *X-*axis. The orientation of the scattered wave was measured via the theta and phi angles. The field monitoring frequency was set at 14.64 GHz, and the boundary conditions were left unspecified for all directions. For a specific scattering source, its RCS value (*σ*) can be expressed via Eq. (11) [48,49]:

(11) σ(dB⋅m2)=10lg(4πSλ2|EsEi|2)

where *S*, *λ*, *E*s, and *E*i are the area of the simulated plate, the wavelength of the incident wave, and the electric field strengths of the transmitted and received waves, respectively. Typically, a lower RCS signal indicates a stronger radar stealth capability of the material. As shown in Fig. 6(d), an uncovered perfect electric conductor (PEC) plate served as a reference, and its RCS signal was divided into upper and lower parts by the metal plate. When a 1.76-mm-thick layer of DFC was coated on the surface of the PEC, the RCS performance was significantly improved. Figures 6(e)–6(g) show the 3D RCS distributions of DFC-1, DFC-2, and DFC-3 within the range from −90° to 90°, which were reduced by 7.77, 17.96, and 5.70 dB·m2, respectively, compared with those of the PEC plate, further demonstrating the superior stealth capability of DFC-2. Figure 6(h) shows the 2D RCS distributions of different samples, where the RCS value of DFC-2 is lower than those of the PEC and other samples. Figure 6(i) shows a magnified comparison between DFC-2 and the PEC. This comprehensive RCS evaluation validates the material’s potential for advanced electromagnetic protection systems, particularly highlighting the superior performance of DFC-2 under far-field conditions.

As illustrated in Fig. 7, the exceptional EMWA performance of the DFC can be attributed to multiple synergistic mechanisms. The irregular polygonal structure optimizes the impedance matching, allowing more incident EMW to enter the interior of the material. When the EMW entered the interior of the material, multiple scattering and reflections occurred within these cubic structures, which prolonged the transmission path of the EMW [50,51]. By regulating the molar ratio of the metal ions, the synergetic optimization of the material’s dielectric and magnetic properties was achieved. Owing to the electronegativity difference between the components, electrons aggregate in a disorderly manner at the interface, resulting in an uneven distribution of space charges [52]. Under the action of an applied electromagnetic field, charges undergo coordinated rearrangement, resulting in a significant interfacial polarization effect, which further enhances the dielectric loss [52,53]. Moreover, the nanosize effect results in a material with a greater specific surface area and stronger surface effects, which favor interfacial polarization and charge accumulation, thereby increasing dielectric loss [32,42]. In addition, as the carbonization temperature increased, the carbon matrix rapidly expanded, the degree of graphitization improved, and many structural defects were introduced. These defects are able to induce charge separation, forming many dipoles in the carbon matrix that serve as active centers for dipole polarization, further enhancing the polarization loss capability of the material [54,55]. In the MOF-derived composites, the abundant NC interfaces and heterogeneous interfaces had synergistic effects. The former promoted dipole polarization, whereas the latter enhanced interfacial polarization [56,57]. Finally, the magnetic loss mechanisms contributed by the magnetic components in the composites, including natural resonance, exchange resonance, and eddy current loss, collectively promoted the efficient absorption and attenuation of the EMW [58]. The energy of the incident EMW was effectively converted into forms such as heat and dissipated through the synergistic action of various dissipation mechanisms, including dielectric loss and magnetic loss [59,60]. The hierarchical design of DFC composites thus achieves superior EMWA performance through the strategic combination of dielectric and magnetic loss pathways.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, DFC was successfully synthesized through a combined solvothermal and controlled carbonization approach. Systematic optimization of the Dy3+/Fe3+ molar ratio (1.2 : 0.8) yielded exceptional dielectric and magnetic properties, as evidenced by the RLmin value of the DFC reaching −56.08 dB and the EAB value reaching 5.12 GHz (12.56–17.68 GHz) at a thickness of merely 1.76 mm. The superior performance originates from the high coordination characteristics of Dy3+. Additionally, under the synergistic action of dielectric loss and magnetic loss, impedance matching was effectively optimized to promote the attenuation of EMW energy. Furthermore, the RCS value of the DFC was 17.96 dB·m2 lower than that of the PEC. This work establishes rare-earth-based bimetallic MOF derivatives as promising platforms for developing advanced EMWA materials.
