## 1 Introduction

MAX phase ceramics are a class of layered ternary ceramics defined by the general formula M*n*+1AX*n* (*n* = 1, 2, 3, or 4), where M is an early transition metal; A is a group 13 or 14 element; X is carbon and/or nitrogen [1−3]. These materials exhibit a unique combination of metallic and ceramic properties, including ease of processing, high electrical and thermal conductivity, corrosion resistance, and oxidation resistance [4−6]. As a result, MAX phase ceramics are considered promising candidates for applications in harsh environments and have attracted significant research interest over the past few decades [7−9]. However, their relatively low strength and fracture toughness under extreme engineering conditions remain major limitations, often leading to catastrophic failures and hindering broader applications [10−13].

Dispersion strengthening is a simple and effective method for improving the toughness and strength of ceramics. Introducing a two-dimensional (2D) reinforcement can further increase the specific surface area, thereby enhancing interfacial bonding. In this context, we propose the use of MXene—a 2D, graphene-like material—to cross-scale reinforce MAX phase ceramics. MXenes are typically synthesized by selectively etching the A-element from MAX phases. Their general formula is M*n*+1X*n*T*x*, where M is a transition metal; X is carbon or nitrogen (*n* = 1–4); T*x* represents surface functional groups [14−16]. MXenes possess a large specific surface area, excellent mechanical properties [17], abundant surface functional groups [18], and high electrical [19,20] and thermal conductivity [21]. These characteristics make MXenes ideal candidates for reinforcing structural composites. They have been successfully applied in carbon fiber surface modification [22,23], epoxy matrix [24,25], metal matrix [26,27], and ceramic matrix reinforcement [28,29]. Compared to traditional reinforcement materials such as SiC whiskers, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), or graphene, MXenes exhibit unique advantages. Their 2D morphology provides a high aspect ratio for load transfer and effective crack deflection, while their core structure of transition metal carbides/nitrides offers excellent thermal stability and structural integrity under high-temperature processing. Even if partial decomposition occurs during the sintering process, the resulting transformation typically maintains the layered carbide morphology, which can still contribute to mechanical reinforcement [30,31]. Furthermore, the abundant surface functional groups on MXenes may promote strong interfacial bonding with the ceramic matrix. These characteristics collectively enable MXenes to serve not only as nanoscale reinforcement materials but also as precursors for *in situ* generation of strengthening phases [28].

Despite this, the behavior of MXenes in high-temperature ceramic environments remains an active research topic. Wyatt *et al*. [30,31] systematically reported the phase transition of Ti3C2T*x* MXene at temperatures exceeding 700 °C. Other research reports on MXene-reinforced structural ceramics (such as Al2O3 [32,33], SiC [34], and Si3N4 [35] composites) also observed this high-temperature phase transition. However, the retained layered structure after phase transition and the improved mechanical properties of the composites highlight the potential of MXenes as effective reinforcements in structural ceramics. It is worth noting that the high-temperature phase transition and susceptibility to oxidation of MXenes originate from the high chemical activity of its surface M atomic layers. In its precursor MAX phase, the metallic bond between M atoms and A-site atoms helps stabilize the M*n*+1AX*n* structure. This also explains why the synthesis temperature of the MAX phase is significantly higher than the phase transition temperature of its derivative MXene. In this regard, we have confirmed in our work [28] on Ti3C2T*x*-reinforced TiC that when Ti3C2T*x* is stabilized by strong interfacial interactions with the TiC matrix, it can undergo high-temperature sintering at 1900 °C without undergoing phase transition. Considering that the MAX phase itself consists of alternating strong and weak bonding layers, the addition of MXenes may introduce a new layered structure into the matrix. Furthermore, MAX ceramics are easily formed into solid solutions at the M-site through transition metal substitution [36,37], which can provide solid solution strengthening. Based on these structural and chemical compatibilities, it is hypothesized that reinforcing MAX phase ceramics with MXenes could offer a unique opportunity to engineer layered ceramic architectures with tunable properties and multiscale strengthening mechanisms.

In this study, Ti3C2T*x* MXene was selected as the reinforcement. Two 211-type MAX phases, Cr2AlC and Ta2AlC, were chosen as matrix materials based on a combination of considerations. First, Cr and Ta, as representatives of 3d and 5d transition metals, respectively, exhibit distinct electronic structures: Cr has more localized d orbitals favoring covalent bonding, while Ta’s more delocalized d orbitals promote metallic bonding. These differences are expected to influence the interaction between the MAX matrix and Ti3C2T*x*, particularly during high-temperature processing and diffusion. Second, both Cr2AlC and Ta2AlC possess well-established synthesis conditions and can be densified via spark plasma sintering (SPS) at relatively moderate temperatures (1400–1500 °C), which facilitates partial retention or controlled transformation of the MXene phase. The MAX and MXene powder was uniformly mixed and consolidated into bulk ceramics via SPS. Interestingly, Ti3C2T*x* exhibited distinct behaviors in the two matrices: Cr2AlC showed partial solid solution and residual TiC*y* phases, whereas Ta2AlC exhibited complete solid solution with no second phase. To elucidate this phenomenon, we performed first-principles calculations based on density functional theory (DFT). The results revealed that Ti ions preferentially diffused along the *c*-axis through interlayer gaps. The diffusion pathway involves surface adsorption onto the Al layer, penetration into interlayer octahedral voids, and eventual substitution at lattice M sites. Energy barrier calculations confirmed that Ti diffusion was more favorable in Ta2AlC than in Cr2AlC. The addition of MXene enhanced the mechanical properties of both MAX phases: the flexural strength and fracture toughness of Cr2AlC increased by 22% and 31%, respectively, while those of Ta2AlC increased by 33% and 15%. However, the dominant strengthening mechanisms are different. For Ta2AlC, solid solution strengthening and grain refinement are the main contributors, with some contribution from dislocation strengthening. In Cr2AlC, all three mechanisms contribute, along with additional second-phase strengthening from residual TiC*y*. Excessive Ti3C2T*x* addition leads to TiC*y* agglomeration, which reduces strengthening efficiency. These findings offer new insights into the toughening of MAX phase ceramics and further exploration of the high-temperature applications of MXene materials.

### 2.1 Synthesis of MAX phases

Comprehensive details about the synthesis of Ti3AlC2 can be found in previous studies [38,39].

Chromium powder (99.99 wt%, < 45 μm, Ningxia Orient Tantalum Industry Co., Ltd., China), aluminum powder (99.7 wt%, < 29 μm, China Northeast Light Alloy Co., China), and graphite powder (99.0 wt%, 18 nm, Tianjin Yiborui Chemical Co., Ltd., China) were used as raw materials. These were wet-mixed in a molar ratio of Cr : Al : C = 2 : 1.1 : 1 using absolute ethanol in a planetary ball mill with steel balls as the milling medium. A ball-to-powder ratio of 10 : 1 was used, and milling was conducted at 220 r/min or 12 h in sealed containers under ambient pressure. The resulting slurry was dried using a rotary evaporator, then pre-compacted in a steel die under a pressure of 30 MPa. The green body was heated under flowing Ar to 1400 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, held for 1 h, and then cooled with furnace.

Similarly, tantalum powder (99.99 wt%, < 45 μm, Ningxia Orient Tantalum Industry Co., Ltd., China), aluminum, and graphite were wet-mixed in a molar ratio of Ta : Al : C = 1 : 1.4 : 1. After 12 h of ball milling in ethanol, the mixture was dried, pre-compacted at 30 MPa, wrapped with carbon paper, and ignited for self-propagating high-temperature synthesis (SHS). The sintered bulk was ground into powder after surface oxide removal. This powder was then heated under flowing Ar to 1500 °C at 15 °C/min, held for 1 h, and and furnace-cooled to room temperature.

The typical microstructure of the initially sintered MAX phase ceramics is shown in Figs. S1**(**a) and S1(b) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). The initially sintered samples were then milled using a drill machine (Z516, Yongkang Dongcheng Fenjin Machinery Factory, China) and sieved through a 325 mesh screen for further use.

### 2.2 Synthesis of MXene

The Ti3C2T*x* MXene was synthesized according to the procedures reported in our previous studies [40,41], each employing different synthesis strategies. Ti3AlC2 powder (1 g) was immersed in a solution containing 1 g lithium fluoride (LiF, 99 wt%, Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., China) and 10 mL of 12 M hydrochloric acid (HCl, AR grade, Shanghai Lingfeng Chemical Reagent Co., China). The mixture was maintained at 35 °C for 24 h to produce a suspension containing multilayered Ti3C2T*x* flakes (the microstructure is shown in Fig. S1(c) in the ESM). The suspension was washed repeatedly with deionized water (resistivity > 18.25 MΩ·cm) and centrifuged at 7000 r/min for 5 min per cycle. Washing was repeated until the pH approached 6. The sediment was dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for 12 h (Fig. S1(d) in the ESM).

### 2.3 Fabrication of composites

MXene powder was mixed with MAX phase powder at contents of 0, 1, 2, and 4 wt% in absolute ethanol and homogenized by wet ball milling using 1 mm-diameter zirconia balls. A ball-to-powder ratio of 5 : 1 was used, and the milling was conducted at 120 r/min for 4 h in sealed containers under ambient pressure. The slurry was dried using a rotary evaporator under vacuum at 50 °C. The resulting mixture was loaded into a BN-coated graphite die and cold-pressed uniaxially at 50 MPa. To verify that the mild ball milling conditions did not damage the layered structure of MXene, Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization was performed on the as-milled composite powder. As shown in Figs. S1(e) and S1(f) in the ESM, large and well-preserved Ti3C2T*x* flakes are clearly visible, confirming that the structural integrity of MXene is retained after processing. Cr2AlC and Ta2AlC composites were sintered at 1400 and 1500 °C, respectively, in accordance with their optimal densification temperatures. Both were sintered using an SPS-20T-10 system (Shanghai Chenhua Science Technology Co., Ltd., China) in an Ar atmosphere, with a heating rate of 100 °C/min and a holding pressure of 50 MPa for 10 min.

The selected MXene addition levels (0, 1, 2, and 4 wt%) were determined based on preliminary dispersion trials and prior reports [27,28]. Additions above 4 wt% tended to cause agglomeration during processing, degrading mechanical performance. This range also allows for direct comparison with prior studies on MXene-reinforced ceramics, which often explore similar low loading levels.

### 2.4 Characterizations

The phase compositions of the synthesized MAX phase powder, MXene powder and sintered composites were characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Rigaku SmartLab, Rigaku Corporation, Japan; Cu Kα radiation). Microstructural observations were conducted using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, S-4800, Hitachi, Japan), and elemental distributions were analyzed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS; Oxford INCA X-Act, UK). The microstructures of the composites were examined by a transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEOL JEM-2100F, Japan). High-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) imaging was performed in scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) mode to provide atomic number (*Z*) contrast. For TEM, composite samples were shaped with a thickness of 30 μm and a diameter of 3 mm. Ion milling was conducted using a precision ion polishing system (model 691, Gatan, USA) to prepare a thin zone in the middle of the sample.

Flexural strength was measured using a three-point bending test on specimens of 3 mm × 4 mm × 36 mm, with a span of 30 mm and a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min. Fracture toughness (*K*IC) was evaluated using the single-edge notched beam (SENB) method on specimens sized 2 mm × 4 mm × 22 mm (span 16 mm) with a 2 mm notch at a crosshead speed of 0.05 mm/min. The *K*IC was calculated using Eq. (1) [42]:

(1) KIC=3FLa2bh2[1.93−3.07(ah)+14.53(ah)2−25.07(ah)3+25.8(ah)4]

where *F* is the fracture load; *L* is the span distance; *b* and *h* are the specimen width and thickness, respectively, *a* is the notch depth. For each material system, five valid specimens were tested to determine flexural strength and fracture toughness. The bulk densities of all sintered samples were measured using the Archimedes method, and the corresponding relative densities were calculated using the ratio of measured to theoretical densities. The detailed values are listed in Table S1 in the ESM.

### 2.5 Computational details

First-principles calculations were performed using the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) [43]. The projector augmented wave (PAW) method [44] and the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional [45,46] were employed. The energy cutoff for the plane-wave basis was set to 520 eV, and a 13 × 13 × 3 *k*-point mesh was used to sample the Brillouin zone.

Spin polarization was considered to accurately describe transition metal systems. A Hubbard U correction was applied to the d-electrons of transition metals: *U*Cr = 2.0 eV [47], *U*Ti = 4.0 eV [48], and *U*Ta = 4.0 eV [48]. These values were selected based on previous studies of MAX phases and related carbides, and are widely accepted for accurately describing the localized electronic behavior of 3d and 5d transition metals. To assess the sensitivity of the computed total energy to the choice of U values, convergence tests were performed by varying *U*Cr and *U*Ta across a reasonable range. As shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM, the final total energy varied by less than 0.01 eV/atom for both Cr2AlC and Ta2AlC systems, indicating that the simulation results are robust and not significantly affected by moderate changes in the *U* parameter. This validates the reliability of our DFT+U approach in predicting cohesive energies and diffusion barriers for the investigated systems. Lattice dynamics were analyzed using a 4 × 4 × 1 supercell. The climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method [49] was used for transition state optimization, ensuring only one imaginary frequency to confirm a first-order saddle point. The cohesive energy (*E*C) was calculated as shown in Eq. (2):

(2) EC=Etotal−nEatomn

where *E*total is the total system energy; *E*atom is the reference energy of each atom; *n* is the number of atoms in the system. Elastic constants were obtained using the stress–strain method.

The adsorption energy (*E*ads) describes the thermodynamic tendency of Ti ions to adsorb onto the MAX phase surface. It was calculated as shown in Eq. (3):

(3) Eads=Etotal−(E1+E2)

where *E*total represents the total energy of the adsorption model of ions on the surface of MAX phase material layer; *E*1 and *E*2 represent the energy of the MAX phase substrate and adsorbed ions respectively. Additional computational details for elastic properties, Debye temperature, etc., are provided in the ESM.

### 3.1 Phase composition and solid solution behavior

XRD patterns of Ti3C2T*x* MXene, Cr2AlC-based samples, and Ta2AlC-based samples (including all compositions: 0, 1, 2, and 4 wt%) are presented in Fig. 1. As shown in (I) of Fig. 1(a), the as-synthesized Ti3C2T*x* exhibits a strong (002) reflection at 2*θ* = 6.14°, confirming its typical layered structure. For Cr2AlC, pressureless sintering (PLS) at 1400 °C yielded diffraction peaks corresponding to a single Cr2AlC phase, with no secondary crystalline phases detected within the XRD detection limit, as shown in (II) of Fig. 1(a). This indicates the effectiveness of the synthesis method and the dominant formation of the intended MAX phase. However, it should be noted that minor amorphous or low-concentration impurities may still exist below the detection threshold of XRD. As shown in (III) of Fig. 1(a), the XRD pattern of Cr2AlC powder mixed with 4 wt% Ti3C2T*x* shows distinct reflections from both Cr2AlC and Ti3C2T*x*, indicating physical mixing without significant interaction prior to sintering. This also suggests that the layered structure of Ti3C2T*x* was retained during ball milling, indicating that the applied shear and impact forces did not damage the MXene sheets to an extent that would lead to the disappearance of their characteristic diffraction peaks. After SPS, the XRD patterns of the Cr2AlC bulk ((IV) of Fig. 1(a)) show no change in phase composition compared to the PLS-prepared powder, confirming that the SPS process does not introduce detectable secondary phases. Notably, as shown in (V)–(VII) of Fig. 1(a), all SPS-sintered Cr2AlC-based composites with 1 to 4 wt% Ti3C2T*x* addition exhibit only Cr2AlC peaks, while no discernible peaks of Ti3C2T*x* or TiC are detected. This could be attributed to several factors, with decomposition or phase transformation of the reinforcing phase during sintering being a particularly probable cause. Other contributing factors may include the low addition content leading to signal overlap with matrix peaks and partial coverage of Ti3C2T*x* flakes by the matrix, which may suppress their characteristic diffraction signals [50−54].

As shown in Fig. 1(b), the PLS-prepared Ta2AlC ((I) of Fig. 1(b)) exhibits diffraction peaks corresponding exclusively to the Ta2AlC phase, with no secondary crystalline phases detected within the resolution limits of XRD. This suggests the dominant formation of Ta2AlC under the applied synthesis conditions. However, as with Cr2AlC, the possible presence of amorphous or trace secondary phases below the detection limit cannot be fully excluded. After adding 4 wt% Ti3C2T*x* to Ta2AlC powder ((II) of Fig. 1(b)), diffraction peaks corresponding to both Ta2AlC and Ti3C2T*x* are clearly observed, indicating that the two phases remain separate prior to sintering. The SPS-sintered Ta2AlC bulk ((III) of Fig. 1(b)) retains the same phase composition as the PLS-prepared powder, confirming its thermal stability during consolidation. Interestingly, in (IV)–(VI) of Fig. 1(b), increasing Ti3C2T*x* content leads to a progressive shift of the main Ta2AlC peaks (e.g., the (103) reflection) toward higher 2*θ* values—from 39.305° (pure Ta2AlC) to 40.047° at 4 wt% Ti3C2T*x* addition. According to Bragg's law, this shift indicates a reduction in lattice spacing, which can be attributed to the substitutional incorporation of smaller Ti atoms into Ta sites within the Ta2AlC lattice. This interpretation is further supported by the monotonic nature of the peak shift, which rules out random impurity effects. Therefore, the observed peak shift confirms lattice distortion and provides evidence that a solid solution has formed between Ti3C2T*x* and the Ta2AlC matrix during sintering.

### 3.2 Microstructural and elemental distribution analysis

The SEM image of the fracture surface of the Cr2AlC sample with 4 wt% Ti3C2T*x* is shown in Fig. 2(a). Distinct agglomerated regions are observed, which differ noticeably in morphology from the surrounding matrix. The EDS line scan along line A on the fracture surface (Fig. 2(b)), together with the enlarged view near line A (Fig. 2(c)), reveals that the agglomerated regions also exhibit a layered structure. However, their morphology is clearly different from the nano-layered structure characteristic of Cr2AlC. The corresponding elemental mapping results (Fig. 2(d)) show a significant enrichment of Ti in these regions, coinciding with the agglomerated structures in Fig. 2(c). Based on previous reports on the phase transformation of MXenes [30,31], these stacked structures are inferred to be TiC*y* phases formed from the transformation of Ti3C2T*x*. In addition, a small but detectable distribution of Ti is observed throughout the matrix, indicating the partial formation of a solid solution between Ti and Cr2AlC. In contrast, the fracture surface of the Ta2AlC sample with 4 wt% Ti3C2T*x* (Fig. 2(e)) shows no obvious agglomerated structures. The corresponding elemental mappings of Ta, Al, Ti, and C (Fig. 2(f)) demonstrate that Ti is homogeneously distributed across the matrix. This uniform distribution suggests that Ti atoms are completely dissolved into the Ta2AlC lattice during sintering, without forming any secondary Ti-containing phases. EDS mapping confirms the presence of Ti in the matrix regions, with no evidence of sharp elemental segregation at the microscale. However, as the measurements were conducted on fracture surfaces, they are qualitative in nature.

To further identify the reinforcing phase in the Cr2AlC–4 wt% Ti3C2T*x* sample, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was conducted. Figure 3(a) shows an HAADF-STEM image, in which the contrast is influenced by local thickness and atomic number (*Z*) differences, and the observed Ti-rich phase is distributed along the grain boundaries, consistent with the SEM observations of the fracture surface. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern acquired from the circled region in Fig. 3(a) is presented in Fig. 3(b). The diffraction spots match well with the TiC phase [55], confirming that the observed reinforcement originates from the *in situ* transformation of Ti3C2T*x* during sintering. Elemental mapping results (Fig. 3(c)) further reveal strong Ti enrichment in the reinforcing phase region. For the surrounding matrix, no sharp segregation of Cr or Al is observed in the analyzed region, though minor local intensity variations exist. These variations may originate from local differences in sample thickness introduced during ion milling for TEM specimen preparation, which can affect the apparent EDS signal intensity, especially for light elements such as Al. A small amount of Ti is also detected within the matrix, indicating the formation of a partial solid solution. These results are in good agreement with the SEM and EDS analyses discussed earlier. To provide complementary evidence, TEM analysis was also conducted on the Ta2AlC sample with 4 wt% Ti3C2T*x* addition. As expected, no distinguishable second-phase particles or residual MXene-derived structures were observed in the sintered Ta2AlC–4 wt% Ti3C2T*x* solid solution, which is consistent with the XRD results indicating complete solid solution formation. The corresponding HAADF-STEM image is presented in Fig. S3 in the ESM, confirming the microstructural homogeneity and the absence of Ti-rich precipitates.

In addition to Ti, the state of the carbon element originating from Ti3C2T*x* was also examined. SEM–EDS and TEM–EDS mappings (Fig. 2 and Fig. S3 in the ESM) both show a uniform distribution of carbon in the Ta2AlC-based samples, without any visible segregation, carbide precipitation, or carbon-rich clustering. This suggests that the carbon either diffused uniformly into the matrix or reacted at a scale below the detection threshold of EDS. Unlike the Cr2AlC system, no TiC-type precipitates were observed in the Ta2AlC-based materials. This may be attributed to the stronger solid solution tendency of Ti in the Ta2AlC lattice, which could facilitate the co-diffusion of Ti and C atoms during sintering. Although direct detection of C incorporation is limited due to its low atomic number, previous studies have shown that Ta2AlC is prone to forming carbon vacancies during high-temperature synthesis, with vacancy concentrations that can reach up to 1015 cm−3 under certain conditions [56]. These structural vacancies may act as reservoirs for accommodating excess carbon atoms released during Ti3C2T*x* decomposition. Therefore, under non-equilibrium sintering conditions such as spark plasma sintering, it is plausible that the excess carbon was incorporated into the MAX phase structure without forming distinct secondary phases. This interpretation aligns with the absence of carbon-related diffraction peaks or carbide phases in the XRD and TEM analyses of Ta2AlC–4 wt% Ti3C2T*x* samples.

### 3.3 First-principles investigation of Ti doping mechanisms in MAX phase ceramics

To investigate the structural and dynamic stability of Ti-doped MAX phase ceramics, four model structures—Cr2AlC, Ta2AlC, CrTiAlC, and TaTiAlC—were constructed by substituting Ti into the M-sites (Fig. 4(a)). After structural optimization, the lattice constants of Cr2AlC and Ta2AlC agreed well with previous reports [47,57], validating the computational parameters. The lattice parameters of the doped systems (Table S2 in the ESM) lie between those of Cr2AlC, Ta2AlC, and Ti2AlC, indicating slight lattice distortion caused by doping. Cohesive energy calculations show that the doped systems remain energetically stable, despite slightly reduced cohesive energies compared to the pristine structures (details in Table S2 in the ESM). These results indicate that Ti incorporation slightly reduces the cohesive energy of both Cr- and Ta-based MAX phases, but the magnitude of reduction is lower in Ta2AlC. This suggests that Ti is more thermodynamically compatible with Ta-based MAX phases, which supports the experimental observation of complete Ti solid solution in Ta2AlC. Their Debye temperatures are also significantly elevated (up to 647.3 K, details in Table S2 in the ESM), suggesting improved thermodynamic robustness. As shown in the phonon dispersion curves (Figs. 4(b)–4(e)), no imaginary frequencies are observed, confirming dynamic stability. Notably, the doped systems exhibit localized acoustic phonon modes near the Γ point, which can be attributed to local atomic distortions, especially in the *a*–*b* plane, induced by heteroatomic substitution.

*

Before entering the bulk lattice, Ti ions are expected to undergo surface adsorption on the Al-terminated (0001) surfaces of MAX phases. This step is a necessary precursor to c*-axis diffusion. Ti ions tend to adsorb at hollow sites formed by surface Al atoms. The calculated adsorption energies are both negative, with values of −0.65 eV for Cr2AlC and −0.42 eV for Ta2AlC, confirming thermodynamically favorable surface binding. The corresponding values of *E*total, *E*1, and *E*2 used in the adsorption energy calculations are also provided in Table S3 in the ESM. Importantly, the lower absolute adsorption energy on Ta2AlC suggests a stronger tendency for Ti ions to penetrate into the bulk, implying that *c*-axis diffusion is more favorable in Ta-based MAX phases.

To elucidate the doping mechanism of Ti in MAX phase ceramics, a 3 × 3 × 1 supercell with a 15 Å vacuum layer was constructed for Cr2AlC and Ta2AlC. Ti ions were introduced at the M-site and analyzed via CI-NEB to simulate substitution and migration behaviors. Based on the symmetry and bonding environment, two dominant diffusion paths were proposed:

(1) Path 1: Ti diffuses along the *c*-axis, initially adsorbing on the surface Al layer, penetrating into the octahedral interlayer, and substituting the central M atom.

(2) Path 2: Ti migrates in the *a*–*b* plane via tetrahedral sites, approaching the substitution site through lateral diffusion channels.

As shown in Fig. 5, the activation energy barrier for Ti diffusion via path 1 in Ta2AlC is 2.635 eV, while Cr2AlC exhibits a slightly lower barrier for lateral migration along path 2 (Fig. S4 in the ESM). This difference arises from the stronger Cr–Al interlayer bonding and shorter *c*-axis in Cr2AlC, which hinder vertical diffusion. Conversely, Ta2AlC features a longer *c*-axis (13.883 Å) and weaker Ta–Al bonding, facilitating interlayer migration. However, despite the slightly lower kinetic barrier in Cr2AlC, the experimental results show that Ti forms a uniform solid solution only in Ta2AlC, while precipitating as TiC*y* in Cr2AlC. This suggests that thermodynamic factors—such as cohesive energy differences and local chemical affinity—play a more dominant role in determining the final solubility behavior. The favorable lattice openness and bonding environment in Ta2AlC promote substitutional doping and are consistent with the absence of second-phase formation, as evidenced by XRD, SEM, and TEM analyses.

### 3.4 Mechanical properties and strengthening mechanisms of MAX phase ceramics with Ti3C2T<i>x</i> addition

As shown in Table S1 in the ESM, the relative densities of Cr2AlC-based composites decrease slightly from 98.12% (0 wt% Ti3C2T*x*) to 98.01% (4 wt% Ti3C2T*x*), while those of Ta2AlC-based composites remain relatively stable, ranging from 98.11% to 98.19%. These consistently high relative density values (> 98%) indicate that all samples achieved excellent densification after spark plasma sintering, minimizing the influence of porosity on the mechanical property evaluation.

The flexural strength and fracture toughness of Cr2AlC and Cr2AlC–Ti3C2T*x* composites with varying filler contents are shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b), respectively. The addition of MXene significantly improves the mechanical performance compared to pure Cr2AlC. With increasing Ti3C2T*x* content, the flexural strength first increases and then decreases, reaching a maximum at 2 wt% (690 MPa), which is 22% higher than that of pure Cr2AlC (567 MPa). In contrast, the fracture toughness continuously increases with MXene content, reaching 6.49  MPa·m1/2 at 4 wt%, representing a 31% improvement over pure Cr2AlC (4.97  MPa·m1/2). In comparison, both flexural strength and fracture toughness of Ta2AlC-based samples (Figs. 6(c) and 6(d)) increase monotonically with increasing Ti3C2T*x* addition. At 4 wt%, the Ta2AlC–MXene solid solution achieves a flexural strength of 679 MPa and a fracture toughness of 7.08 MPa·m1/2, representing improvements of 33% and 15%, respectively, compared to the pure Ta2AlC matrix.

Building upon the observed trends in mechanical performance and microstructural evolution, it becomes evident that multiple strengthening and toughening mechanisms are simultaneously active in the Ti3C2T*x*-reinforced MAX phase ceramics. These mechanisms include solid solution strengthening, grain refinement, dislocation strengthening, and second-phase particle reinforcement, each contributing to varying extents depending on the specific matrix system (Cr2AlC vs. Ta2AlC) and the nature of MXene incorporation. In the Cr2AlC system, second-phase strengthening from *in situ* TiC*y* formation plays an important role. Excessive MXene addition can lead to agglomeration, reducing reinforcement efficiency. As shown in Figs. 7(a)–7(c), agglomerates appear in Cr2AlC–4 wt% Ti3C2T*x* samples. Specifically, the morphologies depicted in Figs. 7(b) and 7(c) exhibit spherical features not observed in the MAX phase matrix (Fig. 7(a)). Furthermore, as the amount of MXene added increases, there is a trend towards an increase in quasi-spherical particles, indicating the characteristics of flaky TiC*y* formed through conventional phase transformation. These features are interpreted as regions where MXene agglomerated and subsequently underwent *in situ* transformation into TiC*y* during sintering. This is supported by their morphology and their absence in lower-reinforcement or differently processed samples. Moreover, the fracture surface of pure Cr2AlC shows a relatively flat intergranular fracture morphology, while the MXene-reinforced sample exhibits transgranular fracture and pronounced grain delamination, as clearly shown in Fig. 7(c) and its inset, indicating enhanced crack deflection and energy dissipation. In contrast, Ta2AlC–MXene samples display refined grain structures with more uniform and compact grain arrangements (Figs. 7(d)–7(f)), suggesting that the addition of MXene inhibits grain growth during sintering. Compared to the relatively coarse and uneven grains in the pure Ta2AlC sample (Fig. 7(d)), the solid solution samples (Figs. 7(e) and 7(f)) exhibit more homogeneous grain sizes with reduced variation, as qualitatively observed from the SEM images. This results in effective grain refinement strengthening, accompanied by solid solution strengthening due to complete Ti diffusion into the Ta2AlC lattice. No second-phase formation is observed, consistent with XRD and TEM analyses. Furthermore, the stronger bonding between Ti and Ta atoms compared to Ti and Cr also contributes to the improved mechanical behavior in the Ta2AlC system. The relatively weak interface bonding in Cr2AlC-based composites limits the strengthening efficiency despite higher TiC*y* content. Interestingly, the increase in fracture toughness with MXene addition is more pronounced in the Cr2AlC-based composites. This can be attributed to the crack propagation path during single-edge-notched bend (SENB) testing, which typically follows the pre-crack direction. In this region, second-phase particles such as TiC*y* can disrupt the crack path, leading to crack deflection and branching, which enhances toughness more effectively than solid solution strengthening. Further visual evidence of the toughening effect is provided in Fig. 8, which shows the typical fracture surface of a Cr2AlC–4 wt% Ti3C2T*x* sample. Crack paths are clearly deflected around second-phase TiC*y* particles formed *in situ* from the transformed MXene phase. These layered materials act as crack deflectors and energy dissipators, leading to enhanced resistance against crack propagation. The observed crack branching and tortuous paths are consistent with increased fracture energy absorption, confirming the role of particle-induced deflection in the observed toughness enhancement. This interpretation is supported by the experimental data shown in Fig. 6 where fracture toughness continues to increase even as flexural strength saturates or declines at higher MXene contents. Similar mismatches between strength and toughness trends have been widely reported in the literature for nanostructured composites [23,25,58−61].

The unique reinforcing behavior of MXene-derived TiC*y* further differentiates it from conventional second-phase particles such as ZrC and ZnO2. Unlike these equiaxed and rigid inclusions, the TiC*y* phase transformed from Ti3C2T*x* retains partial lamellar characteristics and is typically distributed along grain boundaries in layered or flake-like morphologies. These features promote extensive crack deflection and tortuous crack propagation paths, as evidenced by the trans granular fracture and intergranular delamination observed in the MXene-reinforced samples (Figs. 7(c) and 8). As a result, comparable enhancements in mechanical properties can be achieved at much lower additive contents than typically required for conventional ceramic reinforcements [62,63]. Although the strengthening efficiency of MXene in MAX phases is somewhat lower than that of graphene or carbon nanotube reinforcements reported in other studies [64,65], the flexural strength achieved in MXene-reinforced MAX ceramics significantly exceeds those carbon-based systems. Moreover, since MXene has transformed into TiC*y* during sintering, its ceramic core offers superior thermal stability, positioning it as a promising candidate for ultrahigh-temperature ceramic composites compared to carbonaceous additives. These results reinforce the rationale for using Ti3C2T*x* MXene as a particularly suitable additive for enhancing the mechanical performance of MAX phase ceramics, offering structural advantages beyond those of traditional ceramic reinforcements.

To quantitatively evaluate the contribution of each individual mechanism, a decoupling analysis of the overall flexural strength increment (Δ*σ*) was conducted. This analysis is based on a modified theoretical model incorporating the effects of reinforcement phase content, morphology, diffusion behavior, and microstructural features. The model allows the total strength enhancement to be decomposed into separate terms representing idealized strengthening contributions, providing a clearer understanding of the dominant mechanisms in each composite system. A summary of the decoupling model and its main results is presented in Section 3.5, while detailed parameters are provided in the ESM.

### 3.5 Strengthening mechanism decoupling analysis

From the mechanical property tests, it can be observed that the addition of Ti3C2T*x* effectively enhances the strength of MAX phase ceramics. The strength increment, denoted as Δ*σ*, is defined as shown in Eq. (4):

(4) Δσ=σc−σm

where *σ*c is the flexural strength of the Ti3C2T*x*-reinforced MAX ceramic, and *σ*m is that of the unreinforced matrix. To gain further insight into the underlying mechanisms, the total Δ*σ* was quantitatively decomposed into contributions from solid solution strengthening (Δ*σ*S), grain refinement (Δ*σ*G), dislocation strengthening (Δ*σ*D), and second-phase particle reinforcement (Δ*σ*L) (Eq. (5)):

(5) Δσ=ΔσS+ΔσG+ΔσD+ΔσL

The dislocation strengthening component was calculated using the Bailey–Hirsch relation (Eq. (6)):

(6) σD=αGbρ

where *α* = 0.3 (a geometric constant); *G* is the shear modulus (from Table S2 in the ESM); *b* is the Burgers vector (equal to lattice constant *a*, due to basal plane slip [66,67]); *ρ* is the dislocation density.

Dislocation density was estimated via XRD using the Williamson–Hall (W–H) method [68,69]. MAX phases, although classified as ceramics, exhibit distinctive structural features compared to conventional covalent ceramics due to the presence of metallic bonding between the M and A layers. This unique bonding facilitates basal dislocation slip at room temperature, as demonstrated by both theoretical modeling [70] and TEM observations [67]. As dislocation motion and microstrain in such systems primarily evolve at the subgrain level, the W–H method is appropriate for estimating coherent domain size and relative dislocation behavior as shown in Eqs. (7) and (8):

(7) βcosθ=0.9λd+4εsinθ

(8) ρ=23εdb

where *β* is the full width at half maximum (FWHM); *θ* is the Bragg angle, *λ* = 0.154 nm (X-ray Cu Kα source); *d* is the grain size; *ε* is the microstrain. The corresponding W–H plots and fitted values are shown in Fig. S5 and listed in Table S4 in the ESM.

Grain refinement strengthening (Δ*σ*G) was evaluated using the Hall–Petch relationship as shown in Eq. (9):

(9) ΔσG=k(dc−1/2−dm−1/2)

where *d*m and *d*c are the average grain sizes of the matrix and composite, respectively, extracted from W–H analysis; *k* is the Hall–Petch coefficient, taken as 150 MPa·μm1/2 for MAX phase ceramics [71]. It is important to note that SEM-derived grain size statistics were not used in this calculation, as SEM reflects polycrystalline grain boundaries, while deformation in MAX phases is governed by basal slip within individual domains. Therefore, the grain size *d* used in the Hall–Petch model refers to the coherent domain size obtained from W–H analysis, which better captures the structural unit relevant to mechanical strengthening. Unlike conventional ceramics, where plastic deformation is dominated by intergranular dislocation pile-up and grain boundary sliding, MAX phase ceramics exhibit anisotropic plasticity governed by basal plane dislocation slip, kink banding, and delamination along the M–A interface. This makes the notion of a “polycrystalline grain” less physically meaningful for interpreting mechanical behavior. Consequently, conventional SEM-based grain size measurements—typically based on equiaxed grain boundary statistics—are not suitable for evaluating grain refinement effects in MAX phases. This approach is also conceptually supported by studies on nanostructured ceramics and metals, where coherent domains were identified as the key mechanical units, rather than the full polycrystalline grains observed by SEM [72,73].

Solid solution strengthening (Δ*σ*S) was estimated using a lattice distortion-based model as shown in Eq. (10) [74]:

(10) ΔσS=M⋅G⋅ε3/2⋅c1/2

where *M* = 4 (Taylor factor for basal slip); *ε* is the lattice mismatch strain derived from atomic size difference; *c* is the atomic fraction of Ti solute (estimated from EDS mapping). Parameter values and results are summarized in Table S4 in the ESM.

In the Cr2AlC system, a notable discrepancy between the predicted and experimental strength was observed at 4 wt% MXene addition. This deviation is attributed to TiC*y* second-phase formation and agglomeration-induced inefficiency at higher loading levels. To account for this nonlinear behavior, a corrected shear-lag model was introduced for Δ*σ*L as shown in Eq. (11):

(11) ΔσL=Δσideal+Δσcorr

Here, Δ*σ*ideal represents the ideal load-transfer contribution predicted by the classical shear-lag model as shown in Eq. (12) [75]:

(12) Δσideal=Vf⋅σr⋅(1−tanhCC)

where *σ*r = 521 MPa (strength of the Ti3C2T*x* reinforcing phase [28]), and *C* = *ηL*⁄2 is the shear-lag constant. For approximately particulate reinforcements as in this study, *η* becomes geometry-insensitive, Therefore, the constant *C* = 1.4611×107 was obtained by curve fitting to the experimental strength increment trend, and is consistent with the geometry-insensitive assumption for particulate reinforcements in shear-lag theory. The reinforcement volume fraction *V*f was calculated based on the nominal Ti3C2T*x* weight fractions and theoretical densities of each component, assuming complete retention of the additive as a second phase. This estimation does not account for partial Ti solid solution into the MAX matrix, and therefore represents an upper-bound approximation of the effective reinforcing volume. All values are provided in Table S4 in the ESM.

To account for the observed deviation from ideal shear-lag behavior at higher MXene additions, a fourth-order polynomial correction (Δ*σ*corr) in terms of *V*f was introduced. This correction term captures the strength reduction due to reinforcement agglomeration, which becomes significant at elevated additive content. While the polynomial coefficients are system-specific, the correction form is generally applicable to other particle-reinforced ceramic composites exhibiting non-ideal dispersion behavior as shown in Eq. (13):

(13) Δσcorr=aVf2+bVf3+cVf4

with fitted coefficients: *a* = −11,485.22 MPa, *b* = 1.6047×107 MPa, and *c* = −3.3914×108 MPa.

Quantitative parameters for each strengthening mechanism were derived from XRD and EDS analyses, as listed in Table S4 in the ESM, and were used to construct a predictive load-transfer model that aligns well with experimental strength increments. The final values of Δ*σ*L, along with the total decoupled Δ*σ* contributions, are shown in Fig. 9 and listed in Table S4 in the ESM. In Cr2AlC, grain refinement and second-phase strengthening dominate the enhancement, while in Ta2AlC, solid solution and grain refinement are the major contributors. The good agreement between theory and experiment confirms the validity of the model and highlights the different strengthening mechanisms in the two MAX systems.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, the effects of Ti3C2T*x* MXene addition on the mechanical properties and strengthening mechanisms of two MAX phase ceramics, Cr2AlC and Ta2AlC, were systematically investigated through a combination of experimental characterization and first-principles calculations. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:

(1) Ti3C2T*x* significantly improves both strength and toughness, but the enhancement trends differ between the two systems. In Cr2AlC, flexural strength reaches a maximum at 2 wt% addition, while fracture toughness continues to improve beyond this point. In contrast, both properties in Ta2AlC increase consistently with MXene content up to 4 wt%.

(2) SEM and TEM analyses reveal different microstructural responses to MXene addition: Cr2AlC shows partial solid solution accompanied by residual TiC*y* particles, whereas Ta2AlC forms a uniform solid solution without the formation of a second phase. Grain refinement and crack deflection mechanisms were observed in both systems.

(3) DFT calculations reveal that Ti ion diffusion in Ta2AlC is energetically more favorable due to its wider interlayer spacing and weaker Ta–Al bonding. Surface adsorption and interlayer diffusion along the *c*-axis were identified as the dominant doping pathways.

(4) Quantitative decoupling of the flexural strength increment (Δ*σ*) demonstrates that different mechanisms dominate in each system. Grain refinement and second-phase strengthening are the major contributors in Cr2AlC, while solid solution and grain refinement strengthening play leading roles in Ta2AlC. Theoretical predictions closely match experimental data after incorporating a corrected shear-lag model.

This work provides a mechanistic understanding of the distinct strengthening effects induced by Ti3C2T*x* in Cr- and Ta-based MAX ceramics, and offers a generalizable strategy for tailoring high-performance layered ceramics via 2D reinforcement.
