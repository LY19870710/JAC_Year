## 1 Introduction

With the advancement of modern electromagnetic (EM) technology and the development of new electromagnetic warfare models, many novel microwave absorption (MWA) materials with excellent electromagnetic wave (EMW) absorption performance have been developed [1−5]. Traditional MWA materials include mainly polymer-based composites [6,7], carbon-based composites [3,8], metal-based composites [9,10], and magnetic-based materials [11]. Polymer-based MWA materials (such as rubber- [12], epoxy- [13], or polyimide-based composites [14]) are widely used at low and ambient temperatures because of their light weight, flexibility, and ease of processing. However, their poor thermal stability causes thermal softening or decomposition under high temperatures or strong electromagnetic irradiation, leading to significant degradation of their absorption performance and limiting their application in high-power or high-temperature environments. Carbon-based materials (such as carbon black [15], carbon nanotubes [16], graphene [17], and carbon foams [18]) exhibit excellent electrical conductivity and interfacial polarization effects, resulting in good electromagnetic loss performance over a broad frequency range. Nevertheless, these materials are prone to combustion or structural degradation in strongly oxidative atmospheres, which limits their service lifetime [19]. Metal and metal-based composite materials possess high thermal conductivity and excellent structural strength, but their high reflectivity toward electromagnetic waves leads to limited absorption capability and can induce secondary scattering or electromagnetic interference. Moreover, their high density and poor oxidation resistance hinder the design of lightweight stealth structures [20]. Ceramic-based microwave MWA is increasingly pivotal for radar cross-section (RCS) reduction, electromagnetic interference/compatibility (EMI/EMC) control, and survival under high-power microwave (HPM) exposure, and the thermal, oxidative, and mechanical demands surpass the capabilities of polymeric systems [21−25]. Therefore, the pursuit of novel integrated high-temperature-resistant, high-load-bearing, and broadband MWA has become a frontier issue of common concern in both academia and the engineering community [26–28]. Currently, research on ceramic-based MWA materials has focused on two major directions: component design and structural regulation [29,30]. In terms of component design, by introducing functional phases with dielectric loss (such as barium titanate [31,32] and carbon nanotubes [33]) or magnetic loss (such as ferrite [34] and silicon carbide [35]), a multicomponent composite system is constructed to optimize impedance matching and loss capacity [36]. With respect to structural regulation, the wave-absorbing effect is enhanced through mechanisms such as interface polarization and scattering interference via the preparation of porous, layered, or gradient structures [37,38]. Owing to their high specific surface area and multiple reflection paths, porous ceramics not only can reduce the weight of the material but can also improve the efficiency of capturing and dissipating electromagnetic waves, making them an important development direction for lightweight wave-absorbing materials [39,40]. In this context, structure engineering across length scales has emerged as a unifying design paradigm. Recently, Yin *et al*. [41] have focused on the electromagnetic wave absorption characteristics of a subclass of ceramic materials, SiCN-based ceramics and their composites. They reported that the electromagnetic wave absorption performance can be regulated through the control of both the microstructure and composition of SiCN-based ceramics, thereby further enhancing the electromagnetic absorption properties of the ceramics. Li *et al*. [23] have explored the electromagnetic wave absorption performance and practical application requirements of ceramics and ceramic (nano)composites. However, comprehensive reviews on the influence mechanism of microstructural component selection and macro/mesoscopic configurations on the absorption performance of ceramics and ceramic (nano)composites are lacking. This study summarizes the optimized design methods for high-performance structural microwave-absorbing composites, providing valuable references for the integrated design of ceramic-based microwave-absorbing materials with high-temperature resistance and broadband absorption.

## 2 EM absorption fundamentals

Like all other energy forms, electromagnetic energy conforms to the principle of conservation. An electromagnetic wave consists of two coupled fields: the electric field (*E*) and the magnetic field (*H*) [42]. In free space, the frequency and wavelength were * c=1/ε0μ0, namely, the free space permittivity (ε*0) and the free space permeability (*µ*0). When a wave interacts with a medium, its response is described by the complex permittivity (*ε* = * ε − j ε″) and the complex permeability (µ* = *µ*′ − j*µ*′′). The real parts ( ε′,  μ′) correspond to the ability of the medium to store electric and magnetic energies, whereas the imaginary parts ( ε″,  μ″) quantify the dissipation of those energies. Accordingly, electromagnetic attenuation in absorbing materials can be generally divided into two categories: dielectric loss and magnetic loss [43].

### 2.1 Dielectric mechanism

The dielectric loss capacity is primarily determined by the polarization and ohmic losses. Polarization loss can be further categorized into electron polarization, ionic polarization, dipole orientation polarization, and interface polarization (spatial charge polarization). Ionic and electron polarizations typically occur at higher frequencies (103–106 GHz), contributing minimally to electromagnetic wave absorption. Dipole orientation polarization and ionic polarization originate from bound charges within materials, with the former often associated with defects and residual groups in dielectrics. However, these dipoles cannot freely migrate under applied electric fields such as electrons due to limitations imposed by defects and residual groups. At high frequencies, dipoles cannot reorient fast enough to track field changes, which lowers * ε′ and  ε″ and produces typical dispersion features. Interfacial charge accumulation further induces polarization at material boundaries, generating dipoles that contribute to electromagnetic energy dissipation. This relaxation behavior can be interpreted by Cole–Cole plots within the framework of Debye relaxation theory, and the relationship between  ε′ and  ε″ follows Eq. (1). For composites, multiple semicircles are often observed due to potential coexisting Debye relaxations (or polarization mechanisms) [44,45].

(1) (ε′−εs+ε∞2)2+(ε″)2=(εs−ε∞2)2

where  εs represents the static permittivity, while  ε∞ corresponds to the permittivity at very high frequencies. For highly conductive materials, the semicircular feature may not be observable because the dielectric loss is derived mainly from conduction loss. In this case, according to the free electron theory, the imaginary part of the dielectric constant can be expressed by Eq. (2) [46]:

(2) ε″=σ2πfε0

where  σ is the direct-current (DC) electrical conductivity. Studies have found that high electrical conductivity leads to an increase in the imaginary part of the dielectric constant. This indicates an increase in energy loss caused by electrical conduction, namely, ohmic loss. Ohmic loss refers to energy dissipation caused by directional charge movement in absorbing materials, which is governed mainly by the conductivity of the material. According to Eq. (3) [47]:

(3) δ=1πμ0fμrσ

where δ* is the skin depth (m), and *δ* decreases as the electrical conductivity (*σ*), vacuum permeability (*µ*0), relative magnetic permeability (*µ*ᵣ), and frequency (*f*) increase. Consequently, higher conductivity and frequency tend to promote reflection rather than absorption, indicating that excessively high conductivity is unfavorable for electromagnetic absorbing (EMA) materials. Recently, Ma *et al*. [48] have proposed that the permittivity spectrum can be described by the Lorentzian dispersion law, given as Eqs. (4) and (5):

(4) ε′=ε∞+∑in(ε0−ε∞)[1−(f/fri)2][1−(f/fri)2]2+(f/fdi)2

(5) ε″=∑in(ε0−ε∞)[(f/fdi)][1−(f/fri)2]2+(f/fdi)2

where * fd and  fr denote the Debye relaxation frequency and the natural resonance frequency, respectively;  fri and  fdi denote the resonance and damping frequencies of the i*-th oscillator respectively, and *n* is the number of oscillators. This model suggests that the morphology of nanostructured materials should also be taken into account when interpreting resonance behavior.

### 2.2 Magnetic mechanism

Magnetic losses arise mainly from natural resonance, eddy currents, domain wall resonance, exchange resonance, and hysteresis effects [49,50]. Typically, domain wall resonance occurs in the megahertz range, while hysteresis losses are insignificant under weak magnetic fields. Overall, domain wall resonance is most active in the MHz frequency band, whereas hysteresis effects are generally limited to lower frequencies (1–100 MHz) [51]. Exchange resonance generally occurs at frequencies higher than those associated with natural resonance. Consequently, natural resonance and eddy current effects are considered the main sources of magnetic losses. ### 2.3 Methods for evaluating performance of EMW materials

At present, the design of high-performance structural microwave-absorbing composites for radar stealth applications must satisfy two fundamental principles: the impedance matching principle and the electromagnetic loss principle [54]. Transmission line theory is a classical method for analyzing the reflection and absorption properties of electromagnetic waves in absorbing materials. When designing structural microwave-absorbing composites, the impedance matching principle should be considered first. *Z*in is the main parameter used to evaluate the impedance matching characteristics of an EMW material (Eq. (10)).

(10) Zin=Z0μr/εrtan(j(2πfd/cμrεr))

where *Z*0 represents the impedance of free space; * μr and  εr denote the relative permeability and relative permittivity of the absorber, respectively; j is the imaginary unit. When Z*in = *Z*0, the impedance matching is optimal; the composite material reaches a zero-reflection state; the reflection coefficient equals zero. According to Eq. (10), achieving this ideal condition essentially requires the complex permittivity and complex permeability of the absorbing material to be as close as possible. However, owing to the intrinsic properties of materials and the limitations of single-structure designs, ordinary single-layer absorbing composites often fail to meet this stringent requirement [55]. Therefore, it is necessary to design multilayer absorbing composites with gradual impedance transitions or to modify single-layer absorbing composites to improve their performance.

A comprehensive and quantitative understanding of the MWA mechanisms necessitates a detailed investigation of the electromagnetic parameters of the materials, as these parameters are intrinsically linked to their physical and chemical properties. Within the framework of transmission line theory, reflection loss (RL) can be rigorously evaluated via Eq. (11) [56]:

(11) RL=20log|Γ|=20log⁡|Zin−Z0Zin+Z0|

According to transmission line theory, the RL can be expressed as Eq. (12):

(12) RL=20log⁡|μrεrtanh(j2πfdcμrεr)−1μrεrtanh(j2πfdcμrεr)+1|

The attenuation constant (*α*) can be expressed as Eq. (13) [57]:

(13) α=2πfcμ″ε″−μ″ε″+(μ′2+μ″2)(ε′2+ε″2)

where *c* is the speed of light, and *Γ* is the reflection coefficient.

### 3.1 Nanoscale and microscale ceramic-based MWA materials

Nanoscale and microscale structured ceramic-based MWA materials exhibit outstanding advantages, making them indispensable key materials for next-generation stealth and electromagnetic protection technologies [60,61]. Since purely nanoscale ceramic absorbers are relatively limited, nanoscale and microscale designs are analyzed together. Structural designs at these scales can effectively enhance impedance matching, strengthen electromagnetic attenuation, and broaden the absorption bandwidth [62]. At the nanoscale, tuning interfacial structures, introducing defects, and constructing heterostructures can generate polarization effects and multiple scattering effects, thereby significantly improving the absorption efficiency. At the microscale, the design of aerogel-like porous architectures, hierarchical networks, and gradient structures not only achieves lightweight properties but also imparts excellent mechanical strength and thermal stability under extreme conditions. #### 3.1.1 Ceramic fiber MWA materials

Ceramic fiber MWA materials have attracted extensive attention owing to their unique combination of lightweight characteristics, high-temperature stability, and structural flexibility. Unlike dense bulk ceramics, ceramic fibers possess a high aspect ratio and interconnected porous networks, which effectively enhance multiple reflections and scattering of incident EM waves. This structural feature not only prolongs the propagation path of microwaves within the material but also promotes impedance matching between air and the absorber, thereby improving the overall absorption efficiency. From a perspective of material, ceramic fibers such as SiC, SiCN, and SiBCN are commonly employed because of their superior thermal and chemical resistance. SiC, a representative ceramic and semiconductor material, has been widely applied in the field of microwave absorption because of its distinctive electronic characteristics. In addition, SiC possesses superior mechanical strength and environmental durability, making it highly attractive for advanced applications [63]. The introduction of metal elements can effectively enhance the electromagnetic properties of SiC. Hou *et al*. [64] found that Co-modified SiC fibers can effectively broaden the absorption bandwidth by introducing metals to increase dielectric loss and magnetic loss. Feng *et al*. [65] explored SiC nanowire (NW, SiCnw)/SiC ceramic systems and demonstrated that the incorporation of a three-dimensional (3D) SiCnw framework effectively enhanced impedance matching while promoting the favorable dielectric loss behavior. Owing to this architecture, the SiCnws/SiC composites exhibited a notable reflection loss (RL) of −33.2 dB at 9.4 GHz with a thickness of 2.75 mm. Moreover, the effective absorption bandwidth (EAB) reached 4.2 GHz, successfully encompassing the entire X-band frequency range. These findings collectively highlight that 3D fiber-based nanostructures play a crucial role in generating continuous conductive networks and in optimizing impedance matching, thereby strengthening multireflection attenuation mechanisms. Zhang *et al*. [66] prepared nitrogen-doped bean-like SiC nanofibers with heterogeneous compositions and porous structures via a simple electrospinning process combined with high-temperature pyrolysis under a nitrogen atmosphere. The preparation route for these SiC-based nanofibers is illustrated in Fig. 2(a). Owing to their unique structural features, the obtained fibers exhibited a remarkable microwave absorption capability: at a thickness of 4 mm and a frequency of 6.3 GHz, the RL reached −51.8 dB, whereas the EAB extended to 2.8 GHz. Han *et al*. [67] achieved the *in situ* growth of nanoscale SiC nanowires on microscale SiC fibers, thereby constructing a SiCf/SiCnw composite with enhanced electromagnetic properties. The composite demonstrated outstanding microwave absorption performance, delivering an RL of −47.5 dB at 11.4 GHz and an EAB of 2.8 GHz. As illustrated in Fig. 2(b), the proposed absorption mechanisms of the SiCf/SiCnw system are associated with the cooperative contributions of multiple reflection pathways among fibers, diverse polarization processes, destructive interference effects, and the formation of interconnected conductive networks. In addition to SiC-based composites (where SiC serves as the main component) that exhibit excellent microwave absorption performance, compounding these SiC-based composites with other polymers can further enhance their microwave absorption performance. To address the shortcomings of traditional SiC ceramic composites, researchers have elaborately prepared core–shell structured ceramic composites. Zhong *et al*. [68] prepared SiC/SiO2 core–shell nanowires, and these nanowires exhibited an RL of −32.72 dB at a frequency of 13.84 GHz. The results demonstrate that core–shell fiber structures provide more efficient microwave absorption than their single-fiber counterparts. Furthermore, embedding magnetic nanoparticles within ceramic matrices promotes a synergistic balance between dielectric and magnetic losses, which in turn significantly boosts the electromagnetic wave attenuation capacity of the material. Wang *et al*. [69] synthesized lightweight and flexible SiCN nanowires that exhibited excellent electromagnetic wave absorption performance. By precisely controlling the annealing duration, the microstructure and chemical composition of the nanowires could be finely tuned. At a thickness of only 1.95 mm, the nanowires achieved an RL of −53.1 dB, with an EAB spanning the entire Ku-band (12.4–18.0 GHz), as illustrated in Fig. 2(c). The enhanced dielectric polarization arises from the synergistic interactions among defects in carbon, silicon carbide, and silicon nitride nanocrystals, as well as the heterojunction interfaces within the nanowires, which together confer outstanding mechanical robustness and electromagnetic properties. Similarly, Ren *et al*. [70] reported SiCN materials modified with carbon nanowires (CNWs), achieving a minimum RL of −53.1 dB and an EAB of 5.6 GHz over the Ku-band at the same material thickness of 1.95 mm. The enhanced electromagnetic wave absorption capability can be attributed to three aspects: improved electrical conductivity, a unique hierarchical network structure, and an increase in the defect content of each component. Chen *et al*. [71] fabricated lightweight SiBCN ceramic nanofibers via a combination of electrospinning and high-temperature annealing. As illustrated in Fig. 2(d), the amorphous phase serves as an electrically insulating matrix, whereas the β-SiC and free carbon phases function as electrical loss components. The uniform dispersion of free carbon and nanoscale β-SiC within the amorphous SiBCN matrix enhances dielectric loss and microwave absorption by effectively modulating the dielectric constant. Consequently, these nanofibers exhibit tunable electromagnetic wave absorption properties, achieving an RL of −56.9 dB at 10.56 GHz and an EAB of 3.45 GHz at a thickness of 2.6 mm. Overall, ceramic fiber-based MWA materials, including SiC, SiCN, and SiBCN fibers, demonstrate outstanding performance because of their high aspect ratio, interconnected porous networks, and hierarchical nanostructures. The incorporation of metal elements, core‒shell architectures, and heterojunction interfaces effectively enhances dielectric and magnetic losses while optimizing impedance matching. Moreover, 3D fiber networks, nanowire growth, and polymer-derived modifications contribute to multi-reflection, defect-induced polarization, and conductive network formation, collectively improving microwave attenuation. These strategies enable tunable reflection loss and effective absorption bandwidth across X- and Ku-band frequencies, highlighting the versatility of ceramic fiber-based MWA materials as lightweight, thermally stable, and high-performance absorbers for advanced electromagnetic applications.

#### 3.1.2 Ceramic-based particle MWA materials

Nonsilicon-based ceramic MWA materials generally refer to ceramic systems that, in electromagnetic wave absorption and shielding applications, do not rely on silicon (such as SiC, Si3N4, or SiBCN) but instead are constructed primarily on other metallic or nonmetallic element frameworks. Zhou *et al*. [72] synthesized TiCN@N-doped carbon nanocubes, and the low degree of graphitization provided favorable conditions for impedance matching. In addition, the numerous core–shell heterogeneous interfaces contributed to strong microwave absorption performance. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images, along with the reflection loss (RL) values of the TiCN@N-doped carbon nanocubes, are presented in Fig. 3(a). The composites exhibited a maximum EAB of 5.44 GHz at a thickness of 1.88 mm. Ma *et al*. [73] synthesized B4C nanoparticles with a core–shell architecture and an average diameter of approximately 100 nm. A schematic of the core–shell B4C nanoparticles, featuring submicron-scale amorphous carbon layers, is presented in Fig. 3(b). At a thickness of 1.5 mm, these carbon-coated B4C nanoparticles exhibited an RL of −60.8 dB at 15.5 GHz. Du *et al*. [74] reported that the electromagnetic absorption performance of SiOC-based nanocomposites can be modulated by varying the Fe ion concentration. The incorporation of Fe ions effectively enhanced interfacial polarization within the amorphous SiOC matrix and improved the dielectric properties while exerting a negligible influence on the magnetic characteristics. Under these conditions, the composites achieved a reflection loss of −59.60 dB at 5.40 GHz with a thickness of 4.55 mm and an EAB of 2.00 GHz. Zhang *et al*. [75] successfully fabricated a BCN/Fe*x*(B/C/N)*y* nano-architecture via a two-step polymerization and carbothermal reduction method. The morphological schematic is shown in Fig. 3(c). This ceramic structure achieved a minimum RL of −47.97 dB at 11.44 GHz and an absorption bandwidth of 11.2 GHz, covering the 3.76–14.9 GHz frequency range. Qian *et al*. [76] obtained SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres under an argon atmosphere, whose main components are SiC nanocrystals, graphitic carbon, and amorphous SiOC. The minimum RL of the material reaches −67.03 dB, demonstrating excellent electromagnetic wave absorption performance. This is mainly attributed to the synergistic effect of conductive loss and interface/defect polarization loss, and the special structure of SiOC@C ceramic nanospheres enables electromagnetic waves to undergo multiple reflections and scattering inside the material (Fig. 3(d)). Niu *et al*. [77] designed SiO2@Ti3C2T*x*@CoNi microspheres with a hydrangea-like core‒shell structure, in which the surfaces of silica were coated with CoNi nanosheets and MXene nanosheets, and their microwave absorption performance was investigated. The preparation method for the SiO2@Ti3C2T*x*@CoNi microspheres is shown in Fig. 3(e). These microspheres not only generated dielectric loss and magnetic loss but also enhanced interfacial polarization through multilayer interfaces. As a result, a minimum RL of −63.95 dB was achieved at an ultrathin thickness of 1.2 mm, with an EAB of 4.56 GHz. Therefore, this study demonstrates that the ternary core‒shell structural design strategy has a significant effect on improving the microwave absorption performance of EWA materials. Overall, ceramic particle MWA materials exhibit remarkable electromagnetic wave attenuation due to their tailored core‒shell architectures, multi-component compositions, and hierarchical nanostructures. The enhanced absorption performance is attributed primarily to synergistic effects among conductive loss, interfacial/defect polarization, and magnetic loss, as well as optimized impedance matching and multi-reflection pathways within complex structures. These studies highlight that strategies such as heteroatom doping, multilayer core‒shell design, and controlled nanoscale morphology can effectively broaden effective absorption bandwidths, reduce matching thickness, and improve overall reflection loss, demonstrating the significant potential of non-silicon-based ceramics for lightweight, high-performance, and multifunctional MWA materials.

#### 3.1.3 Other ceramic-based MWA materials

In addition to fiber- and particle-structured ceramics, various other forms of ceramic-based MWA materials have been developed to meet the growing demand for multifunctionality, structural diversity, and environmental adaptability. These materials typically feature complex architectures. Dong *et al*. [78] fabricated a hybrid composite comprising SiC whiskers decorated with manganese oxide (MnO*x*) nanoparticles and reduced graphene oxide (rGO) at 800 °C. The synthesis route of the MnONP@SiCw/RGO composites is illustrated in Fig. 4(a). The resulting hybrid exhibited exceptional microwave absorption performance, with a minimum RL of −54.04 dB at 17.9 GHz and an ultra-thin matching thickness of 1.59 mm. This outstanding electromagnetic wave attenuation is attributed primarily to the synergistic interplay between conduction loss and polarization relaxation. Compared with pure MnO*x* and SiC whiskers, the MnONPs@SiCw/RGO composite, with its complex multicomponent composition, demonstrated a markedly superior absorption behavior, which can be attributed to both the synergistic effect of the constituent materials and the optimized balance between impedance matching and attenuation. Liu *et al*. [79] synthesized a single-source precursor (SSP) by chemically modifying graphene oxide (GO) with poly(methylvinyl)silazane (HTT 1800). Subsequently, monolithic RGO–SiCN ceramic nanocomposites were successfully prepared. The SSP structure and the RL values of the RGO–SiCN nanocomposites are shown in Fig. 4(b). The RGO–SiCN nanocomposites demonstrated a remarkable RL of −61.9 dB, accompanied by an EAB of approximately 3.0 GHz at a sample thickness of 2.10 mm. In a related study, Liu *et al*. [80] prepared a series of nickel-doped polymer-derived SiCN ceramics (PDC-SiCN(Ni)). These ceramics exhibited an RL of −78.6 dB at 17.1 GHz with a thickness of 3.5 mm, and the corresponding EAB reached 1.6 GHz, highlighting the pronounced microwave absorption capability of nickel-containing PDC-SiCN systems. Luo *et al*. [81] synthesized SiBCN ceramics containing nanosized crystalline structures such as graphitic carbon and tubular carbon through pyrolysis of benzene-ring-containing precursors. The electromagnetic wave absorption performance and effective absorption bandwidth of ceramics can be tailored through annealing treatment. Among the samples, the one stabilized at 1320 °C exhibited the most favorable absorption behavior, achieving a minimum RL of −71.80 dB and an EAB of 3.65 GHz. A schematic illustration of the proposed microwave absorption mechanism, corresponding to the dielectric and magnetic loss processes in precursor-derived SiBCN ceramics, is presented in Fig. 4(c). Song *et al*. [82] introduced graphene oxide into SiBCN ceramics to enhance their dielectric properties and electromagnetic wave absorption capability. By controlling the graphene oxide (GO) content and annealing temperature, the dielectric properties of the composites were effectively optimized. The RGO-SiBCN composites achieved a minimum RL of −34.56 dB. Furthermore, high-temperature annealing at 1700 °C induced the precipitation of SiC nanocrystals and the formation of nanoscale interfaces. Consequently, the RL and EAB of the SiBCN composites reached −46.73 dB and 3.32 GHz, respectively. Recently, high-entropy ceramics have emerged as a novel class of materials with exceptional structural stability, compositional tunability, and multifunctional properties, offering new opportunities for the development of advanced MWA materials. Wang *et al*. [93] successfully fabricated high-entropy carbide ceramics containing different molar ratios of iron. These high-entropy alloy ceramics exhibited an RL of −61.62 dB at a thickness of 3.16 mm and a frequency of 10.77 GHz. The experimental results indicate that the (Hf(1−*X*)/4Zr(1−*X*)/4Nb(1−*X*)/4Ta(1−*X*)/4Fe*X*)C high-entropy ceramic possesses great potential as a structurally and functionally integrated material, with broad prospects for EMI shielding applications, particularly under extreme environmental conditions. Overall, these studies demonstrate that nonfibrous and nonparticulate nanoscale and microscale ceramic-based MWA materials exhibit remarkable microwave absorption performance across a wide frequency range. The enhanced absorption is attributed primarily to synergistic effects among multiple components, the introduction of conductive or magnetic phases, and the optimization of micro- and nanoscale architectures, which improve dielectric and magnetic loss mechanisms as well as impedance matching. Furthermore, their compositional tunability, structural stability, and controllable porosity allow tailoring of both reflection loss and effective absorption bandwidth, highlighting the significant potential of these materials as lightweight, thermally stable, and multifunctional absorbers suitable for advanced electromagnetic interference shielding and high-performance MWA applications.

### 3.2 Mesoscale ceramic-based MWA materials

In the structural design of ceramic-based microwave absorbing materials, mesoscopic-scale ceramics have garnered increasing attention because of their distinctive advantages over conventional microstructural or composition-based approaches. By integrating discrete subwavelength functional units into the ceramic matrix, both the microwave absorption performance and impedance matching of the composites can be substantially enhanced. These subwavelength units act as efficient scattering centers, which promote multiple reflections and prolong the propagation path of incident electromagnetic waves, thereby increasing energy dissipation within the material. Furthermore, the precise spatial arrangement and hierarchical organization of these functional units can suppress interfacial reflections and optimize the anti-reflection behavior while simultaneously providing enhanced intrinsic dielectric and magnetic loss mechanisms. As a result, the material strongly attenuates incident electromagnetic waves and improves impedance matching over a broad frequency range. These structural strategies not only expand the design flexibility of ceramic-based absorbers but also provide a promising pathway for achieving lightweight, broadband, and high-performance electromagnetic wave attenuation in advanced aerospace, defense, and high-frequency communication applications [94]. The types of mesoscopic ceramic-based microwave absorbing materials used include porous ceramic absorbers [95], multilayer composite ceramic absorbers [96], and continuous fiber-reinforced ceramic matrix composites [97]. #### 3.2.1 Ceramic-based aerogel MWA materials

Ceramic aerogels have extensive application advantages because of their high-temperature resistance, low density, and excellent corrosion resistance [98]. Song *et al*. [99] have used chemical vapor deposition (CVD) to fabricate SiC nanofibers as aerogels with extremely low density. A schematic illustration of the synthesis process of the SiC NFA is shown in Fig. 5(a). When the thickness is 1.9 mm, the aerogel achieves excellent EMW absorption performance at a frequency of 7.04 GHz, with an RL of −39.37 dB. Du *et al*. [100] have employed bacterial cellulose as a scaffold to fabricate carbon nanofiber (CNF) and SiC-CNF aerogel films. As a result, the SiC-CNF aerogel film exhibited a high RL of −53.3 dB at 17.7 GHz and an ultralow thermal conductivity of 0.046 W/(m·K). This outstanding performance is attributed to the impedance matching and interfacial polarization induced by the *in situ* grown silicon carbide nanowires, which significantly enhance the microwave absorption capability of the aerogel. Wang *et al*. [101] synthesized an ultra-lightweight and elastic silicon carbide@silicon dioxide (SiC@SiO2) nanowire aerogel (SNCA) using a low-cost carbon source. The optimized SNCA not only exhibits excellent resilience but also demonstrates outstanding microwave absorption performance, with an EAB of 10.2 GHz and a strong RL of −56.6 dB. Its attenuation mechanism is attributed mainly to multiple reflections, interfacial polarization, and dipole polarization. Yuan *et al*. [102] fabricated polymer-derived SiCN ceramic aerogels (PDCA-SiCNs) via a combined sol–freeze-drying and polymer precursor conversion method. The resulting aerogels demonstrated excellent EMA performance, achieving a minimum RL of −43.37 dB at 7.6 GHz and an EAB of 3.8 GHz. The superior EMA behavior of these nanoscale porous PDCA-SiCN aerogels is attributed to the synergistic interplay of optimal impedance matching, multiple internal reflections, and high dielectric loss. In a related study, You *et al*. [103] prepared high-performance microwave absorbing composites by impregnating mullite fibers with polysilazane. These composites exhibited a minimum RL of −39.38 dB at 12.08 GHz, with the absorption bandwidth nearly spanning the entire X-band. The combination of the intrinsic microwave absorption functionality of mullite fibers and their inherent thermal insulation properties underscores their potential importance in advanced thermal protection systems. Jiang *et al*. [104] reported that the SiBCN/Al2O3 composite ceramic aerogels exhibited excellent EMW absorption performance, with a minimum reflection loss as low as −48.6 dB and an effective absorption bandwidth of 5.8 GHz, along with favorable thermal insulation performance. The enhancement of the composite aerogel’s microwave absorption performance is attributed mainly to the introduction of Al2O3, which improves the impedance matching characteristics of the material, enhances the multiple reflection effects of electromagnetic waves, and promotes the strengthening of interfacial polarization. Zhang *et al*. [105] also investigated silicon boron carbonitride aerogel/mullite fiber (SiBCN/MF) aerogels. The resulting aerogels not only exhibited low density (0.151–0.190 g/cm3) and excellent microwave absorption performance (RL of −67.83 dB) but also had very low thermal conductivity (0.056–0.081 W/(m·K)). To enhance the dielectric properties of polymer-derived silicon carbide ceramics, Bai *et al*. [106] investigated the effects of the morphology and pore structure of porous molybdenum disulfide (MoS2)/SiC nanowire/Si3N4 ceramics (Fig. 5(b)) on their microwave absorption performance. Compared with porous SiC/Si3N4 ceramics, the study revealed that, compared with porous SiC/Si3N4 ceramics, the MoS2/SiC nanowire/Si3N4 composites exhibit superior microwave absorption performance, achieving an EAB of 3.50 GHz at a thickness of 2.38 mm and an RL of −70.48 dB at a thickness of 2.10 mm. Yan *et al*. [107] developed super-flexible SiC nanowire/mullite fiber composite aerogels (SMCA). The EMW absorption properties of SMCA and the corresponding schematic illustration of their absorption mechanism are presented in Fig. 5(c). Remarkably, the maximum compressive stress of SMCA increased from 1.2 to 1254.8 kPa, spanning over three orders of magnitude. In addition, the SMCA exhibited an ultralow thermal conductivity of only 0.026  W/(m·K) at room temperature while maintaining excellent microwave absorption performance, with a minimum RL of −53.8 dB at a matching thickness of 5 mm. Yuan *et al*. [108] grew SiCnws in situ within polymer-derived SiCNO ceramic aerogels to fabricate SiCnw/SiCNO composite aerogels. The study demonstrated that the morphology and phase composition of these composite aerogels could be effectively tuned by adjusting the microwave treatment temperature. The resulting composites exhibited favorable electromagnetic absorption properties, with a minimum RL of −26.5 dB at 10.9 GHz, a thickness of 2.0 mm, and effective absorption bandwidths of 3.2  and 4.8 GHz. Jiang *et al*. [109] controlled the phase composition, nanostructure, and electromagnetic wave absorption performance of SiBCN/SiC nanowire composite aerogels by adjusting the annealing temperature. The prepared aerogels exhibited excellent microwave absorption performance, with a minimum reflection loss of −50.1 dB and an effective adsorption bandwidth of 6.4 GHz, as well as low thermal conductivity (0.052 W/(m·K)) and low density (0.142 g/cm3). The outstanding electromagnetic wave absorption capability of these materials is attributed mainly to the SiC nanowires, which enhance impedance matching, strengthen multiple reflection effects, and intensify interfacial polarization. Zhang *et al*. [110] designed SiC–Si3N4 porous ceramics with whiskers grown inside the pores, which exhibited excellent microwave absorption performance. When the ceramic thickness is 2.7 mm, the minimum reflection loss value of the SiC–Si3N4 porous ceramics reaches −74.09 dB, which is superior to those of previously reported SiC-based absorbing ceramics. This unique “whisker-in-pore” structure optimizes impedance matching and enhances attenuation loss as well as interference cancellation at three scales. Xia *et al*. [111] fabricated a silicon carbide@silicon dioxide (SiC@SiO2) nanowire aerogel (LHSNA) featuring a multi-scale heterogeneous structure built upon a layered hollow fiber framework. The preparation procedure, material composition characterization, and schematic illustration of the microwave loss mechanisms of LHSNAs are shown in Fig. 5(d). The synergistic interface effects and hierarchical microstructure enhance both polarization and conductive losses, enabling the material to achieve an EAB of 5.46 GHz and an RL of −67.62 dB at a thickness of less than 2 mm. Even at 800 °C, the material maintains an EAB of 3.2 GHz within the X-band. Furthermore, owing to the multi-scale heterogeneous architecture and unique phonon–electron synergistic regulation at the SiC@SiO2 interface, the LHSNAs exhibit excellent infrared stealth capabilities. Above all, ceramic aerogels exhibit exceptional microwave absorption performance due to their ultralight, porous, and hierarchical structures. The outstanding electromagnetic wave attenuation is attributed primarily to synergistic effects of multiple mechanisms, including impedance matching, multiple internal reflections, interfacial polarization, dipole polarization, and conductive loss. The unique aergoel structure design further enhances the dielectric properties, optimizes the EAB, and reduces the matching thickness.

#### 3.2.2 Other mesoscale structure ceramic-based EMW materials

Other mesoscale-structured ceramic-based EMW materials have attracted increasing attention because of their unique combination of hierarchical architectures, porosities, and multilayer structure. Dong *et al*. [112] fabricated porous silicon carbide ceramics with *in situ*-grown NWs inside the pores, achieving a minimum RL of −67.4 dB. The EAB reached 8.1 GHz under an Ar atmosphere. The enhanced microwave absorption performance of this specially structured porous silicon carbide ceramic can be attributed to the formation of silicon carbide nanocrystals, nanoscale carbon, and nanowires, which increase the number of boundaries and defects, thereby inducing electronic dipole polarization and interfacial scattering effects. Yu *et al*. [113] synthesized SiC/C composites via a similar method, achieving uniform dispersion of ultrafine silicon carbide nanocrystals and layered carbon networks within an amorphous silicon oxycarbide (SiC(O)) matrix. The increase in structural ordering and free carbon content markedly improved the dielectric properties and electromagnetic absorption performance of the carbon-rich SiC ceramics. Under optimal conditions, a RL of −56.8 dB was observed at 15.2 GHz with a thickness of 1.51 mm, accompanied by a relatively broad EAB (RL 96] fabricated high-performance N/C ceramic aerogels composed of alternating multilayers of wave-transparent silicon nitride (N) and wave-absorbing silicon carbide (C), as illustrated in Fig. 6. These N/C aerogels exhibit ultralow density (~8 mg/cm3), a broad EAB of 8.4 GHz, a strong RL of −45 dB at room temperature, and excellent microwave absorption even at elevated temperatures up to 1000 °C. The primary attenuation mechanism is attributed to a “reflection–absorption–zigzag reflection” process induced by the alternating multilayer architecture, highlighting their suitability for next-generation wave-absorbing devices in extreme thermal environments. Luo *et al*. [114] reported that annealing carbon-rich SiC ceramics at 900–1600 °C induced a phase transition from amorphous to crystalline states, resulting in ultrafine SiC nanocrystals and turbostratic carbon networks dispersed within the SiC(O) matrix. The enhanced structural ordering and increased free carbon content led to significant improvements in the dielectric properties and electromagnetic absorption performance, with the RL reaching −56.8 dB at 15.2 GHz (thickness of 1.51 mm) and an EAB of 4.43 GHz. Liu *et al*. [115] have demonstrated that hierarchical porous SiOC ceramics possess a specific surface area of 51.4 m2/g. The porous architecture contributed to an optimal RL of −47.9 dB at 12.24 GHz with a thickness of 2.3 mm and an EAB of 4.56 GHz. The combination of amorphous SiOC, SiO*x*, and free carbon components enhances dipolar polarization and electromagnetic energy dissipation. Zhou *et al*. [116] have modified silicon nitride ceramics with silicon carbide nanofibers, forming interconnected conductive pathways that increase charge mobility. Additionally, the multiscale network formed by SiC nanofibers, Si3N4 grains, and micropores further enhances the microwave absorption performance, demonstrating the effectiveness of hierarchical conductive networks in improving electromagnetic wave attenuation. Other mesoscale-structured ceramic-based EMW materials also exhibit remarkable microwave absorption performance. Their superior electromagnetic attenuation arises from synergistic effects of multiple mechanisms: enhanced dielectric properties from structural ordering and free carbon content, interfacial and dipolar polarization from boundaries and defects, multi-reflection and zigzag pathways within porous or layered architectures, and interconnected conductive networks. These studies demonstrate that rational design of mesoscale structures can effectively broaden EAB, reduce matching thickness, and improve RL, highlighting their potential as high-performance, multifunctional microwave absorbers for extreme thermal and electromagnetic environments.

### 3.3 Macroscale ceramic-based MWA materials

In the structural design of ceramic-based MWA materials, macroscale engineering strategies have attracted increasing interest because of their ability to regulate electromagnetic responses through bulk architecture optimization. Unlike microscale or mesoscale approaches, macroscale design focuses on tailoring the overall geometry, hierarchical architecture, and large-scale structural features of ceramic matrix composites to achieve superior microwave attenuation. By constructing periodic lattice frameworks [120], gradient structures [121], and multilayer assemblies [122], incident electromagnetic waves can be guided, refracted, or absorbed more effectively, thus extending the propagation path and enhancing energy dissipation within the absorber. Moreover, macroscale structural modulation allows for precise control over impedance matching across a wide frequency range while simultaneously integrating mechanical robustness and thermal stability. Among the macro-scale structural ceramic-based MWA materials, the common types include foam-structured ceramics, 3D-printed ceramics, and other forms of macro-architectured designs. This classification is made because, although all these materials belong to the category of macro-porous ceramics, their pore structure characteristics differ fundamentally. Foam ceramics typically exhibit a non-uniform, randomly distributed porous network, whereas 3D-printed ceramics possess artificially designed and precisely controlled periodic pore architectures. This structural regularity difference not only influences the propagation path, reflection, and multiple scattering mechanisms of electromagnetic waves within the material but also determines the optimization balance between the broadband absorption performance and mechanical strength. These designs not only enable the realization of lightweight and load-bearing multifunctional components but also provide broad-band absorption and adaptive shielding capabilities. Consequently, macroscale design of ceramic matrix composites offers a promising route for developing next-generation high-performance absorbers for applications in aerospace, advanced defense systems, and high-frequency communication technologies. #### 3.3.1 Ceramic foam EMW materials

Macroscale porous structure-absorbing ceramic matrix composites are novel composite materials that utilize ceramics as the matrix and employ porous structures to optimize their electromagnetic absorption performance [123]. This material combines the high-temperature resistance, corrosion resistance, and high strength of ceramics with the unique properties of porous structures, giving it distinctive advantages in the field of electromagnetic wave absorption.

As a type of typical structural design material, most researchers have strived to leverage the distinctive merits of foam-structured materials in terms of the microwave absorption performance of such composites. Li *et al*. [124] have systematically explored the influence of foam structural characteristics on the microwave absorption performance of silicon carbide/carbon (SiC/C) foams by combining experimental measurements with numerical simulations. As shown in Fig. 7(a), the 3D X-ray tomography (XRT) reconstruction, optical images, and EMW absorption properties collectively reveal that the foam architecture effectively promotes diffraction and multiple scattering, enhances interfacial polarization, and improves impedance matching. These mechanisms significantly increase electromagnetic loss, and further optimization of the unit cell size and porosity can lead to superior absorption performance. Zeng *et al*. [125] fabricated a hierarchical SiC-based composite foam with *in situ*-grown silicon nitride nanowires, achieving a strong RL of −50.3 dB and a wide EAB of 3.6 GHz at a thickness of only 1.25 mm, while maintaining excellent mechanical toughness and specific compressive strength. In another study, Zhang *et al*. [126] developed 3D interconnected porous Si3N4 whisker/SiC foam ceramics via foam-gelcasting and *in-situ* catalytic nitridation (Fig. 7(b)). Increasing the Si3N4 whisker content reduced the dielectric loss but enhanced the overall absorption capability. At a thickness of 3.9 mm, the composites exhibited a minimum RL of −66.49 dB at 9.6 GHz, and by tuning the thickness, the effective absorption bandwidth could be extended to fully cover both the X- and Ku-bands. Therefore, foam-structured ceramic-based composites exhibit excellent microwave absorption performance because of their 3D porous architectures. The foam structure enhances electromagnetic wave attenuation through multiple scattering, diffraction, interfacial polarization, and improved impedance matching. Further optimization of parameters such as unit cell size, porosity, and whisker content allows the RL and EAB to be tuned, resulting in lightweight, mechanically robust, and broadband absorbers suitable for X- and Ku-band applications.

#### 3.3.2 3D-printed ceramic-based EMW materials

With the continuous progress in 3D printing technology and ceramic materials, 3D printed ceramic materials have made significant progress in the field of electromagnetic absorption applications [127]. Wang *et al*. [128] fabricated a porous SiCnw/SiC composite via 3D printing and carbothermal reduction techniques. A schematic of the 3D-printed porous biomass–derived SiCnw/SiC composite is shown in Fig. 8(a). The porous foam composite demonstrated a minimum RL of −49.01 dB and a broad EAB of 5.1 GHz while simultaneously exhibiting excellent flexural strength, high thermal conductivity, and superior flame-retardant properties. In addition, Mei *et al*. [129] have employed 3D printing to fabricate porous inclined alumina/silicon carbide whisker (Al2O3/SiCw) honeycomb composites. As illustrated in Fig. 8(b), the processing schematic highlights the structural design, where the composite with a 30° inclination angle achieved an impressive RL of −63.65 dB at 9.8 GHz (thickness: 3.5 mm), with the EAB fully covering the X-band (8.2–12.4 GHz). In a related study, Wang *et al*. [130] have utilized digital light processing (DLP)-based 3D printing to tailor both the composition and microstructure of silicon oxycarbide (SiOC) ceramics. The resulting dense, crack-free electromagnetic metastructures exhibited a gyroid‒shellular triple periodic minimal surface (GS‒TPMS) architecture (Fig. 8(c)). With a porosity of 51%, the GS-TPMS structures delivered effective absorption across the X-band at a thickness of 3.1 mm. Furthermore, Mei *et al*. [120] advanced the design of metamaterial absorbers by integrating Al2O3, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), and SiCnw into SiOC matrices, yielding twisted cross-shaped architectures via a combination of 3D printing and precursor infiltration pyrolysis (PIP). As shown in Fig. 8(d), the resulting composites exhibited a strong RL of −56.84 dB at 2.8 mm, and when the thickness was increased to 3.2 mm, the EAB spanned 8.2–12.4 GHz, fully covering the X-band. 3D-printed ceramic-based materials exhibit remarkable microwave absorption performance because of their precisely controlled geometries, hierarchical porosities, and tailored microstructures. The 3D printing approach enables the optimization of RL, EAB, and impedance matching while simultaneously enhancing the mechanical strength, thermal stability, and flame retardancy. Advanced structural designs, such as inclined honeycombs, GS-TPMS architectures, and twisted cross-shaped metamaterials, further promote multiple scattering, interfacial polarization, and energy dissipation, demonstrating the great potential of 3D-printed ceramics for lightweight, high-performance, and broadband electromagnetic absorbers.

High-performance structural ceramic-based EMW materials play a pivotal role in the design of next-generation high-temperature stealth components. This review systematically summarizes the design principles of structural microwave absorbing composites, provides an in-depth analysis of the microstructural composition as well as the hierarchical features spanning from the microscale to the macroscale in high-performance structural ceramic-based composites, and further elaborates on their absorption mechanisms. Future research on the EMW absorption properties of structural ceramic-based composites should focus on the following key scientific issues:

(1) Optimization of material composition: Investigating the EMW absorption performance of different ceramic constituents and exploring the optimal combination of fibers, matrix, and interfaces. The introduction of novel ceramics, nanotechnology, and advanced composite processing methods can improve the overall properties of composites, thereby achieving an optimal balance between broadband absorption and high load-bearing capacity.

(2) Innovations in structural design: Exploring novel configurations such as metastructures, aperiodic gradient structures, irregular geometries, and bio-inspired architectures. Precise tailoring of microstructural features can further optimize impedance matching and electromagnetic attenuation, thus enhancing the absorption efficiency and effective bandwidth.

(3) Multiscale regulation from microscale to macroscale: High-performance structural ceramic-based composites are simultaneously constrained by thermal, mechanical, and electrical boundary conditions, which impose significant design and material selection challenges. At the microscale, controlling the morphology and structural characteristics can endow the materials with excellent electromagnetic attenuation capabilities. At the macroscale, tailoring the geometry, dimensions, and overall architecture ensures effective impedance matching.

(4) Multifunctionality and intelligence: Through gradient distribution and integrated design, EMW absorption can be coupled with additional functions such as thermal insulation, load-bearing capacity, and electrical conductivity to meet the requirements of different environments and application scenarios. With the aid of advanced manufacturing technologies, structural ceramic-based composites with tailored shapes and architectures can be designed and fabricated. Such intelligent structures can be customized for specific applications, achieving simultaneous optimization of EMW absorption performance and mechanical robustness.
