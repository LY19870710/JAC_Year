## 1 Introduction

Facing increased environmental challenges and energy shortages, the development of new green energy systems to replace traditional fossil fuels has become more urgent [1−3]. Hydrogen (H2) is regarded as an environmentally friendly and renewable energy resource for the future [4]. Its unparalleled virtue lies in the fact that its combustion byproduct is exclusively water. Alkaline water electrolysis (AWE) technology is recognized as one of the most promising methods for H2 production. However, its widespread adoption has been impeded by high associated costs, and its global market share remains negligible, at less than 4% [5−8]. Reducing the cost of AWE for the production of green H2 is a common challenge for countries around the world. The limited elemental abundance and high cost of noble metal electrocatalysts, such as Pt and RuO2, limit their large-scale application [9,10]. Therefore, the development of bifunctional non-precious metal electrocatalysts with high catalytic activity, low cost, and excellent stability is essential to significantly improve the energy efficiency of AWE [11]. Recent studies have revealed a range of low-cost bifunctional electrocatalysts derived from transition-metal oxides. These include Co3O4 [12], NiO [13], and Fe2O3 [14].

To enhance the bifunctional activity of these electrocatalysts, the electronic structure can be modulated by controlling the oxygen vacancy (VO) density, which enhances the electron conductivity, promotes H2O adsorption to nearby active sites, and notably enhances the absorption of ·OH species and alters the absorption free energy of oxygen-containing transitional species, thus promoting oxygen evolution reaction (OER) activity [15−17]. Li *et al*. [18] successfully synthesized three-dimensional (3D) porous heterojunctions of N-NiMoO4/Ni/CNT catalysts with a high content of VO by using hydrothermal and continuous H2 calcination methods. These vacancies synergistically modulated the electronic structure of the heterostructure N-NiMoO4/Ni/CNTs, promoting the desorption of reaction intermediates and accelerating both the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and the OER. Zhang *et al*. [12] successfully synthesized dual-defect Co3O4 nanoarray electrocatalysts via a gas-phase fluorination strategy. Their study revealed that the deliberate introduction of F− and oxygen vacancy defects in Co3O4 can synergistically overcome the energy barrier limitation of the Volmer step, markedly enhancing the intrinsic activity of the HER. However, Miao *et al*. [19], while investigating the OER in the bis-chalcogenide PrBaCo2O6−*δ* with oxygen vacancies, discovered that an abundance of oxygen vacancies does not enhance the process. Instead, the introduction of excessive oxygen vacancies significantly reduces the intrinsic OER activity. This may be caused by the formation of surface trap-state structures by oxygen vacancies at high current densities during the OER [20]. This discovery highlights the importance of regulating the type of oxygen vacancies conducive to catalytic reactions.

Filling the aforementioned “excessive” oxygen vacancies, also known as trap-state oxygen vacancies (TS-VO), with nonmetallic elements of varying electronegativity and atomic radius presents an intriguing avenue for research [21,22]. Previous studies have indicated that filling the VO of metal oxides with sulfur (S) atoms can create active sites for catalytic reactions and potentially compensate for the lack of ligands to stabilize other oxygen vacancies [23,24]. On this basis, we demonstrate the feasibility of retaining ordered oxygen vacancies by showing that S atoms preferentially fill the TS-VO of the Fe2O3 nanobelts during the filling process.

As predicted by density functional theory (DFT) calculations, these long-range ordered oxygen vacancies retained after S atoms fill the TS-VO can serve as fast channels for electron transport and significantly improve the bifunctional electrocatalytic activity of Fe2O3 nanobelts. Moreover, the presence of S atoms eliminates the adverse effect of TS-VO on the catalytic reaction, successfully stabilizes the structure of the ordered oxygen vacancies, and can be used as the active site of the electrocatalytic reaction, which plays the role of “killing three birds with one stone. In addition, theoretical calculations show that S atom filling lowers the free energy barrier for the formation of OOH* from O* and optimizes the ∆*G*H* of the Fe2O3 surface, accelerating both the HER and the OER. Thus, in 1 M potassium hydroxide (KOH), HNBs-VO(LRO)-S exhibited ultralow HER overpotentials (226 mV@100 mA·cm−2) and OER overpotentials (262 and 306 mV@100 mA·cm−2) relative to those of pristine HNBs-VO. The bifunctional water cell consisting of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S achieved a current density of 100 mA·cm−2 at a cell voltage of 1.92 V. This material well meets the requirements of practical water decomposition and outperforms most of the reported catalysts.

#### 2.1.1 Chemicals and reagents

The reagents sodium palladium chloride (Na2PdCl4), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), and caprolactam were procured from Aladdin Industrial Co., China. Magnesium nitrate hexahydrate (Mg(NO3)2·6H2O), citric acid (C6H8O7), anhydrous ethanol (C2H5OH), acetone (CH3COCH3), KOH, potassium bromide (KBr), dicalcium chloride (KCl), and vitamin C (C6H8O6) were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China. Iron ascorbate foil (Fe) was procured from Qingheguangtai Metal Materials Co., Ltd., China. All reagents were of analytical grade and were utilized directly without further purification.

#### 2.1.2 Preparation of cubic palladium nanoparticles (Pd NPs)

The synthesis of Pd NPs was achieved via the PVP modification method. First, 3 mL aqueous solution (containing 0.057 g Na2PdCl4) was fully dissolved for 10 min at 60 °C in a thermostatic water bath. Subsequently, 8 mL aqueous solution (containing 0.105 g PVP, 0.060 g ascorbic acid, and 0.600 g KBr) was sonicated in an ultrasonic cleaner for 5 min to fully dissolve it, and the thermostatic water bath was adjusted to 80 °C with magnetic stirring for 10 min. Finally, the above two solutions were mixed, and the mixed solution was placed in a thermostatic water bath with magnetic stirring at 80 °C for 3 h and cooled to room temperature.

Three times the volume of acetone was added to the prepared Pd NP solution and centrifuged at 10,000 r·min−1 for 10 min. The solution was then washed three times with ethanol to remove unbound PVP. Finally, the product was dispersed in 7.5 mL anhydrous ethanol to form a suspension of cubic Pd NPs at a concentration of 2.746 mg·mL−1.

#### 2.1.3 Preparation of magnesium oxide gels

Magnesium oxide gels were prepared via the sol–gel method. A stable magnesium oxide gel was obtained using 8.700 g magnesium nitrate hexahydrate Mg(NO3)2·6H2O and 5.140 g urea CO(NH2)2 as raw materials, 7.040 g citric acid C6H7O8·H2O and 2 mL anhydrous ethanol as stabilizers, and 18 mL ultrapure water, which was heated at 80 °C for 1 h in a constant-temperature water bath.

#### 2.1.4 Preparation of hematite nanobelts

Fe2O3 nanobelts (HNBs-VO) were prepared according to the process outlined in Fig. 1. The procedure involved several sequential steps for precise fabrication. Initially, the Fe sheet underwent meticulous sanding with #800, #1000, #1500, and #2000 sandpaper until a smooth surface was achieved. The Fe sheet was subsequently subjected to ultrasonic cleaning twice with acetone and anhydrous ethanol to ensure thorough cleanliness.

A uniform coating of 0.05 mL magnesium oxide gel was applied to the polished iron sheet via a homogenizer. After drying in an oven at 50 °C for 1 h, annealing was carried out in a tube furnace at 350 °C with the holding time of 30 min and a heating rate of 5 °C·min−1, resulting in the formation of a preoxidized layer on the iron substrate.

A simple drop-coating method was subsequently employed to apply 0.2 mL of prepared Pd NPs onto the preoxidized layer. The assembly was then sealed in a crucible and wrapped in a double layer of aluminum foil. Hematite nanobelts (HNBs-VO) were synthesized through annealing in a tube furnace at 450 °C for 2 h at a heating rate of 3 °C·min−1. Our previous work showed that the prepared HNBs-VO samples that underwent additional annealing in a tube furnace could form in situ* arrays of hematite nanobelts with long-range ordered oxygen vacancies [25].

The HNBs-VO sample was placed in a crucible containing 40 mg S powder and annealed for 2 h at 250 °C with a temperature increase rate of 5 °C·min−1 in an argon atmosphere. This procedure led to the filling of TS-VO by S atoms while preserving the ordered oxygen vacancies, denoted as HNBs-VO(LRO)-S. Following the same annealing conditions at 250 °C for 2 h with a heating rate of 5 °C·min−1, HNBs-VO were subjected to annealing in a crucible containing 100 mg S powder in an argon atmosphere, resulting in the overfilling of S in the sample, which was labeled HNBs-S.

#### 2.2.1 Material characterizations

A field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; JSM-6700F, JEOL, Japan) was used to study the micromorphology of the samples. The samples were analyzed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) on a transmission electron microscope (JEM 2100F, JEOL, Japan) with an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The phase composition and crystal structure of the samples were investigated via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D/MAX-2400, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.5406 Å) at voltages and currents of 40 kV and 40 mA, respectively, with a scanning range of 10°–80° and a scanning speed of 10 (°)·min−1. The chemical composition of the surface elements of the samples was characterized via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB 250XI, Thermo Fisher, USA); and Raman signal measurements were performed on a spectrometer (inVia, Renishaw, UK) at an excitation source of 532 nm.

#### 2.2.2 Electrochemical measurements

The electrocatalytic performance was carried out in a standard three-electrode system on an electrochemical workstation (CHI660E, China), in which HNBs-VO (HNBs-VO(LRO)-S or HNBs-S), saturated calomel electrodes (1 M KOH), and graphite rods were used as the working, reference, and counter electrodes, respectively, at a temperature of 25 °C, with an aqueous electrolyte of 1 M KOH (pH = 14). For the HER and OER tests, the samples were first preactivated by cyclic voltammetry (CV) for 100 cycles at a scan rate of 300 mV·s−1 to obtain stable CV curves. Linear scanning voltammetry (LSV) was performed in an aqueous solution of 1 M KOH at a scan rate of 5 mV·s−1, and the recorded data were corrected for 90% *iR* to account for the voltage drop between the reference and working electrodes. To evaluate the double-layer capacitance of the catalyst, CV curves were measured at various scan rates (20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mV·s−1) under potential windows of −0.50 to −0.20 V (HER) and 0.03 to 0.33 V (OER), and capacitance of the double layer (*C*dl) was estimated by plotting difference in current density (Δ*J)* vs. the scan rate at different potentials. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed over a frequency range of 10−1–105 Hz, with a potential amplitude of 5 mV and an OER of 1.71 V vs. RHE, and a HER of −0.27 V vs. RHE. A two-electrode electrolytic cell was assembled using HNBs-VO(LRO)-S as both the cathode and anode catalysts in a 1 M KOH solution. Long-term stability tests were performed for 100 h at a current density of 100 mA·cm−2.

### 2.3 Theoretical calculations

Theoretical calculations were performed via the CASTEP module of Materials Studio. We used the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) generalization under the generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) as an exchange-correlation generalization to perform energy and periodic structure calculations and selected the (001) face of Fe2O3 as the crystal face where the reaction occurs. The planar model used 2 × 2 × 1 Fe2O3 supercells and set up three filling types (without S atom filling, filling the trap-state oxygen vacancies with S atoms while preserving the ordered oxygen vacancies, and filling the trap-state oxygen vacancies and ordered oxygen vacancies with S atoms). Structural relaxation was performed until the force of atom convergence reached 0.05 eV·Å−1. In energy calculations, to avoid interlayer interactions, a vacuum layer of 15 Å was added in the *z*-direction, and a *k*-point of 2 × 2 × 1 was taken in the Brillouin zone to describe the inverse easy space. A plane wave with an energy cutoff of 450 eV was used for all calculations, and the maximal displacement value was set to 2×10−3 Å.

### 3.1 Material characterizations

The microscopic morphology of the prepared samples was analyzed via FESEM. As illustrated in the flow chart in Fig. 1, we initially synthesized HNBs-VO as a precursor for the sulfidation heat treatment, as shown in Fig. S1(a) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). HNBs-VO mixtures were subsequently heated at 250 °C for 2 h in an argon environment in the presence of S powder. During this process, S atoms were evaporated, excited, and doped into the structure of HNBs-VO, which changed the oxygen vacancy structure, forming HNBs-VO(LRO)-S and HNBs-S. As depicted in Figs. S1(b) and S1(c) in the ESM, after vulcanization heat treatment, HNBs-VO(LRO)-S and HNBs-S exhibit morphologies similar to those of HNBs-VO. HNBs-VO, HNBs-VO(LRO)-S, and HNBs-S with the same morphological characteristics were obtained. All of them were characterized by significant nanobelts with lengths of approximately 1–4 μm and widths of approximately 100 nm (Fig. S1(d) in the ESM). This observation indicates that the sulfidation heat treatment process had a minimal effect on the morphology of the Fe2O3 nanobelts. In addition, a clear layered structure can also be observed from the cross-sectional SEM images of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S and HNBs-S, as shown in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b), respectively. It can be hypothesized that the layered structure consists of an iron matrix layer, an oxide columnar crystal layer, a Pd NP layer, and an S-doped one-dimensional Fe2O3 nanobelt layer from the bottom to the top. Furthermore, the vulcanization heat treatment did not change the layered structure during the growth of the nanobelts. The crystal structures of the different catalysts were evaluated via XRD, as shown in Fig. 2(c). The results revealed that all the samples presented characteristic peaks corresponding to the hexagonal phase α-Fe2O3 (JCPDS No. 85-0987, space group: *R** 3¯c*), the cubic structure Fe3O4 (JCPDS No. 79-0417, space group: *Fd** 3¯m*), and the cubic phase Fe (JCPDS No. 87-0721, space group: *Im** 3¯m*). In addition, the diffraction peak at 2*θ* = 40.2° can be attributed to the (111) crystal plane of Pd, but its diffraction intensity is low, which may be due to the relatively low content of Pd [26]. After S atoms were filled into the VO in the HNBs-VO mixture, the XRD patterns did not show significant peak shifts, and no additional peaks attributed to the formation of sulfides were observed (Fig. S2(a) in the ESM). These findings indicate that the crystal structure of the Fe2O3 nanobelts was not significantly altered by the heteroatom modification [23]. Owing to the inhomogeneous distribution of cubic Pd NPs during the nanobelt growth process, which leads to high oxygen partial pressure in the region with Pd and low oxygen partial pressure in the region without Pd, the crystal structure formed in the region with low oxygen partial pressure will contain many oxygen vacancies to form Fe3O4. Thus, the characteristic peaks of Fe3O4 will be observed in the XRD pattern.

The oxygen vacancy state and S doping of the materials were further analyzed via Raman spectroscopy, and the results are shown in Fig. 2(d). We can observe typical hematite phases in HNBs-VO, HNBs-VO(LRO)-S, and HNBs-S. The characteristic peaks at 215 and 494 cm−1 represent the A1g mode, whereas the characteristic peaks at 286, 405, and 607 cm−1 are attributed to the Eg mode [27,28]. The strong spectral band at approximately 650 cm−1 in the HNBs-VO sample is due to the presence of many oxygen vacancies in the hematite phase, some of which are reduced to magnetite. Notably, a redshift and narrowing of the half-height width are observed for both HNBs-VO(LRO)-S and HNBs-S after the sulfidation heat treatment. This is attributed to the filling of oxygen vacancies by S atoms, which improves the crystallinity of the surface in the otherwise defective state and causes stress changes. Interestingly, HNBs-S showed an S Raman peak at 241 cm−1 (purple-labeled bright band), while HNBs-VO(LRO)-S, which also underwent vulcanization heat treatment, did not. This may be because the S atoms fill some ordered V oxygen vacancies along with the surface TS-VO during the sulfidation heat treatment of HNBs-S. A small amount of S monomers covered the catalyst surface after cooling. To verify this result, different samples were prepared via sulfurization heat treatment with 80 and 160 mg of S powder under the same parameters. Their Raman spectra are shown in Fig. S2(b) in the ESM. As the amount of sulfur powder used exceeded 40 mg, a new peak gradually appeared at the purple-labeled bright band and increased with increasing amount of sulfur powder used, which verified that the new peak was the S monomer. From the above Raman analysis results, we conclude that in HNBs-VO(LRO)-S with 40 mg of sulfur powder for sulfidation heat treatment, S only partially fills the oxygen vacancies. In the HNBs-S thermally treated with 100 mg of sulfur powder, S filled all types of oxygen vacancies, accompanied by a very small portion of S present as monomers attached to the surface layer of the catalyst [29].

XPS was used to study the surface chemical state and bonding structure of the samples. In the O 1s XPS spectrum, the low binding energy peak at 529.5 eV represents oxygen within the hematite lattice (OL), the medium binding energy peak at 531.0 eV is attributed to oxygen vacancies (OV), and the high binding energy peak at 534.2 eV correlates with surface-adsorbed oxygen (OS) [30−32]. Compared with that of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S (Fig. 2(e)), the OS peak area share of HNBs-S increased (Fig. 2(g)), possibly because the S atoms not only filled some ordered oxygen vacancies in TS-VO but also a small number of S monomers covered the surface layer of the catalysts. Thus, this increase in the OS peak area share was caused by the presence of a small amount of S monomers. The percentage of the area of the OV peak in relation to the total oxygen-related peak (OL + OV + OS) is described in detail in Table S1 in the ESM, which shows the different degrees of filling of the oxygen vacancies by S in HNBs-VO(LRO)-S and HNBs-S. The XPS spectra of S 2p exhibit three typical peaks at 162.5, 163.7, and 168.0 eV (Figs. 2(f) and 2(h)) that belong to S 2p3/2, S 2p1/2, and sulfur‒oxygen bonding (S–O), respectively, indicating the successful filling of S atoms. The peaks of S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2 are attributed to the bonding of sulfur and iron [23,33]. Figure S3 in the ESM shows the XPS high-resolution spectra of Fe 2p, Pd 3d, and Mg 1s of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S and HNBs-S. S‒Fe bonds are formed because S atoms coordinate with Fe after filling with oxygen vacancies, whereas the uncoordinated lone pair of electrons of may form S‒O bonds with O atoms in the adsorbed state, thus lowering the energy of the system and leading to the enrichment of sulfur monomers on the surface of the catalyst and the formation of S‒O bonds with H2O or **·**OH in the air [34].

TEM observations of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S and HNBs-S samples were performed to provide more detailed information about HNBs-VO(LRO)-S and HNBs-S samples after vulcanization heat treatment. Figure 2(i) shows high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S with facet spacings of 0.25 and 0.36 nm corresponding to the (1 1¯0) and (110) crystallographic facets of Fe2O3, respectively. As depicted in the black box of Fig. 2(i), the SAED pattern exhibits a series of spots corresponding to the (110) and (1 1¯0) planes of the hematite phase. In addition, the elemental mapping diagrams (Figs. 2(k)–2(n)) show the presence of the elements Fe, O, and S, indicating the successful filling of S into the Fe2O3 nanobelts. Interestingly, HNBs-VO(LRO)-S is observed with clearly visible ordered bands that are axially parallel to the nanobelts. This indicates that the S atoms only fill the TS-VO while retaining the ordered oxygen vacancies (the filling schematic is shown in Fig. 2(o)), confirming the above XPS analysis [35,36]. The HRTEM image of HNBs-S is shown in Fig. 2(j). Notably, a clear lattice mismatch band (pink dashed line) appears in its interior, indicating that the S atoms are filled with too high a concentration, and the ordered oxygen vacancies are also filled after the TS-VO are filled first. Moreover, an amorphous layer of approximately 2 nm in size appeared on its surface (between the yellow dashed lines), which was caused by a small amount of S monomers covering the surface layer of the catalyst after cooling, confirming the analysis of the new peak in the Raman spectrum at 241 cm−1 [29].

### 3.2 Electrochemical HER

To investigate the effect of the synergistically ordered oxygen vacancy structure of S atoms on the HER activity of Fe2O3 nanobelts, a standard three-electrode electrolytic cell in 1 M KOH was used to study the HER performance of the samples. Figure 3(a) presents the LSV curves of the HER with the different catalysts. HNBs-VO(LRO)-S clearly showed the best electrocatalytic H2 precipitation ability, with an overpotential of only 226 mV at a current density of 100 mA·cm−2, which was significantly lower than those of HNBs-S (275 mV at 100 mA·cm−2) and HNBs-VO (298 mV at 100 mA·cm−2). As shown in Fig. 3(b), the Tafel slopes of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S, HNBs-S, and HNBs-VO are 99, 125, and 114 mV·dec−1, respectively, whereas the low Tafel slopes of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S indicate superior HER activity and efficient charge transfer. Figure 3(c) shows the overpotential vs. Tafel slope, which more intuitively illustrates that the HNBs-VO(LRO)-S samples with synergistically ordered oxygen vacancy structures of S atoms have better HER activity. To investigate the electrode kinetics at the electrode/electrolyte interface during the HER process, we performed EIS tests. Figure 3(d) shows the Nyquist plots and electrode equivalent circuits for the different catalysts in a 1 M KOH electrolyte at the same potential (−0.27 V vs. RHE). The results of the data processed by the equivalent circuit are shown in Table S2 in the ESM. Here, we found that *R*ct of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S (2.02 Ω) was significantly lower than that of HNBs-S (5.39 Ω) and HNBs-VO (6.18 Ω), which indicated that the kinetic process of the HER for HNBs-VO(LRO)-S was faster [37]. The HNBs-VO(LRO)-S system also had the lowest solution resistance (*R*int) (3.49 Ω), which indicates that the HNBs-VO(LRO)-S system has a faster charge transfer rate and higher conductivity. This kinetic difference highlights the advantage of synergistically ordered oxygen vacancies for the S atoms in HNBs-VO(LRO)-S.

### 3.3 Electrochemical OER

For the overall water splitting (OWS) reaction, the OER is the main limiting factor for OWS efficiency, and improved OER performance is important for OWS [41]. To investigate the effect of the synergistically ordered oxygen vacancy structure of S atoms on the OER activity of Fe2O3 nanobelts, the OER performance was tested in a standard three-electrode electrolytic cell with 1 M KOH solution. Figure 4(a) shows the LSV curves of the OER with different catalysts. Notably, HNBs-VO(LRO)-S also showed the best electrocatalytic oxygen precipitation ability. The overpotentials of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S were 262 and 306 mV at current densities of 10 and 100 mA·cm−2, respectively. This value was significantly lower than those of HNBs-S (324 mV at 10 mA·cm−2 and 405 mV at 100 mA·cm−2) and HNBs-VO (396 mV at 10 mA·cm−2 and 495 mV at 100 mA·cm−2). As shown in Fig. 4(b), the Tafel slopes of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S, HNBs-S, and HNBs-VO are 40, 65, and 76 mV·dec−1, respectively. The lowest Tafel slope was observed for HNBs-VO(LRO)-S, indicating superior OER activity and efficient charge transfer. Figure 4(c) shows the overpotential vs. Tafel slope, which more intuitively illustrates the superior OER activity of HNB-VO(LRO)-S with an S-atom synergistically ordered oxygen vacancy structure. To investigate the electrochemical kinetic properties of the electrode and the electrolyte during the OER process, we performed the corresponding EIS tests. Figure 4(d) shows the Nyquist plots and electrode equivalent circuits for the different catalysts at the same potential in a 1 M KOH electrolyte. We list the results of the data processed by the equivalent circuits in Table S4 in the ESM. The *R*ct of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S (0.79 Ω) is significantly lower than that of HNBs-S (1.58 Ω) and HNBs-VO (6.18 Ω), which suggests that the OER kinetic process of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S is more rapid. In addition, the *R*int (2.28 Ω) of the HNBs-VO(LRO)-S system is also the lowest, which indicates that the HNBs-VO(LRO)-S system has a relatively high charge transfer rate and conductivity.

### 3.4 Electrocatalytic performance of HNB-V<sub>O</sub>(LRO)-S for OWS

Given the excellent electrocatalytic performance of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S in the HER and OER, we further used it as a bifunctional electrocatalyst to evaluate the effect of OWS. The HNBs-VO(LRO)-S were configured as the anode and cathode and assembled into an alkaline water cell in a 1 M KOH aqueous solution. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the current density of the fully solvated water system reached 100 mA·cm−2 at a cell voltage of 1.92 V. Both electrodes produced rapidly dense bubbles under the current. For an in-depth evaluation of its long-term stability, we performed continuous 100 h chronopotentiometry of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S at a constant current density of 100 mA·cm−2 (Fig. 5(c)). Encouragingly, the potential fluctuations after 100 h were negligible, indicating very strong stability. By observing the SEM and XRD images of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S before and after undergoing the OWS reaction (Figs. S8(a) and S8(b) in the ESM), we found that, apart from a slight decrease in the XRD peak intensity, there were no significant changes in the morphology or crystal structure, which further confirmed the excellent stability of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S for overall electrocatalytic water splitting. Notably, after 100 h of OWS operation, the polarization curves of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S were almost indistinguishable from those before the previous test (Fig. 5(b)). The comparative analysis in Table S6 in the ESM confirms that HNBs-VO(LRO)-S outperforms the vast majority of recent reports in terms of OWS.

### 3.5 Mechanism of the high catalytic activity of HNBs-V<sub>O</sub>(LRO)-S

DFT simulations were employed to explore the potential electrocatalytic mechanisms. To better understand the influence of ordered oxygen vacancies, theoretical models of HNBs-VO, HNBs-VO(LRO)-S, and HNBs-S were constructed (Fig. S9 in the ESM). To verify the compatibility between the chosen model and the actual synthesized material, we compared the XRD calculations of the theoretical model with the actual XRD results. The results show that the errors between the experimentally measured peaks and the peak positions obtained from the theoretical calculations are within reasonable limits, which confirms the consistency between the lattice parameters of the theoretical model and those of the actual materials (Fig. S10 in the ESM). Since the water dissociation step is a vital pathway for the alkaline HER, we conducted DFT calculations to investigate the effects of HNBs-VO, HNBs-VO(LRO)-S, and HNBs-S on water dissociation (Fig. S11 in the ESM). The activation energy of water decomposition on the model HNBs-VO(LRO)-S was the lowest at only 0.53 eV, which confirms that the addition of sulfur can significantly promote water dissociation and accelerate the HER process, eventually favoring the whole HER process in alkaline media. The structures of the adsorbed intermediates on the surface were subsequently optimized (Fig. 6(c) and Fig. S12 in the ESM). The OER pathway in alkaline solutions generally encompasses four steps [42], as articulated in Eqs. (2)–(5):

Figure 6(a) shows the free-energy diagram of the OER pathway at an applied potential (U* = 1.23 V). The rate-determining step (RDS) for HNBs-S and HNBs-VO(LRO)-S is the formation of OOH* from O*, whereas for HNBs-VO, it is the formation of O2 from OOH*. Notably, the limiting step free energy (∆*G*RDS) of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S is significantly lower, suggesting that long-range ordered oxygen vacancies retained after S atoms fill the TS-VO can serve as fast channels for electron transport and significantly improve the bifunctional electrocatalytic activity of Fe2O3 nanobelts [43]. Moreover, S atom filling lowers the free-energy barrier for the formation of OOH* from O* [44].

In alkaline electrolytes, the HER is initiated by the Volmer reaction [45], where water molecule is the proton source (Eq. (6)). H2 may subsequently be produced via two pathways: the Tafel reaction (Eq. (7)) and the Heyrovsky reaction (Eq. (8)). Figure 6(b) shows the free-energy diagram of the HER pathway. For HER catalysts, near-zero *∆G*H* values (free energy of the adsorbed H2 intermediate H*) imply optimal H* adsorption and H2 desorption. The *∆G*H* (free energy of uptake of the H2 intermediate H*) values for HNBs-VO, HNBs-VO(LRO)-S, and HNBs-S are −0.55, −0.32, and 0.37 eV, respectively. Apparently, the presence of ordered oxygen vacancies likewise accelerated the HER-catalyzed reaction kinetics and improved the catalytic activity of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S [46]. Moreover, S atom filling optimizes the *∆G*H* of the Fe2O3 surface [44].

To further reveal the mechanism of the high catalytic activity of HNBs-30, *in situ* electrochemical Raman measurements were performed. During the OER process, the Raman features in the range of 480–600 cm−1 (Fig. 7(a)) may be associated with the formation of OOH*, as described in Eq. (3) [47]. In addition, a small peak at approximately 699 cm−1 was observed as the applied bias increased, which was attributed to the formation of FeOOH [48]. This may be due to the conversion of some Fe2O3 or FeS*x* to FeOOH during the catalytic process. During the HER process, the increase in the vibrational peak at 677 cm−1 (Fig. 7(b)) suggests that the structure of the ordered oxygen vacancies can serve as an effective active site for the catalytic reaction [49]. These findings are consistent with the DFT calculations discussed previously.

Figures 7(c) and 7(d) show schematic diagrams of the HER and OER processes of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S. In HNBs-VO(LRO)-S, the pristine surface-trapped state oxygen vacancies trap S atoms for Fe–S coordination, which both stabilize the ordered oxygen vacancy structure of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S, and the S atoms can serve as active site I [23]. In addition, the retained ordered oxygen vacancy structure can serve as a channel for the directional transport of charge and as active site II [50,51]. Therefore, the introduction of an appropriate amount of sulfur atoms in HNBs-VO(LRO)-S can effectively regulate the electronic structure of the catalyst and synergistically promote the HER/OER catalytic reaction on the surface of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S efficiently with ordered oxygen vacancies. However, for HNBs-S with the introduction of excess sulfur atoms, its S atoms first overfilled the oxygen vacancy structure to the extent that the ordered oxygen vacancy structure, which facilitates the catalytic reaction, was damaged along with the filling of the TS-VO. Second, the sulfur monomers covering its surface mask the active sites of the catalytic reaction, leading to a decrease in its HER/OER catalytic activity. Therefore, HNBs-VO(LRO)-S with S-atom synergistic ordered oxygen vacancies have superior HER/OER bifunctional activity, and this modification provides a new idea for the design of bifunctional electrocatalysts.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, one-dimensional Fe2O3 nanobelt arrays (HNBs-VO(LRO)-S) with sulfur atoms that synergize with the ordered oxygen vacancy structure were successfully prepared via Pd-catalyzed oxygen reduction and sulfidation heat treatment. The sulfide thermal treatment did not destroy the microstructure or crystal structure of the nanobelts, the S atoms filled only the oxygen vacancies in the trap state, and the ordered oxygen vacancy structure was retained. The Fe–S coordination structure formed by filling the oxygen vacancies in the trap state of the S atoms not only stabilizes the ordered oxygen vacancy structure of the HNBs-VO(LRO)-S complex from being destroyed during the catalytic reaction but also serves as an active site. The ordered oxygen vacancy structure still provides a directional fast channel for charge transport in the catalytic reaction and can also serve as the active site of the catalytic reaction. Moreover, S atom filling lowers the free energy barrier such that the formation of OOH* from O* optimizes *∆G*H* of the Fe2O3 surface. The combined advantages synergistically promoted the HER/OER bifunctional catalytic activity of HNBs-VO(LRO)-S. Under 1 M KOH conditions, HNBs-VO(LRO)-S presented lower OER (262 and 306 mV@100 mA·cm−2) and HER (226 mV@100 mA·cm−2) overpotentials. In addition, when HNBs-VO(LRO)-S was assembled as the anode and cathode to form an OWS electrolier, the required cell voltage was 1.92 V at a current density of 100 mA·cm−2, and a long-term stability of 100 h was maintained. This ingenious synergistic mechanism of vacancy filling provides new insights into the defective design of bifunctional electrocatalysts.
