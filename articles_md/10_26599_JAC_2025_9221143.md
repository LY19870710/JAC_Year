## 1 Introduction

Traditional fossil fuel combustion has relatively low efficiency and causes severe environmental pollution; however, it remains the dominant power generation method. With the rapidly increasing global demand for low-carbon and renewable energy sources, technologies such as wind and solar power are limited by high initial costs and dependence on environmental conditions. However, fuel cells, particularly solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs), have garnered significant attention because of their high efficiency, low emissions, and adaptability to various fuels [1,2]. As an electrochemical energy conversion device, SOFCs can directly convert chemical energy into electrical energy without combustion, thereby reducing energy losses and pollution. Although hydrogen is an environmentally friendly fuel for SOFCs, its high storage, transportation, and production costs limit its commercial development. However, owing to their wide fuel applicability, SOFCs can use hydrocarbons that are easy to store and transport as fuel, such as methane, natural gas, and biomass fuel [3−5]. Biomass ethanol, derived from the fermentation of sustainable agricultural waste (e.g., straw and bagasse), has outstanding renewable characteristics and carbon-neutral potential, making it an excellent fuel choice for SOFCs. The use of biomass ethanol not only reduces fuel costs but also decreases reliance on conventional fossil fuels, driving the transition of the energy structure toward a greener and low-carbon future [6−8]. Among the various design strategies for SOFCs, the tubular solid oxide fuel cell (T-SOFC) configuration has been proposed for the optimized integration of additional biomass ethanol catalysts.

In the field of T-SOFCs, Ni-based ceramic anodes are favored for their excellent catalytic activity and electrical conductivity, especially for Ni–8 mol% yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) anodes [9,10]. However, when hydrocarbon fuels such as ethanol are used, the Ni–YSZ anode is prone to carbon deposition due to incomplete oxidation caused by the catalytic cleavage of C‒H bonds, leading to the accumulation of carbon in the anode. This block leads to a decrease in the number of active sites on the anode catalyst, preventing further oxidation of the fuel and thus reducing the performance and stability of the fuel cell [11−13]. To address this issue, researchers have explored various methods. By impregnating the anode with an efficient gadolinium-doped cerium oxide (Ir-GDC) catalyst, the cell was able to operate stably in a dry ethanol environment for over 600 h, with only slight performance degradation and no carbon deposition [14]. Compared with costly noble metal catalysts, CeO2 is favored because of its low cost, abundant resources, excellent catalytic activity, and anti-carbon deposition ability. Additionally, cerium-based catalysts can be combined with other materials to meet specific catalytic needs, making them highly favored in the fields of energy conversion and catalytic chemistry [15,16]. Furthermore, carbon deposition can be improved by adding oxidants, including water, air, or CO2, to hydrocarbon fuels to adjust their thermodynamic conditions [17−21]. However, introducing large amounts of water or air will reduce the overall efficiency of the fuel cell and increase system complexity, so the proportion of oxidants in the fuel flow must be precisely controlled. Moreover, adding a fuel reforming layer to the Ni-based metal ceramic anode surface is another effective method for improving carbon tolerance. Park *et al*. [22] coated a NiFe alloy catalyst layer on a NiO–YSZ anode surface, which exhibited good catalytic performance and stability in methane fuel. Papadam *et al*. [23] reported a high-entropy alloy reforming catalyst for wet ethanol fuel, which significantly improved the cell performance and stability.

CeO2 performs excellently as an anode reforming layer in enhancing catalyst performance, but some limitations still exist [24−26]. First, CeO2 has relatively low electronic conductivity, which may limit electron transfer efficiency when used as an anode reforming layer. This is particularly important when hydrocarbon fuels are used, as higher electronic conductivity is required to facilitate the oxidation reactions of the fuel. Second, although CeO2 and its doped materials exhibit some catalytic activity in the oxidation reactions of hydrocarbon fuels, their catalytic activity still needs improvement compared with that of traditional Ni–YSZ anode materials, especially under high-temperature conditions. Furthermore, although CeO2 has some anti-carbon deposition ability, a certain degree of carbon deposition still occurs when hydrocarbon fuels are used, particularly in terms of durability under long-term operation, which requires further investigation. While noble metal-doped CeO2 shows better catalytic performance, its preparation cost is relatively high. Traditional alloy catalysts suffer from limitations in terms of their oxygen capture capability, electrochemical performance, fuel adaptability, and long-term operational stability. For example, combining NiFe alloys with CeO2 can enhance the oxygen capture ability of the alloy catalyst, thereby compensating for the aforementioned deficiencies [27,28]. Extensive research has shown that the *in situ* exsolution of nanoparticles on a substrate to form active metal heterointerfaces can significantly increase the number of catalytically active sites. Additionally, it enhances the concentration of oxygen vacancies on the electrode surface, thereby improving the catalyst activity, conversion efficiency, and coking resistance [29,30]. Therefore, this study improves the catalytic ability and anti-carbon deposition capability of CeO2 through the doping of two transition metals, which offer low cost and high catalytic activity.

In this study, a Ni–YSZ anode-supported T-SOFC was fabricated via the impregnation-co-firing method with a NiFe@Ni0.1−*x*Fe0.1−*y*Ce0.8O2−*δ* (R-NFCO) internal reforming catalyst layer coated onto the anode surface. The electrochemical performance, durability, and anti-carbon deposition ability of the T-SOFC were systematically evaluated under different low-concentration biomass ethanol-carbon oxide (C2H5OH–CO2) mixed atmospheres (C2H5OH : CO2 = 2 : 1, C2H5OH concentrations of 5%, 10%, and 15%). The results demonstrated that the T-SOFC with the reforming layer exhibited excellent electrochemical performance and stability in low-concentration C2H5OH–CO2 atmospheres. This is mainly attributed to the efficient catalytic effect of the reforming layer, which enhances the anode activity and promotes the conversion of ethanol and carbon dioxide [31−33]. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed that the catalyst surface has a strong adsorption capacity for water, which is beneficial for ethanol catalysis. COMSOL Multiphysics simulations and experimental results also confirmed that reforming the catalyst layer optimized the internal molar concentration distribution of the cell, significantly improving its performance.

## 2 Experimental

*Synthesis and fabrication:* NFCO powder was synthesized via the ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-citrate combustion method. First, 150 mL of distilled water and 15 mL of concentrated nitric acid were added to a beaker. The raw materials Ni(CH3COO)2·4H2O (99.9%, Aladdin, China), Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (99.5%–101.0%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), and Ce(NO3)3·6H2O (99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) were weighed according to the molar ratio (Ni : Fe : Ce = 0.1 : 0.1 : 0.8). The chemicals were sequentially added to the beaker and heated under stirring until fully dissolved, resulting in a homogeneous solution. Next, monohydrate citric acid (99.5%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) and EDTA (99.5%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) were weighed according to the molar ratio (metal ions : monohydrate citric acid : EDTA = 1 : 1 : 0.8) and added to this mixture. After dissolution, a certain amount of ammonia solution was added to adjust the pH of the solution to 7–8 [34]. The resulting solution was then transferred to an evaporating dish and heated with stirring in an electric furnace until the solution spontaneously combusted. After complete combustion, the obtained powder was sintered at 800 °C for 3 h.

The preparation process of the cell was as follows: NiO, YSZ, and starch (with a weight ratio of 5 : 5 : 1) were used as raw materials and mixed with 1.8 wt% triethanolamine (TEA), 3 wt% polyethylene glycol-400 (PEG-400), 3 wt% dioctyl phthalate (DOP), and 3.6 wt% polyvinyl butyral (PVB) in a 50 wt% ethanol solution. This mixture was used to prepare the NiO-YSZ anode substrate for the T-SOFC. The mixture was ball-milled to obtain a uniform anode slurry, and bubbles were removed from the slurry via a vacuum pump. The anode slurry was then dipped into a glass tube mold and dried at room temperature. This process was repeated 15 times to obtain an anode green body of suitable thickness. Finally, the anode green body was demolded in water, dried, and sintered at 1000 °C for 3 h to obtain the anode substrate. Six grams of NiO, 4 g of YSZ, and 30 g of ethanol were ball-milled for 24 h. Then, 0.5 g of PEG-400, 0.5 g of DOP, and 1 g of PVB were added, and the mixture was ball-milled for an additional 12 h to prepare the anode functional layer (AFL) slurry. The NiO−YSZ anode support was then immersed in the AFL slurry and pre-sintered at 400 °C for 30 min. Next, 3 g of YSZ powder and 30 g of ethanol were ball-milled for 12 h, followed by the addition of 0.1 g of PVB and ball milling for 6 h to prepare the electrolyte slurry. The electrolyte film was then coated onto the surface of the AFL and sintered at 1400 °C for 10 h. For the Gd0.1Ce0.9O2−*δ* (GDC) slurry, 10 g of GDC, 0.2 g of Hypermer KD1, and a certain amount of acetone were ball-milled for 24 h. After that, 20 g of turpentine-ethyl cellulose (5%) solution was added, and the mixture was ball-milled for another 24 h to obtain the GDC slurry. The GDC slurry was then coated onto the surface of YSZ and sintered at 1250 °C for 3 h. Then, NFCO and turpentine-ethyl cellulose (10%) were mixed at a 1 : 2 weight ratio, ground together to obtain the reforming layer slurry, and applied to the inner surface of the tubular fuel cell. La0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3−*δ* (LSCF) and GDC powders were mixed at a 7 : 3 weight ratio, a certain amount of turpentine-ethyl cellulose (10%) was added, and the mixture was ground to obtain the cathode slurry. The slurry was then applied to the GDC surface with an effective area of 0.6 cm2 and sintered at 1000 °C for 3 h. The preparation process of the T-SOFCs is shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

*Basic characterization:* The preparation of reforming catalyst NFCO and the powder reduced at 800 °C for 5 h were characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) in the range of 20°–80° with a Cu-Kα radiation source. The microstructure of a single cell was examined by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Gemini-300, Carl Zeiss, Germany). The elemental valence states were analyzed via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), whereas the morphology of the reduced NFCO powder was studied via a field emission transmission electron microscope (FETEM; Tecnao G2 F20, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A Raman spectroscope (SENTERRA, Bruker Optics, Germany) was used to check for carbon deposition on the anode. A Fourier transform infrared spectroscope (FTIR; VERTEX80V, Bruker, Germany) was employed to analyze the chemical composition and functional group information of the reduced NFCO powder. The composition of the exhaust gases was analyzed via a gas chromatograph (GC; GC9790II, Fuli Instruments, China).

*Electrochemical measurements:* Wet H2 (~3% H2O) and C2H5OH–CO2 (C2H5OH : CO2 = 2 : 1, with 30 mL·min−1 N2 as the carrier gas) were used as fuels. The electrochemical performance of the anode-supported T-SOFC single cell was tested via Zennium XC (ZAHNER, Germany) under static air conditions. A two-dimensional cell model was constructed and simulated via COMSOL Multiphysics 6.2 software to simulate the molar concentration distribution of substances inside the cell under real working conditions. The performance of the catalyst was theoretically predicted via DFT calculations with the DMol3 software package. The detailed parameters for the COMSOL simulations and DFT calculations are provided in the ESM. The calculated ethanol conversion rate is shown in Eq. (1):

(1) C2H5OHconversion=CO+CH4+CO2output−CO2input2C2H5OHinput×100%

In this experiment, the temperature of the liquid ethanol was adjusted to change its saturated vapor pressure [34]. The ethanol was heated via a thermostatic water bath, with the temperature displayed and controlled by a temperature controller. The partial pressure of ethanol at the inlet was controlled by regulating the temperature of the water bath, which was based on the saturated vapor pressure of ethanol at different temperatures. Ethanol fuel partial pressures of 5%, 10%, and 15% were tested, corresponding to water bath temperatures of 17.9, 30, and 37.4 °C, respectively. N2 was used as a carrier gas to deliver ethanol vapor into the anode chamber. By adjusting the carrier gas flow rate and ethanol vapor partial pressure, the gas ratio was determined, and the test setup is shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM. To prevent ethanol vapor from condensing in the pipelines, thermal insulation measures were applied to the inlet piping, including heating and insulating tapes, as well as wrapping and sealing with refractory cotton and heat-resistant tape.

## 3 Results and discussion

Figure 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of the NFCO powder before and after hydrogen reduction, revealing the fluorite cubic structure of CeO2 (JCPDS 04-003-2611) without any secondary phase impurities. After hydrogen reduction, additional peaks belonging to the NiFe alloy (JCPDS 01-085-4088) are observed [33,35]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed a distinct heterogeneous structure with noticeable nanoparticles, where fine particles were attached to the substrate (Fig. 1(b)). Precise measurements yield interplanar spacings of 0.204 and 0.177 nm for the NiFe particles and the CeO2 matrix, corresponding to the (111) and (200) planes of NiFe, respectively, which is consistent with the XRD results. Additionally, interplanar spacings of 0.270, 0.270, and 0.191 nm correspond to the (111), (200), and (220) planes of CeO2, respectively. Furthermore, energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis (Fig. 1(c)) indicates that the exsolved nanoparticles are enriched primarily with Ni and Fe, whereas the substrate consists of Ce and O, confirming that the exsolved nanoparticles are NiFe alloys.

Figures 1(d) and 1(e) show the XPS results of NFCO before and after hydrogen reduction. Figure 1(d) presents the Ce 3d5/2 and Ce 3d3/2 spectra. The peaks centered at 916.3, 907.2, 900.6, 898.1, 888.3, and 882.1 eV can be assigned to Ce4+, whereas the peaks at 903.1 and 884.5 eV correspond to Ce3+. The Ce3+ content in the hydrogen-reduced R-NFCO (22.5%) is greater than that in the unreduced NFCO (16.5%), which significantly enhances the catalyst activity. This is because a higher Ce3+ content leads to the formation of more oxygen vacancies, which provide additional adsorption sites and enhance the catalyst's ability to adsorb reactants. Moreover, an increase in Ce3+ can also lower the activation energy of the reaction, making the reaction proceed more easily [36]. As shown in Fig. 1(e), the O 1s spectra contain two components at 528.9 and 531.3 eV. The peak at a higher binding energy corresponds to surface adsorbed oxygen (Oad), and the peak at a lower binding energy corresponds to surface lattice oxygen (Olatt) [37]. After reduction, the Oad content increases from 29.5% to 38.6%, which is related to the formation of oxygen vacancies and is beneficial for the catalytic performance of the reforming catalyst [38]. Figure S3(a) in the ESM presents the Ni 2p spectra, with peaks at 855.5 and 872.5 eV corresponding to Ni2+. After reduction, a Ni0 peak at 852.2 eV is observed, confirming the formation of the NiFe alloy. Figure S3(b) in the ESM shows the Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 spectra. The two peaks at 710.6/729.0 and 716.8/733.2 eV correspond to Fe2+ and Fe3+, respectively. After hydrogen reduction, a metallic Fe0 peak (707.5 eV) appears [28], confirming the precipitation of the NiFe alloy. XPS analysis at room temperature cannot accurately reflect real conditions under high-temperature reduction. This is because the concentration of Ce3+ and oxygen vacancies may decrease during cooling and exposure due to changes in environmental conditions, such as the atmosphere and temperature. Under high-temperature reduction in a H2 atmosphere, the concentrations of Ce3+ and oxygen vacancies further increase. Therefore, the R-NFCO material is a high-oxygen-vacancy cerium-based catalyst with NiFe alloy heterostructures.

A fixed-bed reactor (Fig. S4 in the ESM) was used to characterize the catalytic effect of the NFCO catalyst in a C2H5OH–CO2 atmosphere. The gas composition of the exhaust from the reactor was measured via gas chromatography. As shown in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b), the contents of H2, CO, CH4, and CO2 in the exhaust increase as the fuel concentration increases from 5% to 15% at 700 °C because a relatively high fuel concentration leads to the generation of more products from the reforming and decomposition of C2H5OH–CO2. At a 10% ethanol concentration, as the temperature increases from 650 to 800 °C, the catalyst activity improves due to the more favorable kinetics and thermodynamics at higher temperatures, thus enabling more complete fuel gas reforming. After hydrogen reduction, the ratio of H2 to CO in the exhaust is much greater than that before reduction, which is due to the formation of a NiFe alloy on the catalyst during hydrogen reduction, significantly increasing the number of active sites. These active sites can more effectively adsorb and activate C2H5OH and CO2, promoting their conversion into synthetic gases [39]. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 2(c) and 2(d), the ethanol conversion rate is the lowest in the absence of the reforming catalyst. Compared with the unreduced NFCO, the R-NFCO catalyst significantly enhances ethanol conversion.

Figures 3(c)–3(f) show the *I*‒*V*‒*P* (current‒voltage‒power) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) curves of the T-SOFC with and without a reforming catalyst layer under H2 fuel at 650–800 °C. The maximum power densities of the T-SOFC without a reforming layer under H2 at 650, 700, 750, and 800 °C are 394, 659, 966, and 1286 mW·cm−2, respectively. Under the same conditions, the maximum power density of the T-SOFC with a reforming layer is 396, 685, 1025, and 1380 mW·cm−2, which is slightly greater than that of the tubular fuel cell without reforming layers. The cell’s ohmic impedance (*R*Ω) and total impedance (*R*t) can be obtained from the high- and low-frequency *X*-axis intercepts in the EIS plot, and their difference corresponds to the polarization resistance (*R*p). The *R*Ω values of the cells with and without the reforming layer are very similar. When the T-SOFC without a reforming layer is tested under H2 at 650, 700, 750, and 800 °C, the *R*p values are 0.651, 0.391, 0.291, and 0.252 Ω·cm2, respectively. Under the same conditions, the *R*p values of the reforming layer cell are 0.709, 0.426, 0.292, and 0.228 Ω·cm2, indicating that the polarization resistance difference between the two cells is minimal. As shown in Fig. 3(g), a comparison of the performance of tubular cells under H2 at 650–800 °C from recent literature indicates that our tubular cell has higher performance in terms of maximum power density under H2 fuel compared with most previously reported studies [41−57]. As shown in Fig. 3(h), the thermal cycling test from 300 to 700 °C under H2 revealed that after 100 cycles of heating and cooling (the cell also underwent 8 cycles from high temperature to room temperature), the cell maintained a relatively high voltage, indicating good thermal cycle stability.

Figure 4(a) illustrates the catalytic mechanism of R-NFCO for the C2H5OH–CO2 fuel reforming reaction. C2H5OH and CO2 are adsorbed onto the NiFe alloy surface, where they undergo efficient decomposition and dry reforming to generate syngas. These gases are then transported through the porous anode to the triple-phase boundary for oxidation, producing H2O and CO2 as reaction products. During the reforming process, some carbon deposition may occur on the catalyst surface; however, oxygen vacancies on CeO2 can adsorb H2O and facilitate its reaction with the deposited carbon, thereby effectively mitigating carbon accumulation and maintaining the catalyst's activity and stability. Figures 4(b) and 4(d) show the *I*‒*V*‒*P* curves of the T-SOFC with and without the reforming layer at 700 °C under different concentrations (5%, 10%, and 15%) of C2H5OH‒CO2 fuel. The experimental results show that the maximum power densities of the cell with the reforming layer at each concentration are 538 mW·cm−2 (5%), 614 mW·cm−2 (10%), and 608 mW·cm−2 (15%), which are significantly greater than those of the cell without the reforming layer (490, 550, and 559 mW·cm−2, respectively). This indicates that the reforming layer promotes fuel conversion and increases the synthesis gas content, thus enhancing the cell performance. Additionally, the maximum power densities at the 10% and 15% concentrations are quite similar, suggesting that further increasing the fuel concentration has a limited impact on performance improvement [58]. Figures 4(c) and 4(e) display the EIS curves of cells with and without the reforming layer under different fuel concentrations. At 700 °C, the *R*Ω values of the cells with and without the reforming layer are almost the same across all fuel concentrations. As the fuel concentration increases from 5% to 15%, *R*p decreases for both cases. The *R*p values for the cell without the reforming layer are 0.573, 0.541, and 0.521 Ω·cm2, whereas those for the cell with the reforming layer are 0.561, 0.520, and 0.515 Ω·cm2 under the same conditions. *R*p significantly decreases due to the increased fuel supply [59], which improves mass transfer and reduces concentration polarization, thus lowering *R*p. *R*p of the reforming layer cell is notably lower than that of the cell without the reforming layer, possibly because the R-NFCO reforming catalyst can effectively promote fuel conversion and increase the synthesis gas content [33]. The electrochemical performance at 20% and 30% fuel concentrations is shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM, where high fuel concentrations may lead to a reduced diffusion rate within the cell, limited mass transfer, and increased carbon deposition, which decreases electrode activity. Therefore, further study was not pursued.

To further analyze the factors influencing the impedance of different fuels, a distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis was used to distinguish the rate-limiting steps in the T-SOFC (Fig. S7 in the ESM) [59−61]. When C2H5OH–CO2 is used as the fuel, the polarization process involves four different reaction processes. The DRT peaks for the three different ethanol fuel concentrations follow a similar pattern. These peaks are labeled P4, P3, P2, P1, and P0 from high to low frequency. The P0 peak at low frequencies represents the gas diffusion process in the porous electrode. In the mid-frequency range, the appearance of the P1 peak is due to the adsorption of H on the anode surface and the adsorption of oxygen on the cathode surface. The P2 and P3 peaks correspond to the oxygen reduction processes of O2 → O− and O− → O2−, respectively. The P4 peak, located in the high-frequency range, is due to the combination of the gas adsorption process with the diffusion of oxygen ions to the three-phase boundary and the movement of gas ions within the anode [62]. Figures S7(c) and S7(d) in the ESM show a comparison of the polarization resistance values corresponding to the P0–P4 decomposition peaks. With increasing fuel concentration, the peak areas of P0–P4 gradually decrease, with the most significant change occurring in the P0 peak. This reflects that the increase in fuel concentration accelerates fuel mass transfer and reforming reactions, thereby improving performance. A comparison of the cells with and without the reforming layer shows that the resistance of the P4 process in the reforming layer cell decreases most noticeably at all three fuel concentrations. This is related to the accelerated reaction at the three-phase boundary and improved fuel mass transfer, which enhances the overall performance of the cell. Although there is a slight increase in resistance at the P1 peak, the reforming layer overall improves the cell performance at different fuel concentrations by reducing the resistance of other processes [63]. The catalytic effect of R-NFCO facilitates fuel conversion and electrochemical reactions, thus improving the cell efficiency and output power.

Figure 5(a) shows the current change of the T-SOFCs with and without an internal reforming catalyst during a long-term stability test for 100 h at 700 °C under a 10% C2H5OH–CO2 (2 : 1) atmosphere with a discharge at 0.8 V. The cell with the reforming layer maintains stable performance without degradation, whereas the cell without the reforming layer shows significant performance degradation, indicating that the reforming layer can effectively reduce the formation of carbon deposition on the anode. Figure S8 in the ESM displays the *I*‒*V*‒*P* and EIS curves of the cells with and without the reforming layer after 100 h. The open-circuit voltage of the cell without the reforming layer is significantly lower than that of the cell with the reforming layer. The maximum power density of the cell without the reforming layer is only 410 mW·cm−2, whereas that of the cell with the reforming layer is 550 mW·cm−2. After 100 h, the polarization resistance of both cells increased, which was due to carbon deposition covering the active sites on the anode, hindering gas diffusion and increasing the charge transfer resistance [64]. However, the impedance of the cell without the reforming layer is greater than that of the cell with the reforming layer because the cell without the reforming layer is more susceptible to carbon deposition due to the lack of effective catalytic material for fuel conversion. Carbon deposition can cover the active sites and obstruct gas diffusion, whereas the reforming layer provides better catalytic activity, helping to maintain efficient fuel conversion, reduce carbon deposition, and minimize other side reactions, thereby maintaining a lower cell impedance. Figure 5(b) shows the Raman spectra of carbon deposition on the anode side of the cells with and without the reforming layer under a 10% C2H5OH–CO2 (2 : 1) atmosphere. The D peak represents amorphous carbon, and the G peak corresponds to graphitic carbon. In carbon deposition analysis, the peak area ratio typically refers to the ratio of the D peak to the G peak (*I*D/*I*G ratio) [65,66]. The fitted calculations reveal that the *I*D/*I*G ratio for the cell without the reforming layer is greater than that of the cell with the reforming layer. This indicates that there is more amorphous carbon in the anode of the cell without the reforming layer, suggesting that more carbon deposition has occurred, further demonstrating that the R-NFCO reforming layer material can effectively prevent carbon deposition.

To better analyze the catalytic reactions occurring within the T-SOFC, gas chromatographs were used to measure the exhaust gas composition and content collected under long-term discharge at a 10% C2H5OH–CO2 concentration. Figures 5(c) and 5(d) show that during long-term discharge at 0.8 V, the exhaust gas composition of the cell without the reforming layer differed significantly from that of the cell with the reforming layer. The CO2 content is much greater in the cell without the reforming layer because the CO2 in the cell without the reforming layer cannot effectively participate in the reforming reaction. Instead, it participates in carbon deposition, which covers the active sites. Additionally, excessive CO2 reduces the concentration of active species, leading to a decrease in cell performance. The H2 and CO contents in the cell without the reforming layer are much lower than those in the cell with the reforming layer because the reforming layer provides catalytically active sites on the anode, promoting the conversion of fuel (C2H5OH–CO2) to H2 and CO. The cell without the reforming layer lacks this catalytic activity, resulting in a lower conversion efficiency. During the 100 h long-term test, the H2 and CO contents in the reforming layer cell remained stable and much higher than those in the cell without the reforming layer. As time progresses, the H2 and CO contents in the latter gradually decrease, which is due to carbon deposition occurring on the electrode surface, which covers the active sites and reduces the production of H2 and CO [39]. Figures 5(e) and 5(f) show the schematic distributions of the molar concentrations of exhaust gases under simulated operating conditions. The two-dimensional model simulated via COMSOL (Fig. S9 in the ESM) shows the molar concentration distributions of H2, CO, and CO2 inside the cell with and without the reforming layer. The simulation results closely match the exhaust gas composition concentrations measured experimentally, validating the accuracy of the model. Specifically, in the cell with the reforming layer, the concentrations of H2 and CO are significantly higher, indicating that the reforming layer effectively promotes the conversion of ethanol and CO2, increasing the syngas yield and thereby enhancing the performance of the cell. In contrast, the cell without the reforming layer has a higher CO2 concentration, suggesting insufficient participation in the reaction, which can lead to carbon deposition and reduced cell performance. Through a comparison of the simulation and experimental results, we not only validated the accuracy of the model but also gained a deeper understanding of the material transport and reaction processes within the cell.

To further investigate the surface chemical properties of the NFCO catalyst before and after hydrogen reduction treatment, FTIR analysis was performed, and the results are shown in Fig. 6(a). A broad peak appears at approximately 3415 cm−1, which corresponds primarily to the stretching vibration of surface-adsorbed water molecules or hydroxyl groups (–OH) [67]. These findings indicate that the NFCO surface has high hydrophilicity and hydroxyl formation ability. Water (or hydroxyl species) on the NFCO catalyst can participate in the internal reforming process of hydrocarbons, which contributes to its stability and carbon tolerance in an ethanol-carbon dioxide atmosphere. After hydrogen reduction, the –OH peak of the R-NFCO sample weakens, which is related to changes in the surface structure during reduction, such as the formation of oxygen vacancies or the reduction of metal species. These changes can affect the interaction between the surface and water molecules. The experiment also indicates that the hydration capability of NFCO is sufficient to maintain stable operation of the fuel cell under ethanol-carbon dioxide fuel.

As shown in Fig. 6(b) and Fig. S10 in the ESM, through DFT calculations, the adsorption energy of H2O and the differential charge density for different CeO2-based catalysts were further analyzed. InCeO2, the adsorption energy of H2O is the lowest among the four materials, indicating that it has the weakest ability to adsorb H2O. The introduction of oxygen vacancies in CeO2-Ov results in an increase in the adsorption energy to 0.58 eV, which is attributed to the additional adsorption sites provided by the oxygen vacancies. The introduction of the NiFe alloy significantly increased the adsorption energy of H2O to 1.04 eV. In CeO2–Ov–NiFe, the adsorption energy is slightly lower than that of CeO2–NiFe, at 0.84 eV. Overall, the introduction of NiFe alloy significantly enhances the adsorption capacity of water molecules, facilitating the adsorption and conversion of fuel and thereby improving the fuel reforming efficiency. Moreover, the presence of oxygen vacancies further enhances the adsorption of water molecules on the CeO2 surface, helping to improve the catalyst’s carbon tolerance [36]. Calculating the differential charge density can reveal the redistribution of electrons during the adsorption process [68]. As shown in Fig. 6(c), yellow represents positive charges, and blue represents negative charges. The NiFe alloy is able to transfer more electrons, indicating that the alloy significantly enhances the adsorption of water molecules. The presence of oxygen vacancies enhances the adsorption of water molecules on the CeO2 surface but weakens the adsorption on the NiFe alloy, which is consistent with the calculated adsorption energy results.

## 4 Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the impact of the NFCO reforming layer on the electrochemical performance and stability of a T-SOFC in a low-concentration C2H5OH and CO2 atmosphere. The results show that the reforming catalyst significantly enhances the catalytic conversion efficiency of ethanol fuel, effectively increasing the yield of syngas, thereby significantly increasing the maximum power density of the tubular cell (538, 614, and 608 mW·cm−2 at 5%, 10%, and 15% fuel concentrations, respectively), which is significantly superior to that of the cells without the reforming layer. Moreover, a long-term test with 10% ethanol-carbon dioxide fuel further demonstrated the excellent stability of the tubular cell with a reforming layer. FTIR and DFT calculations revealed the surface hydrophilicity and hydroxyl formation ability of NFCO reforming materials, as well as the enhanced adsorption capacity of water molecules compared with that of CeO2. The improvement in these abilities enables the reforming catalyst to have significant resistance to carbon deposition, thereby maintaining its catalytic activity and stability. These results indicate that the NFCO reforming layer plays a crucial role in enhancing the performance and stability of T-SOFCs operating on ethanol-carbon dioxide fuel.
