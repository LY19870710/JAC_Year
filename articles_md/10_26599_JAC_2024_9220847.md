## 1 Introduction

MAX phases are layered materials with a hexagonal crystal structure consisting of early transition metal (M), group A element (A), and carbon, nitrogen, or boron (X). These materials possess strong M–X and weak M–A chemical bonding simultaneously, leading to a combination of metallic and ceramic properties that have garnered significant interest from researchers. The physical and mechanical properties of the MAX phases are particularly noteworthy, as they exhibit excellent oxidation resistance [1], good irradiation resistance [2], excellent thermal shock resistance [3], and good damage tolerance [4]. Therefore, the MAX phases have promising applications in aerospace [5], high-speed trains [6], nuclear power plants [2,7], etc. To further enhance the properties of the MAX phases, many approaches have been tried, such as strong magnetic field alignment [8,9], hot forging [10], and solid solution [11,12]. Although the method of solid solution has effectively improved the properties, according to the Gibbs phase rule, the total number of phases in the system is positively related to the number of components in the system, which is not beneficial for material design. Until 2004, the high entropy effect was discovered in the study of alloys that synthesized a single-phase product and owned the following four characteristics: high entropy effect, lattice distortion, hysteresis diffusion effect, and cocktail effect [13].

High-entropy ceramics were first demonstrated in 2015 [14], originating from high-entropy alloys, i.e., Δ*S*conf ≥ 1.5*R* for high-entropy materials and 1*R* ≤ Δ*S*conf conf is calculated through Eq. (1):

(1) Δsconf=−R∑i=1nxiln⁡xi

where *R* is the molar gas constant, *n* is the total number of elements, and *x**i* is the concentration of the *i*th element. Since the research on high-entropy carbides, oxides, borides, etc., have achieved remarkable results [15–17], similarly, the MAX phase materials are promising to be designed with high entropy as they can achieve solid solution in M-, A-, and X-sites with a considerable number of potential permutation matrix [18–23], and the realization of the MAX phase in multi-component solid solution has been achieved. However, the research on the high-entropy (five elements) MAX phase is not progressing very successfully. Unlike other high-entropy ceramics [15–17], MAX phase ceramics present more challenges due to the existence of stable competitive phases during synthesis. Moreover, the as-synthesized M*n*+1AlC*n* phase does not fit the designed molar ratio formula [24]. This issue can complicate the production of high purity high-entropy MAX phase ceramics and needs to be overcome.

Recently, the research on the high-entropy MAX phase has mainly focused on the M-site substitution [25]. Bao *et al*. [26] reported the successful synthesis of M-site high-entropy ceramics, named M2AlC–MC (M = Ti, Zr, Hf, Nb, Ta) composites. The two phases in the ceramics were approximately 55.20 and 42.25 vol% for M2AlC and MC, respectively. Due to the existence of MC, the properties were not further tested. Additionally, Tan *et al.* [27] attempted to synthesize (TiVCrNbTa)2AlC MAX phases by multiple steps of hot-pressing sintering using the commercial 211 MAX phase powders as raw materials. It was found that the addition of Cr caused the transformation from 211 phases to 413 phases, and only small amounts of Cr were found to be soluble in the M-site, consistent with previous works [24]. The lack of Cr resulted in less than 5 elements in the M site or a conformational entropy of less than 1.50*R*.

Although both M-site and A-site can be doped, the M-site substitution is of primary importance: on the one hand, the M–X and M–A bonds are the source of the unique performance of MAX phases; on the other hand, the M-site substitution can also be used as raw materials for high entropy MXene [28,29], which has a broad application. However, the M-site substituted MAX phase is affected by the impurity phase as well as the actual solid solution into the M-site elements with insufficient variety and low content, which limits the study of the intrinsic structure and properties of the high-entropy MAX phases. Therefore, the study of M-site high-entropy MAX ceramics urgently needs to address three issues: (i) to ensure the type of solid solution elements (≥ 5) and sufficient solid solution content; (ii) to improve the purity of the high-entropy MAX phase; (iii) to understand the properties of the high-entropy MAX phase ceramics. As a member of the structural material family, medium-entropy designed MAX phases have been studied [30,31]; however, research on high-entropy MAX phases has not been reported.

In this paper, the adjacent IVB and VB group elements Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, and Ta were selected as solid solution elements in the M-site. Among these, Ti, V, Nb, and Ta were fixed, while Zr and Hf were used as the fifth element. The (Ti0.2V0.2Nb0.2Ta0.2M0.2)2AlC (M = Zr, Hf) high-entropy MAX phase ceramics were *in-situ* synthesized by spark plasma sintering (SPS) using commercial elemental powders. This work employed a convenient one-step synthesis method, and the as-synthesized samples exhibited relatively high purity, with the products matching the formulation ratios. The Rietveld refinement of X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were used to characterize phase composition, lattice parameters, and microstructure of the high-entropy (TiVNbTaM)2AlC (M = Zr, Hf) ceramics. The physical and mechanical properties of the high-entropy (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics were investigated systematically. Overall, this work provides new insights into the synthesis and properties of high-entropy MAX phases and potential implications for developing advanced materials with unique properties for advanced applications, for example, structural components, protective coverings, or wear-resistant parts [31], in fields such as nuclear energy and aerospace.

### 2.1 Sample preparation

Commercial Nb, Ti, Ta, V, Hf, Zr, Al, and graphite powders (99.9% purity, 500 mesh, ENO High-Tech Material Development Co., Ltd., China) were mixed by ball milling at 50 r/min for 10 h. The ball-to-material ratio employed was 2 : 1, approximately occupying half of the volume within the milling tank. Agate beads were chosen as abrasive materials for this milling process, employing a ratio of eight large beads to two small beads. Furthermore, the milling tank was filled with argon to maintain an inert atmosphere throughout the process. The composition of the powders was designed to follow the molar ratio of Ti : V : Nb : Ta : M : Al : C = 0.4 : 0.4 : 0.4 : 0.4 : 0.4 : 1.1 : 0.95 (M = Zr, Hf). Excess aluminum was added as it tended to volatilize at high temperatures. The amount of graphite was intentionally kept low because C-defects could stabilize most of the aluminum-containing MAX phases [32]. The mixed precursor was consolidated in a spark plasma sintering (SPS) furnace (SPS-20T-10, Chenhua Technology Co., Ltd., China) with a heating rate of 50 ℃∙min−1 under 30 MPa in a flowing argon atmosphere. After dwelling for 20 min at 1350 ℃, the specimen was naturally cooled down to ambient temperature. Finally, the surface contamination from the graphite foil was removed by using a diamond abrasive disc.

### 2.2 Characterization

The phase compositions of the (TiVNbTaM)2AlC (M = Zr, Hf) samples were analyzed by using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 ADVANCE A25X, Bruker, Germany). Archimede’s method was employed to determine the density of the samples. The fracture and etched surfaces of the synthesized samples were observed by a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Inspect F50, FEI, USA). For the measurement of the grain size, over 100 grains were counted.

The electrical conductivity was measured using a four-probe method at ambient temperature by an FT-300 series resistivity tester (FT-300A1, Ningbo Rooko Instrument Co., Ltd., China). The dimension of the specimens was 1 mm × 1 mm × 10 mm. The coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of the samples was measured using a thermal expansion analyzer (NETZSCH DIL 402C, Selb, Germany). The measurements were conducted in an argon atmosphere with a heating rate of 10 ℃/min. The temperature range spanned from room temperature to 1200 ℃. CTE was determined using a bar specimen with a dimension of 4 mm × 4 mm × 15 mm. The thermal conductivity (*λ*) of the samples was calculated using Eq. (2):

(2) λ=a⋅cp⋅ρ

where *a* represents the thermal diffusivity (mm2∙s−1), *c**p* is the heat capacity (J·g−1·K−1), and *ρ* is the density (g∙cm−3). The measurements were carried out using a column specimen with the dimension of *ϕ*12.5 mm × 2 mm, employing a laser thermal conductivity meter (NETZSCH LFA467, Selb, Germany).

Vickers hardness was measured by a Vickers hardness tester (HVS-50, Lianer Co., China) at loads of 10–500 N with dwell time of 15 s. The specimens were machined perpendicular to the pressure direction and tested for flexural strength, fracture toughness, and compressive strength. The flexural strength was measured by a three-point bending method. The dimension of the specimens was 1.5 mm × 2 mm × 18 mm. The fracture toughness was measured using a single-edge notched beam method (SENB). The specimen size was 2 mm × 4 mm × 18 mm, and the notch was 2 mm in length and 0.2 mm in width. The dimension of specimens for compressive strength testing was 2 mm × 2 mm × 4 mm. The flexural strength, fracture toughness, and compressive strength were tested using an electronic universal testing machine (YC-100KN, Oneyice Co., China). The crosshead speeds adopted for flexural strength, fracture toughness, and compressive strength testing were 0.5, 0.05, and 0.5 mm∙min−1, respectively. The span for testing flexural strength and fracture toughness was 16 mm.

### 2.3 Theoretical calculation

First-principles calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) [33] based on density functional theory (DFT). The projector augmented wave (PAW) approach [34] was used to describe the ion-electron interactions. The exchange-correlation interactions were modeled by the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) form [35]. Moreover, the cutoff energy has been taken as 500 eV after the convergence test. The convergence criteria were taken to be 10−5 eV and 0.03 eV∙Å−1 accordingly for energy and force. A 1 × 1 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack *k*-point mesh was set. Since the MAX phase is a ternary layered structure, the van der Waals force is involved through the Grimme-D3 scheme [36] to correctly address the interactions between the layers. For high entropy MAX phases, a 5 × 4 × 1 supercell containing 160 atoms was used, and a supercell of the rocksalt structure containing 200 atoms was used. The chemical disorder in the ceramic materials was modeled using special quasirandom structures (SQS) with Monte–Carlo simulated annealing algorithm [37].

The XRD patterns of (TiVNbTaM)2AlC (M = Zr, Hf) ceramics indicated a single series of diffraction peaks, which were similar to those of single-component M2AlC (M = Ti, V, Nb, Ta) phases. It is seen that (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics were a single phase, while the (TiVNbTaHf)2AlC ceramics contained a few impurities of carbides.

The density functional theory (DFT) has been widely used in research on the MAX phases [46–48]. To compare the propensity of Zr and Hf systems to form carbides, the formation enthalpies of the products of the Zr and Hf systems were calculated. The formula of carbides obtained by EDS is listed in Table 3. The structure models of the high-entropy MAX phase and the entropy-enhanced carbides are shown in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b), respectively. The formation enthalpies of (TiVNbTaM)2AlC (M = Zr, Hf) and (Ti0.12Nb0.12Ta0.21M0.55)C (M = Zr, Hf) carbides were quantized by the equation: * ΔH=(Hcrystal−lHM−mHA−nHX)/(l+m+n), where M, A, and X are composite elements, and l*, *m*, and *n* are the number of elements in the unit cell. *H*crystal is the enthalpy of the unit cell, and *H*M, *H*A, and *H*X are accordingly the enthalpy of each composite atom in the bulk phase. The result indicated that both high-entropy MAX phase and entropy-enhanced carbides have negative production enthalpies, indicating that all products could be synthesized. The formation energy of (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC and (TiVNbTaHf)2AlC are close to −0.602 and −0.609 eV/atom, respectively. However, the production enthalpy of (Ti0.12Nb0.12Ta0.21Hf0.55)C at −0.859 eV/atom is lower than that of −0.801 eV/atom for (Ti0.12Nb0.12Ta0.21Zr0.55)C. It means that (Ti0.12Nb0.12Ta0.21Hf0.55)C is easier to produce in the system. This could be a contributing factor that why carbides were more likely to produce during the synthesis of (TiVNbTaHf)2AlC.

The density of (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC and (TiVNbTaHf)2AlC carbide bulks were measured using Archimede’s method. The density values obtained were 6.50 and 7.44 g∙cm−3, respectively. It was determined that (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC has the theoretical density of 6.74 g∙cm−3. Therefore, the synthesized (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC bulk has the high relative density of 96.44%.

In the present research, the entropies of the (TiVNbTaM)2AlC (M = Zr, Hf) ceramics were calculated to be 1.577R* and 1.577*R*, respectively, exceeding the high-entropy criterion value of 1.50*R* and exhibiting the success of high entropy configuration. It is crucial to emphasize that (TiVNbTaHf)2AlC–MC represents a novel high-entropy MAX phase composite with significant research potential, such as in moissanite studies and ferromagnetic behavior [49,50]. However, given the specific focus of this paper, an in-depth exploration of the properties of high-entropy composites is not within the scope of the present investigation.

To further insights into the physical and mechanical properties of high purity (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics, here, five (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC bulks were prepared for systematical characterization.

### 3.2 Physical properties

Table 4 presents the room-temperature electrical conductivity of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics. For comparison, the electrical conductivities of single-component M2AlC (M = Ti, V, Nb, and Ta) ceramics were also listed [40,51–53]. The heat capacity increases from 0.30 to 0.53 J·g−1·K−1 in the temperature range of 25–1200 ℃, and the thermal conductivity of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics increases from 8.98 to 23.02 W·m−1·K−1 in the temperature range of 25–1200 ℃. To comprehend the differences in the electronic thermal conductivity between the high-entropy MAX phase and single-component MAX phase at room temperature, the electronic thermal conductivity of both high-entropy (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics and four single-component MAX phase ceramics was presented in Fig. 7. Notably, the high-entropy (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics exhibit significantly lower electronic thermal conductivity of 7.00 W·m−1·K−1 compared to single-component MAX phases. This reduction could be attributed to the diminished mobility of electron carriers, primarily limited by scattering events arising from non-periodic crystal structures, such as short-range disorder and crystal defects caused by the solid solution of multi-elements at the M-site. This phenomenon holds particular significance within the context of high-entropy materials [60,61]. Furthermore, the extra-nuclear electrons of the transition metal might also impact carrier mobility according to Motto’s theory [62,63]. Additionally, the refinement of grain size induces a great increase in the number of grain boundaries, contributing to an overall decrease in thermal conductivity [64]. In summary, the high-entropy (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics exhibit lower phonon and electron thermal conductivity, highlighting their unique thermal properties in comparison with the traditional MAX phases.

### 3.3 Mechanical properties

Figure 8 displays the Vickers hardness of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics as a function of the testing load ranging from 10 to 500 N. The inset SEM image is the indent induced at the load of 100 N. It is seen that the hardness value decreases from 9.80 to 7.50 GPa with the increasing load from 10 to 500 N. The decreasing tendency is following the common indentation-size effect (ISE) [40]. Notably, the Vickers hardness of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics is significantly higher than those of single-component MAX phases from 4.2 to 6.1 GPa at a load of 10 N. In addition, it is observed that in the indent of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics, there are no cracks at the diagonals except the damage modes of grains pushing-out and delamination, exhibiting the good damage tolerance [42,65].

Table 4 lists the typical physical and mechanical properties of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics compared to those of single-component MAX phases. It is seen that the flexural strength of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics is 507 MPa, higher than those of single-component MAX ceramics, from 384 to 413 MPa. The compressive strength of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics is 1364 MPa, greatly higher than those of single-component MAX ceramics, from 670 to 804 MPa. Here, the fracture toughness of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics (5.62 MPa·m1/2) is lower than those of single-component MAX phases (5.70–7.70 MPa·m1/2). It seems that the high entropy design of MAX phases is the same as carbides which also present higher hardness and flexural strength but lower fracture toughness [66,67]. The improvement in the mechanical performance of the high-entropy (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics stems from multiple factors. Firstly, as shown in Fig. 3(c), the high-entropy (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics have smaller grains compared with the single-component MAX phase [40,42,51,52], resulting in the formation of more grain boundaries that effectively impede crack propagation [68], which is beneficial for enhancing hardness, flexural strength, and compressive strength. Additionally, according to the size difference formula [68]:  δsize=∑i=1nxi(1−ri/r)2 where n* is the total number of elements, *x**i* is the concentration of the *i*th element, *r**i* is the radius of the *i*th component element, and *r* is the average covalent radius, the size difference for the high-entropy (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics could reach 5.17%, which means that the solid solution of five transition elements with different radii at the M-site induces significant lattice distortion, further augmenting the strength of ceramics. Furthermore, researchers have observed a substantial presence of dislocations in the high-entropy ceramics, the existence of these dislocations is likely associated with the enhanced hardness and strength, potentially involving the pinning effect of dislocations on the slip systems [69,70].

Part of the typical physical and mechanical properties of multiple-component 211 MAX phases was presented in Fig. 9 to highlight the effect of the entropy-enhanced design. Figure 9(a) presents the thermal conductivity of (TiNb)2AlC, (TiVNb)2AlC, (TiVNbTa)2AlC, and (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics. The thermal conductivity of the high-entropy (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics was notably low as 8.98 W·m−1·K−1. According to the previous literature [68], the lattice distortion, defects, and multiple transition metal element solid solution caused the electron and phonon scattering as the main reason for reducing the thermal conductivity of the MAX phases. The calculations reveal that the high-entropy (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics exhibit the highest lattice distortion, reaching 5.17%, surpassing 4.12% of (TiVNb)2AlC [63], and 3.81% of (TiVNbTa)2AlC [68]. Regarding mechanical properties which are illustrated in Fig. 9(b), owing to the combined influence of fine-grain strengthening and lattice distortion, the high-entropy (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics demonstrate the highest hardness of 9.80 GPa, and the flexural strength of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics is 507 MPa, higher than 481 MPa of (TiNbTa)2AlC [27] and 438 MPa of the (TiVNbTa)2AlC [66] ceramics.

## 4 Conclusions

High entropy (TiVNbTaM)2AlC (M = Zr, Hf) ceramics were successfully synthesized by using the *in-situ* reaction of element powders through spark plasma sintering. The refined lattice parameters and XRD data of (TiVNbTaM)2AlC (M = Zr, Hf) ceramics were clearly determined. The calculated entropies of (TiVNbTaM)2AlC (M = Zr, Hf) ceramics were 1.577*R* and 1.577*R*, respectively. It was found that the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics had high purity and was suitable for property characterization.

(1) The measured electrical conductivity of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics was 0.96×106 Ω−1·m−1 at 25 ℃. The thermal expansion coefficient of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics was determined to be 3.65×10−6 K−1 in the temperature range of 25–1200 ℃. The heat capacity and thermal conductivity of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics were 0.30 J·g−1·K−1 and 8.98 W·m−1·K−1, respectively, at 25 ℃.

(2) In comparison with single-component M2AlC MAX phases, the Vickers hardness of (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC was higher to be 9.80 GPa at a load of 10 N, the flexural strength of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics was 507 MPa, and the compressive strength of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics was 1364 MPa, indicating the remarkable hardening-strengthening effect. Whereas, the fracture toughness of the (TiVNbTaZr)2AlC ceramics showed a low value of 5.62 MPa·m1/2.

(3) The hardening and strengthening effects were ascribed to that the high entropy configuration was beneficial for forming ultrahigh-density dislocations that could strongly pin the dislocations and impede the crystal plane slipping.
