## 1 Introduction

It is well-accepted that many of the intriguing properties of nanomaterials are attributed to their nanometric grains (typically 1–100 nm). However, nanograins are a double-edged sword. On the one hand, nanograins endow nanomaterials with advantageous properties, whereas on the other hand, nanograins typically suffer from severe grain coarsening, which compromises the nanoscale properties [1−3]. The large driving force for coarsening of nanograins can be ascribed to the fact that the surface-to-volume ratio of grains rapidly increases with decreasing grain size, resulting in a large percentage of atoms being located at grain boundaries (GBs). GBs typically show less ordered atomic arrangements compared to the region within grains; therefore, GBs are in a high-energy state. Hence, tailoring the microstructure and composition of GBs is the key to improving the thermal stability of nanograins.

Nearly two decades ago, material scientists realized that in a broader context, GBs can be considered as “two-dimensional (2D) interfacial phases” that have their own atomic microstructure and composition. The 2D interfacial phases can undergo first-order or continuous transitions, driven by variations in thermodynamic potentials such as temperature, pressure, and chemical potentials. It is important to note that these 2D interfacial phases cannot exist independently and require adjacent bulk phases for stability. The term “complexion” has been proposed to describe these special 2D GB phases [4,5]. Dillon *et al*. [4] first observed six discrete GB complexions in doped Al2O3 ceramics. Since then, different GB complexions have been observed in a wide range of ceramics, such as Si3N4 [6], WC [7], SrTiO3 [8], ZrO2 [9], SiC [6], and more [10]. The formation and transition of GB complexions can cause abrupt changes in the chemistry and properties of GBs, thereby critically influencing the sintering process, grain growth behavior, and many other boundary-sensitive properties of ceramics [11−13]. For example, Al2O3 ceramics may have different GB complexions depending on the added dopants and processing temperature [14,15]. Ordered GB complexions can be formed in Mg-doped Al2O3 ceramics, which could hinder grain coarsening [4], whereas other dopants, such as Si and Ca, could promote the formation of disordered GB complexions that result in enhanced kinetics and abnormal grain growth [15,16]. Most of the results reported so far only investigated the effects of GB complexions with only one or two dopants. Zhou *et al*. [17] proposed that high-entropy GB complexions can be formed by multi-element codoping and that these complexions can inhibit grain coarsening by significantly reducing the GB energy. However, they did not report direct experimental observations of high-entropy GB complexions. The influences of multi-element codoping on GB complexions remain unclear, and the hidden potential of GB complexions for inhibiting grain coarsening has yet to be explored.

To date, ceramic scientists have devised various strategies to impede grain coarsening. The utilization of nano-sized precursor powder can not only facilitate the densification process but also yield bulk ceramics with reduced grain sizes compared with those of micron-sized precursor powder [18]. Rapid sintering passes through the low-temperature surface diffusion stage and directly enters the high-temperature sintering stage through rapid heating, rendering it an effective way to inhibit grain coarsening [19,20]. However, these aforementioned strategies fail to prevent coarsening during the application of nano-ceramics in medium- or high-temperature environments. The introduction of a secondary phase can also enhance the thermal stability of nanograins. By pinning GBs and reducing their mobility, the secondary phase effectively inhibits coarsening [21,22]. However, it is challenging to achieve a uniform distribution of secondary phases, which may have detrimental effects on other properties. Solute segregation at GBs has been shown to reduce GB energy and weaken the driving force of grain growth [23,24]. Nevertheless, most reported studies have only investigated the addition of one or two types of solute atoms [23−25], limiting the extent of the GB energy reduction. Therefore, further exploration is needed to fully understand and harness the potential of solute segregation as a strategy.

In this study, we proposed an effective strategy to inhibit grain coarsening by constructing GB complexions with multiple dopants co-segregated. From a thermodynamics viewpoint, the energy of GB complexions can be reduced due to dopant co-segregation, resulting in a smaller driving force for coarsening [23,26]. From a kinetic viewpoint, multiple dopants located at GB complexions reduce the mobility of GBs through a solute drag effect [27], analogous to sluggish diffusion in high-entropy materials [28]. To verify the feasibility of this strategy, we doped five dopants in a ZrO2–SiO2 nanocrystalline glass ceramic (NCGC). The selected dopants co-segregated at GBs between ZrO2 NCs, forming GB complexions. The grain coarsening effect of the tailored GB complexion was verified during annealing.

The second important goal of this study is to obtain atomic-scale, three-dimensional (3D) experimental identification and quantification of GB complexions since these methods are fundamental to the manipulation and utilization of GB complexions. The advancements in transmission electron microscopy (TEM) techniques have made obtaining atomic-scale microstructure and composition information about GB complexions routine [29,30]. However, one obvious limitation of TEM techniques is that they are projection-based techniques and can only provide 2D information, which makes GB complexions far from well understood because of their structural and compositional complexity. Atom probe tomography (APT) is a powerful technique that provides 3D elemental mapping with nearly atomic resolution [31]. In this study, with the help of laser-pulsed APT and by optimizing the instrumental variables (laser pulse energy, base temperature, etc.), we obtained full 3D reconstruction of GB complexions on the atomic scale that were not readily visible previously. In addition, we characterized the GB complexions by scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). The underlying mechanisms of the effects of GB complexion on coarsening inhibition are discussed.

### 2.1 Synthesis of raw powder

The doped raw powder was synthesized by a sol–gel method. Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) and zirconium n-propoxide (Zr(OPr)4) solutions (both from Aladdin Industrial Co., China) were utilized as the precursors of SiO2 and ZrO2, respectively. A diluted HCl solution (0.4 mol/L), TEOS, and ethanol were mixed to initiate the hydrolysis of TEOS, followed by the hydrolysis of Zr(OPr)4 with a concentrated HCl solution (12 mol/L) as a catalyst. The volume ratio between TEOS and Zr(OPr)4 was varied to obtain 65% ZrO2–35% SiO2 (molar ratio) sol. The hydrolyzed TEOS and Zr(OPr)4 were mixed to form clear sol. Reagent-grade chloride salts, including AlCl3, CaCl2, LaCl3, CsCl, BaCl2, and YCl3 (Aladdin Industrial Co., China), were used as raw materials for the doping of Al, Ca, La, Cs, Ba, and Y, respectively. Equimolar amounts of the above chloride salts were dissolved in the sol. The doping concentration relative to the total molar content of 65% ZrO2–35% SiO2 was set to 2%. For instance, 0.02 mol of AlCl3, 0.02 mol of CaCl2, 0.02 mol of LaCl3, 0.02 mol of CsCl, 0.02 mol of BaCl2, and 0.02 mol of YCl3 were added to 1 mol of 65% ZrO2–35% SiO2 sol. After gelation at 60 °C for a few hours, stiff gel was obtained, followed by drying at 120 °C for 12 h. Fine powder was obtained after planetary ball milling and sieving, followed by calcination at 600 °C for 1 h in a muffle furnace to remove the residual organic matter.

### 2.2 Fast hot pressing and annealing treatments

The raw powder was consolidated by a direct current electric field-assisted fast hot-pressing instrument (FHP 828, HAATN Technology Co., Ltd., China) to obtain densified NCGCs. The powder was loaded in a graphite die and prepressed at a pressure of 15 MPa. The FHP instrument started to detect the temperature at 700 °C. The sample was heated from 700 to 800 °C within 1 min, followed by heating at 800 °C for 2 min, during which the pressure was gradually increased to 50 MPa. After dwelling, the sample was heated from 800 to 1100 °C at a heating rate of 100 °C/min. Then, the sintering temperature was further increased from 1100 to 1230 °C at a heating rate of 50 °C/min. The sample was incubated at 1230 °C for 4 min. After cooling to room temperature within the instrument, the sintered samples were polished to remove the covered graphite sheet. Annealing treatments of the sintered samples were conducted in a muffle furnace. Three dwelling temperature points were selected, namely, 800, 1000, and 1200 °C. The dwelling time was 4 h for each dwelling temperature. After dwelling, the samples were cooled to room temperature within the furnace. The samples with a composition of 65% ZrO2–35% SiO2 (molar ratio) without doping were prepared and regarded as the control group. The powder synthesis, sintering, and annealing processes were identical to those of the doped samples. The diameter and thickness of the sintered samples were 20 and 2 mm, respectively. To study the effect of annealing duration on grain coarsening, the undoped control group and the co-doped sample were also annealed at 1000 °C for 8 and 12 h.

## 3 Material design concept and dopant selection

The construction of the GB complexion by a multiple dopant co-segregation strategy is schematically demonstrated in Fig. 1(a). We carefully selected six dopants and doped them into an NCGC. Among NCGCs with varying ZrO2 contents, the 65 mol% ZrO2–35 mol% SiO2 NCGC exhibited the highest flexural strength at room temperature [34]. Therefore, it exhibits significant potential for practical applications. If the coarsening of ZrO2 NCs can be effectively suppressed through the GB complexion strategy, the application of NCGC could be extended to medium- or even high-temperature environments. This serves as a crucial motivation behind our selection of 65 mol% ZrO2–35 mol% SiO2 NCGC for investigation. Theoretically, the selected dopants should show a strong segregation tendency at GBs between adjacent ZrO2 NCs. We anticipate that the dopants co-segregate at GBs, forming a 2D GB complexion. Investigating whether the GB complexion is a crystalline superstructure or an intergranular glass film (IGF) is interesting and of great scientific significance. The dopant selection criteria are shown in Fig. 1(b). It has been proven that the GB segregation is largely affected by the ionic radii and valence states of both dopant cations and the host cation [35]. Dopant ions that have a large ionic radius mismatch (*ε*) with the host Zr4+ ion (84.0 pm) are pushed to GBs instead of dissolving in the ZrO2 lattice. The mismatch (*ε*) is calculated as *ε* = (*r*h − *r*d)/*r*h × 100%, where *r*h and *r*d are the ionic radii of the host ion and the dopant ion, respectively. In addition, ZrO2 GBs are positively charged, and dopant ions with valence states lower than +4 can form a negative space charge cloud at GBs, which could also enhance the GB segregation tendency of the dopant ions [24,30]. Considering the above two criteria, we selected five dopant ions, i.e., Cs+, Ba2+, La3+, Ca2+, and Al3+ (Fig. 1(b)). They all had large *ε* with the host Zr4+ ion, and their valence states were lower than +4. Hence, theoretically, they should co-segregate at ZrO2 GBs. Y3+ dopant was also added, mainly to stabilize ZrO2 in the tetragonal phase, since the intense tetragonal-to-monoclinic phase transformation might result in fracture of the samples during cooling.

In this study, for the following three reasons, we selected glass-ceramics rather than polycrystalline ceramics (PCs) without a glass phase to study the effects of GB complexion. First, it is challenging to achieve 100% densification in PCs. The presence of residual nanopores located at GBs hindered grain coarsening through a pinning effect, which made it difficult to evaluate the effects of the GB complexion. In comparison, ZrO2–SiO2 NCGCs were fully densified in this study, avoiding the above problem. Second, there were two types of interfaces in ZrO2–SiO2 NCGCs, i.e., ZrO2 GBs and ZrO2/SiO2 heterointerfaces. It would be interesting to investigate the effects of multiple dopants on heterointerfaces. Third, it was not easy to prepare bulk polycrystalline ceramics with grain sizes smaller than 200 nm. Ultrafine powder and special sintering techniques (e.g., ultrahigh-pressure spark plasma sintering) are typically needed. However, the sizes of ZrO2 NCs in the nondoped and doped NCGCs were 40.8 nm (Fig. 8) and 193.5 nm (Fig. 9), respectively. No ultrafine powder or special sintering technique was used. Smaller grains have a larger coarsening driving force. Compared with polycrystalline ceramics with micron or submicron grains, GCs with grains smaller than 200 nm were more suitable for evaluating the effects of GB complexion.

### 4.1 XRD measurements and STEM observations

The sample was predominantly composed of tetragonal ZrO2 (t-ZrO2) and monoclinic ZrO2 (m-ZrO2) (Fig. 2). Weak diffraction peaks of quartz were detected, indicating that a small amount of quartz was also formed. The sample contained 35 mol% SiO2, but only a small amount of quartz was formed. Hence, the majority of the SiO2 in the sintered sample was amorphous, which was in accordance with our previous results [36,37]. No crystalline phase(s) containing dopants (Cs, Ba, La, Ca, Al, or Y) was detected (Fig. 2).

The microstructure of the sample was characterized by STEM. In the BF-STEM image (Fig. 3(a)), many ZrO2 NCs with a spherical morphology and dark contrast were observed. Their sizes ranged from 100 to 200 nm. The amorphous SiO2 component showed a bright contrast, and ZrO2 NCs were distributed in the SiO2 matrix. In addition to ZrO2 NCs and SiO2 matrix, a third phase with a dark contrast was also formed, as indicated by the yellow arrows (Fig. 3(a)). They did not show a specific morphology; instead, they adhered to the periphery of ZrO2 NCs and bridged the adjacent NCs. These phases were termed the “bridging phase”. The bridging phase connecting adjacent ZrO2 NCs can potentially serve as either a channel or a barrier for the coarsening of ZrO2 NCs. Therefore, a comprehensive investigation into the microstructure and chemical composition of the bridging phase is warranted. The existence and morphology of the bridging phase can be more clearly distinguished in the HAADF-STEM image (yellow arrows, Fig. 3(b)). The bridging phase showed a bright contrast, which was similar to that of ZrO2 NCs, indicating that the average atomic number (Z*) of the bridging phase was close to that of ZrO2 since the image contrast is proportional to *Z*2 in the STEM-HAADF images. In contrast, the amorphous SiO2 matrix appeared to have a dark contrast (Fig. 3(b)). Interestingly, some of ZrO2 NCs showed alternating stripe-shaped contrast, and the thickness of the stripe was ~20 nm (Fig. 3(c)). The selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (insert of Fig. 3(c)) of the ZrO2 NC sample, marked by the dashed purple circle, showed that an alternating stripe-shaped contrast was formed because of twinning. As shown in the HR-TEM image (Fig. 3(d)), the twin boundary between two adjacent lamellar nanotwins can be clearly observed, as indicated by the dashed white line. The formation of nanotwins could be ascribed to the tetragonal to monoclinic phase transformation that occurred in ZrO2 NCs [38].

The characteristics of the bridging phase were characterized in detail. The bridging phase connected or partially covered ZrO2 NCs (Fig. 4(a)). The bridging phases in three representative regions (R1, R2, and R3) were characterized with HR-TEM. In R1, the boundary between ZrO2 NC and the bridging phase is outlined by the dashed purple line in Fig. 4(b). Lattice fringes can be clearly observed in the bridging phase, and the SAED pattern of the bridging phase showed organized diffraction spots, indicating that the bridging phase was a crystalline phase (Fig. 4(b)). In R2, the bridging phase was located between the amorphous SiO2 matrix and ZrO2 NCs (Fig. 4(c)), while in R3, the bridging phase resided between two adjacent ZrO2 NCs, and its thickness was ~10 nm (Fig. 4(d)). ### 4.2 APT analysis

Although the above STEM results revealed the microstructure and composition of the GB complexion, there are two limitations. First, the amorphous SiO2 acted as a matrix (Fig. 3), making it difficult to confirm that the EDX signals of Si and O originated from the GB complexion (Fig. 6(d)) rather than from the matrix. Second, the STEM results revealed only 2D information about the GB complexion. The lack of 3D morphology does not allow for a comprehensive understanding of GB complexion. To solve the above two issues, APT analysis was conducted. It is challenging to target the GB complexion when preparing nanometer-sized needle specimens with FIB. In this study, an immersion mode, i.e., a magnetic field between the SEM column and the sample, was used to improve the image resolution and better identify GBs between ZrO2 NCs. This allowed the targeted GBs to be more accurately positioned into the apex of the final sharpened specimen (Fig. S1 in the ESM).

Monatomic and molecular ions of the host elements (Zr, Si, and O) and dopants (Al, Y, Ca, La, Ba, and Cs) were identified in the mass spectrum (Fig. 7(a)). Hf is a common impurity of Zr(OPr)4 solution; hence, Hf molecular ions were also detected. The 3D ion map of Zr (purple dots) shows the targeted GBs between two adjacent ZrO2 NCs (left panel of Fig. 7(b)). From the 3D ion map of Si (red dots, right panel of Fig. 7(b)), it can be clearly seen that Si was enriched in the GB complexion. The dataset also captured a small portion of the bridging phase (~23 at% Si) on the top of the map. The videos (Videos S1–S9 in the ESM) demonstrated the 3D distribution of the constituting elements. Note that Cs was not enriched enough to be resolved from the mass spectrum in this region. Therefore, it is not possible to create an ion map for Cs. The videos of the significantly segregated elements, including Y (Video S2 in the ESM), Ca (Video S3 in the ESM), La (Video S5 in the ESM), and Si (Videos S7 and S9 in the ESM), showed that the GB complexion had a 3D flake-like morphology, although it is important to note that visualization will vary based on the 3D grid parameters and selected isosurface threshold. To the author’s knowledge, this is the first time that the morphology of a GB complexion is experimentally visualized in a 3D space.

### 4.3 Grain coarsening behaviors

To investigate the effects of the bridging phase and GB complexion on the grain coarsening behavior of ZrO2 NCs, the multi-element co-doped and undoped 65% ZrO2–35% SiO2 samples were annealed at different temperatures, with the undoped samples serving as the control group. Annealing at 800 and 1100 °C did not significantly alter the phase composition of the undoped sample, while most of the t-ZrO2 phase transformed to m-ZrO2 after annealing at 1200 °C (Fig. S5(a) in the ESM); moreover, a small amount of cristobalite formed. The multi-element co-doped samples showed similar phase composition evolution during annealing, except that a small amount of cristobalite and quartz formed after annealing at 1200 °C (Fig. S5(b) in the ESM). m-ZrO2 disappeared in the multi-element co-doped sample after annealing at 1000 °C, while it remained in the control sample (Fig. S5 in the ESM). Due to rapid sintering, ZrO2 NCs in the as-sintered samples were metastable. During annealing, the phase transformation and coarsening of ZrO2 NCs occurred simultaneously. The phase transformation during annealing was complicated because both t-to-m and reverse m-to-t transformations occurred simultaneously [2].

The grain coarsening behaviors during annealing were investigated by measuring the sizes of ZrO2 NCs. Typical SEM images of the control group are displayed in Fig. 8. The SiO2 matrix was removed by etching, exposing ZrO2 NCs. ZrO2 NCs had a near-spherical morphology in the as-sintered sample, and the sizes of NCs exhibited a Gaussian distribution, with an average value of 40.8 nm (Fig. 8(a)). After annealing at 800, 1000, and 1200 °C, significant coarsening of NCs occurred (Fig. S6(a) in the ESM), with their sizes increasing to 49.9 nm (Fig. 8(b) in the ESM), 50.4 nm (Fig. 8(c)), and 88.8 nm (Fig. 8(d)), respectively. Compared with the sizes of ZrO2 NCs in the as-sintered sample, those in the 800 °C- and 1000 °C-annealed samples increased by ~25%, while those in the 1200 °C-annealed sample increased by 117.6%, indicating that ZrO2 NCs showed very strong coarsening kinetics.

Typical SEM images of the multi-element co-doped samples are shown in Fig. 9. ZrO2 NCs also showed spherical morphologies. The average size of the as-sintered samples was 193.5 nm, which was much larger than that of the as-sintered control group. This could be because a eutectic phase with a low melting point was formed due to the addition of multiple dopants, resulting in the occurrence of liquid phase sintering [43,44]. The occurrence of liquid phase sintering can be verified by comparing the densification curves of the undoped sample and the multi-element co-doped sample (Fig. S7 in the ESM). The high-temperature liquid phase significantly enhanced mass transport and GB mobility during sintering to accelerate coarsening of ZrO2 NCs. In comparison, the undoped ZrO2–SiO2 samples processed by solid-state sintering exhibited much lower mass transport and GB mobility than the multi-element co-doped samples. Hence, ZrO2 NCs in the as-sintered multi-element co-doped samples were much larger than those in the as-sintered undoped samples. Interestingly, the average sizes of ZrO2 NCs in the 800 °C- and 1000 °C-annealed samples were 199.7 nm (Fig. 9(b)) and 195.5 nm (Fig. 9(c)), respectively, which were not significantly different from the sizes of the as-sintered sample (Fig. S6(b) in the ESM). This meant that ZrO2 NCs in the multi-element co-doped samples exhibited high thermal stability and strong coarsening resistance up to 1000 °C.

After annealing at 1200 °C, the average size increased to 231.9 nm (Fig. 9(d)), which was significantly different from the values of the as-sintered, 800 °C- and 1000 °C-annealed samples (Fig. S6(b) in the ESM), indicating that coarsening of ZrO2 NCs occurred at 1200 °C. Compared with the sizes of ZrO2 NCs in the as-sintered sample, those in the 1200 °C annealed sample increased by only 19.8%, which was much smaller than that of the control group (117.6%). The formation of a liquid phase during annealing remains uncertain. The highest annealing temperature reached 1200 °C, which was higher than the estimated liquid phase formation temperature (~1020 °C, Fig. S7 in the ESM), but no external pressure was applied during annealing. In contrast, sintering involved an applied external pressure of 50 MPa, which could result in higher pressures between powder particles and lower formation temperatures of the liquid phase. Based on the absence of significant coarsening in the multi-element co-doped sample after annealing at 1200 °C (Fig. 9(d)), we believe that no liquid phase was formed during annealing.

To study the effect of different durations on grain coarsening, the dwelling time of the undoped and multi-element co-doped samples at 1000 °C was extended to 8 and 12 h, followed by SEM observation and grain size measurements (Fig. S8 in the ESM). The average grain sizes of the undoped sample were 48.6 and 52.6 nm after 8 and 12 h of annealing, respectively, while those of the multi-element co-doped sample were 199.1 and 203.3 nm after 8 and 12 h of annealing (Fig. S8 in the ESM), respectively. By comparing these results with those of the as-sintered and 4 h annealed samples (Fig. 10), it can be concluded that the annealing duration (at 1000 °C) did not significantly affect the grain coarsening of the ZrO2 nanocrystallite in either the undoped or the multi-element co-doped sample. Compared with the annealing duration, the annealing temperature plays a more important role in grain coarsening.

### 4.4 Coarsening inhibition mechanism

From the above results (Figs. 8 and 9), it can be seen that ZrO2 NCs in the multi-element co-doped samples showed much greater thermal stability and coarsening resistance than those in the undoped control group during annealing. On one hand, this is because ZrO2 NCs in the as-sintered undoped control group (40.8 nm) had much smaller sizes than those in the as-sintered doped samples (193.5 nm). Therefore, ZrO2 NCs in the undoped control group had much greater coarsening kinetics due to their large specific surface area. On the other hand, it is reasonable to speculate that the high thermal stability and coarsening resistance of ZrO2 NCs in the multi-element co-doped samples can be attributed to the influence of the GB complexion with multiple co-segregated dopants.

GB migration driven by curvature is the most common growth mode in polycrystalline materials. The GB energy (thermodynamic factor) and GB mobility (kinetic factor) jointly determine the grain growth rate (*v*), which can be expressed by Eq. (1) [45]:

(1) v=M⋅P=M0exp(−QMRT)2Υb/r

where *M* is the GB migration rate; *P* is the driving force; *M*0 is the preexponential term; *Q*M is the activation energy of GB mobility; *R* is the ideal gas constant; *T* is the absolute temperature; *Ƴ*b is the interface energy per unit area; *r* is the radius of the grain. According to Eq. (1), there are two strategies for inhibiting grain coarsening: One is a thermodynamic strategy, and the other is a kinetic strategy. The thermodynamic strategy is to reduce the GB energy and weaken the driving force. Dopant(s) segregation at GBs can reduce the GB energy and thus reduce the driving force of grain coarsening, which can be expressed as Eq. (2) [46]:

(2) Υgb=Υ0−Tgb(RTlnXBbulk−ΔHseg)

where *Ƴ*gb and *Ƴ*0 are the GB energies with and without dopant(s) segregation, respectively; *Ƭ*gb is the solute excess in GB; Δ*H*seg is the enthalpy of segregation; * XBbulk is the bulk solute content. For the doped systems with negative segregation enthalpies, the GB energy decreases gradually with increasing excess solute atoms (i.e., dopant(s) segregation). The effectiveness of this strategy has been confirmed by many theoretical and experimental results [47−50]. For example, Nafsin et al*. [46] found that the addition of Gd dopant to ZrO2 nanoceramics resulted in the segregation of Gd at GBs, reducing the GB energy from 1.01 to 0.51 J/m2, so that the ZrO2 nanograins with sizes of 15 nm only coarsened to ~35 nm after annealing at 1100 °C for 4 h. However, the size of the ZrO2 nanograins in the undoped samples increased to ~65 nm under the same heat treatment conditions. Our previous results showed that in undoped ZrO2–SiO2 NCGCs, GBs between ZrO2 NCs were “clean” without solute atoms [37]. In this study, the co-segregation of multiple dopants resulted in a great excess of solute atoms at GBs, which significantly reduced the GB energy of ZrO2 NCs.

The reduction in the GB energy by dopant(s) segregation can also be understood from the perspective of entropy. According to the Gibbs isothermal adsorption theory (Eq. (3)):

(3) dΥgb=−SgbdT−∑iTidui

where *S*gb is the GB entropy per unit area, and *Ƭ**i* and *μ**i* are the density of the *i*th component at GB and its chemical potential, respectively. The larger the GB entropy is, the faster the GB energy decreases with increasing temperature. However, the effect of the GB entropy on hindering the grain coarsening of nanomaterials has not attracted enough attention in previous studies. Most of the previous experimental and computational studies focused on reducing the GB energy by reducing the segregation enthalpy (Δ*H*seg) of the dopants at GB [25,46,51,52]. Meanwhile, typically, only one or two kind(s) of dopant(s) segregated at GB, which is not enough to reduce the GB energy to the maximum extent. In this study, up to four solute atoms (Si, Ca, La, and Y) showed significant enrichment at the GB complexion (Fig. 7(c)). In addition, the GB complexion also contained Al, Ba, and Cs. Hence, the entropy of GBs between ZrO2 NCs significantly increased due to the co-segregation of multiple dopants, which resulted in a decrease in the GB energy (Eq. (3)) and GB migration (Eq. (1)).

The kinetic strategy for hindering grain coarsening is to reduce the GB migration rate through second-phase pinning and the dragging of atoms that segregate at GB [46,53]. Once dopants are segregated at GBs, they have a strong tendency to reside at GBs. As GBs move, they have to pull these dopants. In addition, segregated atoms have specific coordination surrounding them (cloud), and the cloud may extend into the lattice depending on the charge and size of the segregated atoms. GBs must also drag clouds, which significantly reduces the mobility of GBs. In this study, the dopants segregated at the GB complexion had different charges, sizes, and mobility, and they significantly reduced GB migration, enhancing the coarsening resistance of ZrO2 NCs during annealing. In addition, the “bridging phase” (quartz solid-solution, Fig. 4) that adhered to the periphery of ZrO2 NCs may act as a barrier for the diffusion of Zr atoms, which also contributed to the coarsening resistance of ZrO2 NCs.

One limitation of this study is that there is a large difference in grain size between the undoped (40.8 nm, Fig. 8) and doped samples (193.5 nm, Fig. 8) before annealing. The best situation is that the undoped and doped samples had similar grain sizes before annealing. Then, we compared their sizes after annealing at different temperatures. However, the liquid-phase sintering that occurred in the doped samples led to significant grain growth, which is beyond our control. The comparison of grain sizes between the multi-element co-doped samples and the control sample is challenging due to their distinct sintering mechanisms. Moreover, it is difficult to increase the grain size of the undoped sample to ~193.5 nm. However, we can think of this question from another perspective. For the undoped samples, compared with those of the as-sintered sample, the sizes of ZrO2 NCs in the 800 °C- and 1000 °C-annealed samples increased by ~25%, while those in the 1200 °C-annealed sample increased by 117.6% (Fig. 8). For the doped samples, grain coarsening did not occur after annealing at 800 and 1000 °C. The size growth rate was 19.8% after annealing at 1200 °C (Fig. 9), which was much smaller than that of the undoped sample (117.6%). As we discussed above, in addition to the smaller coarsening kinetics of ZrO2 NCs in the doped samples compared with those in the undoped samples, we believe that the GB complexion also contributes to the strong coarsening resistance of ZrO2 NCs in the doped samples.

The thickness of the GB complexion was ~2.5 nm (Fig. 7), which was thicker than that of commonly observed GBs in polycrystalline ceramics (typically thinner than 1 nm). One possible reason was that to ensure dopant segregation at ZrO2 GBs, we doped a large number of dopants (12 mol%) into NCGC. Reducing the dopant content might reduce the thickness of the GB complexion. During grain coarsening, atoms diffuse across GBs, moving from one grain to the other. It is reasonable to speculate that GB complexion with a thickness of ~2.5 nm would make it more difficult for atoms to diffuse than for commonly observed GBs with thicknesses less than 1 nm since GB complexion can be regarded as a barrier for atom diffusion. If one doped a large number of dopants into polycrystalline ceramics and the dopants segregated to GBs, a GB complexion with a thickness greater than 1 nm would also form. This complexion also hindered the diffusion of atoms and grain coarsening. Hence, we believe that the strategy we proposed would work for both glass-ceramics and polycrystalline ceramics.

## 5 Conclusions

In this study, we constructed GB complexions in ZrO2–SiO2 NCGC via a multi-element co-segregation strategy. The NCGC was predominantly composed of ZrO2 NCs distributed in a SiO2 matrix. Ultrathin layers of GB complexions (~2.5 nm) were formed, and they were crystalline ZrO2 solid-solution superstructures with co-segregated dopants. In addition, a “bridging phase” consisting of quartz solid solution was formed and adhered to the periphery of ZrO2 NCs and bridged the adjacent NCs. The GB complexions were first visualized in 3D space by the APT technique, which showed that they had a flake-like morphology. Dopant co-segregation at the GB complexion reduced the GB energy, and the mobility of GB was also suppressed through the drag of the co-segregated dopants. The “bridging phase” hindered the diffusion of Zr atoms. The GB complexions and the “bridging phase” synergistically hindered the coarsening of ZrO2 NCs from both energetic and kinetic perspectives. As a result, the sizes of ZrO2 NCs remained unchanged even after annealing at 1000 °C for 4 h. Compared with the sizes of ZrO2 NCs in the as-sintered sample, those in the 1200 °C annealed sample increased by only 19.8%. This work advances our understanding of GB complexions and highlights a new route for designing and fabricating nanomaterials with high coarsening resistance.
