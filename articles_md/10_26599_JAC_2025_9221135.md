## 1 Introduction

The rapid development of technologies such as wireless communication, radar detection, and satellite navigation has enabled electromagnetic waves (EMWs) to be widely used as information carriers in both industrial and civilian fields [1]. However, the corresponding problems of electromagnetic interference and pollution in these fields are becoming increasingly severe. EMW-absorbing materials, as a special type of functional material capable of effectively attenuating or eliminating the energy of incident EMWs, are highly valuable for mitigating the negative impacts of EMWs [2,3]. Despite decades of development in EMW-absorbing materials, most of them still face application limitations due to inadequate oxidation resistance and low Curie temperatures [4,5]. EMW-absorbing materials capable of efficient performance in high-temperature environments have become a focal point of interest in aerospace, nuclear energy, and civil applications. Unfortunately, current high-temperature EMW-absorbing materials not only face challenges related to their chemical stability under extreme environments but also, more critically, encounter significant difficulties in achieving impedance matching across wide temperature ranges. This challenge primarily arises from the significant variations in the temperature-dependent permittivity caused by temperature increases. Despite some studies on high-temperature EMW absorbing materials recognizing that the challenge of achieving wide-temperature-range EMW absorption stems from the temperature sensitivity of the permittivity, no further strategies or methods have been proposed on the basis of fundamental mechanisms related to the temperature-dependent permittivity to address this issue effectively. Therefore, on the basis of the evolution mechanism of temperature-dependent permittivity, designing materials at the molecular scale to achieve temperature-insensitive permittivity is more instructive and universal for the development of high-temperature EMW absorbing materials. Furthermore, the aforementioned fields often impose more stringent requirements on the additional properties of EMW absorbing materials, including low density, thinness, and high strength [6,7]. The simultaneous realization of these requirements relies on a comprehensive consideration of material types, dielectric property design, and preparation processes. Nonetheless, achieving both high-temperature dielectric performance and structural design in EMW-absorbing materials remains a formidable challenge. Therefore, polymer-derived ceramics (PDCs) with customizable performance and flexible shaping methods are ideal materials for EMW absorption in extremely high-temperature environments [8].

PDCs are a class of methods for obtaining ceramic products through the pyrolysis of macromolecular polymers, with the resulting products typically belonging to the Si–O–C–N–B system. During the pyrolysis process of PDCs, the dehydrogenation of hydrocarbon groups within the polymer chains results in the generation of a substantial quantity of free carbon [9]. The dispersion of free carbon throughout the ceramic matrix significantly enhances the dielectric properties of the ceramic products. However, for high-temperature EMW absorbing materials, there are significant drawbacks to enhancing the dielectric performance through the incorporation of free carbons, as these free carbons are susceptible to rapid oxidation or even sublimation in high-temperature environments. Therefore, it is imperative to design the molecular structure and chemical composition of the precursor polymer to achieve efficient high-temperature EMW absorption of PDCs. Among various methods, heterointerface engineering has been demonstrated to be an effective strategy for enhancing the dielectric properties of materials [10,11]. The heterointerface induces a built-in electric field due to the disparate work functions of the materials involved, thereby facilitating dipole polarization under alternating electromagnetic fields. By increasing the interfacial polarization, the inherent permittivity requirement for EMW absorption can be significantly reduced [12]. Furthermore, our previous research demonstrated that the permittivity of high-conductivity materials significantly increases with increasing temperature, posing a substantial challenge for achieving optimal impedance matching across a broad temperature range. This phenomenon can be attributed to the stronger influence of temperature on conductivity than that of dipole polarization [13]. Therefore, enhancing the polarization capability to minimize the reliance of permittivity on conductivity is unquestionably an effective strategy for constructing temperature-insensitive permittivity materials.

In addition, PDCs also possess distinct advantages in the precise shaping of ceramics. The “liquid-to-solid” transformation characteristics of PDCs provide them with flexible shaping ability [14]. Among them, template-based shaping methods offer PDCs convenient capabilities for structural design. The pore shape, porosity, and number of pores in the template affect the pore structure of the ceramic products, thereby influencing the porosity, density, thermal conductivity, strength, and other physical properties of the ceramics. This could also present additional opportunities for the expansion of PDC functionalities.

Herein, we propose a strategy to modulate the conductivity and enhance polarization at the molecular scale to effectively mitigate the temperature sensitivity of the permittivity. In the polymer-derived SiOCN ceramic matrix, free carbon is transformed *in situ* into a multiheterointerface composed of low-conductivity β-SiC and β-Si3N4 via carbothermal reduction reactions. The formation of multiple heterointerfaces significantly enhances the interface polarization capacity and reduces the dependence of the permittivity on the conductivity, thereby improving the temperature robustness of the permittivity. Moreover, by utilizing a self-sacrificing template method, the phase composition and dielectric properties of the ceramic products can be further controlled and optimized. The resulting multiheterointerface lightweight ceramics (MHLCs) exhibit a range of comprehensive properties, including good formability, low thermal conductivity, low density, high strength, resistance to extremely high temperatures, and temperature-insensitive dielectric properties. Moreover, the MHLCs demonstrate efficient EMW absorption across a broad temperature range from 25 to 900 °C at a consistent thickness. These exceptional physical properties of MHLCs demonstrate their adaptability to intricate electromagnetic environments. This approach offers a means to achieve wide-temperature-range absorption and multifunctional integration of high-temperature EMW absorbing materials, expanding the potential application of high-temperature EMW absorbing materials in extreme environments.

## 2 Experimental

**Preparation of the carbon-rich Si/SiO****2****/poly(methyl)silazane (PSZ) composite precursor.** Carbon-rich PSZ was prepared using divinylbenzene (DVB) as the carbon source. Typically, linear PSZ (ZG-A02, Zhonggui New Material Co., Ltd., China) and DVB (80% mixture of isomers, Macklin, China) were mixed at a mass ratio of 2 : 1 under an inert atmosphere (using the Schlenk technique) and stirred for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, high-purity silica micropowder (SiO2; 99.8%, 300–400 nm, Aladdin, China) and silicon powder (Si, 99.9%, 1 μm, Aladdin, China) were added to carbon-rich PSZ at mass ratios of 8 : 0, 8 : 1, 8 : 2, and 8 : 3, respectively. The mass ratio of silica micropowder to PSZ was fixed at 1 : 10. Ultimately, the mixture was stirred for 1 h at room temperature to obtain a uniform suspension.

**Preparation of MHLCs.** MHLCs were prepared via a self-sacrificial template method. Melamine foam (MF; 16 g/cm3, Zhengzhou FoamTech Nano Materials Co., Ltd., China) was used as the template, and the carbon-rich Si/SiO2/PSZ composite precursor was completely infiltrated into the melamine foam through a vacuum-assisted impregnation method to obtain ceramic green bodies. The green bodies were subsequently pyrolyzed under a nitrogen flow at 1300 °C for 6 h to obtain the MHLCs. The resulting MHLCs underwent calcination at 700 °C for 6 h to eliminate unreacted free carbon. The MHLCs were designated 0Si-MHLCs, 1Si-MHLCs, 2Si-MHLCs, and 3Si-MHLCs, corresponding to the mass ratios of SiO2 micropowder to Si powder in the precursors of 8 : 0, 8 : 1, 8 : 2, and 8 : 3, respectively.

**Characterization.** The microstructures of the MHLCs were analyzed by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Gemini SEM 360, Zeiss, Germany) and a field emission transmission electron microscope (FE-TEM; F200, JEOL, Japan) with an energy-dispersive spectroscopy detector (EDS; JED-2300T, JEOL, Japan). Geometric phase analysis (GPA) was performed via the GPA v4.10 plugin within DigitalMicrograph. The surface microstructural details of the samples were further investigated via a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; LSM 900, Zeiss, Germany), and 3D reconstruction of the pore structure was performed via ConfoMap. The phase structure of the MHLCs was characterized via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα irradiation (*λ* = 1.540598 Å) at 40 kV and 40 mA. *In situ* heating XRD measurements (up to 800 °C) were also carried out to examine the phase stability of the MHLCs. The sample was spread on a platinum sample plate after being ground. The measurement was conducted in ambient air at a heating rate of 10 °C/min within a temperature range of 25–800 °C. A Fourier transform infrared spectroscope (FT-IR; IRTracer 100, Shimadzu, Japan) was used via the potassium bromide (KBr) pellet method. A Raman spectroscope (DXR 2xi, Thermo Fisher, USA) was used at an excitation light source of 532 nm. An FT-IR spectroscope (IRTracer 100, Shimadzu, Japan) and an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Thermo ESCALAB Xi+, Thermo, USA) were used to characterize the chemical structure of the MHLCs. Monochromatic Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV) was used as the X-ray source for the XPS analysis. Electromagnetic parameter testing, which employs a vector network analyzer (VNA; Ceyear 3672c, Ceyear, China) and a microwave heating furnace, was employed to assess the temperature-dependent permittivity of the MHLCs within the frequency range of 8.2–12.4 GHz. The temperature of the furnace during the test varied from 25 to 900 °C while maintaining a heating rate of 10 °C/min. Before the permittivity data were collected, the sample was held at the corresponding temperature for 5 min. Other performance test methods or characterization details (material characterization, dielectric loss fitting, and molecular and electromagnetic simulation techniques) are presented in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

### 3.1 Preparation strategy for MHLCs

The shrinkage, porosity, yield, and physicochemical properties of PDCs are closely related to their chemical composition and pyrolysis conditions. During the pyrolysis process, bond dissociation, recombination, and transformation of volatile molecules significantly affect the properties of PDCs [15]. Therefore, refining and modifying PDCs at the molecular structure level, along with implementing a rational pyrolysis process, are crucial strategies to produce advanced PDCs. To address the electromagnetic environment under extreme conditions, we synthesized SiOCN-based ceramics with excellent high-temperature EWA and lightweight properties on the basis of a specific set of design principles. These principles involve three key requirements: i) the PDCs should effectively attenuate the EMW at the highest possible temperature and the widest possible temperature range. ii) PDCs must possess sufficient high-temperature oxidation resistance to be applicable in extremely aerobic environments. iii) The PDCs must be as lightweight as possible while exhibiting adequate mechanical robustness. To meet the first two criteria, we sacrificed the free carbon clusters within the amorphous SiOCN ceramics, promoting their further transformation into low-conductivity, high-temperature-resistant polycrystalline structures. The objective is to enhance the dipolar polarization capability and diminish the reliance of the permittivity on the conductivity by establishing more heterointerfaces, thereby achieving the dual purpose of enhancing the high-temperature stability and reducing the temperature sensitivity of the permittivity. To address the final requirement, we employed a method that constructs porous structures at the micro- and nanoscale through templating and chemical modification, resulting in porous SiOCN ceramics without compromising their structural robustness.

On the basis of the above key requirements, the synthetic pathway for fabricating MHLCs consists of four major steps, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). In the first step, DVB was used as a linker molecule, which was integrated into the crosslinked network of the PSZ precursor through hydrosilylation reactions. The incorporation of DVB enhances the spatial entanglement of polymer chains, leading to a higher ceramic yield [16]. Additionally, other studies have correlated decomposition behavior with both pore evolution and the resulting pore size when DVB is utilized as a linker molecule. The resulting pore structure can reduce the overall density of ceramics while increasing their specific surface area [17]. Furthermore, the sp2-carbon domains generated during the decomposition of DVB create favorable conditions for the *in situ* growth of heterostructures. In the subsequent pyrolysis process, on the one hand, the amorphous carbon will undergo a carbon-thermal reduction reaction with the silicon source to form SiC and Si3N4 heterostructures. On the other hand, the MF organic framework gradually decomposes, introducing a complex pore structure within the ceramic matrix and ultimately yielding porous SiOCN-based ceramics enriched with heterointerfaces (Fig. 1(c)). The EDS mapping results revealed the uniform distribution of the various elements inside the ceramic (Fig. 1(d)). The CLSM image and the three-dimensional reconstructed image further demonstrate the formation of a ceramic network skeleton with uneven pore sizes (Fig. 1(e)). As shown in Figs. 1(b) and 1(f), the MHLCs exhibit excellent conformality, with an overall pyrolysis linear shrinkage rate of 28.7% and a density of 1.03 g/cm3.

### 3.2 Pyrolysis phase evolution of MHLCs

To address the limitations of carbon-based dielectric networks in terms of oxidation resistance and to enhance the interfacial polarization capability, heterostructures were constructed *in situ* within an amorphous SiOCN matrix via a chemical vapor deposition route (Fig. 2(a)). In a high-temperature environment, the volatilization of precursor materials and the formation of solid components initially occur. Moreover, the decomposition and carbonization of DVB resulted in the generation of significant amounts of free carbon within the amorphous ceramic network. As the temperature increased to 1300 °C, a reaction took place between Si and SiO2, producing gaseous SiO (Reaction (1)) [18,19]. Subsequently, gaseous SiO reacts at various sites aided by the reducing action of free carbon, leading to the formation of Si3N4 and SiC heterostructures (Reactions (2)–(4)). The impact of the pyrolysis temperature and precursor composition ratio on the crystal structure of the ceramics was investigated by conducting conventional and *in situ* heating XRD measurements on the MHLCs. The XRD patterns of the MHLCs formed with different precursor component ratios are shown in Fig. S1 in the ESM. The increasing Si powder content in the precursor led to a more pronounced distinction in the diffraction peaks corresponding to β-SiC (PDF#29-1129) and β-Si3N4 (PDF#82-0698), indicating a gradual increase in heterostructures within the SiOCN matrix. An excess of Si powder clearly led to the emergence of diffraction peaks corresponding to unreacted Si in the XRD patterns. Furthermore, although single-phase SiO2 can also generate SiC and Si3N4 under the reducing action of C, it requires higher temperatures [20−22]. Therefore, the conventional XRD results revealed that at the same preparation temperature, single-phase SiO2 did not induce the growth of the SiC and Si3N4 crystal structures (0Si-MHLCs). Additionally, excessively high temperatures can easily induce crystallization, cracking, and warping of PDCs, thereby affecting their density and mechanical properties.

The *in situ* heating XRD results show that upon heating the MHLCs to 800 °C under air, the diffraction peaks associated with β-SiC (PDF#29-1129) and β-Si3N4 (PDF#82-0698) maintain excellent stability, thereby confirming the proposed reaction pathway and substantiating the stability of the ceramic’s crystal structure at high temperatures (Fig. 2(b)). The FT-IR spectra reveal the typical polymer-to-ceramic transformation bond rearrangement (Fig. 2(c)). The spectra show the presence of all the expected bonds, i.e., N–H (3430 cm−1), Si–N (940 cm−1), Si–NH–Si (1238 cm−1), Si–O (1100 cm−1), and Si–C (816 cm−1) [23,24]. The increase in the signal intensity of the Si–N and Si–C groups also provides evidence for the structural evolution of SiC and Si3N4 at high temperatures. The heterostructures formed by the interlacement of β-SiC and β-Si3N4 within the amorphous SiOCN ceramic matrix are prominently displayed in the TEM image (Fig. 2(d)). The crystal lattice fringes of 0.251 and 0.233 nm correspond to the (111) plane of β-SiC and the (111) plane of β-Si3N4, respectively. High-resolution high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) and EDS images revealed the elemental distribution in the MHLCs, further demonstrating the uniform growth of the heterostructures (Figs. S2(a)–S2(e) in the ESM). The elemental content information indicates that MHLCs contain an appropriate amount of N, which is conducive to enhancing stability at extreme temperatures (Fig. S2(f) in the ESM). Notably, GPA indicates that the *ε**xx* and *ε**yx* strain components in the heterointerface region exhibit obvious tensile and compressive strain variations (Fig. 2(e)) [25]. This is due to the lattice mismatch and corresponding dislocations generated at the heterointerface, which cause significant strain fluctuations [26,27]. In addition, many dispersed turbostratic carbon domains can be observed in the TEM images (Fig. S3 in the ESM). These turbostratic carbon domains, as conductive components, have triggered improvements in the ceramic dielectric properties. Raman spectroscopy clearly revealed the evolution process of the sp2 carbon domains. The D and G peaks located at 1350 and 1590 cm−1, respectively, clearly prove the composite pattern of amorphous carbon and sp2 carbon domains. With the gradual formation of the heterostructure, the decreasing ID/IG further confirms the more abundant distribution of sp2 carbon domains (Fig. S4 in the ESM). We also note that with increasing Si powder content, the Si–C (101.8 eV) and Si–N (102.5 eV) signals from the XPS results increase accordingly (Fig. 2(f)). Similar phenomena can also be observed in the C 1s and N 1s spectra, where the corresponding contents of different chemical bonds can be identified from Figs. 2(g) and 2(h) [28,29]. A significant increase in the content of the C–Si bond (283.5 eV) in the C 1s spectra and the N–Si bond (397.5 eV) in the N 1s spectra can be clearly observed. These results provide evidence for the evolution mechanisms of SiC and Si3N4 heterostructures.

### 3.3 Thermal insulation and mechanical properties of MHLCs

The limited thermal stability of most EMW-absorbing materials restricts their applicability in high-temperature environments. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the thermal stability of the MHLCs was assessed through thermogravimetric (TG) analysis and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) in an air atmosphere. As the temperature increased, the weight evolution of the MHLCs exhibited two distinct stages. When the initial temperature increased to 1000 °C, no discernible changes in weight were observed. Subsequently, there was a slight increase in the weight of MHLCs as the temperature increased to 1200 °C, which coincided with the occurrence of peak heat flow. We speculate that the slight increase in weight is attributed to surface oxidation of β-SiC, as the residual weight gradually tends to increase with increasing SiC content [30]. Importantly, during the entire heating process, the MHLCs exhibited a maximum mass change of no more than 2.5%, indicating exceptional thermal stability. The excellent thermal stability of MHLCs is crucial for prolonged exposure to high-temperature conditions without degrading performance.

Additionally, thermal insulation is another important function of MHLCs. To further reveal the thermal insulation mechanism, nitrogen adsorption‒desorption isotherms and pore size distributions of the MHLCs were obtained (Fig. 3(b)). According to the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) classification, the adsorption‒desorption isotherms of the samples all exhibit type IV isotherms, indicating that, in addition to the micrometer-sized pores left by the melamine foam decomposing inside the MHLCs, there are also mesopores and macropores. Additionally, the absence of a distinct saturation adsorption plateau at high pressures results in the formation of an H3 hysteresis loop, indicating the presence of unfilled slit pore structures [31,32]. The pore size distribution plot also confirms the irregular pore structure of the MHLCs. This hierarchical pore structure not only increases the specific surface area and reduces the bulk density but also effectively reduces heat transfer (Fig. 3(c)). Figure 3(d) shows the thermal conductivity of the MHLCs at different temperatures (25−1000 °C). For porous materials with pore sizes smaller than 1 mm, the thermal conductivity ( λeff) can be considered the sum of the radiative thermal conductivity ( λrad), solid-phase thermal conductivity ( λs), and gas-phase thermal conductivity ( λg), where  λs and  λg are commonly regarded as parameters associated with density and pore size, and  λrad is heavily influenced by temperature [33,34]. Owing to their low density and multilevel pore structure, the thermal conductivity of MHLCs is only 0.37 W/(m·K) at 25 °C. With increasing temperature,  λrad gradually increases, leading to a trend of increasing  λeff. Even in the extreme environment of 1000 °C, the  λeff of the MHLCs was only 0.76 W/(m·K). In addition, infrared images of the MHLC were recorded via an infrared thermal camera (T560, FLIR, USA) following heating at 500 °C for 30 min. After being heated for 30 min, the surface temperature of the MHLC cube with a side length of 20 mm stabilized at approximately 162 °C, indicating its potential application in thermal insulation components.

To verify the mechanical properties of the MHLCs, tests were conducted on their compressive and flexural performance (Fig. 3(e)). The compressive stress‒strain curve indicates that, owing to the high porosity characteristics of MHLCs, there is a sudden decrease in strength after the stress reaches its maximum value, followed by pseudoductile failure. This phenomenon is attributed to the stress concentration caused by the presence of pores. When subjected to external loads, specific regions surrounding the pores may experience elevated stress levels. Consequently, surpassing a critical threshold of external load can induce localized failure within the ceramic material, resulting in a gradual collapse of the structure rather than an immediate overall failure. Despite the mechanical property challenges posed by the porous structure, MHLCs still demonstrate commendable compressive strength, with a maximum value reaching 19.3 MPa. Moreover, MHLCs exhibit exceptional bending strengths. The bending strength can be calculated via Eq. (5) [35,36]:

(5) σ=3FL2bh2

where σ* is the bending strength, *F* is the load applied at the center of the sample, *L* is the span length, *b* is the sample width, and *h* is the sample thickness. Reassuringly, the MHLCs reach a maximum bending strength of 33.55 MPa at a strain of 4.8%. Compared with other recently reported porous ceramic materials, MHLCs exhibit significant advantages in terms of bending strength, bulk density, and thermal conductivity (Fig. 3(f) and Table S1 in the ESM) [37−42]. The outstanding mechanical and thermal insulation properties of MHLCs represent their multifunctional integration of high-temperature resistance, load-bearing capacity, and heat insulation, thus demonstrating irreplaceable application potential in extremely complex working conditions. Unlike traditional EMW materials, which have single-function limitations, MHLCs have emerged as promising candidates for harsh environments such as thermal protection systems for high-speed aircraft, thermal end components in aerospace engineering, and thermal management systems in new energy domains.

### 3.4 Temperature-driven electron transfer kinetics process of MHLCs

To gain deep insight into the effects of heterointerface formation and temperature on carrier transitions and dipole movements at the atomic level, *ab initio* molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were employed to investigate the structural changes in MHLCs at different temperatures. Primarily, structural models of amorphous SiOCN and β-SiC/SiOCN/β-Si3N4 multilayer structures were constructed, where SiOCN forms heterointerfaces with both β-SiC and β-Si3N4 to simulate the crystal structure of MHLCs (Fig. S5 in the ESM). The optimized MHLC model subsequently underwent thermodynamic simulations under the NVT ensemble (canonical ensemble), with the temperature gradually increasing to 1473 K, to investigate its structural evolution behavior (Fig. 4(a)). As the temperature increases, the MHLC model clearly experiences the formation of carbon clusters and volume expansion due to the diffusion of C and N atoms. Atomic differential charge density analysis indicated that the introduction of β-SiC and β-Si3N4 phases promoted the formation of distinct heterointerface structures within the MHLCs system. The Si, O, C, and N atoms in the interface region exhibit significant charge redistribution characteristics, thereby substantially expanding the dipole polarization region of the composite (Fig. 4(b)). A quantitative analysis of the planar average charge density distribution along the *Y*-axis within the temperature range of 300–1473 K revealed that the electron mobility significantly increased with increasing temperature. Positive (negative) values indicate the accumulation (depletion) of electrons. The electrons at the heterointerfaces located at 4 and 10 Å clearly exhibit a stronger electron transfer efficiency (Fig. 4(c)). In addition, the structural evolution characteristics of the MHLC model at different temperatures indicate that the increase in atomic amplitude induced by thermal vibrations leads to the diffusion of C and N atoms, resulting in volume expansion and the formation of carbon clusters. The radial distribution function (RDF) curves further reveal that as the temperature gradually increases to 1473 K, the intensity of the first coordination peak of the Si–O bond significantly decreases, with the bond length increasing to 1.66 Å (Fig. 4(d)). The Si‒C bond length decreases from 1.88 to 1.83 Å, accompanied by a noticeable reduction in the coordination number (Fig. 4(e)). The Si–N bond length also decreases from 1.77 to 1.73 Å, with a slight decrease in the coordination number (Fig. S6 in the ESM). The thermal vibration-induced breaking and diffusion of the chemical bonds lead to an increased inhomogeneity of the electron cloud distribution, thereby increasing the dynamic polarizability of the MHLCs. Analysis of the density of states (DOS) revealed the impact of heterostructure formation on the electronic energy level distribution. The formation of SiC and Si3N4 enhances the structural order and reduces the energy level of the ceramics, which substantially narrows the intrinsic band gap of the MHLCs, thereby indicating a significant improvement in electron mobility (Fig. S7 in the ESM). A comparison of the evolution characteristics of the DOS distribution of the system under the action of different temperature fields reveals that thermal vibration-induced chemical bond reconstruction leads to the generation of new energy levels in the conduction band (CB) region (Fig. 4(f)). The spin‒orbit coupling effect is significantly enhanced under high-temperature conditions, resulting in a pronounced level splitting phenomenon within the intrinsic band gap, accompanied by the formation of additional subband structures. With the formation of the subband structure, the carrier excitation barrier decreases from 1.44 to 1.01 eV, thereby increasing the carrier concentration and mobility. This results in MHLCs exhibiting excellent electronic migration characteristics under high-temperature conditions.

The heterointerface formed by β-SiC and β-Si3N4 significantly influences the overall dielectric properties of the ceramics. Complex permittivity ( ε′ and  ε″) and complex permeability ( μ′ and  μ″) are crucial parameters for characterizing the electromagnetic properties of materials [43,44]. Since MHLCs consist of nonmagnetic components, they present significantly low values for both  μ′ and  μ″, which suggests a limited capacity for magnetic loss (Fig. S8 in the ESM). As shown in Fig. 4(g), with increasing Si concentration in the precursor, significant increases in both  ε′ and  ε″ were observed. The heterointerface induces the accumulation of free charges at the interfaces, leading to the formation of dipoles due to their nonuniform distribution. When subjected to an alternating electric field, these dipoles undergo relaxation polarization, ultimately resulting in an increase in the complex permittivity. In addition, compared with the amorphous SiOCN ceramic matrix, the growth of β-SiC crystals with higher conductivity enhances the overall electronic transfer capability of the ceramics, thereby promoting the formation of a conductive network. The establishment of a conductive network provides the conditions to improve the conduction loss capability of MHLCs, resulting in a significant increase in  ε″. However, the excessive Si content of the precursor can also lead to an incomplete reaction of Si (3Si-MHLCs), which adversely affects electron transfer and leads to a decrease in complex permittivity. To further elucidate the contributions of polarization loss and conduction loss to the dielectric loss capability of MHLCs, analytical calculations of the frequency-dependent conduction loss ( εc″) and polarization loss ( εp″) were conducted (Eqs. (S1) and (S2) in the ESM) [45]. When the frequency is constant, the conductive loss is clearly influenced by the electrical conductivity of the material, whereas the polarization loss is affected by the dipole relaxation time. Figure 4(h) indicates that a significant increase in conductive loss occurs after the formation of heterostructures within the ceramic matrix. However, as the heterostructure content further increases, the conductive loss only slightly increases. This suggests that the heterostructures are highly beneficial for enhancing the overall electronic transfer capability of the ceramics. Nevertheless, the relatively low intrinsic conductivity of the heterostructures prevents significant improvements in ceramic conductivity when the percolation threshold is not reached. The decrease in the conductive loss capacity of 3Si-MHLCs also confirms the detrimental impact of excessive Si powder on the formation of conductive networks. Furthermore, the observed gradual increase in the polarization loss of the MHLCs is consistent with expectations, thereby demonstrating that the dipole relaxation polarization induced by the heterointerface plays a predominant role in enhancing the dielectric performance. The Cole‒Cole curves also demonstrate that the gradual establishment of a heterointerface transforms the dielectric loss mechanism of MHLCs from solely conductivity loss to a combination of polarization loss and conduction loss (Fig. 4(i)). In summary, the increase in the dielectric properties of MHLCs can be attributed to the generation of interfacial dipole polarization induced by the heterointerface, and the formation of conductive networks is improved by heterostructures with increased conductivity (Fig. 4(j)).

### 3.5 Temperature-dependent dielectric properties of MHLCs

To further investigate the mechanism of the evolution of the dielectric properties of MHLCs at elevated temperatures, we characterize their temperature-dependent permittivity from 25 to 900 °C. As shown in Figs. 5(a)–5(d), both * ε′ and  ε″ exhibit a gradually increasing trend with increasing temperature. According to the modified Debye relaxation theory, this phenomenon is attributed primarily to the increase in conductivity and the shortening of the relaxation time ( τ(T)) caused by the increase in temperature (Eqs. (S3) and (S4) in the ESM) [46]. The semiconductor properties of β-SiC and β-Si3N4 result in a negative temperature coefficient of resistivity, thereby enhancing the conductivity of MHLCs at elevated temperatures. Moreover, the increase in temperature supplies energy for the thermal motion of dipoles, facilitating their orientation changes under an electric field and consequently expediting the polarization process. In addition,  ε′ and  ε″ exhibit significant insensitivity to temperature increases. Even when the temperature increases from 25 to 900 °C, the maximum value of  ε′ changes from 4.45 to 6.47, whereas the maximum value of  ε″ changes from 0.88 to 2.21. The insensitivity of the temperature-dependent permittivity of MHLCs can be attributed to the disparity in the impact of temperature on the conductivity and relaxation time (Eqs. (S5) and (S6) in the ESM). As the temperature increases, the conductivity follows a negative exponential relationship, resulting in a corresponding increase in the conductive loss component ( εc′′(T)) of  ε′′(T). Despite the exponential decrease in  τ(T) caused by the temperature increase, the polarization loss component ( εp′′(T)) of  ε′′(T) is less affected by  τ(T). To further reveal the effects of temperature on  εp′′(T) and  εc′′(T) and clarify the transformation process of the dielectric loss mechanism in MHLCs with respect to temperature, the  εp′′(T) values of the MHLCs were calculated, and the Cole‒Cole curves at different temperatures were investigated (Figs. 5(e)–5(h)). The proportion of polarization loss can be further expressed by the polarization loss factor ( εP′′(T)/ε′′(T)). As the heterointerface in the MHLCs gradually formed, a significant upward trend in the polarization loss factor was observed. However, as the temperature increases, the polarization loss factor gradually decreases. This finding indicates that the conductive loss capability of MHLCs is significantly enhanced at higher temperatures, further demonstrating that temperature has a more pronounced effect on the conductive loss component. Furthermore, the Cole‒Cole curves at different temperatures indicate that as the temperature increases, the dielectric loss mechanism of MHLCs transitions from multiple dielectric loss mechanisms to a single mechanism characterized primarily by conductive loss. Therefore, by constructing low-conductivity β-SiC and β-Si3N4 heterostructures in the SiOCN matrix and subsequently forming multiple heterointerfaces, it is possible to effectively mitigate the substantial increase in temperature-dependent permittivity caused by the temperature sensitivity of the conductivity. This temperature-insensitive dielectric property of MHLCs provides the potential for effective EMW absorption across a wide temperature range.

The EMW attenuation capability of MHLCs in practical applications was further verified through a simulation calculation for a radar cross section (RCS) on a 38 m long aircraft model made of a perfect electric conductor (PEC). The RCS values of the aircraft were compared before and after a 3.40 mm layer of 2Si-MHLCs was applied at a temperature of 900 °C. After implementing a planar EMW irradiation model at 10 GHz, the average RCS of the 2Si-MHLCs-covered model can be reduced by 20.78 dB·m2 (Fig. 6(f)). Furthermore, it can be observed from the longitudinal (Figs. 6(g) and Fig. S12(a) in the ESM) and frontal (Figs. 6(h) and Fig. S12(b) in the ESM) RCSs of the model that regardless of whether it is in the large planar area or the angular region, there is a significant reduction in the radar scattering characteristics of the model, resulting in an average RCS below −2 dB·m2. The remarkable EMW absorption properties exhibited by the MHLCs can be attributed to the synergistic regulation of the heterointerface engineering and microstructure. The formation of multiphase heterostructures not only significantly enhances interfacial polarization but also promotes the generation of new electronic energy levels. These heterogeneous structures and the sp2 carbon domains produced by pyrolysis jointly form a conductive network of ceramics, thereby appropriately enhancing the conductive loss capacity. Furthermore, the high porosity of MHLCs not only contributes to their unique lightweight properties but also facilitates sufficient reflection and scattering of EMW within the ceramic. The synergy of these EMW loss mechanisms lays the foundation for MHLCs to achieve excellent EMW absorption performance simultaneously under both ambient and high-temperature conditions. Figure 6(i) clearly illustrates the evolution of the dominant form of dielectric loss in high-conductivity materials as the temperature increases, as well as the adverse effects of the rapid increase in conductivity on EMW absorption. This further reveals the fundamental concept of this work, which aims to develop high-performance high-temperature EMW absorbing materials by limiting the conductivity of the materials and enhancing interfacial dipole polarization.

## 4 Conclusions

In summary, a novel lightweight high-temperature EMW-absorbing ceramic was successfully fabricated via *in situ* construction of a multiheterointerface combined with a self-sacrificial template strategy. The formation of a multiphasic heterostructure consisting of β-SiC and β-Si3N4 within the amorphous SiOCN matrix during pyrolysis significantly enhances the charge transfer efficiency at the heterointerface, leading to an improved capability for interface dipole polarization in ceramics. The increase in the interface dipole polarization effectively mitigates the dependence of the permittivity on the conductivity, thereby reducing the temperature sensitivity of the permittivity. The resulting multiheterointerface lightweight ceramics exhibit excellent thermal stability (up to 1000 °C), low density (1.03 g/cm3), low thermal conductivity (0.37 W/(m·K)), and high bending strength (33.55 MPa). These exceptional physical properties provide the necessary conditions for MHLCs to meet the requirements of various application scenarios. Furthermore, the MHLCs demonstrate outstanding temperature-insensitive EMW absorption performance, effectively absorbing EMW within the temperature range of 500 to 900 °C at a thickness of 3.4 mm and further expanding the temperature range to 25 to 900 °C at a thickness of 9.41 mm. Therefore, MHLCs show promise as EMW absorbing coatings or structures for applications in high-temperature environments. Overall, the lightweight dielectric ceramic preparation method proposed in this study provides technical feasibility for synthesizing various high-performance high-temperature EMW absorbing materials suitable for extreme environments.
