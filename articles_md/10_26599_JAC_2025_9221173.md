## 1 Introduction

Thermal barrier coatings (TBCs), which provide thermal insulation, have been developed to protect turbine engines—the heart of aircraft—thereby increasing their efficiency [1−3]. Yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) is currently the most widely used TBC material because of its low thermal conductivity and similarity in the coefficient of thermal expansion to the superalloy substrate [4]. With the development of aircraft engines toward higher thrust-to-weight ratios, turbine engines are subjected to increasingly higher operating temperatures. Consequently, corrosion caused by environmental deposits (mainly CaO, MgO, Al2O3, and SiO2, collectively known as CMAS) has become the primary cause of TBC failure [5−7]. CMAS, a multicomponent molten glass formed from ingested dust, volcanic ash, and other airborne particulates, typically has a melting point between 1100 and 1250 °C and has low viscosity and high fluidity [8−10]. Under high temperatures, CMAS melts and wets TBC surfaces, infiltrates through cracks and pores, and reacts with TBCs, leading to phase transformation, thermal stress, and premature delamination of TBCs [9,11−14]. This poses a serious threat to engine safety. Therefore, it is necessary to take measures to protect against CMAS to extend the lifespan of TBCs. This problem has garnered increasing attention worldwide [15−17].

For decades, most researchers have focused on developing novel TBC materials to improve resistance against CMAS corrosion, including novel ceramics such as modified YSZ, rare-earth zirconates, and rare-earth silicates [18−20]. However, these materials still suffer significant performance degradation when exposed to molten CMAS at high temperatures. Fang *et al*. [21] conducted thermal cycling-CMAS tests using a burner rig setup on YSZ and YbYSZ (Yb2O3 and Y2O3 costabilized ZrO2) TBCs. The results revealed delamination in the YbYSZ coatings, although the lifetime was improved by approximately 33% over that of the YSZ coatings. Han *et al*. [22] demonstrated that alumina-doped YSZ TBCs presented superior CMAS corrosion resistance than conventional YSZ did, but CMAS could still infiltrate through cracks and pores, ultimately leading to delamination. These findings suggest that developing novel TBC materials alone is insufficient to solve the CMAS corrosion problem, highlighting the urgent need for alternative strategies.

One promising approach is to apply a protective layer onto the TBC surface to serve as the first line of defense against CMAS attack. When molten CMAS comes into contact with TBCs, it wets and infiltrates into TBCs. The protective layer above the TBC can effectively prevent this process, slowing CMAS penetration and reaction, thereby extending the service life of the underlying TBC. Hence, applying a protective layer onto the TBC surface could be an effective way to alleviate CMAS attack [23,24]. As reported by Hasz *et al*. [25−27], CMAS-protective coatings can be classified into three types: impermeable, sacrificial, and nonwetting to CMAS. Among them, Pt and Gd2Zr2O7 are considered typical impermeable and sacrificial layers, respectively, for mitigating CMAS-related damage. Liu *et al*. [28] reported that depositing a Pt layer on the surface of TBC can inhibit CMAS infiltration. Ozgurluk *et al*. [29] prepared Gd2Zr2O7/YSZ TBCs via electron beam-physical vapor deposition and demonstrated their superior CMAS resistance over YSZ TBC, which is attributed to the protective function of Gd2Zr2O7, which reacts with CMAS, slowing melt penetration. However, these impermeable and sacrificial layers lose their function over long periods. For example, in the presence of long-term CMAS exposure, the Pt layer became discontinuous, and the Gd2Zr2O7 coating became increasingly thin. Hence, these well-investigated protective layers cannot meet the requirements of CMAS protection.

Therefore, a novel strategy for developing protective layers is to reduce CMAS wettability, on the basis of which it is difficult for molten CMAS to adhere to and stay on TBCs. As a result, the CMAS issue could be solved at the source. To achieve this goal, researchers have developed CMAS-phobic surfaces through microstructural design, notably via laser surface modification and plasma spray-physical vapor deposition (PS-PVD) [30−32]. Guo *et al*. [33] fabricated a hierarchical microstructured surface on (Gd0.9Yb0.1)2Zr2O7 (GYbZ) pellets via ultrafast laser direct writing to investigate the potential of biomimetic architectures in resisting molten volcanic ash wetting. Subsequently, biomimetic GYbZ TBCs were prepared from PS-PVD, which demonstrated excellent molten volcanic ash phobicity attributed to the lotus-leaf-like dual-scale microstructure. Sun *et al*. [34] reported that femtosecond laser texturing could significantly increase the hydrophobicity of YSZ by constructing hierarchical micro-nanostructures, thereby effectively reducing the wettability of CMAS. Zhuo *et al*. [35] prepared a laser-textured and Al-modified PS-PVD TBC that exhibited superhydrophobicity, inhibiting molten CMAS wetting. Despite these advantages, the broader application of laser surface modification or PS-PVD is limited by high costs, technical complexity, and stringent equipment requirements.

Recent studies have shown that GdPO4 has excellent resistance to CMAS corrosion [36]. The key is the formation of a bilayer-structured apatite layer at the interface between GdPO4 and molten CMAS, which consists of an acicular Ca3Gd7(PO4)(SiO4)5O2 upper layer and a compact Ca7Gd3(PO4)5(SiO4)O2 lower layer. The compact layer effectively prevents CMAS infiltration, and the acicular upper layer significantly resists wetting of the molten CMAS. Additionally, some apatite compounds have been reported to be used as TBC materials [37]. Inspired by these findings, it is expected that this type of bilayer-structured apatite layer can be used on TBC surfaces to protect them against CMAS wetting and penetration. However, conventional deposition methods such as atmospheric plasma spraying (APS), electron beam-physical vapor deposition (EB-PVD), and PS-PVD make it difficult to construct this protective layer on TBCs, primarily because of their distinct bilayer architecture. To overcome these limitations, a novel strategy is proposed: the preparation of a thin GdPO4 layer on the TBC surface, which can react with molten CMAS to form a continuous bilayer-structured apatite layer. The innovation of this method lies in the *in situ* construction of a bilayer-structured apatite protective layer with low wettability and penetration resistance to molten CMAS through the regulation of pretreatment parameters and the comprehensive evaluation of the high-temperature stability of the protective layer and the effect of low wettability resistance on penetration. To achieve this, it is key to precisely control the interfacial reaction of CMAS and GdPO4, which involves many influencing factors, such as the reaction temperature, time, and CMAS concentration.

In this study, a protective layer with low wettability and penetration resistance to molten CMAS was investigated. The layer consists of a bilayer apatite structure with an acicular upper layer and a compact lower layer, formed in situ through the reaction between rare-earth phosphates and prereacted powder, in which GdPO4 is merely an intermediate. Therefore, the CMAS corrosion behavior of bulk materials is not the focus of this study. The preparation process of the bilayer-structured layer samples, as well as their high-temperature structural stability, phase stability, thermal cycling performance, CMAS wetting behavior, and CMAS infiltration resistance, were investigated. The mechanisms for bilayer-structured apatite layer formation and its resistance to CMAS wetting and penetration were also clarified.

## 2 Experimental

GdPO4 powder was prepared via the chemical coprecipitation method [38]. Gd2O3 (99.99%, Zibo Ruibokang Rare Earth Materials Co., Ltd., China) was first dried in a muffle furnace (SX-G05165, Tianjin Zhonghuan Electric Furnace Co., Ltd., China) at 900 °C for 4 h to remove moisture and carbonates. After accurate weighing, the dried Gd2O3 was dissolved in concentrated nitric acid (65%, Jiangtian Chemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) to obtain a transparent Gd(NO3)3 solution. The (NH4)2HPO4 powder (99%, Jiangtian Chemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) was subsequently dissolved in deionized water and then slowly added to the Gd(NO3)3 solution, after which it was repeatedly washed with deionized water and ethanol via a centrifuge until the pH reached 7, and then dried to obtain the precursors. After calcination in a muffle furnace at 900 °C for 4 h, the precursors were transformed into GdPO4 powder. The powder was ground and sieved through a 200-mesh sieve, pressed into pellets with a diameter of 15 mm via cold pressing at 300 MPa for 10 min, and sintered in a muffle furnace at 1600 °C for 10 h.

CMAS powder with a composition of 33CaO–9MgO–13AlO1.5–45SiO2, a widely recognized composition in CMAS corrosion studies [39,40], was prepared via the solid-state reaction method. The CaO, MgO, Al2O3, and SiO2 powders (99.9%, Jiangtian Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd., China) were weighed according to the target molar ratio after heat treatment at 900 °C for 4 h. The weighed powders, absolute ethanol, and zirconia balls (at a mass ratio of 1 : 1.2 : 1.5) were placed in a ball mill jar and mixed via a planetary ball mill (QM-3SP4, Nanjing Nan-Da Instrument Co., Ltd., China) at 400 r/min for 10 h to obtain a uniform mixture. The resulting slurry was dried in a drying oven (101-2BS, Tianjin Hongnuo Instrument Co., Ltd., China) at 120 °C for 10 h, followed by heat treatment in a muffle furnace at 1550 °C for 1 h to ensure complete melting, after which the melt was rapidly removed and poured into deionized water to obtain CMAS glass. The glass was then ground to powder in a mortar and sieved through a 200-mesh sieve for subsequent experiments.

For the preparation experiments of the bilayer-structured apatite layer samples, different concentrations of CMAS powders (3, 5, 10, and 15 mg/cm2) were evenly applied to the GdPO4 pellets via a 200-mesh sieve. The samples were then heat-treated at different temperatures (1200, 1250, 1300, and 1350 °C) for various durations (5, 10, 20, 30, 60, and 120 min) to obtain the optimal processing conditions. All subsequent experiments were conducted using bilayer-structured apatite layer samples prepared under these optimized conditions. To evaluate long-term thermal stability, the optimized samples were subjected to long-term heat treatments at 1250 °C for 10, 30, and 50 h. In addition, thermal cycling tests were performed at 1200 °C to assess resistance to thermal shock. During each cycle, the samples were held at 1200 °C for 5 min and then removed and air-cooled to room temperature. Each test was conducted with three samples to ensure reproducibility. For the CMAS wetting experiments, CMAS pellets with a diameter of 5 mm were placed on the bilayer-structured apatite layer samples and subjected to heat treatment at 1250 °C for 20 h. The experiments were performed at 1250 °C for 5 and 30 min, with three samples tested for each condition. The contact angle measurements were conducted by capturing scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images and analyzing them via ImageJ 1.8.0 software. For each sample, the contact angle was measured six times, and the average value was calculated to ensure accuracy. In the CMAS corrosion experiments, the bilayer-structured apatite layer samples were evenly covered with CMAS powder at a concentration of 20 mg/cm2, a value commonly employed in related studies to ensure representativeness and comparability [23,41]. The coated samples were then heat-treated at 1250 °C for 4 and 20 h.

The SEM (Nanosem 430, FEI, USA) equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS; IE 350, Penta FET X-3, UK) was operated at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV to observe the cross-sectional microstructures and perform compositional analysis of the samples. An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; D8 Advanced, Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation was employed to characterize the phase structure to assess the long-term high-temperature phase stability of the bilayer-structured apatite layers. The scanning range was from 10° to 80° at a rate of 0.2 (°)/s, and the current and voltage were 40 mA and 40 kV, respectively.

#### 3.1.1 Interface reaction control by regulating the temperature and CMAS concentration

Temperature is a key factor affecting the reaction between CMAS and GdPO4, directly influencing the reaction rate and the microstructure of the interface reaction layer [42]. Although the melting point of CMAS (1240 °C) used in this study is higher than 1200 °C, previous studies have shown that CMAS can partially melt and react with TBC materials near this temperature [43,44]. Therefore, interface reactions were conducted at 1200 °C as well as at higher temperatures (1250–1350 °C) to produce the bilayer-structured apatite layer. Figure 1 shows cross-sections of the bilayer-structured apatite layer samples after heat treatment at 1200–1350 °C for 1 h. The bilayer-structured apatite layers all exhibit a bilayer structure consisting of a compact lower layer with dark contrast and an upper layer with light gray contrast, which is consistent with previous findings [36]. As shown in Fig. 1(a), the interface reaction layer is bilayer-structured, but the acicular morphology typical of the upper layer is not distinctly observed. The upper layer of the bilayer-structured apatite layer depicted in Fig. 1(b) has a significant acicular structure. The reaction between CMAS and GdPO4 is significantly enhanced when the temperature is increased to 1300 and 1350 °C, as shown in Figs. 1(c) and 1(d). The products in the upper layer of the bilayer-structured apatite layer exhibit a blocky rather than acicular structure, and numerous acicular and blocky crystals with light gray contrast are diffusely distributed in the residual CMAS. From the above observations, we determined that 1250 °C is more suitable for constructing the desirable bilayer-structured apatite layer.

The microstructure of the bilayer-structured apatite layer is also affected by the concentration of CMAS. Hence, different amounts of CMAS (3–15 mg/cm2) were covered with GdPO4, followed by heat treatment at 1250 °C for 1 h, and the cross-sectional microstructures are shown in Fig. 2. The thickness of the residual CMAS layer gradually increases with increasing CMAS concentration. At a CMAS concentration of 3 mg/cm2, the residual CMAS layer is discontinuous, and the acicular products are only found in the area with residual CMAS (Fig. 2(a)). At a CMAS concentration of 5 mg/cm2, a thin residual CMAS layer (26 μm) and a relatively continuous reaction layer are observed, as expected for the bilayer-structured apatite layer (Fig. 2(b)). The higher-magnification image in Fig. 2(b) (inset) reveals that the acicular upper layer is generally continuous. More importantly, the underlying compact lower layer is sufficiently continuous to effectively prevent molten CMAS penetration. At a CMAS concentration of 10 mg/cm2, the thickness of the residual CMAS layer is approximately 45 μm, and the acicular layer has poor continuity. At a relatively high CMAS concentration (15 mg/cm2), the residual CMAS layer is approximately 135 μm thick, with no acicular layer, as shown in Fig. 2(d). Therefore, to construct a desirable bilayer-structured apatite layer, the content of CMAS used to react with GdPO4 was selected to be 5 mg/cm2.

#### 3.1.2 Interface reaction control by regulating the reaction time

Here, we regulate the reaction time to control the bilayer-structured apatite layers. Figure 3 shows the influence of heat treatment time on the formation of the bilayer-structured apatite layers. Cross-sectional microstructures of the bilayer-structured apatite layers resulting from the reaction of GdPO4 with 5 mg/cm2 CMAS at 1250 °C from 5 to 120 min. The bilayer-structured apatite layers all exhibit a bilayer structure composed of an acicular upper layer and a compact lower layer, both of which change with time. The compact lower layer varies in thickness. When the heat duration is less than 60 min, the thickness of the compact lower layer increases significantly with time, reaching 3.6 μm within the first 60 min, as shown in Figs. 3(a)–3(e). After 60 min, the rate of thickening noticeably decreased, with the layer measuring 4.3 μm at the 120-min mark. The Levenberg‒Marquardt iterative algorithm was used to fit the curve and obtain the functional equation. The increase in the thickness of the compact lower layer slows down and reaches a theoretical maximum of 4.64 μm, as shown in Fig. 4.

Notably, the acicular layer changes not only in thickness but also in its crystal growth orientation. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the reaction between CMAS and GdPO4 at 1250 °C for 5 min is sufficient to form a continuous and compact lower apatite layer (approximately 1 μm thick), but the upper layer consists of sparsely distributed acicular apatite crystals. After 10 min of reaction, the acicular crystals grow to lengths ranging from 1.0 to 4.0 μm, exhibiting an inconsistent orientation, as shown in Fig. 3(b). The number of acicular crystals increases from 20 to 30 min, with their growth direction gradually becoming perpendicular to the compact lower sublayer, as shown in Figs. 3(c) and 3(d). As time further increased to 60 min, the length of the acicular crystals increased, and their growth orientation became uniform, with alignment perpendicular to the compact layer, as shown in Fig. 3(e), which is the desired characteristic against CMAS wetting and adhesion. When the duration is extended to 120 min, some acicular crystals lose their aligned growth orientation and intersect, and some become coarser, as shown in Fig. 3(f). Hence, 60 min is sufficient to construct a desirable bilayer-structured apatite layer for CMAS protection.

Apatite is a compound that exhibits compositional variability within a defined range. The general chemical formula of apatite can be written as A10−x*(BO4)6C2−*y*, which crystallizes in a hexagonal phase with space group *P6*3/*m* (No. 176) [45]. In this structure, A represents monovalent and divalent alkali metal ions (e.g., Na+, Ca2+) or trivalent rare-earth ions (e.g., Gd3+), B represents tetravalent to hexavalent cations (e.g., P5+, Si4+), and C denotes anions (e.g., O2−, OH−). The A-site adopts two distinct coordination environments: the 4f site (AⅠ, CN = 9, *C*3 symmetry) and the 6 h site (AⅡ, CN = 7, *C**s* symmetry) [46,47]. Along the *C*-axis, the AⅠO9 tetrakaidecahedron at the 4f sites forms face-shared chains, whereas the AⅡO7 decahedron at the 6 h sites is connected through vertex- and edge-sharing, with adjacent polyhedra further bridged by the vertices of BO4 tetrahedra [48,49]. In this study, apatite is represented by the formula Ca2+*x*Gd8−*x*(PO4)*x*(SiO4)6−*x*O2, where Gd3+ preferentially occupies the 6 h site (AⅡ), and Ca2+ resides at the 4f site (AⅠ). The structure is stabilized by a network of interconnected PO4 and SiO4 tetrahedra.

Figure 6 shows a schematic illustration of the bilayer-structured apatite layer construction mechanism under different conditions. During the construction of the bilayer-structured apatite layer, two competing mechanisms operate synergistically: (i) diffusion-recrystallization, which determines the formation of the compact Gd-poor apatite lower layer, and (ii) dissolution-reprecipitation, which determines the formation of the acicular Gd-rich apatite upper layer. Previous studies have shown that the formation enthalpy of apatite decreases with the replacement of Gd3+ by Ca2+ and SiO44− by PO43− [50], indicating greater thermodynamic favorability for Gd-poor apatite. Consequently, Gd-poor apatite forms preferentially over Gd-rich apatite, resulting in the preferential formation of a compact lower layer. The formation of the compact lower layer is closely related to ion migration at the CMAS/GdPO4 interface. As shown in Fig. 7, the EDS scan across the CMAS/GdPO4 interface clearly distinguishes the Gd-rich acicular upper layer from the compact Gd-poor lower layer. The Gd concentration profile shows a rapid increase from the CMAS side to a relatively high and stable level within the acicular upper layer, followed by an abrupt decrease to a lower plateau across the compact lower layer, and finally a sharp rise close to the GdPO4 substrate. In contrast, the Ca concentration profile exhibits a stepwise decrease: It declined to a stable level across the acicular upper layer, then decreased slightly to a plateau through the compact lower layer, and finally remained nearly constant at the substrate. This variation in concentration reveals that interdiffusion occurs between CMAS and GdPO4, indicating that diffusion plays a key role in forming the interfacial reaction layer. Additionally, the Gd-poor apatite layer is compact and continuous, without apparent pores or residual melt, exhibiting microstructural features consistent with a gradual recrystallization process following ion diffusion, which ultimately leads to the formation of a compact Gd-poor apatite phase. At high temperatures, when GdPO4 and CMAS are in contact, Gd3+ ions diffuse from GdPO4 into the molten CMAS, creating numerous vacancies in the original lattice, and Ca2+ ions diffuse from CMAS to occupy these vacancies. In addition, a small portion of PO43− is replaced by structurally similar SiO44−, triggering structural reconstruction from the monazite-type GdPO4 lattice to a hexagonal apatite phase. This process results in the formation of a compact Gd-poor apatite layer. As Ca2+ is progressively consumed from CMAS and Gd3+ continues to enrich the CMAS, the conditions favor the secondary reprecipitation of Gd-rich apatite, which subsequently grows into acicular crystals via a screw dislocation growth mechanism [51].

The construction of the bilayer-structured apatite layer strongly depends on the temperature. At 1200 °C, CMAS (with a melting point of 1240 °C in this study) is partially melted and reacts weakly with GdPO4 [10,52], resulting in a relatively thin bilayer-structured apatite layer, as shown in Fig. 6(a). At 1250 °C, CMAS completely melts and reacts with GdPO4, forming a desirable bilayer-structured apatite layer, as shown in Fig. 6(b). At higher temperatures (1300 and 1350 °C), the enhanced interface reaction between CMAS and GdPO4 facilitates the rapid formation of the compact Gd-poor apatite layer through diffusion-recrystallization, concurrent with partial thermal decomposition to form Ca3(PO4)2 [53]. The excessive diffusion of Gd3+ and PO43+ into the CMAS melt reduces the concentration gradients across the interface, thereby suppressing the oriented growth of acicular apatite and resulting in the random dispersion of Gd-rich apatite throughout the CMAS melt, as shown in Fig. 6(c).

The continuity of the bilayer-structured apatite layer is closely related to the CMAS concentration. At a low CMAS concentration, molten CMAS tends to agglomerate on the GdPO4 surface, primarily due to its low surface energy [36], resulting in some isolated molten regions that hinder the development of a continuous reaction layer, as shown in Fig. 6(d). At high CMAS concentrations, the abundance of Ca2+ promotes the continuous formation of Gd-poor apatite, making it difficult to establish conditions favorable for the formation of acicular Gd-rich apatite, as shown in Fig. 6(f). At an appreciable CMAS concentration, the desired bilayer-structured apatite layer can be successfully formed, as shown in Fig. 6(e).

The formation of the bilayer-structured apatite layer also has a distinct dependence on time. For the compact Gd-poor apatite layer, the initial abundance of Ca2+ and SiO43− promotes its formation and growth, with the thickness reaching 3.6 μm within 60 min. As time increases, the concentration of Ca2+ in the residual CMAS decreases, and the thickening compact layer poses a greater barrier to diffusion, thereby slowing further growth of the compact layer. After 120 min, the compact lower layer reaches a thickness of 4.3 μm, approaching the theoretical limit of 4.64 μm. The formation of the acicular Gd-rich apatite layer mainly depends on the diffusion of Gd3+ into the CMAS. In the early stage, only a limited amount of Gd3+ diffuses into the melt, resulting in the formation of small apatites lacking acicular morphology, as shown in Fig. 6(g). With prolonged reaction time, Gd-rich apatite grows preferentially perpendicular to the substrate surface [54], forming an acicular apatite layer. After 60 min, these acicular apatites are uniform in length and orientation, as shown in Fig. 6(h). When the heat treatment exceeds 60 min, the axial growth rate of the acicular crystals significantly decreases, accompanied by an increase in size. This morphological evolution correlates with the thinning of the residual CMAS layer, indicating that reduced diffusion may induce a shift in the growth mode. Moreover, the growth fronts of adjacent crystals may impede the growth of others, causing grain misorientation and reducing the structural order of the acicular layer, as shown in Fig. 6(i).

Interestingly, no Gd-rich apatite phase forms within the thickened Gd-poor apatite layer. As shown in Fig. 7, the Gd concentration rapidly increases and stabilizes at a high level in the acicular Gd-rich apatite upper layer, whereas it sharply decreases to a low and stable level in the compact Gd-poor apatite lower layer. The low Gd concentration in this compact lower layer is insufficient to induce the nucleation of Gd-rich apatite, and its compact microstructure further restricts the infiltration of molten CMAS. Consequently, the Gd-rich apatite does not form in the growing Gd-poor apatite layer but rather nucleates and grows directly from the molten CMAS. As the Ca concentration gradually decreases, Gd3+ ions diffusing into the CMAS preferentially accumulate near the upper region of the compact lower layer, creating a dynamic chemical environment that favors the precipitation of Gd-rich apatite via the dissolution‒reprecipitation mechanism. This restricts its formation to the upper layer, promoting outward growth along the surface of the compact apatite layer rather than growth within the lower layer.

Overall, a desirable bilayer-structured apatite layer can be prepared by regulating the reaction temperature, time, and CMAS concentration, and according to this research, the optimization conditions cover 5 mg/cm2 CMAS on GdPO4 pellets followed by heat treatment at 1250 °C for 1 h. In the obtained bilayer-structured apatite layer, the compact lower layer is sufficiently continuous, with its thickness approaching the limit value, and the acicular upper layer is regular and ordered. No sign of other products except apatites exists. All subsequent experiments were performed on samples prepared under these conditions.

Moreover, a significant amount of Gd3+ is replaced by Ca2+ and diffuses into the melt, contributing to the formation and growth of the acicular apatite crystals. Despite the increase in thickness, the microstructure of the interface reaction layer is preserved without any destruction. The EDS results (Table 2) of regions H and I in Fig. 11(c) show that the chemical composition of the interface reaction layer remains unchanged, indicating superior stability even under exposure to excessive CMAS at 1250 °C for 20 h.

The EDS mapping results in Figs. 11(d)–11(g) demonstrate that the bilayer-structured apatite layer still has excellent ability to prevent CMAS infiltration even after prolonged exposure for up to 20 h. This exceptional and desirable attribute can be attributed to two synergistic mechanisms: (i) the stable compact lower layer physically prevents CMAS infiltration, and (ii) the compositional modification of CMAS when it contacts the bilayer-structured apatite layer. As shown in Fig. 11(a), EDS point analysis reveals that the Si/Ca ratio of the residual CMAS increases to 1.8 after 20 h of exposure, which is 32% higher than the initial value. A higher Si/Ca ratio in CMAS results in a larger viscosity of the melt [57]. As Ca and Si in CMAS continuously participate in interfacial reactions to form apatite, the Si/Ca ratio in the residual CMAS increases noticeably, leading to increased melt viscosity. As a result, CMAS infiltration is retarded.

### 3.3 Molten CMAS wetting behavior on the bilayer-structured apatite layer

The low wettability of molten CMAS on TBC surfaces means that small amounts of CMAS adhered to the TBCs. In the above section, it is confirmed that the compact lower layer of the bilayer-structured apatite layer contributes to the CMAS infiltration-inhibiting ability, and in the following section, we discuss the function of the acicular upper layer from the view of reducing the molten CMAS wettability.

The wettability of CMAS is evaluated primarily by measuring the diameter of the spreading area and the contact angle in high-temperature wetting angle experiments [58,59]. As noted in Section 3.2, long-term heat treatment has some effect on the microstructure of the bilayer-structured apatite layer, which may affect CMAS wettability. Hence, high-temperature wetting angle experiments were performed on the bilayer-structured apatite layers (referred to as the BA layer) and on those after heat treatment for 20 h at 1250 °C (referred to as the H-BA layer), and the results are shown in Fig. 12. On the BA layer, the CMAS droplet diameter is 6.9 mm at 5 min and expands to 8.3 mm at 30 min, with a spreading rate of 0.028 mm/min. The contact angle decreases from 23.0° at 5 min to 17.4° at 30 min, a reduction of 5.6°. On the H-BA layer, the droplet diameter is 7.1 mm at 5 min and expands to 9.2 mm at 30 min. The contact angle is 31.4° at 5 min and decreases to 16.7° at 30 min, a reduction of 14.7°. This finding indicates that long-term heat treatment of the bilayer-structured apatite layer has no negative influence on the molten CMAS wettability, mainly because of the excellent microstructure stability of the bilayer-structured apatite layer. Gd2Zr2O7 is considered a CMAS-resistant TBC material, the CMAS contact angle of which is 6.5° [36], which is lower than that of the bilayer-structured apatite layer in this study. Therefore, the bilayer-structured apatite layer exhibits superior nonwettability against molten CMAS, which could effectively limit CMAS adhesion and spread, mitigating its harmful effects on TBCs.

The surface energy of a material is a key factor influencing CMAS wettability. In general, materials with higher surface energy are more easily wetted. As shown in Fig. 13(a), for the bilayer-structured apatite layer at the initial stage of the wetting experiment, molten CMAS has difficulty completely wetting the surface in a short time because of the low surface energy of the Gd-rich and Gd-poor apatite [36], thereby maintaining a large contact angle. Furthermore, the uniform distribution of acicular apatite crystals results in gaps in the acicular upper layer. The unremoved CMAS droplets gradually infiltrate through the gaps between the acicular crystals and adhere to the compact lower layer under the influence of gravity as wetting continues. The step structure formed by the acicular crystals creates a pinning effect on the three-phase contact line of the CMAS droplet [60]. When the droplet needs to stride over the steps, it must overcome a high energy barrier, which confines it below the steps and restricts the spreading of the molten CMAS, thus maintaining a large contact angle, as shown in Fig. 13(a).

As shown in Fig. 8, the acicular upper layer transforms into a hollow web-like structure after long-term heat treatment. In this case, the molten CMAS is mainly in contact with the raised portion of the surface of the acicular upper layer composed of Gd-rich apatite to keep the air in the pores, which is consistent with the Cassie model (Eq. (1)) [61,62]:

(1) cos⁡θc=f1⋅cos⁡θ1+f2⋅cos⁡θ2

where θ*c is the equilibrium contact angle of the Cassie model; *θ*1 and *θ*2 are the intrinsic contact angles of the droplet on the two substances, respectively; *f*1 and *f*2 are the area fractions of the two substances on the droplet surface. When one of the substances is air, the intrinsic contact angle of the droplet on air is 180° (*f*1 + *f*2 = 1). Eq. (2) can be expressed as [33]:

(2) cos⁡θc=fs⋅(cos⁡θs+1)−1

where *θ*s is the intrinsic contact angle of a droplet on a solid surface, and *f*s is the area fraction of the solid on the liquid surface, which plays a decisive role in the surface contact angle (*θ*c).

The gas‒liquid‒solid three-phase contact approaches a gas‒liquid contact as *f*s approaches 0°, leading *θ*c to approach 180°. Therefore, reducing the solid fraction (*f*s) is beneficial for increasing the surface contact angle (*θ*c) [59]. Considering the hollow web-like structure of the upper layer, the direct contact area is reduced when CMAS droplets are adhered, thereby maintaining a large contact angle and hindering the wetting and spreading of CMAS, as shown in Fig. 13(b). Thus, excellent nonwettability against molten CMAS of the bilayer-structured apatite layer is preserved, even as the surface structure is altered by long-term heat treatment.

The nonwettability against molten CMAS of the bilayer-structured apatite layer is attributed mainly to the acicular upper layer. The low surface energy of apatite inhibits CMAS wetting, and the acicular microstructure restricts its spread via a pinning effect. Under actual turbine blade conditions, the strong centrifugal force generated by high-speed rotation works synergistically with the dual protection of the acicular upper layer, facilitating rapid removal of CMAS and effectively preventing its infiltration into the coating. In addition, the low adhesion work between CMAS and Gd-rich apatite suggests weak interfacial bonding, further promoting CMAS detachment under such conditions [33,36].

Therefore, the acicular apatite upper layer contributes to low CMAS wettability, which effectively prevents the adhesion and spreading of molten CMAS, fundamentally alleviating the CMAS corrosion issue. Even if some CMAS remains on the bilayer-structured apatite layer surface, the continuous and compact apatite lower layer can act as a barrier to prevent CMAS infiltration. In addition, the interaction between CMAS and the bilayer-structured apatite layer can alter the CMAS composition, increasing its viscosity and slowing its infiltration. Therefore, the bilayer-structured apatite layer has a three-level barrier against CMAS, making it a highly promising CMAS protective layer.

## 4 Conclusions

In this study, the preparation of bilayer-structured apatite layers, as well as their high-temperature stability and CMAS resistance, were investigated. The conclusions are as follows:

(1) The bilayer-structured apatite layers consisting of an acicular Gd-rich apatite upper layer and a compact Gd-poor lower layer were prepared by regulating the pre-reaction temperature, time, and CMAS concentration. The optimal conditions were covering 5 mg/cm2 CMAS on GdPO4 pellets, followed by heat treatment at 1250 °C for 1 h.

(2) The bilayered apatite layers retained their microstructure integrity without phase transformation at 1250 °C for 50 h, revealing their excellent high-temperature stability. It also showed superior thermal shock resistance, surviving 100 cycles at 1200 °C without cracking or delamination. Even after exposure to excess CMAS at 1250 °C for 20 h, the bilayer-structured apatite layer retained an intact structure and completely inhibited CMAS infiltration into the underlying GdPO4.

(3) The low surface energy and unique structure of the bilayer-structured apatite layer enabled the CMAS droplets to maintain a large contact angle (17.4°), approximately three times greater than that of Gd2Zr2O7, another advanced CMAS-resistant TBC material, indicating excellent nonwettability against molten CMAS. The bilayer-structured apatite layer after heat treatment at 1250 °C for 20 h still maintained the low wettability of the molten CMAS on the surface, demonstrating excellent resistance to CMAS under long-term high-temperature service conditions. This property effectively reduces CMAS adhesion and facilitates CMAS removal under actual operating conditions. Therefore, the bilayer-structured apatite layer has great potential as a promising protective layer for TBCs.

An innovative strategy is proposed in this study to address the CMAS corrosion issue in TBCs. The wetting and infiltration of molten CMAS are effectively suppressed by constructing a protective layer of bilayer-structured apatite. This approach is expected to fundamentally resolve the corrosion issue at its root by reducing the wettability of molten CMAS and decreasing the adhesion of CMAS on the TBC surface through the controlled construction of a bilayer-structured apatite protective layer. In future work, we will focus on directly applying this bilayer-structured apatite protective layer onto TBC surfaces.
