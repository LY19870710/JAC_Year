## 1 Introduction

The rapid advancement of technology in recent decades has led to increased reliance on electronic devices for communication, navigation, and various consumer applications. However, this technological boom has also given rise to significant challenges, primarily due to electromagnetic interference and pollution, which can degrade the performance and reliability of electronic systems [1−5]. Consequently, there is a growing demand for effective electromagnetic wave (EMW)-absorbing materials that can mitigate these interference and pollution effects. EMW-absorbing materials have the capacity to absorb incident electromagnetic waves significantly, effectively converting electromagnetic energy into thermal energy [6,7]. These materials play crucial roles in improving device functionality, enhancing privacy and security, and ensuring compliance with regulatory standards regarding electromagnetic emissions [8−10]. In recent years, many studies have been conducted in the field of EMW-absorbing materials, such as interface construction [11], electrical conductivity [12,13], defect manipulation [14,15], morphology control [16,17], heteroatom doping [18,19], and heterogeneous structure design [20]. Among these approaches, atom doping is recognized as an effective method for manipulating the intrinsic electronic structure of crystals, thereby enhancing the attenuation capacity of electromagnetic waves [21,22].

Transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) have attracted extensive attention and research attention because of their special two-dimensional structure, large specific surface area, adjustable band structure, and band gap, customizable anionic and cationic components, and various phase structures [23,24]. Among them, MoS2 is a representative TMD characterized by molybdenum atoms sandwiched between two layers of sulfur atoms, exhibiting a hexagonal crystal structure [25]. Owing to variations in the arrangement and stacking order of the S–Mo–S atomic layers, MoS2 has multiple phases, with the most commonly and extensively studied being the 1T and 2H phases, making it suitable for a variety of applications, including photodetectors, transistors, and optoelectronic devices [26,27]. The 1T phase of MoS2 demonstrates metallic properties, with an electrical conductivity that is 107 times greater than that of 2H-MoS2 [28]. This unique coexistence of distinct phases and their significant property differences facilitate the reconciliation of impedance matching and attenuation capability in MoS2 as EMW absorbing materials.

One notable attribute of MoS2 is its ability to incorporate various dopants and form hybrid structures with other materials. MoS2 is easily engineered to produce diverse geometries with distinctive surfaces and rich interfaces, making it widely regarded as a promising novel EMW absorbing material [29]. Compared with those of the dielectric component in EMW-absorbing composites, the dielectric and EMW absorption properties of MoS2 itself have not been fully explored or improved [30]. The high tunability of MoS2, which involves morphology regulation, hierarchical structure design, phase engineering, and particularly doping modifications, enables it to function independently as an EMW absorber, which is fundamental for the development of high-performance MoS2-based EMW-absorbing materials. Specifically, heterogeneous atom doping has been demonstrated to modulate the electrical and dielectric properties of MoS2. Cheng *et al*. [31] employed first-principles calculations to investigate the stability and electronic structure of MoS2 doped with elements from groups IVB, VB, and VIB, confirming the feasibility of creating doped structures with transition metals such as Mn, Fe, Co, and Zn as substitutes for Mo. In the context of the experimental synthesis of EMW-absorbing materials, hydrothermal doping stands out as a more accessible and high-yield technique than the sophisticated and elaborate methods used for doping TMDs, such as chemical vapor deposition, ion implantation, and solid-state reactions [30]. The effectiveness of Co [32], Cu [33], Ni [34], and Mn [35]-doped MoS2 has been validated through hydrothermal methods. Fe is distinguished by its unique physicochemical properties, along with being abundant, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly, making it a popular candidate for extensive applications and research in EMW absorption [36,37]. Doping MoS2 with Fe can alter its electronic structure and bandgap characteristics, providing additional electronic states and generating additional active sites [38]. Despite previous investigations into the EMW absorption properties of transition metal-doped MoS2, there is still insufficient understanding of the structural changes, charge transfer processes, and evolution mechanisms that enhance its EMW absorption performance. Therefore, it is crucial to establish clear and profound connections between Fe substitution doping and enhanced polarization loss mechanisms, which will provide valuable insights for designing high-performance EMW absorbing materials and elucidating the corresponding electromagnetic mechanisms.

Herein, MoS2 featuring a high proportion of the 1T phase was synthesized for the first time via a hydrothermal method with varying concentrations of doped Fe. The effects of doping on the 1T–2H phase transition, lattice distortion, and collapse, second phase formation and evolution, dielectric properties, and EMW absorption performance were systematically investigated. The results demonstrate that Fe doping is an effective strategy for tuning the phase composition and electronic properties and boosting the EMW absorption performance of MoS2. This work provides valuable insights for modifying TMDs, optimizing impedance matching characteristics, and improving energy attenuation capabilities, ultimately paving the way for the development of novel EMW absorbing materials.

### 2.1 Materials

Ammonium molybdate tetrahydrate (H24Mo7N6O24·4H2O, analytical grade (AR)), thiourea (CH4N2S, AR), ethyl alcohol, and Iron chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, ≥ 98%) were purchased from Aladdin, Inc., China. All the chemicals were of analytical grade and were used without further purification. The deionized (DI) water used in this experiment was homemade in the laboratory.

### 2.2 Synthesis of MoS2 and Fe-doped MoS2

The synthesis process of in situ Fe-doped MoS2 is illustrated in Fig. 1. On the basis of our previous work [39], 1T phase-rich MoS2 self-assembled micron flowers were synthesized through a facile hydrothermal method. Typically, 2.119 g H24Mo7N6O24·4H2O and 4.567 g CH4N2S were fully dissolved in 60 mL DI water. The solution was magnetically stirred for 30 min, changing from a colorless state to a light blue transparent state. The solution was then transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless-steel hydrothermal mixture and heated at 200 °C for 18 h. After the mixture cooled to room temperature, the black powder was centrifuged and washed with water and alcohol three times each. After the last ethanol wash, high-purity 1T-MoS2 powder was obtained by air drying for 12 h at room temperature and named sample FM0.

The in situ* Fe doping experiment is a modification of the aforementioned experiments. After the solution formed a light blue transparent ligand solution, 0.0163, 0.0328, 0.1004, 0.1709, and 0.3608 g FeCl3·6H2O were added to the solution, corresponding to Fe doping ratios of 0.5%, 1%, 3%, 5%, and 10%, respectively. The solution was magnetically stirred for another 20 min until the solution became uniform and stable again. The solution was then transferred to a Teflon-lined stainless-steel hydrothermal mixture and heated at 200 °C for 18 h. After the mixture cooled to room temperature, the black powder was centrifuged and washed with water and alcohol three times each. After the last ethanol wash, all the samples were obtained by air drying for 12 h at room temperature and named samples FM1, FM2, FM3, FM4, and FM5, respectively (Fe-doped MoS2 samples 1–5).

### 2.3 Characterizations

The morphology and element mappings of the samples were obtained by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Merlin Compact, ZEISS, Germany; with an accelerating voltage of 20 kV). The lattice structure and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern were observed via a transmission electron microscope (TEM; Talos F200X, Thermo Scientific, USA). High-angle angular dark field-scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images of the lattice structure were obtained via an aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscope (JEM ARM 200F, JEOL, Japan) equipped with a cold field emission gun operated at 200 kV. A powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Empyrean, Panalytical Instruments, the Netherlands; with Cu Kα radiation) was employed to determine the crystalline structure of all the samples across the range of 5°–90° at a scanning step of 10 (°)·min−1. The stage information of the elements and phase composition in all the samples was obtained via an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA). The specific surface area and pore size information of the samples were obtained via a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis meter (BSD-PS1/2, Best Instrument, China).

### 2.4 Calculation and simulation details

All first-principles calculations of the atomic and electronic structures were performed via the CASTEP program within the Materials Studio 2020 package, which is based on plane-wave density functional theory (DFT). The exchange‒correlation effects were represented via the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) function. The energy convergence criterion was set to 1×10−5 eV·atom−1, with a maximum force of 0.05 eV·Å−1, a maximum stress of 0.05 GPa, and an energy cutoff of 450.0 eV. The Brillouin zone integration employed a *k*-point mesh density of 3 × 3 × 1. A vacuum gap of approximately 20 Å was maintained to ensure negligible interactions between the system and its mirror images.

The CST Studio Suite 2020 was employed to simulate the radar cross section (RCS) signal of the EMW absorbing materials. A widely recognized square double-layer panel model (200 mm × 200 mm) based on a metal backplane was constructed. The top layer, which serves as the EMW absorber, had a thickness of 1.61 mm, whereas the bottom layer was composed of a perfect electric conductive (PEC) layer with a thickness of 0.5 mm. The monitoring frequency was set to 16.5 GHz. The model was positioned in the *X*O*Y* plane, with EMW propagation directed negatively along the *Z*-axis and polarization oriented positively along the *X*-axis.

## 3 Results and discussion

XRD was employed to determine the phase structure of the sample. As shown in Fig. 1(b), the diffraction peaks at 9.4°, 18.6°, 32.2°, and 57.4° are indexed to (002), (004), (100), and (110) crystal planes of 1T-MoS2, respectively [41,42]. After the introduction of Fe atoms into the system, the strongest peak at approximately 9.4° is slightly weakened, indicating that Fe doping somewhat reduces the crystallinity of 1T-MoS2. Owing to the smaller size of the Fe atom than of the Mo atom, the doping of Fe leads to a shift in the MoS2(002) crystal plane diffraction peak toward a higher angle. After the raw data are fitted to obtain precise peak positions, the (002) crystal plane diffraction peaks for samples FM0 to FM5 are determined to be 9.36°, 9.48°, 9.46°, 9.44°, 9.46°, and 9.48°, respectively (Fig. 1(c)). This means that the (002) crystal plane spacings are 9.44, 9.32, 9.34, 9.36, 9.34, and 9.32 Å, respectively. The shift of the (002) crystal plane diffraction peak also proves that Fe is successfully doped into the lattice of MoS2. The heterogeneous Fe atoms in the lattice of MoS2 cause strong lattice distortion due to differences in the outer electronic structure and atomic size. As illustrated in Figs. 1(e) and 1(f), owing to the smaller atomic size of Fe relative to that of Mo, the atoms surrounding the heterogeneous Fe atom collapse toward it, causing distortion of the lattice. In addition, the introduction of Fe atoms changes the pristine charge distribution, resulting in strong charge interactions. Compared with pure MoS2, Fe doping sites exhibit more electron aggregation regions, which destroy the original symmetric periodic positive and negative charge distributions and enhance the dipole effect, as shown in Fig. 1(f2). As the amount of introduced Fe increases and saturates within the MoS2 lattice, a second phase, FeS2, gradually emerges. The diffraction peaks at 28.52°, 33.05°, 37.08°, 40.77°, 47.43°, and 56.28° are indexed to the (* 1¯11), (200), ( 1¯02), ( 1¯ 1¯2), (0 2¯2), and ( 1¯ 1¯3) crystal planes of the FeS2 crystal (JCPDS Card No. 71-1680), respectively.

The morphology of sample FM0 is a micron flower shape assembled from MoS2 nanosheets. The micron-scale flowers are approximately 3 μm in diameter, as depicted in Fig. 2(a1). The lamellar structure allows multiple reflections and scattering of the EMW and expands the EMW path, which is significant for dissipating electromagnetic wave energy [43]. The self-assembled structure of the nanosheets greatly increases the porosity, thus optimizing the impedance matching [44]. In addition, the interconnected nanosheets constitute a conductive network path, which generates an induced current under the stimulation of an external alternating electric field, rapidly consuming electromagnetic wave energy [45]. This three-dimensional hierarchical structure with abundant voids forms a sufficient air interface, which can be used as a resistance-capacitance junction to induce space charge accumulation and promote polarization loss [46]. As the amount of Fe introduced into the solution system gradually increased, the light blue color of the original ligand solution gradually changed to light yellow, as shown in Fig. 1(d). The Fe doping process slightly affects the self-assembly process of MoS2 nanosheets, resulting in some scattered MoS2 nanosheets, as shown in Figs. 2(b1), 2(c1), and Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). A second phase with a cube shape appeared in sample FM3, as illustrated in Fig. 2(b1). The element mapping clearly reveals that the sample is rich in Fe (Fig. 2(b4)). On the basis of the XRD diffraction peaks in Fig. 1(b), the cubes in Fig. 2(b1), measuring approximately 2 μm, are FeS2. The morphology of the FeS2 second phase gradually evolves with increasing Fe concentration, forming an angular star shape, as shown in Fig. 2(c1) and Fig. S1 in the ESM.

XPS was used to analyze the chemical state of the element and the phase composition of MoS2. The presence of Fe, Mo, S, C, and O signals can be clearly observed from the full XPS spectra, as shown in Fig. 2(d). The signal of Fe is generally weak, especially for samples FM0–FM3. The XPS high-resolution spectral deconvolution results of Mo 3d are typically used to determine the phase composition of MoS2. As shown in Fig. 2(e), the peaks located at approximately 232.9, 232.1, 229.6, and 228.9 eV are characterized as 2H-Mo 3d3/2, 1T-Mo 3d3/2, 2H-Mo 3d5/2, and 1T-Mo 3d5/2, respectively, whose area ratios can be used to determine the 1T-2H phase ratio of MoS2 [28]. The 1T phase percentage of samples FM0–FM5 after the integration of the 1T and 2H-MoS2 signal peaks is summarized in Fig. 2(h). The percentage of the 1T phase in the synthesized sample FM0 reaches 76%. The introduction of Fe atoms inhibits the growth of the 1T crystal structure, resulting in a continuous decrease in the proportion of the 1T phase. The proportion of the 1T phase in sample FM5 was only 51%. This reveals that the introduction of Fe can trigger the phase transformation of MoS2 from the 1T phase to the 2H phase. In addition, the peak at approximately 235.2 eV is derived from Mo6+ produced by a small amount of oxidation. There is also an interference signal of S 2s at approximately 226 eV [47].

With respect to XPS S 2p spectra in Fig. 2(f), there are several peaks representing 2H-S 2P1/2 (163.2 eV), 1T-S 2P1/2 (163.0 eV), 2H-S 2P3/2 (162.0 eV), and 1T-S 2P3/2 (161.3 eV) [42,48]. In samples FM1‒FM5, an additional signal peak corresponding to the Fe‒S bond emerges at the higher energy end, indicating that charge transfer results from the introduction of Fe [49]. As shown in Fig. 1(e2), Fe doping weakens the charge aggregation of the S atoms around it, thereby causing the binding energy to shift toward higher energy. The specific Fe‒S binding energy is presented in Fig. 2(h) to better illustrate its trend of variation. The binding energy gradually increases, indicating a stronger charge interaction within the system. This enhancement is attributed, on the one hand, to the increased amount of Fe doped in the lattice and, on the other hand, to the greater formation of the second phase FeS2. Strong electronic interactions can lead to changes in ionic valence states, which inevitably results in lattice disorder and the formation of new phases [50]. This observation is consistent with the XRD results. For the XPS spectrum of Fe 2p, peaks at 725.5, 721.2.5, 711.9, and 708.9 eV can be seen in Fig. 2(g), corresponding to Fe3+ 2p1/2, Fe2+ 2p1/2, Fe3+ 2p3/2, and Fe2+ 2p3/2, respectively [51]. Owing to the absence or smaller amount of Fe doping, it is difficult for samples FM0‒FM2 to observe the Fe signal peak from the background noise, as shown in Fig. 2(g) and Fig. S2 in the ESM.

The variation in the specific surface area of Fe-doped MoS2 was analyzed via BET nitrogen adsorption‒desorption isotherms. As shown in Fig. 2(i), these samples show typical type IV isotherms, suggesting the mesoporous structure of the materials. The specific surface areas of samples FM0, FM3, and FM5 are 12.3, 21.9, and 14.8 m2·g−1, respectively. This occurred because the introduction of Fe destroys the original complete MoS2 self-assembled micron flower to a certain extent, forming many non-self-assembled nanosheets and thus increasing the specific surface area. When excess Fe was introduced, the appearance of the second-phase FeS2 resulted in a decrease in the specific surface area. As shown in the illustration in Fig. 2(i), the pore size distribution of these samples ranges from 20 to 40 nm, confirming the presence of a rich hierarchical structure within the composites, which facilitates sufficient space charge polarization and optimal impedance matching.

Figure 3 shows the morphology, lattice fringes, and atomic structure of samples FM0 and FM3 in the TEM images. As shown in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b), MoS2 appears as a micron flower-like structure assembled from nanosheets. In Figs. 3(h) and 3(i), clear and well-defined lattice structures of 1T-MoS2 and 2H-MoS2 can be observed. The lattice spacing of 0.3 nm corresponds to the (100) plane of 1T-MoS2, whereas 0.27 nm corresponds to the (100) plane of the 2H phase. Owing to the significantly greater atomic number of Mo than of S, the contrast of S atoms in the HRTEM image of the 1T phase is too weak to be detected, resulting in clearly defined triangular structures representing individual Mo atoms, as shown in Fig. 3(c) [52]. In contrast, within the 2H phase structure, the overlapping of S atoms in the Z* direction leads to signal superposition, allowing for the observation of S atomic signals, which ultimately manifests as a honeycomb structure, as shown in Fig. 3(d) and Fig. S3 in the ESM [53]. Fe doping clearly leads to an increased proportion of the 2H phase and introduces grievous lattice distortion and discontinuous lattice fringes, as illustrated in Fig. 3(i). These lattice defects result in stronger internal stress, disrupting the charge distribution within the pristine lattice and causing charge redistribution and accumulation, thereby facilitating polarization loss [54]. In addition, in the HAADF-STEM image in Fig. 3(l), the presence of a Mo vacancy can be clearly observed on the monolayer MoS2. The abundant vacancies disrupt the surrounding charge balance, leading to atomic displacement and lattice distortion, which induces defect dipoles and enhances electron migration and hopping characteristics [55,56]. Figure 3(e) illustrates the cubic morphology of FeS2, which is surrounded by a small number of MoS2 nanosheets. The weakly contrasted diffraction rings corresponding to the (200) and (311) planes in the SAED pattern confirm its structural characteristics and relatively low crystallinity (Fig. 3(g)). The 0.27 nm interplanar spacing observed in Fig. 3(f) corresponds to the (200) plane of FeS2 [57]. Interestingly, the HAADF-STEM image of MoS2 in Fig. 3(j) clearly reveals a moiré lattice, which is formed by the mutual rotation of the two atomic layers by 10° (Fig. 3(k)). The moiré lattice of MoS2 introduces new energy bands and exhibits the characteristics of a topological insulator, resulting in the generation of additional charge carriers and an improvement in carrier mobility [58,59]. This advancement promotes the formation of heterostructures between different two-dimensional materials and provides new design pathways for enabling heterojunction polarization.

The EMW absorption performance of all the samples is presented in Fig. 4. A reflection loss (RL) of less than −10 dB indicates that 90% of the incident EMW energy is absorbed, which is commonly referred to as effective absorption [60]. The frequency range where RL 2 nanosheets and the high conductivity of 1T-MoS2 [61]. The sample FM0 demonstrates weaker absorption performance, characterized by a narrow effective absorption bandwidth (i.e., EAB = 4.5 GHz, 10.9–15.5 GHz) and lower absorption intensity (minimum reflection loss (RLmin) = −26.8 dB, 17.7 GHz). This is attributed to the impedance mismatch caused by excessive conductivity. At low doping concentrations, the impact on the microwave absorption performance remains relatively weak, as evidenced by the lower absorption capabilities of samples FM1 and FM2 in Figs. 4(b) and 4(c). In contrast, the sample FM3 exhibits enhanced EMW absorption performance, achieving EAB = 5.1 GHz (12.6–17.7 GHz) with a thickness of 1.77 mm and RLmin = −60.6 dB at 16.5 GHz with a thickness of 1.61 mm, as shown in Fig. 4(d). This improvement is attributed to the introduction of additional defects, vacancies, lattice distortion-induced polarization losses, and interfacial polarization caused by the second phase caused by Fe doping. In general, the response of materials to EMW can be evaluated by the permittivity ( εr=ε′−iε″) and permeability ( μr=μ′−iμ″) of the materials. It is generally believed that  ε′ and  μ′ represent the storage capacity of the materials for EMW energy, whereas  ε″ and  μ″ represent the dissipation capacity for electromagnetic energy [64]. Both MoS2 and FeS2 are nonmagnetic materials, so their permeabilities are close to ( μr=1+0i), with some test fluctuations, as shown in Fig. 5(d). With respect to the permittivity curves, we found that all samples exhibited an obvious frequency dispersion phenomenon in the 1–18 GHz range, which is typically defined as dielectric dispersion behavior and is attributed to the increase in hysteresis in the polarization response when the high-frequency electric field changes. Sample FM0 results in the greatest  ε′ and  ε″ due to the high conductivity of the high-purity 1T phase, as shown in Fig. 5(a). The real part of the permittivity ranges from 18.1 to 8.9, and the imaginary part ranges from 10 to 5.1. The incorporation of Fe into the MoS2 lattice has two contrasting effects on the dielectric response. On the one hand, Fe doping induces a phase transition from the 1T to 2H phase in MoS2, which has been reported to directly cause a reduction in the permittivity [39]. On the other hand, the introduction of Fe leads to an increase in defects, which disrupt the lattice periodicity and trigger extensive defect-induced polarization, serving as polarization centers that increase dielectric loss [65]. For samples FM1 and FM2, the significant decrease in the 1T MoS2 masks the favorable factors contributing to the permittivity (Fig. 2(h)), resulting in a relatively weak dielectric loss capability. In contrast, for sample FM3, the reduction of the 1T phase to samples FM1 and FM2 occurs at a relatively slow rate, allowing the aforementioned enhancement factors to dominate. Furthermore, a greater number of 2H phases emerge and grow on pristine 1T phase flakes, creating more 1T‒2H interface [28]. The newly formed FeS2 also establishes heterogeneous interfaces with the surrounding MoS2 (Fig. 3(e)). These heterogeneous interfaces, characterized by different Fermi levels, facilitate charge transfer and accumulation, leading to the dissipation of EMW energy under alternating electric fields [11]. Ultimately, this results in an enhanced capability for electromagnetic wave loss in sample FM3. For sample FM4, there is a relatively pronounced reduction in the 1T phase. Conversely, the newly formed FeS2 attaches to the preexisting cubic FeS2, forming angular star-shaped aggregates that do not provide sufficient enhancement of interfacial polarization, as shown in Fig. S1 in the ESM. In the case of sample FM5, in addition to significantly greater interface polarization from the secondary phase, stronger charge transfer between the Fe and S atoms also contributes to the enhanced polarization effects within the system (Fig. 2(h)) [49]. The dielectric loss factor in Fig. 5(c) is commonly used to evaluate the dielectric loss capacity of materials. Samples FM3 and FM5 have large dielectric loss factors, which emphasizes the important role of heterogeneous atoms, defects, and interface polarization. A smaller  Δ value indicates better impedance matching characteristics, which allows more EMW to enter the materials rather than being strongly reflected at the surface. As the amount of Fe doping increases, the deep blue region on the delta map gradually diminishes, reflecting an optimization of the impedance characteristics. This enhancement can be attributed to the introduction of vacancies, heteroatoms, and lattice distortions resulting from Fe doping, as well as the reduction in the 1T phase and the effects of heterojunctions. To better elucidate the actual microwave absorption and radar stealth characteristics of the materials under far-field conditions, the RCS was simulated via CST software. Consequently, the synthesized absorbers were coated as a layer stealth onto a PEC measuring 200 mm × 200 mm, simulating the effect of absorbing materials on reducing the target RCS in realistic environments, as shown in Fig. 6(i). When the plane wave is incident perpendicularly, the reflected signal reaches its maximum and gradually decreases as the angle increases. Among all the simulation results, the PEC plate has a markedly higher RCS value than the other samples do, indicating that the synthesized absorbing coating has a substantial stealth effect. Notably, the sample FM3 has the lowest RCS value, confirming its superior absorbing performance compared with the other samples. To provide a clearer comparison of the effectiveness of the absorbing coating in reducing the RCS of the PEC plate, we monitored the RCS values of each sample at several angles and calculated the reductions relative to the PEC [73], as shown in Fig. 6(k). The results indicate that sample FM3 achieves a maximum RCS reduction of 35 dB, with at least a 5.9 dB reduction at 60°, thus demonstrating superior stealth performance.

## 4 Conclusions

Overall, we successfully incorporated Fe into MoS2 with a high proportion of the 1T phase via *in situ* doping through a hydrothermal method, resulting in a series of impactful effects and evolutionary processes. The doping of Fe led to lattice distortion and collapse, inducing a 1T‒2H phase transition in the MoS2 lattice, whereas excessive Fe doping facilitated the formation and evolution of a second phase. The synergistic effects of heterogeneous atoms, vacancies, interfaces, and phase transitions optimized the polarization loss and conductive loss, significantly enhancing the EMW absorption performance. Notably, the gradual increase in Fe doping effectively reconciled the contradiction between attenuation capability and impedance matching, allowing the sample FM3 to achieve a remarkable EAB of 5.1 GHz at 1.77 mm and an RLmin of −60.6 dB at 1.61 mm. From this perspective, this study presents a novel experimental approach for tuning the electrical properties of MoS2 and for the development of high-performance EMW absorbers.
