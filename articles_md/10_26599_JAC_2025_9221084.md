## 1 Introduction

With the vigorous development and widespread application of 5G technology, electromagnetic waves have significantly improved the convenience for both humans and society [1−6]. Moreover, electromagnetic wave pollution has attracted extensive interest, as it not only distorts precision instruments but also threatens human health [7−11]. Therefore, the design and development of efficient electromagnetic wave-absorbing materials have garnered extensive attention from the scientific community. Most researchers have explored bifunctional electromagnetic wave-absorbing materials, such as those with thermal insulation [12,13] and anticorrosive (inorganic corrosion) properties [14−16], whereas excellent electromagnetic wave-absorbing materials with antibacterial properties are rarely reported for application in organic corrosion environments.

In the medical field, many electromagnetic wave-absorbing coatings are applied in medical apparatuses and instruments to ensure that the device works properly or reduces electromagnetic wave pollution. In addition, the application of antibacterial materials also plays an important role in the field of medical devices [17]. On the one hand, antibacterial materials can effectively prevent infection by inhibiting or killing bacteria and improving the quality of life and treatment effect of patients; on the other hand, electromagnetic wave-absorbing coatings can be corroded by bacteria, failing their electromagnetic wave absorption performance. Therefore, the development of an electromagnetic wave-absorbing coating with antibacterial properties is urgently needed.

The heavy use of antibiotics has allowed bacteria to develop resistance, leading to bacteria being unable to be killed by conventional antibiotics [18]. Metal-based antibacterial materials, such as Ag-based materials, are among the most promising sterilization agents for preventing biofilm formation and bacterial adhesion [19]. Xu *et al.* [18] designed and prepared Co(Ni,Ag)/Fe(Al,Cr)2O4 composites for antibacterial application and reported that Ag-containing composites can kill *E. coli-* and *S. aureus*-based photothermal conditions. They also argued that the Fe(Al,Cr)2O4 component can promote the release of Ag+ ions to achieve the progressive release of Ag+ ions. Guo *et al.* [20] prepared Ag/SiOC composite fibers by electrospinning. The Ag/SiOC fiber mats exhibited excellent antibacterial activity against *E. coli* and *S. aureus*. Although Ag-containing composites possess advantageous sterilization performance, the common metal Ag cannot be used as an electromagnetic wave absorber owing to its high density and impedance mismatch.

In recent years, heterogeneous interface engineering, which can effectively regulate impedance matches and electromagnetic parameters, has gained popularity in the scientific community [21,22]. To date, various composites with multiple components have been developed to increase the attenuation ability of electromagnetic waves via interface polarization [23−25]. Sun *et al.* [26] synthesized one-dimensional Ag@NC‒Co@NC core‒shell composites that exhibited efficient electromagnetic wave absorption, achieving a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −57.6 dB. Liu *et al.* [27] rationally designed hollow CuS@CoS2 nano boxes through interface engineering, which showed excellent electromagnetic wave absorption performance resulting from the synergistic effect of interfacial polarization and multiple scattering. Liu *et al.* [28] designed hollow multishell SiC@C@polyaniline (PANI) nanoparticles via a template method combined with an *in situ* polymerization method. They argued that the PANI shell can improve conduction loss and that multishell structures can optimize impedance matching. These results indicate that rationally designing the components and interfaces can significantly enhance the electromagnetic wave absorption performance. In addition, heterogeneous interface engineering can prevent the aggregation of metal particles, resulting in the progressive release of antibacterial agents. Therefore, exploring advanced materials with both electromagnetic wave absorption and antibacterial properties via heterogeneous interface engineering technology is a practical and desirable approach.

In this work, SiOC/Ag composites were fabricated via a simple liquid phase method. Ag nanoparticles were embedded in the carbon-rich SiOC nanospheres, forming many heterointerfaces, which can attenuate electromagnetic wave energy via interfacial polarization loss. In addition, the high surface areas of the SiOC/Ag composites increase the number of reflections and scattering of incident electromagnetic waves, increasing the energy consumption. Finally, a variety of dissipation mechanisms endow the SiOC/Ag composites with excellent electromagnetic wave absorption performance, with an RLmin value of 58.03 dB. Moreover, the SiOC/Ag composites exhibit good sterilization by the reactive oxygen species (ROS) mechanism, which indicates that they have significant potential applications for the coating of medical devices.

### 2.1 Materials

Tetraethoxysilane and dimethyl dimethoxy silane were supplied by Zhengzhou Alpha Chemical Co., Ltd. 3-Aminophenol, AgNO3 standard solution (0.1 mol/L), and formaldehyde were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Absolute ethyl alcohol, ammonium hydroxide, and deionized water were utilized without further purification.

### 2.2 Fabrication of SiOC/Ag composites

First, the SiOC nanospheres were prepared by reacting tetraethoxysilane with dimethyl dimethoxy silane [22] in an alkaline solution. Then, 1 g of 3-aminophenol, 1.4 mL of formaldehyde, and 10 mL of AgNO3 standard solution were added to the above alkaline solution containing SiOC nanospheres. The SiOC/Ag precursor was subsequently obtained after stirring for 24 h, centrifuging, and drying. Finally, the as-obtained SiOC/Ag precursor was pyrolyzed in an Ar atmosphere at 800 °C for 4 h to fabricate SiOC/Ag composites. The as-obtained composites were labeled SiOC/Ag-1, SiOC/Ag-2, and SiOC/Ag-3, corresponding to 10, 15, and 20 mL of AgNO3 standard solution, respectively, where *Y* in SiOC/Ag-3-*Y* is the content of SiOC/Ag-3 in the electromagnetic wave absorption test.

### 2.3 Characterizations

A scanning electron microscope (SEM; Helions Nanolab 600i, USA) was performed to characterize the morphology of the SiOC/Ag composites. An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; X′Pert, the Netherlands) was used to investigate the phase composition of the SiOC/Ag composites. A transmission electron microscope (TEM; CM300, USA) was performed to characterize the microstructure of the SiOC/Ag composites. An X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; Thermo Fisher, USA) was used to record the surface element composition of the SiOC/Ag composites. The specific surface area and pore size distribution of the SiOC/Ag composites were investigated via a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller analyzer (BET; BSD-660S A3, China). The room-temperature electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements of the SiOC/Ag composites were recorded by an EPR spectrometer (Emxplus 10/12, Bruke, Germany). The release of Ag+ was recorded via an inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometer (ICP, 2060T, China).

### 2.4 Electromagnetic wave absorption tests and radar cross-section (RCS) simulation

The relative complex permittivity of the SiOC/Ag composites was recorded by a vector network analyzer (E5063A, Keysight, USA) via a coaxial method. A total of 30 wt% of the SiOC/Ag composites were mixed with 70 wt% paraffin wax. Test samples with an inner diameter of 3.04 mm and an outer diameter of 7 mm were prepared for analysis, where *Y* in SiOC/Ag-3-*Y* is the content of SiOC/Ag-3 in the electromagnetic wave absorption test.

RCS simulation was performed via the CST Studio suite 2023 software. The simulation model consists of a layer of a perfect electric conductor (PEC) and a layer of SiOC/Ag absorbers that have the same length and width (200 mm × 200 mm) with thicknesses of 1 mm and 2.82 mm. The monitoring frequency was 7.28 GHz. This model was arranged in the *XOY* plane, with the *Z*-axis being the incident direction of the electromagnetic wave. The optimal electromagnetic wave absorption model was developed over a range of detection angles from −60° to 60°.

### 2.5 Antibacterial experiments

First, Luria−Bertani (LB) liquid medium (2.5 g of LB broth medium and 100 mL of deionized water) and LB solid medium (2.5 g of LB broth medium, 1.5 g of agar powder, and 100 mL of deionized water) were added to a high-pressure steam sterilization pot and sterilized at 121 °C for 15 min. Second, *E. coli* and *S. aureus* single colonies were placed into 3 mL of LB liquid medium in a thermostatic oscillator at 37 °C for 24 h with a shaking rate of 200 r/min, resulting in a bacterial suspension. The bacterial suspension was subsequently diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to 1×10−6 CFU/mL. Subsequently, 1 mg of the sample was added to 5 mL of the diluted bacterial suspension and cultured in a 37 °C incubator for 12 h without light radiation, 20 min with light radiation, 40 min with light radiation, and 60 min with light radiation. Finally, after the culture was complete, the bacterial mixture was continuously diluted 10 times with PBS solution to 10−3, and 100 μL of the mixture was evenly coated on LB solid medium. The samples were cultured in a constant-temperature incubator at 37 °C for 16 h, and the number of colonies was recorded. The antibacterial efficiency (death rate (*D*r) of *E. coli* and *S. aureus*) was calculated via Eq. (1):

(1) Dr=(N1−N2)/N1×100%

where *N*1 and *N*2 represent the number of colonies in the blank control and SiOC/Ag samples, respectively.

## 3 Results and discussion

A schematic diagram of the synthesis of the SiOC/Ag composites is shown in Fig. 1. First, SiOC nanospheres are obtained through the hydrolysis‒polycondensation of tetraethoxysilane and dimethyl dimethoxysilane in an ammonia environment [22]. Two kinds of nanospheres can be found in the SEM images of the SiOC/Ag composites, as shown in Fig. 1. The larger nanospheres aggregate to form a beaded structure, which is conducive to the movement of carriers. The smaller nanospheres are randomly distributed on the surface of the larger spheres, and the number of small nanospheres increases with increasing silver nitrate content. In addition, many fine particles can be found in the necks of two larger nanospheres, which form rich interfaces to attenuate electromagnetic wave energy via interfacial polarization.

Figure 2(a) shows typical XRD patterns of the as-obtained SiOC/Ag composites. The drum peak is located at approximately 24°, which results from the synthetic effect of the representative diffractive peak of amorphous SiOC (located at 23°) [29] and carbon (located at 26°) [30]. The other five diffractive peaks located at 38.34°, 44.56°, 64.69°, 77.56°, and 81.75° correspond to the (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) crystal planes of Ag (PDF#04-0783), respectively [31], which indicates that these composites consist of amorphous structures and Ag nanocrystals. XPS can be used to determine the elemental composition of the composites, as presented in Fig. 2(b). Six representative peaks of Si 2p, Si 2s, C 1s, Ag 3d, N 1s, and O 1s are found in these composites. The C 1s peak (Fig. 2(c)) can be fitted into three peaks corresponding to C–C/C=C, C–N, and C=O bonds, and C–N can improve the electrical conductivity of the carbon structure, thereby increasing conduction loss to improve the electromagnetic wave attenuation ability.

To further investigate the microstructure features of the SiOC/Ag composites, TEM results are depicted in Fig. 3. The Ag nanoparticles are randomly embedded in the larger nanospheres, and the number of Ag nanoparticles increases as the AgNO3 content increases. The size of most Ag nanoparticles is in the range of 10–20 nm, and the diameter of a few nanoparticles increases to 50 nm (Figs. 3(a)–3(c)). From the high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) images (Figs. 3(d)–3(f)) of the fine nanoparticles, lattice fringes with a crystal plane spacing of 0.24 nm can be clearly observed, which correspond to the (111) crystal plane of Ag. Interestingly, all the Ag nanoparticles are coated with a layer of medium with a crystal face spacing of 0.34 nm. According to the EDS results (Figs. 3(g)–3(l)), Si, C, and O are evenly distributed across larger nanospheres (diameter ≈ 200 nm), and Ag is distributed in the outer shell, which is composed of C. Therefore, the crystal face spacing of 0.34 nm belongs to the (002) crystal plane of C pyrolyzed from phenolic resin. The BET results (Figs. 2(e) and 2(f)) indicate that three nanospheres have high specific surface areas, which are 345.29, 495.32, and 559.79 m2/g for SiOC/Ag-1, SiOC/Ag-2, and SiOC/Ag-3, respectively. A larger specific surface area with a mesoporous structure is conducive to forming polarization centers, thereby increasing the amount of electromagnetic wave energy consumed [32].

where Z*0 and *Z*in are the free space impedance (*Z*0 = 377 Ω) and input impedance of the electromagnetic wave-absorbing materials, respectively. *ε*r and *μ*r denote the relative complex permittivity and permeability of the electromagnetic wave-absorbing materials, respectively. *d* represents the material’s thickness. *f* and *c* represent the frequency and the velocity of the incident electromagnetic waves, respectively.

As depicted in Figs. 4(a) and 4(b), the real and imaginary parts of the permittivity decrease with increasing electromagnetic wave frequency, which results from the frequency dispersion effect [35]. The relative complex permittivity and dielectric loss tangent increase with increasing Ag content, and all the curves exhibit fluctuations in the frequency range of 2–18 GHz due to the interfacial polarization behavior of the SiOC/Ag composites. In addition, the SiOC/Ag composites have no magnetic component; therefore, the relative complex permeability is considered to be 1. Debye relaxation is an important index for reflecting the interface polarization phenomenon, which can be expressed via Debye’s equation * ((ε′−εs+ε∞/2)2+(ε′′)2=(εs−ε∞/2)2), where ε*s represents the static dielectric constant, *ε*∞ represents the optical frequency dielectric constant; * ε′ and  ε″ ⁡stand for the real part and the imaginary part of the dielectric constant  [36,37]. All the  ε′– ε″ plots (Figs. 4(d)–4(f)) exhibit 3–4 semicircular curves, indicating that multiple polarizations occur in the SiOC/Ag composites, such as at the SiOC/C and Ag/C interfaces.

As shown in Figs. 4(g)–4(l), the loading content of the SiOC/Ag composites was set to 30%. The SiOC/Ag-1 composite has an RLmin value of 17.52 dB at 18 GHz, indicating weak electromagnetic wave absorption performance. With increasing Ag content, the RLmin values of the SiOC/Ag-2 and SiOC/Ag-3 composites reached −29.8 and −21.35 dB, respectively. Although the SiOC/Ag-2 composites have stronger RLmin values, the effective absorption bandwidths (EABs) of the SiOC/Ag-3 composites can cover the entire C, X, and Ku bands by adjusting the matching thickness. To further optimize the electromagnetic wave absorption performance of the SiOC/Ag-3 composites, the RL values were calculated by changing the SiOC/Ag-3 composite load. Figures 5(a)–5(c) show that the relative permittivity of the SiOC/Ag-3 composites increases with increasing load, and the *ε*′–*ε*′′ curves (Fig. 5(d)) exhibit longer tails and steeper slopes as the SiOC/Ag-3 composite load increases, which can result from higher conductivity. The HR-TEM images of the fine nanoparticles revealed that the Ag nanoparticles were coated with a layer of carbon. The SiOC ceramic matrix is a transmitting-wave medium with low conduction; therefore, the carbon layer and Ag nanoparticles act as absorbing-wave media. In the aggregated state of Ag nanoparticles, SiOC/Ag can form a network with a low conduction mode (low Ag content) and a high conduction mode (high Ag content), which determine the dielectric properties or electrical conductivity of SiOC/Ag composites, as shown in Fig. 6. The carbon-coated Ag nanoparticles will form a high electrical conductivity model if they are in contact with each other, where free electrons can travel fast over long distances, thereby improving the electrical conductivity or *ε*′′, respectively. If the carbon-coated Ag nanoparticles are not in contact with each other, where the circuit is formed mainly through electronic hopping, the conductivity or *ε*″ will be significantly reduced because the SiOC ceramic matrix acts as a barrier for transport.

Figure 7 shows the RL values for different SiOC/Ag-3 contents. Similarly, SiOC/Ag-3-20 also exhibits weak electromagnetic wave absorption performance. When the load of SiOC/Ag-3 increases to 40%, the RLmin value reaches −58.03 dB with a matching thickness of only 2.82 mm, indicating excellent electromagnetic wave absorption performance. However, for SiOC/Ag-3-50, as the SiOC/Ag-3 content further increased, its RLmin value was only −14.95 dB (Figs. 7(g) and 7(h)). In electromagnetic wave absorption, impedance matching (*Z*m = *Z*in/*Z*0 = * μrεrtanh(2πfdμr⋅εrc)) and the attenuation constant (α* = * 2πfc[(μ′′ϵ′′−μ′ϵ′)+(μ′′ϵ′′−μ′ϵ′)2+(μ′ϵ′′+μ′′ϵ′)2]12)  are two significantly important factors in evaluating the performance of materials [2,38,39]. According to previous work [40], if the Z*m value is between 0.53 and 1.93, the RL value of the materials is less than −10 dB; thus, this material can be used as an electromagnetic wave-absorbing material for civilian purposes. As shown in Figs. 8(a)–8(d), SiOC/Ag-3-40 exhibits excellent impedance matching, and SiOC/Ag-3-50 shows poor impedance matching, which prevents electromagnetic waves from entering the materials. The conductivity of the test samples increases with increasing SiOC/Ag content, resulting in more reflection than incidence owing to the skin effect. In addition, the order of the average attenuation constant is as follows: *α*SiOC/Ag-3-50 = 241.46 > *α*SiOC/Ag-3-40 = 117.55 > *α*SiOC/Ag-3-30 = 82.97 > *α*SiOC/Ag-3-20 = 32.51, which means that SiOC/Ag-3-50 has the best attenuation ability. However, excessive loss ability could result from enhanced conductivity, which causes an eddy current on the surface of the materials under an alternating electromagnetic field. Therefore, most incident electromagnetic waves are reflected rather than absorbed. As a result, the SiOC/Ag-3-50 composite has the strongest attenuation ability, but its poor impedance matching limits the electromagnetic wave efficiency. The SiOC/Ag-3-40 composite has a moderate attenuation ability with excellent impedance matching, resulting in superior electromagnetic wave absorption performance.

Figure 14 shows the electromagnetic wave absorption performance mechanism of the SiOC/Ag composite. First, the high specific surface area of the SiOC/Ag composite increases the number of reflections and prolongs the propagation path of the electromagnetic wave, which enhances the consumption of electromagnetic wave energy [41,42]. Second, the interconnected conductive carbon network of the SiOC/Ag composite facilitates the transport of free electrons, leading to conductive loss to attenuate electromagnetic wave energy under an alternating electromagnetic field. Finally, the numerous heterointerfaces between SiOC, carbon, and Ag nanoparticles result in electron accumulation owing to the difference in conductivity, causing interfacial polarization loss. Therefore, the superior electromagnetic wave absorption performance of the SiOC/Ag composite can be achieved by the synergistic effects of multiple reflections, conductive loss, and interfacial polarization.

The antibacterial effects of the SiOC/Ag composites on E. coli* and *S. aureus* are shown in Figs. 10 and 11. The antibacterial efficiency of the as-synthesized SiOC/Ag composites against *E. coli* and *S. aureus* was investigated under light exposure. The LB-agar plate results indicate that SiOC/Ag has a slight bactericidal effect without light, but the effect is not obvious, which is due to the release of Ag+. The release rates of Ag+ during the antimicrobial process of SiOC/Ag-1, SiOC/Ag-2, and SiOC/Ag-3 are 9.810, 13.307, and 13.102 ppm, respectively. Usually, Ag+ can interact directly with *E. coli* and *S. aureus* proteins, resulting in bacterial dissolution, destroying the cell membranes of *E. coli* and *S. aureus* and rendering them inactive. However, after visible light was applied, 97% of the *E. coli* and 93% of the *S. aureus* were killed after light exposure for 60 min when the SiOC/Ag-1 composite was used as an antibacterial agent. The antibacterial efficiency of SiOC/Ag-2 and SiOC/Ag-3 was 99% for *E. coli* and *S. aureus*. The excellent antibacterial performance of SiOC/Ag composites can be achieved after light exposure, which may be attributed to the generation of ROS [43]. In addition, the SEM images (Figs. 12(b) and 12(c)) indicate that the surfaces of untreated *E. coli* and *S. aureus* are smooth and that their structures are intact. After bacteria were cultured with SiOC/Ag powder together, surface depressions and deformations began to appear on the surfaces of *E. coli* and *S. aureus*, and even their cell membranes were severely destroyed [44,45] (Fig. 12(d)–12(i)). These phenomena indicated that the SiOC/Ag composites exhibited profound antibacterial activity. To further investigate the antibacterial mechanism, the EPR results of different SiOC/Ag composites are shown in Fig. 13. There are four characteristic peaks with a relative intensity ratio of 1 : 2 : 2 : 1, corresponding to the peaks of ·OH under light radiation (Fig. 13(a)). Figure 13(b) shows six characteristic peaks of ·O2−, which indicate the generation of superoxide radicals under light radiation. Additionally, there are no signs of ·OH or O2− in the EPR spectrum under no light radiation conditions (Figs. 13(c) and 13(d)). The above results illustrate that the superior antibacterial performance of SiOC/Ag composites under visible light radiation is attributed mainly to ROS sterilization (Fig. 14). The carbon shell-coated Ag nanoparticles allow slow release of Ag+, achieving continuous and long-term antibacterial effects. ## 4 Conclusions

In summary, SiOC/Ag composites with both electromagnetic wave absorption and antibacterial performance were successfully obtained through a liquid-phase method combined with heat treatment. The synergistic effect of interfacial polarization and conductive loss endows the SiOC/Ag composites with strong electromagnetic wave absorption performance. The SiOC/Ag-3 composite exhibited RLmin of −58.03 dB with a matching thickness of only 2.82 mm. The antibacterial features of the Ag ions released from the carbon shell-coated Ag nanoparticles and the generation of ROS under light radiation ensured that the SiOC/Ag composites achieved excellent antibacterial ability against *E. coli* and *S. aureus*. Furthermore, combined with RCS simulations, the as-obtained SiOC/Ag composites also exhibited superior radar stealth properties. Therefore, this study offers a simple design approach for developing SiOC ceramic-based composites with excellent antibacterial and electromagnetic wave absorption performance for medical devices.
