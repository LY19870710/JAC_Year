## 1 Introduction

With increasing interest in easing the energy crisis while reducing environmental pollution, thermoelectric (TE) materials have attracted significant attention in recent years owing to their ability to directly convert waste heat into electricity [1−6]. The efficiency of thermoelectric conversion depends on the dimensionless figure of merit, which is defined as *ZT* = *α*2*σT*/*κ*t, where *α*, *σ*, *T*, and *κ*t represent the Seebeck coefficient, the electrical conductivity, the absolute temperature, and the total thermal conductivity, respectively. Moreover, *κ*t includes the carrier thermal conductivity (*κ*e) and the lattice thermal conductivity (*κ*L) [7,8]. High-performance thermoelectric materials should possess excellent electrical properties, high power factor (PF = *α*2*σ*) and low thermal conductivity. These thermoelectric parameters highly depend on the carrier concentration. Therefore, tuning the carrier concentration is simple and effective for optimizing the TE performance [9]. The key to improving the TE performance is tuning the carrier concentration by doping or off-stoichiometry [10] while introducing lattice defects, such as point defects [11], pores [12], dislocations [13], *in situ* precipitates [14,15], and the formation of solid solutions [16,17]. Additionally, owing to the relatively independent parameter (*κ*L), decreasing the lattice thermal conductivity is another effective approach for improving the thermoelectric performance. Lattice vibrations cause heat conduction in most semiconductor materials [18]. These strategies have been employed to obtain superior thermoelectric performances in typical thermoelectric materials such as Bi2Te3 [19], SnSe [20,21], Bi2S3 [22], and Cu2−*x*S [23,24]. In particular, *in situ* precipitates can enhance phonon scattering while maintaining electrical transport.

Copper sulfides (Cu2−*x*S, 0 ≤ *x* ≤ 0.2), as superionic conductors (p-type semiconductors), exhibit the characteristics of phonon–liquid electron crystals with appropriate electrical conductivity and low thermal conductivity [2,3,25]. The raw materials of copper sulfides are low-cost and toxic, and environmentally friendly energy-conversion materials have more opportunities for commercial applications if they possess excellent performance. According to the content of *x*, Cu2S, Cu1.96S, and Cu1.8S differ at room temperature, although they all change to cubic structures at high temperatures [26]. Among these materials, Cu1.8S is a potential TE material candidate for commercial applications owing to its excellent electrical conductivity and stability. This material exhibits two distinct crystal structures: a hexagonal phase (*R** 3¯m*) at 300 K and a cubic phase (*Fm** 3¯m*) beyond 360 K. Dennler *et al*. [27] concluded that Cu1.8S exhibited excellent electrical stability compared with other materials. In the field of thermoelectric materials, it has been demonstrated that adjusting the content of Cu vacancies and introducing nanoparticles within the matrix are effective in optimizing the TE properties of Cu1.8S-based materials. Therefore, introducing extra electrons into the Cu1.8S matrix by decreasing the number of Cu vacancies or doping with Cu2+ [25], In3+ [28], Co3+ [29], and Pb2+ [30] can balance the carrier concentration and enhance the Seebeck coefficient of copper sulfides. Furthermore, multiscale lattice defects can be generated by incorporating nanoparticles into the copper sulfide matrix, thus enhancing phonon scattering and reducing *κ*L, such as by compositing with graphene [31], WSe2 [32], and BiCuSeO [33].

In this study, to simultaneously tune the carrier concentration while constructing the complex structure in copper sulfides, the natural mineral jamesonite (FePb4Sb6S14) was introduced into the Cu1.8S material by melting reaction and spark plasma sintering. Jamesonite is the natural mineral with a monoclinic crystal structure that exhibits low electrical conductivity and thermal conductivity. The electrical resistivity of FePb4Sb6S14 is 121 Ω·cm at room temperature, with a lower electrical conductivity of 8.26×10−3 S·cm−1. The Seebeck coefficient of FePb4Sb6S14 is approximately 200 μV·K−1 at room temperature. An image of the natural minerals is shown in Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM). This mineral decomposes into PbS, Sb2S3, and FeS at 1023 K as Reaction (1) [34−36]:

(1) FePb4Sb6S14→1023K4PbS+3Sb2S3+FeS

Therefore, the *in situ* phase separation can occur during the synthesis process, and multiple and multiscale second phases (PbS, Sb2S3, and FeS) can be evenly distributed in copper sulfides, leading to increased phonon scattering and reduced thermal conductivity. Additionally, the intrinsically high hole concentration of Cu1.8S can be decreased by doping with high-valence ions from the mineral, such as the substitution of Cu+ with Fe2+, which is advantageous for improving the Seebeck coefficient and reducing the carrier thermal conductivity of copper sulfides. Ultimately, benefiting from *in situ* precipitates and an adjusted carrier concentration, the Cu1.8S+0.5 wt% FePb4Sb6S14 sample exhibited a highly enhanced *ZT* value of 1.1 at 773 K. Furthermore, owing to the homogeneously distributed precipitates, the mechanical properties (Vickers hardness and Young’s modulus) of the copper sulfide-based bulk composites were slightly strengthened.

### 2.1 Material synthesis

In this study, all stoichiometric Cu1.8S+*x* wt% FePb4Sb6S14 (jamesonite) samples were obtained using Cu (grain, 99.999%, ZhongNuo Advanced Material (Beijing) Technology Co., Ltd., China) and S (grain, 99.999%, ZhongNuo Advanced Material (Beijing) Technology Co., Ltd., China) as raw materials. Cu1.8S+*x* wt% FePb4Sb6S14 (*x* = 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75) powders were mixed and sealed in quartz glass tubes (pressure below 9×10−4 Pa). According to the solid-state reaction method, the samples were heated from 293 to 1373 K for 18 h, kept at 1373 K for 12 h, cooled to 1073 K for 24 h, and kept at 1073 K for 7 d. After the reaction, the furnace was switched off, and the mixture was cooled to 293 K. The obtained ingots were cracked for 10 min at 800, 500, and 300 r·min−1 in a protective atmosphere (95 vol% Ar+5 vol% H2) by high-energy ball milling to obtain small particles. The powders were sintered at 773 K for 5 min in *ϕ*20 graphite molds under an axial pressure of 50 MPa and vacuumed to below 6 Pa using a spark plasma sintering system (SPS211-X, Sumitomo, Japan). Subsequently, the sintered bulk material was fabricated as elongated strips or circular disks for various purposes.

### 2.2 Characterizations

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; MiniFlex600, Rigaku, Japan) was used to analyze the phase structures of the bulk samples from 20° to 60° at a speed of 10 (°)/min. An X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS; K-Alpha+, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to determine the valences of various elements in the Cu1.8S+*x* wt% FePb4Sb6S14 sample. The full and high-resolution nuclear spectra of the samples were collected and calibrated using the C 1s binding energy at 284.8 eV. The microstructures of the bulk samples with SPS were observed via a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; JSM-6460, JEOL, Japan) and a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM; JEM-F200, JEOL, Japan), and the distribution of elements was analyzed by an electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA; 1730H Shimadzu, Japan). The carrier concentration (*n*) and mobility (*μ*) of the samples were measured using a Hall effect measurement system (Ecopia, HMS-700, Republic of Korea). The electrical resistivity (*ρ*) and Seebeck coefficient (*α*) were measured using a measuring system (CTA, Cryoall, China) in a helium atmosphere from 323 to 773 K. The thermal conductivity (*κ*) was calculated as *κ* = *Dc**p**d*, where *κ* is the total thermal conductivity; *D* represents the thermal diffusivity; *c**p* is the specific heat capacity; *d* is the density of bulk specimens. The thermal conductivity (*D*) and specific heat capacity (*c**p*) were obtained using the laser flash method (LFA457, NETZSCH, Germany) and a synchronous thermal analyzer (STA449, NETZSCH, Germany) under a fuller Ar atmosphere from 323 to 773 K. The density of each sample was measured using the Archimedes method. The hardness and Young’s modulus were measured using the continuous stiffness and three-dimensional (3D) nanoblitz methods (iMicro, KLA, USA).

### 3.1 Phase structure and microstructures

The XRD patterns of all the Cu1.8S bulk composites with and without FePb4Sb6S14 are shown in Fig. 1(a). The main diffraction peaks of all the samples matched well with the hexagonal structure of Cu1.8S (*R** 3¯m*, PDF#47-1748) at room temperature. The XRD patterns of the natural jamesonite powders are shown in Fig. S2 in the ESM; the diffraction peaks of the mineral powders correspond to those of monoclinic FePb4Sb6S14 (*P*21, PDF#42-1391). However, there are no obvious diffraction peaks of FePb4Sb6S14 for any of the CuS-based composites because FePb4Sb6S14 can decompose during the synthesis process, resulting in various precipitates, and their contents are below the detection limit of XRD. Notably, the main diffraction peaks of the bulk samples shift to lower angles, as shown in Fig. 1(b), which is attributed to lattice expansion caused by sulfur volatilization. This phenomenon commonly occurred in the Cu2−*x*S system [37]. Furthermore, with an increase in the amount of FePb4Sb6S14, the main peak corresponding to the (0120) plane gradually shifts to a lower angle, which is consistent with the calculated lattice parameters: *a* and *c* gradually increase with increasing content, as shown in Fig. 1(c). The more notable expansion of the matrix lattice can be ascribed to the increased sulfur volatilization caused by the decomposition of FePb4Sb6S14 during the sintering process. The sulfur in the generated PbS, Sb2S3, and FeS is also easily volatilized. The gradually reduced relative density of the Cu1.8S-based specimens with increasing FePb4Sb6S14 indicates the elemental emission of the samples, as shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM.

To investigate the valence states of the various elements in the bulk composites, XPS was performed on the Cu1.8S+0.5 wt% FePb4Sb6S14 sample. Figure 2(c) shows that the binding energy peaks at 161.9 and 163.9 eV result from S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2, respectively, demonstrating the existence of S2−. Furthermore, the peaks at 531.6 and 539.5 eV are attributed to Sb 3d5/2 and Sb 3d3/2, respectively, illustrating the presence of Sb3+ [40], as shown in Fig. 2(f). These results are almost consistent with the valence states of each element in FePb4Sb6S14, suggesting that the valence states of various elements except Fe do not change.

The microstructure of the Cu1.8S material was decorated with natural FePb4Sb6S14. FESEM was used to observe the morphology of the fractured surface of Cu1.8S with and without FePb4Sb6S14, as shown in Fig. 3. An increase in the number of pores was observed in the Cu1.8S bulk samples with an increase in FePb4Sb6S14. The intrinsically dense structure of the pristine Cu1.8S sample gradually changes to a porous structure after the introduction of natural FePb4Sb6S14. These pores are mainly distributed along the grain boundaries, which is mainly ascribed to the introduction of FePb4Sb6S14 and S volatilization during the sintering process, as shown in Figs. 3(b)–3(d). Additionally, the grain size of the bulk materials gradually increases with increasing content, which is an uncommon phenomenon because pore interfaces and precipitates are capable of inhibiting the motion of grain boundaries during the sintering process. In this case, the grain size increases from 3 μm for pure Cu1.8S to approximately 15 μm for the Cu1.8S+0.75 wt% FePb4Sb6S14 sample. There are two main reasons for the increased grain size of the doped samples. First, Fe doping in the Cu1.8S lattice promotes element dispersion and material migration; this phenomenon has been reported in other thermoelectric systems [41,42]. Second, there may be some low melting point compounds in the grain boundaries introduced by natural mineral doping, which promote grain growth as sintering aids.

### 3.2 Thermoelectric performance

The electrical properties of all the samples at different temperatures (323–773 K) are shown in Fig. 6. The electrical conductivities (*σ*) of all samples show a trend of initially increasing and then decreasing, which indicates that the conduction mechanism that the material changes from semiconductor conduction to metal conduction. There is a turning point at approximately 354 K owing to the phase transition of Cu1.8S from a hexagonal structure to a cubic structure. A *σ* sharp decrease was observed with the addition of FePb4Sb6S14. The carrier concentration (*n*) and mobility (*μ*) were used to evaluate *σ*, as shown in Fig. 6(c). After FePb4Sb6S14 is doped, *n* and *μ* of the doped samples are significantly lower than those of the pure Cu1.8S bulk sample, with *n* changing from 5.5×1020 to 4.5×1020 cm−3. *n* and *μ* are significantly reduced, as described as follows: First, Cu+ of Cu1.8S is occupied by Fe2+; therefore, the extra electrons are annihilated with holes in the matrix. According to Eqs. The carrier effective mass (*m**) of copper sulfides can be simulated using the single parabolic band (SPB) model. *m** increases from 0.47*m*0 to 0.64*m*0 with increasing FePb4Sb6S14 content, as shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM, which is benefited from the improved *α*. In a previous study, Zhang *et al*. [29] reported that co-doping can significantly tune the carrier concentration and optimize the electronic band structure of Cu1.8S. The convergence of the energy band edge is enhanced, and the involvement of the lone electrons from the d orbital of Co results in better local electronic conductivity. Fe and Co are adjacent and can partially have the same effect on the density of states in this study. Thus, by optimizing the carrier concentration, PF improves from 1300 to 1590 μW·m−1·K−2 at 773 K, showing a tendency similar to that of *α*, as shown in Fig. 6(d). Therefore, the addition of natural FePb4Sb6S14 can nearly maintain PF of CuS-based composites at low to moderate temperatures.

Because of the coupling relationship between the electrical and thermal properties, the variation in carrier concentration not only causes a change in conductivity but also affects the carrier thermal conductivity. Therefore, the total thermal conductivity of a semiconductor consists of two parts: the carrier thermal conductivity (*κ*e) and the lattice thermal conductivity (*κ*L). When there is only one carrier in the material, *κ*e can be calculated as *κ*e = *LTσ* based on the Wiedermann–Franz law, where *L* is the Lorenz number (*L* is related to the Fermi level and the scattering factor). However, the regular method for evaluating *L* is not applicable to Cu1.8S materials. The migration of Cu ions can contribute to the ionic conductivity at high temperatures, which increases with the increase in the temperature and content of Cu vacancies. Thus, the contribution of Cu vacancies cannot be ignored, leading to uncertainty in *L*. This is also why Cu1.8S has a high conductivity, conforming to the electrical properties discussed previously [43].

The thermal conductivities (*κ*t) of Cu1.8S+*x* wt% FePb4Sb6S14 (*x* = 0, 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75) bulk samples are shown in Fig. 7(b), which is calculated by *κ*t = *Dc**p**d*. The thermal diffusivities (*D*) of all the specimens are shown in Fig. 7(a). The turning point of the thermal conductivity at approximately 354 K originates from the phase transition of Cu1.8S. When the crystal structure of Cu1.8S changes to the cubic phase after the phase transition process, the disordered Cu+ enhances phonon scattering, resulting in lower thermal conductivity [28]. Furthermore, the thermal conductivity of the samples gradually decreases with increasing FePb4Sb6S14 content, which is mainly ascribed to the strengthened scattering effects on the phonons caused by the extra interfaces. The extra interfaces are generated by the introduced precipitates and pores. The pore interfaces along the grain boundaries can effectively scatter low-frequency phonons, while the nanoprecipitate interfaces contribute to scattering low-to-mid-frequency phonons, further reducing the thermal conductivity at moderate temperatures. In addition, dislocations and point defects play an important role in reducing the thermal conductivity in the medium-temperature range. The minimum *κ* is 1.2 W·m−1·K−1 for the Cu1.8S+0.75 wt% FePb4Sb6S14 sample, which is significantly lower than that of the other samples. In this study, the main causes for the reduction in thermal conductivity are a decrease in the carrier concentration and an increase in the number of phonon scattering centers. First, extra electrons were introduced by the substitution of Cu+ with Fe2+; FePb4Sb6S14 doping reduced the carrier concentration and, therefore, the electrical conductivity. A decrease in *σ* weakened the contribution of carrier transport to the thermal conductivity. Second, the relative density decreases from 98% to 95% after the introduction of FePb4Sb6S14 into Cu1.8S. The multiscale pores observed in the FESEM image introduce an extra interface into the matrix. The *in situ* precipitates (PbS, Sb2S3, and FeS) generated by the decomposition of FePb4Sb6S14 at 1023 K were observed using EPMA and TEM. The interfaces between the pores and the precipitates can strongly scatter low-frequency phonons at low temperatures. Third, Fe2+ ions enter the Cu1.8S lattice, and point defects can effectively scatter phonons at high frequencies, which is beneficial for reducing the thermal conductivity at high temperatures. Therefore, after the introduction of FePb4Sb6S14, all-scale phonons are strongly scattered by the extra interfaces of the pores, *in situ* precipitates, dislocations, and point defects.

*
Fig. 7Temperature dependence of TE properties of Cu1.8S+x* wt% FePb4Sb6S14 samples: (a) thermal diffusivity (*D*), (b) thermal conductivity (*κ*), (c) frequency-dependent spectral lattice thermal conductivity calculated using Debye–Callaway model, and (d) schematic of multiscale phonon scattering mechanisms.

Multiple phonon scattering mechanisms in materials favor the reduction of the lattice thermal conductivity (*κ*L). To explore the influence of multiple scattering mechanisms on *κ*L, the classical Debye–Callaway model was calculated [44], which can identify the key factors of scattering phonons with different frequencies, as shown in Fig. 7(c). This model is mainly based on Eq. (5) (other equations are shown in the ESM):

(5) κL=kB2π2v(kBTh)3∫0θD/Tx4exτv−1(ex−1)2dx

where *x* is defined as *hω*/(*k*B*T*); *k*B is the Boltzmann constant; *θ*D is the Debye temperature; *h* is a reduced constant; *ν* is the average phonon group velocity; *ω* is the phonon angular frequency; *T* is the absolute temperature. In this study, multiple-phonon scattering includes U–N processes (* τU−N−1), interfaces ( τIF−1), point defects ( τPD−1), dislocations ( τDS−1), and precipitates ( τP−1). Therefore, the relaxation time is the result of the interaction of multiple scattering mechanisms, which can be expressed as Eq. (6):

(6) τc−1=τU−N−1+τIF−1+τPD−1+τDS−1+τP−1

As shown in Fig. 7(c), the regions with different colors represent the contributions of different scattering mechanisms to the reduction in the lattice thermal conductivity. According to the Debye–Callaway model, low- and high-frequency phonons are mainly strongly scattered by precipitates, extra interfaces, and point defects [45]. The dislocations are also scattered in the low-frequency region. The multiple scattering mechanisms are shown in Fig. 7(d). The all-scale phonons are strongly scattered in the Cu1.8S+0.5 wt% FePb4Sb6S14 composite sample, resulting in a reduction in the lattice thermal conductivity.

In general, owing to PF maintained by the tuned carrier concentration and the significantly reduced thermal conductivity through the enhanced phonon scattering caused by multiple scattering mechanisms, the peak ZT* of 1.1 is obtained at 773 K for Cu1.8S+0.5 wt% FePb4Sb6S14, which is 141% higher than that of pure Cu1.8S (0.44), as shown in Fig. 8(a). Compared with other copper-sulfide TE materials [46−52], this work is in a leading position, as shown in Fig. 8(b).

### 3.3 Mechanical properties

Generally, the mechanical properties of a material deteriorate when various multiscale pores appear in a matrix. However, due to the superior hardness of the *in situ* precipitates (PbS—1.5–2.5 GPa, Sb2S3—2–2.5 GPa, and FeS—3–4 GPa), the mechanical properties of Cu1.8S are improved. To further determine the mechanical properties, the Vickers hardness (*H*) and Young’s modulus (*M*) of the samples were measured via microhardness analysis. After polishing, each sample was measured over an area of 300 μm × 300 μm (approximately 900 points). The radius of curvature of the tip was 20 nm, and the load was 20 mN. Notably, the average hardness and modulus of pure Cu1.8S are approximately 0.86 and 37.39 GPa, respectively, which are lower than the values of 0.87 and 40.27 GPa for the Cu1.8S+0.5 wt% FePb4Sb6S14 sample shown in Fig. 9. The Young’s moduli and Vickers hardness values of the other samples are shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM. The Vickers hardness and Young’s modulus of all the samples change with the doping amount, as shown in Figs. 9(c) and 9(f). With an increase in the FePb4Sb6S14 content, the hardness of all the samples remains nearly unchanged or even increases slightly. The change in hardness is attributed to the combined effects of porosity and second-phase dispersion strengthening [53]. Some blue lines, which are lower than the average hardness, indicate that they are caused by multiscale pores. However, the lines higher than the average hardness results from the *in-situ* precipitates in the matrix, which causes dispersion strengthening. The trend of the Young’s modulus is the same as that of the Vickers hardness, which increases by 18% compared with that of pure Cu1.8S.

The maximum conversion efficiency (η*max) was calculated by *ZT*ave. *ZT*ave of 0.21 was obtained in the temperature range of 323–773 K for the Cu1.8S+0.5 wt% FePb4Sb6S14 sample, and *η*max reaches 3.75%, which is greater than the value of 2.38% for pure Cu1.8S, as shown in Fig. 10(b). Moreover, the service stability of copper sulfides is extremely important. Three consecutive measurements of the electrical transport properties of the Cu1.8S+0.5 wt% FePb4Sb6S14 sample were carried out in the temperature range from 323 to 773 K. As shown in Fig. 11, after three tests, the performance of the sample is stable, which proves that the TE properties and the thermal stability of Cu1.8S are optimized via the introduction of jamesonite.

According to Fig. 12, there is an immune change in the electrical transport properties of all the Cu1.8S+0.5 wt% M (M = FeS, PbS, Sb2S3, and FeS+PbS+Sb2S3+Sb2O3) samples, indicating that it is difficult for the external ions of the single compounds to enter the lattice of the Cu1.8S material. The electrical conductivity of the Cu1.8S+0.5 wt% FePb4Sb6S14 sample is lower than that of the other samples, which is mainly caused by the decrease in carrier concentration and mobility, suggesting that the Fe2+ ions in natural jamesonite are more easily doped in Cu1.8S. In addition, more lattice defects are introduced in the jamesonite-added sample, leading to enhanced scattering effects on both carriers and phonons. Although the electrical conductivity is satisfactory, the thermal conductivity of the Cu1.8S+0.5 wt% FePb4Sb6S14 sample obviously decreases. By comparison, there are fewer categories and fewer lattice defects in the single compound-added (FeS, PbS, Sb2S3 and FeS+PbS+Sb2S3+Sb2O3) samples, resulting in a decrease in thermal conductivity that is not as obvious as that in the Cu1.8S+0.5 wt% FePb4Sb6S14 sample. Additionally, dispersion strengthening is the main reason for the enhanced mechanical performance of the Cu1.8S–jamesonite sample. There are fewer precipitates in the Cu1.8S+0.5 wt% M (M = FeS, PbS, Sb2S3 and FeS+PbS+Sb2S3+Sb2O3) samples. The trace components of the mineral contribute to the complexity of the system and improve the thermoelectric performance. Therefore, adding jamesonite is more beneficial than adding FeS+PbS+Sb2S3+Sb2O3 to the thermoelectric and mechanical properties of Cu1.8S.

## 4 Conclusions

Cu1.8S+*x* wt% FePb4Sb6S14 (*x* = 0, 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75) bulk composites were successfully prepared by melting and spark plasma sintering. *In situ* phase separation occurred during the synthesis process by the decomposition of natural jamesonite, resulting in the generation of multiple precipitates. The carrier concentration of the Cu1.8S material was significantly altered by Fe2+ doping, leading to an improved Seebeck coefficient and a maximum power factor increase of 1590 μW·m−1·K−2 for the Cu1.8S+0.50 wt% FePb4Sb6S14 sample. In addition, multiscale lattice defects were introduced. The interfaces of the pores and *in situ* precipitates (PbS, Sb2S3, and FeS) were produced in the bulk composites, resulting in dramatically reduced thermal conductivity and enhanced Vickers hardness and Young’s modulus of the composites. Ultimately, the Cu1.8S+0.5 wt% FePb4Sb6S14 sample achieved a peak *ZT* value of 1.1 at 773 K. This study provides an efficient strategy for constructing complex microstructures using natural minerals as a source to induce *in situ* phase separation, which has been effective in optimizing the thermoelectric and mechanical properties of CuS-based materials.
