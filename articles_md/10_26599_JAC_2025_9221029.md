## 1 Introduction

Thermoelectric (TE) materials can directly convert heat into electrical energy, offering numerous advantages, such as the absence of moving parts, compactness, long-term stability, and quiet operation [1,2]. This makes TE materials highly attractive for applications in waste heat recovery, portable power generation, and cooling technologies. The TE performance of a given material is evaluated by the figure of merit, *ZT* *= S*2*σT*/*κ*, where *S*, *σ*, *T*, and *κ* represent the Seebeck coefficient, the electrical conductivity, the absolute temperature, and the thermal conductivity, respectively [3,4]*.* A high *ZT* value indicates a high power factor (*S*²*σ*) combined with a low *κ*, which is crucial for efficient energy conversion. However, optimizing these parameters independently is challenging because of their intrinsic interdependence. Over the past two decades, various optimization mechanisms have been proposed, including resonant levels [5], band convergence [6], modulation doping [7], size effects [8], and defect engineering [9]. TE materials, including PbTe [10], SnSe [11], CoSb3 [12], SiGe [13], and half-Heusler [14], were demonstrated with significant progress in achieving high *ZT* values. High *ZT* values are critical for commercial TE modules, but achieving a high average power factor (*PF*av) is often more economically feasible, particularly when abundant, low-cost heat sources are available [15]. Unfortunately, high *PF*av values are rare in current TE materials.

Recently, the n-type Mg3Sb2 compound has garnered significant attention because of its unique electronic band structure, characterized by sixfold degeneracies in the M and L bands and twofold degeneracies in the K band [16]. This compound is particularly attractive for practical applications owing to its low-cost components and favorable mechanical properties [17,18]. However, a critical challenge lies in the high vapor pressure of magnesium, which results in uncontrollable electron “killer” Mg vacancies (VMg″) in Mg3Sb2 [19]. These vacancies cause a marked decline in the TE performance of Mg3Sb2. To address the adverse effects of VMg″ on n-type properties, various strategies have been implemented. These strategies involve introducing additional Mg to increase the formation energy of VMg″ [19,20], doping with transition elements to increase the occupancy rate of Mg sites [21], and adjusting the sintering parameters to control VMg″ [22]. As a result, notable *ZT* values are obtained, such as ~1.87 at 773 K for Y–Se co-doped samples [23], ~1.85 at 723 K for Mn–Te co-doped samples [24], and ~1.8 at 773 K for Y-doped Mg3(Sb,Bi)2 samples [25]. However, off-stoichiometry addition of Mg may become ineffective for removing Mg vacancies under prolonged sintering at high temperatures [26]. Although doping can minimize VMg″, it often causes a decrease in carrier mobility (*μ*), leading to suboptimal TE performance at near room temperature (RT) [27]. In materials where grain boundary scattering (GBS) dominates, increasing the grain size can increase *μ* due to the reduction in GBS, but the *S* and carrier concentration (*n*) remain essentially unchanged [28]. Theoretically, GBS is the primary scattering mechanism near RT in polycrystalline Mg3Sb2, while acoustic phonon scattering (APS) plays a more significant role at higher temperatures. Thus, increasing the grain size in polycrystalline Mg3Sb2 samples can weaken the GBS, significantly improving the *σ*, particularly near RT.

Experimental efforts have shown that coarse-grained samples can be obtained by optimizing the preparation conditions and doping elements. Methods such as melting with Ta tube sealing [29,30], elevating the sintering temperature [31], Mg-vapor annealing [32], and doping with elements such as Nb [33], Cu [34], Mo [35], and Mn [27] were employed with some success in enlarging the grain size. However, the use of relatively expensive Ta tubes and complex annealing equipment can increase manufacturing costs, reducing the cost-effectiveness of n-type Mg3(Bi,Sb)2. Indeed, high-temperature sintering can enhance the grain size and *ZT*, but further grain growth through prolonged heat treatment is limited because of significant Mg loss at high temperatures [32], which may induce p-type characteristics. Therefore, mitigating the impact of GBS on polycrystalline materials remains a critical challenge.

In this work, we propose liquid-phase hot-pressing sintering (LPS) of Mg3(Sb,Bi)2 alloy by introducing an excess of Mg, which can effectively fill Mg vacancies and significantly suppress GBS. Accordingly, the room-temperature electrical conductivity of Mg3(Sb,Bi)2 was greatly improved without a negative effect on the *S*, leading to a high *PF*av of ~25.3 μW·cm−1·K−2 and a *ZT*av of ~1.03 between 323 and 623 K. Moreover, W segregates preferentially at triple junction grain boundaries (GBs) after adding a certain W, forming grain boundary complexes, which can lower interfacial barriers and allow more high-energy carriers to transfer, resulting in an increase in *S*. Density-functional theory (DFT) calculations suggest that the grain boundary complexes are metallic bonding characteristics, thus enhancing electrical conductivity. As a result, a maximum *ZT* of ~1.4 at 623 K and an ultrahigh *PF*av of ~26.2 μW·cm−1·K−2 between 323 and 623 K are attained in W0.06Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01–4% alloys via a novel, cost-effective strategy for optimizing the electric properties.

## 2 Materials and methods

Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.5-based samples were fabricated via high-energy ball milling (HEBM) and hot pressing (HP). Raw materials, including Mg power (99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), Sb, Bi, and W powers (99.99%, Aladdin, China), as well as Te pieces (99.999%, Alfa Aesar, USA), were accurately weighed according to the nominal compositions, loaded into a stainless-steel ball milling jar in a glovebox, and subjected to initial HEBM for 10 h. The as-obtained nano-powders were mixed with Mg powders and then subjected to a second HEBM for 30 min. The resulting powders were loaded into a graphite die and immediately sintered by hot pressing at 1073 K under a pressure of ~45 MPa for 30 min. The synthesis flowchart of Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.5-based samples is shown in Fig. 1(a).

### 2.1 Characterization methods

An X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Cu-Kα, *λ* = 1.5406 Å) was employed to determine the phase composition of materials. The lattice parameters were refined from the experimental data using Jade software, which employs a pseudo-Voigt profile function for profile fitting [36]. The structural refinement of Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01–*x*% Mg samples was based on the Mg3Sb2 model (ICSD code#71-0404). Microstructural characterization and composition analysis were performed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Nova NanoSEM 450, FEI, USA) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; 2100 PLUS, JEOL, Japan) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The sample surfaces were etched by 4 wt% nitric acid alcohol at room temperature after 5-s intragranular corrosion of equilibrium grain boundary. The average grain size (*D*ave) distribution was analyzed via ImageJ software. The sintered disk samples were cut into bars of 3 mm × 3 mm × 10 mm and disks of * ϕ10 mm × 2 mm. Bar samples were used to simultaneously measure σ* and *S* using a commercial system (LSR-3, Linseis, Germany). *κ* was calculated using *κ* = *Dc**p**ρ*, where *D*, *c**p*, and *ρ* are the thermal diffusivity coefficient, specific heat capacity, and density, respectively. *D* was measured on a laser flash system (LFA 457, Netzsch, Germany). *c**p* was determined by the following polynomial equation: *c**p* = 3*NR* (1 + 1.3 × 10−4*T* − 4 × 103*T*−2)/*M*w, where 3*NR* = 124.71 J·mol−1·K−1; *M*w is the molecular weight (g·mol−1) of the formula unit; *T* is the temperature, *K* [35,37]. *ρ* was measured using the Archimedes method. The room-temperature Hall coefficient (*R*), carrier concentration (*n*), and carrier mobility (*μ*) were measured by the four-probe van der Pauw method [38]. Hardness indentation was characterized by an automatic micro-Vickers hardness measurement system (KB30S-FA, KB, Germany) at a load of 100 gf for 15 s. The heat flow and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) were measured by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) using a Netzsch 449F3 apparatus (USA). The diffuse reflectance Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was conducted using a Nicolet 6700 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA).

### 2.2 First-principles calculations

First-principles calculations were performed using DFT with the all-electron projected augmented wave (PAW) method, as implemented in the Vienna *ab initio* simulation package (VASP) [16,39]. The semi-local generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the fully relativistic Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange correlation functional was employed [40,41]. A convergence criterion of 10−5 eV in energy and a plane wave kinetic energy cutoff of 400 eV were adopted. A force threshold of 0.01 eV·Å−1 was applied for all the atomic optimizations. During geometric relaxation, all the atoms were allowed to relax, with the Brillouin zone sampled using a Monkhorst–Pack *k*-mesh of 2 × 3 × 1. For interface models, only the atomic positions were relaxed to achieve structural optimization. To prevent long-range interactions during boundary condition calculations, a vacuum layer of 15 Å along the *c*-axis was included. Two interface models were developed: (1× 3) Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5 (001)/(1× 3) Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5(001) and (1× 3) Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5(001)/(5×3) W(110)/(1× 3) Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5(001). All the interfaces exhibited an ideal lattice matching degree with a mismatch error of < 5%.

## 3 Results and discussion

The XRD patterns of Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01–*x*% Mg (*x* = 2–5) samples prepared by the LPS method are shown in Fig. 1(b). The observed diffraction peaks align closely with those of standard Mg3Sb2 (PDF#71-0404), indicating no significant impurities. Notably, certain phases, such as Mg (5%–10% in weight), are extruded from the graphite mold during hot-pressed sintering (Fig. S1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). This is because the sintering temperature of 1073 K exceeds the melting point of Mg (*T*m = 923 K) [42], causing the excess Mg to be expelled as a liquid phase. This expulsion can facilitate liquid phase sintering, leading to grain coalescence, which affects the electrical and thermal transport properties of this material in turn. Moreover, lattice parameters *a* and *c* tend to decrease with increasing Mg content (Figs. S1 and S2 in the ESM), indicating that excess Mg can exceed its solubility limit and react with the matrix to form more Mg*x*Bi*y* phases, which can be decomposed into a Bi-rich phase due to their instability [43,44]. To further investigate the morphological characteristics of the as-fabricated samples, Figs. 1(c)‒1(f) show typical SEM images of Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01–*x*% Mg (*x* = 2–5) bulks after etching. Evidently, the average grain size increases after the addition of Mg, from ~9.3 μm for the *x* = 2% sample to ~19.2 μm for the *x* = 4% sample, indicating a 106% increase. This growth in the average grain size of Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5 is closely related to the LPS process [45].

During the LPS process, the Mg phase, acting as the liquid phase, infiltrates into the spaces among the Mg3Sb2 particles. In the binary Mg‒Sb system [42], Mg3Sb2 is soluble in the Mg liquid phase. The solubility (*Cr*) of a cluster with radius (*r*) can be described using the Gibbs−Thomson equation [46,47]. Equation (1), which accounts for the effect of curvature on solubility, is given by

(1) Cr=C0exp⁡(2γVmrRT)

where *C*0 is the solubility of a flat interface; *γ* is the surface energy of the particle; *r* is the radius of the particle; *V*m is the molar volume of the solute; *R* is the universal gas constant; *T* is the absolute temperature.

Regions with a greater radius of curvature (like tiny particles or sharp edges) tend to have a higher equilibrium concentration of Mg3Sb2 in the surrounding liquid phase. This is due to the Gibbs–Thomson effect, where higher curvature leads to a higher equilibrium concentration of solute in the liquid phase. In contrast, regions adjacent to larger particles, which have a smaller radius of curvature and smoother surfaces, will exhibit a lower equilibrium concentration of Mg3Sb2 in the liquid phase. This creates a gradient in the Mg3Sb2 concentration from areas with high curvature to areas with low curvature. Smaller Mg3Sb2 particles or regions with sharp curvatures are thermodynamically unstable. This instability leads to the continuous dissolution of Mg3Sb2 monomers into the liquid phase. These dissolved Mg3Sb2 monomers migrate towards larger Mg3Sb2 particles, where the lower equilibrium concentration in the liquid phase near these particles promotes their precipitation. As the sintering process progresses, Mg3Sb2 particles undergo rearrangement and coalescence due to capillary forces generated by the Mg liquid phase. Notably, a moderate Mg content can facilitate grain coarsening by accelerating mass transfer within the liquid phase. However, a thick Mg-rich layer around the grains is formed when the Mg content becomes excessive, restricting their growth through the Zener pegging effect [48]. This phenomenon aligns with the trend observed in our samples; i.e., the initial grain size increases, followed by a slight reduction. This coarsening of Mg3Sb2 grains reduces the GBS and positively impacts the *μ*w and *σ*, which is discussed in detail below.

TEM and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) were utilized to investigate the nano- and microstructures of the samples (Fig. 2). A large number of second phases ranging from ten to hundreds of nanometers in diameter are observed within the matrix (Fig. 2(a)). These second phases are identified as Bi, crystallized in the * R3¯m space group along the [ 100] zone axis (inset of Fig. 2). The bright-field scanning TEM (STEM) image and corresponding EDS mapping of the second phase provide further confirmation of the Bi-phase precipitation (Fig. 2(b)). Moreover, the interfaces between Bi precipitates and the matrix, as well as the alternating Moiré fringes, are depicted in Fig. 2(c), which can be attributed to composition fluctuations in n-type Mg3(Sb,Bi)2. These fringes indicate that the grains are highly crystalline and the Bi nanodomains are embedded within the matrix. Actually, these interfaces and fringes can facilitate the energy filtering effect by scattering low-energy carriers more efficiently, promoting high-energy carrier transport and enhancing S* [49]. The oxidation of Mg is also observed on the sample surface, which has also been reported in other works [50,51]. The interplanar spacings of 2.23 and 2.12 Å correspond to the (110) and (111) planes of Mg3Sb2, respectively (Fig. 2(d)). Furthermore, distinct dislocations, marked as “⊥” in inverse fast Fourier transform images, are identified within the nanodomains and likely stem from strain transformations (Fig. 2(e)) [52].

Figure 3(a) shows the temperature-dependent S* of Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01–*x*% Mg bulks in the temperature range of 300–623 K. The negative values confirm n-type semiconductor behavior, with electrons being the major charge carriers. Notably, compared with the Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01 bulk materials reported in previous studies [31,35], our samples exhibit a similar *S* and significantly higher electrical conductivity at nearly room temperature (Fig. 3(b)). In n-type Mg3(Sb,Bi)2 bulk, the Mg vacancies (VMg″) are the primary defects at the grain boundaries, creating trapping states [53]. According to the trapping state model [54], these states can capture carriers, reducing *n* and forming a potential barrier (*E*b) that impedes carrier movement between crystallites [28]. The magnitude of *E*b determines the transition temperature from GBS to APS [35]. The addition of excessive Mg preferentially located at the grain boundaries enables it to diffuse and replenish VMg″, thereby decreasing *E*b. Indeed, *E*b decreases from ~48 meV for Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01 sample [19] to ~10 meV for our samples (Fig. 3(c)). Moreover, no obvious difference in *E*b is observed, which is mainly attributed to the uniform microstructures of our samples, namely, the lower concentration of Mg vacancies and holes. Consequently, *E*b decreases from ~48 meV for Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01 sample to ~10 meV for our samples [20] (Fig. 3(c)). The reduction in *E*b weakens GBS, leading to a shift in the carrier scattering mechanism from GBS to APS, as evidenced by the increasing trend in *σ* (Fig. 3(b)).

To better understand the carrier transport behavior in TE bulk materials with charged grain boundaries, a two-phase model was adopted [55]. The *σ* and *S* can be expressed as Eqs. (2) and (3):

(2) σtot−1=(1−tGBP)−1σG−1+tGBPσGB−1

(3) S=SG

where * σtot,σG,  σGB, S*G, and * tGBP represent the total σ*, the grain *σ*, the grain boundary *σ*, the grain size (*S*), and the size fraction of the grain boundary, respectively. Clearly, *σ* is strongly correlated with * tGBP (or grain size), while S* remains nearly independent of it. The introduction of Mg during the LPS process leads to an increase in the grain size, which consequently reduces * tGBP. This grain growth also diminishes the grain boundary resistivity, which is associated with the potential barrier in the semiconductor. These effects are beneficial for enhancing room-temperature σ* in our polycrystalline materials without a negative impact on *S*. For example, the room-temperature *σ* rises from 104 S·cm−1 [31] for the Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01 samples with *D*avg of ~1 μm to 523 S·cm−1 for our sample, with *D*avg of ~19 μm. Moreover, the room-temperature *σ* values of our samples are much greater than those of other n-type Mg3(Bi,Sb)2 alloys fabricated by the methods of ball-milling (BM)+SPS/HP [33,35], melting+SPS/HP [16,56], and Sb-flux+Mg-vapor saturation annealing methods [57,58].

To evaluate the electrical transport properties of Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01–*x*% Mg bulks, the weighted mobility (*μ*w) as proposed by Synder *et al*. [59] was calculated using the experimentally measured *S* and *σ* (Fig. 3(d)). In Refs [31,35], the *μ*w values for grain sizes of 1 and 9.31 μm exhibited temperature dependency of *T*1.74 and *T*1.37, respectively, which align with ionized impurity scattering (*T*1.5). In contrast, our coarse-grained sample demonstrates metallic behavior with the APS mechanism, showing *T*−1.5 dependence over the tested temperature range. The positive temperature dependence was initially explained by ionized impurity scattering but was later attributed to GBS. Experimental evidence indicates that increasing the grain size to mitigate GBS can induce negative temperature dependence, thereby improving the TE performance at room temperature. Consequently, the sharp increase in *μ*w in our sample can be ascribed to the larger grain size obtained by the LPS method, increasing from 43 cm2·V−1·s−1 for the 1 μm sample to 287 cm2·V−1·s−1 for the 19 μm sample at 300 K.

To gain further insights, we describe the hall carrier mobility (*μ*H) vs. carrier concentration dependence of our coarse-grained sample using a single Kane band (SKB) model (Fig. 3(e)). The experimental data are in good agreement with the SKB model, yielding a calculated deformation potential coefficient (*E*def) = 13.8 eV and a constant effective mass (*m**) = 1.3*m*e (where *m*e is the electron mass), which can confirm the presence of APS for the *x* = 4% Mg sample, as further evidenced by the *n*H-dependent *ZT* curves (Fig. S4 in the ESM). Notably, our coarse-grained samples exhibit high *μ*H values, although the increase in *m** from 1.05*m*e for Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01 to 1.30*m*e for our sample is generally adverse to *μ*H. This can be explained by the inverse relationship between the squared *E*def and *μ*H, as described by Eq. (4) [60]:

(4) μH=2πℏ4eClNv5/3md5/2(KBT)3/2Edef2F−1/2(η)F0(η)

where * ℏ is the reduced Planck constant;  Cl is the elastic constant for longitudinal vibrations;  Nv is the band degeneracy;  KB is the Boltzmann constant;  md is the total density of the state effective mass;  Fn(η) is the Fermi integral. E*def reflects the intensity of charge carrier scattering by the acoustic phonons [50,60]. The decrease in *E*def from 30.1 eV for Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01 [35] to 13.8 eV for our sample indicates significant suppression of the carrier scattering strength. This reduction can be attributed to the addition of excess Mg during the HP process, which attenuates the strength of the electron‒phonon coupling. The weakened *E*def ultimately enhances *μ*H, thereby contributing to the higher *σ*.

The sharp increase in *σ* and high *S* result in a significantly enhanced *PF*, particularly in the low-temperature range. The Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01–4% Mg sample achieves a maximum *PF* of ~25.8 μW·cm−1·K−2 (Fig. 3(f)) and a *PF*avg of ~25.3 μW·cm−1·K−2 at 323–623 K (Fig. 3(g)). Regarding the thermal transport properties, the temperature dependence of total *κ* and lattice thermal conductivity (*κ*l) is shown in Fig. 3(h). Notably, neither *κ* nor *κ*l increases with increasing grain size. These results suggest that GBS significantly affects carrier transport by introducing a potential barrier at the grain boundary, while it exerts less influence on phonons. This is primarily because phonons, being less coupled with the electric field, have mean free paths that are much smaller than the grain size [30]. Benefiting from the excellent electrical properties, *ZT* increases sharply from 0.4 to 0.6 at low temperatures, and meanwhile the maximum *ZT* at 623 K increases from 0.8 to 1.3 (Fig. 3(i)).

Based on the optimizing composition of Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01–4% Mg, we further introduced W at the (GBs to improve the TE performance at high temperatures. XRD analysis shows that all the diffraction peaks of W*y*Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01–4% Mg samples match well with the standard card, with no impurity peaks (Fig. 4(a)). The lattice parameters *a* and *c* increase initially due to W substitution at Mg defect sites, while they subsequently decrease when the W concentration exceeds 0.04, since the segregated W phases at the grain boundaries induce microstrain in the lattice (Fig. 4(b)). The average grain size for the *y* = 0 samples is 19 μm (Fig. 1(e)), while for the *y* = 0.06 sample, the grain size decreases to 14.9 μm (Fig. 4(e)). Furthermore, there is a noticeable enrichment of W-rich areas at the triple junction grain boundaries, indicating W segregation in these regions (Figs. 4(f)–4(h)). The accumulation of W at the GBs, accompanied by the formation of complexion, reduces the total grain boundary energy due to excess solute. This reduction in energy decreases the driving force for grain growth, resulting in smaller grain sizes [35], which is consistent with our observed trend. These W segregations act as conductive channels for charge carriers, thus enhancing *σ*, similar to that in Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01Cu0.01 [34].

Figures 5(a)–5(c) show the measured temperature-dependent *S*, *σ*, and *PF* of W*y*Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01–4% Mg sample. After the addition of elemental W, both *S* and *σ* improve at higher temperatures but decrease at nearly room temperature, likely due to W enrichment at the grain boundaries. This enrichment induces additional scattering, turning the mechanism from pure APS into mixed scattering [61]. At high temperatures, W segregation at grain boundaries leads to the formation of metal‒semiconductor interfaces, and the high conductivity of W modifies these interfaces by lowering the interfacial barrier compared with the original Mg interface. This reduction in the barrier effectively scatters low-energy carriers while allowing more high-energy carriers to pass, thereby enhancing *S*. A similar phenomenon was also observed in Ta-modified Mg3Sb2 [62], where the Ta inclusions reduce interfacial barriers and enhance *S*. Meanwhile, the metallic nature of W at grain boundaries increases *σ* [63], as discussed below. The combined effects result in an increase in *PF* from 23.1 to 28.7 μW·cm−1·K−2 for the *y* = 0.06 sample, with a *PF*av of ~26.2 μW·cm−1·K−2 in the 323–623 K range (Fig. S5 in the ESM). This peak *PF* exceeds or is comparable to the values reported for Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.5 systems (Fig. 5(d)), indicating the competitive electronic transport properties of our samples. Furthermore, the *μ*w values of our samples prepared via W addition are among the attractive ones for Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5-based systems (Fig. S6 in the ESM). Unfortunately, the *κ* tends to increase with W. This increase is likely related to the enrichment of W and Mg, which affects the *κ* through their intrinsic nature [62]. When the contributions from the high *κ* of the Mg-rich phase (156 W·m−1·K−1) and W-rich phase (174 W·m−1·K−1) outweigh the reduction in *κ* caused by phonon scattering, both the *κ* and *κ*l increase (Fig. S7 in the ESM). Furthermore, no bipolar transport of our sample is observed at higher temperatures, primarily due to the increased band gap (Fig. S8 in the ESM).

To further explore the impact of W addition on the electronic transport properties of Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5, DFT calculations were performed. Mg3Sb2 features an inverse α-La2O3-type structure (space group *P** 3¯m*1), consisting of anionic [Mg2Sb2]2− layer blocks and cationic Mg2+ sheets stacked along the *c* axis [20], while the W crystal structure is a body-centered cubic structure (space group *Im** 3¯m*). Based on surface energy analysis, the (001) and (110) surfaces were selected to construct slab models for interface studies [67,68], including the original Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5(001)/Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5(001) and W-modified Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5(001)/W(110)/Mg3Sb1.5Bi0.5(001) interfaces (Fig. 6(a) and Fig. S9 in the ESM). Optimized interface spacings of 2.6 and 2.9 Å are determined for the respective configurations to ensure structural stability (Fig. S10 in the ESM). The electron localization functions (ELFs) reveal the main bonding differences among the interfaces (Fig. 6(b)). In the original interface, the shared electrons between the Mg2 and Sb atoms confirm covalent bonding within the bulk. After W is introduced, the ELF near the interface slightly decreases, reflecting a shift towards more metallic bonding. Charge density difference (CDD) analyses further illustrate significant charge transfer at the W-modified interface, with metallic bonding (Figs. 6(c) and 6(d)) [69]. These results demonstrate that W-induced metallic bonding at the interface modifies the charge distribution and enhances electronic transport, providing a foundation for improved electrical and mechanical properties.

In addition to attaining commendable TE performance, robust mechanical properties such as hardness and fracture toughness are equally essential for ensuring the long-term durability and reliability of TE materials, especially in mass-market and miniaturized applications [70,71]. Figures 8(a) and 8(b) show the microhardness and indentation morphologies of WyMg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01–*x*% Mg bulk samples, with hardness increasing from 0.93 GPa for the *y* = 0 sample to 1.07 GPa for the *y* = 0.06 sample, demonstrating that even a slight addition of W significantly reinforces the material hardness. Compared with other TE materials, including Mg3.2Co0.01Sb0.5Bi1.49Te0.01 [71], (Bi2Te2.7Se0.3)0.94B0.06 [72], PbTe [73], (Ag2Se)0.975(2AgSbSe2)0.025 [74], and Pb/SnSe [75] (Fig. 8(c)), the W-modified Mg3(Sb,Bi)2 alloys exhibit a notable advantage in hardness. Furthermore, the W addition also improves the fracture toughness (*K*ic) from 0.96 to 1.03 MPa  m (inset of Fig. 8(c)), likely because of the formation of metal‒semiconductor interfaces due to W segregation at grain boundaries, which alters the crack pathways and mitigates crack propagation [71]. Moreover, thermogravimetric and DSC tests further confirm the excellent thermal stability of the samples, with no observable weight loss within the tested temperature range (Fig. S11 in the ESM). These improvements reinforce the elastic and plastic deformation capabilities of the material, providing superior mechanical stability and reliability, making Mg3(Sb,Bi)2 alloys highly suitable for practical TE device applications.

## 4 Conclusions

We fabricated n-type Mg3(Sb,Bi)2 with an ultrahigh power factor by introducing elemental Mg and W. The introduction of excess Mg during the hot-pressing process creates a wetting Mg phase that induces liquid phase sintering. This process compensates for Mg vacancies, ensuring n-type conductivity and increasing the average grain size, thereby reducing grain boundary scattering near room temperature. The increased grain size enhances the weighted mobility and electrical conductivity with minimal impact on the Seebeck coefficient (*S*). Building on these optimizations, the addition of W further improves the high-temperature TE performance. W segregates preferentially at triple junction grain boundaries, forming conductive complexes that exhibit metallic bonding characteristics. These metal‒semiconductor inclusions reduce interfacial barrier scattering for low-energy carriers and allow more high-energy carriers to pass, thereby enhancing *S*. Consequently, the metallic nature of W at the grain boundaries boosts the *σ*, leading to peak *PF* of 28.7 μW·cm−1·K−2 and *PF*av of ~26.2 μW·cm−1·K−2 between 323 and 623 K. This combination yields an optimal *ZT* of 0.6 at 300 K, an average *ZT* of 1.03 within 323–623 K for Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01–4% Mg, and a *ZT* of 1.33 at 623 K for W0.06Mg3.2Sb1.5Bi0.49Te0.01–4% Mg. Moreover, the as-fabricated Mg3(Sb,Bi)2-based TE materials present excellent mechanical properties, including a Vickers hardness of 1.08 GPa and a fracture toughness of 1.03 MPa· m. This work provides valuable insights into the composition and structural design of advanced TE materials.
