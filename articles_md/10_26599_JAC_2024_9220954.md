## 1 Introduction

Stealth technology enables stealth jets such as F-22, J-20, and Su-57 to efficiently avoid detection by opponent detection systems. This significantly enhances their capacity to survive and strike the battlefield, making them very important for national defense security [1−7]. Microwave and infrared imaging are two crucial technologies used for locking and tracking stealth fighters [8], with a total threat ratio of up to 90%, as shown in Fig. 1(a) [9,10]. As counter-detection technologies, microwave absorbing materials (MAMs) [11−20] and low-infrared emissivity materials are crucial for improving the stealth capabilities of fighter aircraft [21−25].

*
Fig. 1(a) Schematic diagram of radar and infrared detection for stealth fighters; (b) Bistatic simulation RCS (dBsm) of F35; (c) temperatures of aircraft whose velocity and altitude being 1.5 March and 5000 m, respectively; (d) dimensionless CRI of aircraft as a function of detection angle for both MWIR and longwavelength infrared (LWIR) detection bands.

Significant advancements have been achieved in single-band stealth technology over the past few decades [26−30]. However, single-band stealth technology is not capable of adjusting to multiband composite detectors as composite detection systems continue to evolve [21]. Among multiband cooperative detection methods, microwave (X-band: 8.2–12.4 GHz) and midwavelength infrared (MWIR: 3–5 μm) bands are often used to track aircraft. On the basis of the simulation results shown in Figs. 1(b) and 1(d), the jet nozzle of the stealth aircraft is the source of radar and infrared signal detection [31,32]. Considering the high-temperature condition of the jet nozzle for stealth fighters (Fig. 1(c)), the development of a microwave-infrared compatible stealth coating applied at elevated temperatures is urgently needed.

Owing to disparities in the stealth principles, microwave-infrared compatible stealth faces major challenges [24]. Typically, two methods are employed to realize microwave-infrared compatible stealth. One approach is to develop MAMs and low-infrared emissivity materials separately. Then, a layer of the low-infrared emissivity coating was applied to MAM [33]. However, low-infrared emissivity coatings degrade the microwave absorption performance of the MAM because of the reflection of incident microwaves. Another way is to fabricate metamaterials on the surface of MAMs [34−36]. A metamaterial, which possesses a periodic arrangement, is intentionally created to fulfill particular optical features. Typically, it is composed of conductive metals possessing low spectrum emissivity [37]. Through the design of the metamaterial, it is cooptimized in terms of impedance matching and infrared emissivity properties [35,38], resulting in the realization of microwave-infrared compatible stealth [34,39]. However, the metamaterial has many drawbacks, including complex design, poor oxidation resistance, and performance degradation under high-temperature service conditions [40]. This severely restricts its practical application.

A perfect microwave-infrared compatible stealth material should be composed of simple ingredients that can not only balance microwave dissipation and low infrared emissivity but also have a simple preparation process and high operating temperature. Owing to excellent attributes, 8 wt% yttria-stabilized zirconia (8YSZ) is widely used in the fields of thermal barrier coatings [41−43], solid oxide fuel cells [44], catalysis [45], gas sensors [46], medicine [47], etc. The application of 8YSZ in solid oxide fuel cells is based mainly on its special oxygen ionic conductivity characteristics at high temperatures, and its conductivity can reach approximately 0.02 S/cm at 800 °C [48]. On the basis of free-electron theory, suitable conductivity is conducive to the dissipation of incident microwaves [49], which implies that 8YSZ may be a promising MAM, especially when it is applied at high temperatures. Yin et* *al*. [50] prepared YSZ/silicon carbide composites by chemical vapor infiltration, and their reflection loss and electromagnetic interference properties were further revealed. Recently, Zhang *et* *al*. [51] investigated the effects of phase interfaces in SmFeN/YSZ composites on electromagnetic wave-absorbing properties. To the best of our knowledge, however, the dielectric and microwave-absorbing properties of 8YSZ single-component materials, especially at high temperatures, have not been reported in the literature. In addition to its microwave absorbing property, the infrared emissivity of 8YSZ has been reported at room temperature, and the results reveal that 8YSZ presents low infrared emissivity, indicating that it is a potential infrared stealth material [52−54]. However, the temperature dependency of the spectral emissivity of 8YSZ has not been reported in the literature.

In view of their potential application as microwave-absorbing and low-infrared emissivity materials at high temperatures, they are expected to realize radar-infrared-compatible stealth performance via a single 8YSZ component. Thus, the dielectric, microwave absorbing, and infrared emissivities of the 8YSZ coating prepared by atmospheric plasma spraying (APS) technology are revealed and discussed systematically in the present work. The results revealed that it possesses better microwave-infrared compatible stealth at high temperatures, including strong microwave absorption (RLmin = −50 dB), thinness (1.5 mm), good effective absorption bandwidth (EAB, 2 GHz), and low spectral emissivity (*ε* < 0.3). Notably, this work opens up a new application of 8YSZ as a microwave-infrared-compatible stealth material, breaking the traditional notion that microwave-infrared-compatible stealth materials should be explored in the pool of “complex” multiple-component or metamaterial.

### 2.1 Coating preparation

An 8YSZ coating was sprayed on a superalloy substrate by APS with commercial feedstock powder (Sanspuri New Materials Co., Ltd., China). The superalloy was grit-blasted into a 46-mesh corundum grit before being sprayed to increase the bonding strength of the coatings. This was followed by ultrasonic cleaning with acetone. Before the fabrication of the 8YSZ coating, a bond coat (NiCrAlY) with a thickness of approximately 20 μm was prepared with a GTS-5500 model APS system (Praxair-TAFA, USA) with an SG-100 plasma torch. Finally, the 8YSZ coating was sprayed on the bond coat via the same APS system and plasma torch. The related spray parameters are listed in Table 1. For comparison, a zirconia (ZrO2) coating was prepared using the same method, and the spraying parameters were 8YSZ. Finally, the sprayed coating was stripped from the superalloy substrate by machining, and the related characterization and property measurements of the stripped coating were conducted.

### 2.2 General characterization

The phase analysis of the coating was conducted via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; MiniFlex600, Rigaku Corporation, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (*λ* = 1.54186 Å), and the diffraction pattern was further refined via GSAS II software. The surface morphology and chemical constituents of the feedstock powder and coating were detected by a scanning electron microscope (SEM; G300, Carl Zeiss, Germany). For the microregional element and phase distributions of the coating, selected area electron diffraction (SAED) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) were performed with a transmission electron microscope (Tecnai G2 F20, FEI, USA) equipped with an EDS instrument (XFlash 5030, Bruker, Germany). Most X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) work has been carried out via a surface science instruments spectrometer (XPS; ESCALAB 250, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) with an Al target emitting K radiation (target energy of 1486.6 eV, power of 150 W, and a spot size of 500 m). X-ray photoelectron spectra were analyzed by peak-fitting software, with the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV binding energy (BE) used to adjust for surface electrostatic charging.

### 2.3 Relative permittivity measurement

The complex relative permittivity (*ε*r) and permeability (*μ*r) of coatings with dimensions of 22.86 mm × 10.16 mm × 1.5 mm were measured through a waveguide method by using a two-port vector network analyzer (N5227B, Keysight, USA) in the frequency range of 8.2–12.4 GHz at six temperature spots, namely, 25, 200, 400, 600, 800, and 900 °C. They are derived from the scattering parameters, including *S*11 (input reflection coefficients), *S*12 (reverse transmission coefficients), and S21 (forward transmission coefficients). The detailed calculation equations are shown in Eqs. (1)–(4):

(1) T=(S11+S21)−R1−(S11+S21)R

(2) R=(S112−S122)+12S11±[(S112−S122)+12S11]2−1

(3) T=exp⁡(−j2πλ0μrεr−(λ0λc)⋅d)

(4) R=μr1−(λ0λc)2/μr1−(λ0λc)2μrεr−(λ0λc)2−1μrεr−(λ0λc)2−1μr1−(λ0λc)2/μr1−(λ0λc)2μrεr−(λ0λc)2+1μrεr−(λ0λc)2+1

where *R* and *T* represent the reflection and transmission coefficients, respectively; *d* is the thickness of the measured sample; *λ* is the wavelength. *λ*0 and *λ*c represent the transmission wavelength and cut-off wavelength of electromagnetic waves in the air, respectively. During measurement, the as-prepared samples were placed vertically in the middle of the waveguide cavity, which was heated from room temperature to 900 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min. To achieve precise measurements at a particular temperature, the sample was kept at each temperature for 10 min to allow the testing instrument to stabilize before taking the measurement.

### 2.4 Infrared emissivity measurement

The temperature dependence of the infrared emissivity (*ε*(*T*)) of the samples was measured via an infrared emissivity measurement instrument (EMI-1000; National Institute of Metrology, China). By measuring the infrared radiation energy of the blackbody (*L*b(*T*)) and sample (*L*s(*T*)) under the same test conditions, *ε*(*T*) was obtained via Eq. (5):

(5) ∫λ1λ2ε(T)=∫λ1λ2Ls(T)dλ/∫λ1λ2Ls(T)dλ∫λ1λ2Lb(T)dλ∫λ1λ2Lb(T)dλ

where *λ*1 and *λ*2 represent the lower and upper limits of infrared wavelengths, respectively.

### 2.5 Conductivity measurement

Through a resistivity measurement system (RMS-1650; Partulab Technology Co. Ltd., China), the ionic conductivity (*σ*) of the coating was measured via the four-probe method according to Eq. (6):

(6) σ=ln2π×d×IV

where *d*, *I*, and *V* represent the thickness, the current, and the voltage, respectively.

### 2.6 Theoretical calculation and simulation

The charge density difference of the material was determined via first principles by density functional theory (DFT) and via a plane-wave pseudopotential method by the CASTEP package in Materials Studio 2019 [55,56]. The primitive cell was multiplied by a factor of two in *x*, *y*, and *z* directions, leading to an approximately tetragonal supercell of 7.16 Å × 7.16 Å × 10.33 Å containing 16 ZrO2 formula units or 14 Zr atoms, two Y atoms, and 31 oxygen atoms. The ultrasoft pseudopotentials in reciprocal space were used to determine the reciprocity between the atomic core and valence electrons, and the valence electron configurations for Y, Zr, and O atoms were 4d15s2, 4d25s2, and 2s22p4, respectively. Brillouin zone sampling was performed on a dense *k*-point mesh of 2 × 2 × 1 points. A plane-wave basis cutoff energy of 600 eV was chosen to ensure that the calculations converge for total energy, displacement, force, and stress acting on the atoms. The exchange-correlation potential within the generalized gradient approximation is described by Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) in geometry optimization.

ANSYS HFSS 2023 R1 was applied to simulate radar cross-section (RCS) results at a frequency of 11 GHz. The simulation model was established as a square with two layers, where the upper set was an absorbing layer, and the bottom set was a perfect conductive layer (PEC). The size of PEC was 180 mm × 180 mm × 1 mm, and 180 mm × 180 mm × 1.5 mm was used for the absorbing layer. A model plate was placed on the *X*–*O*–*Y* plane, and linearly polarized plane electromagnetic waves were incident from the positive direction of the *Z*-axis to the negative direction.

## 3 Results and discussion

Through the corresponding feedstock powder, ZrO2 and 8YSZ coatings were prepared via air plasma spraying technology above the superalloy. A schematic diagram of the preparation, feedstock microstructure, and element distribution is depicted in Figs. S1(a)–S1(c) in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), respectively. The feedstock presents a spherical aggregate powder with a homogeneous composition distribution, which is conducive to coating deposition. The microstructures of the as-sprayed coatings of ZrO2 and 8YSZ are presented in Fig. S2 in the ESM, which reveals that the coating is composed of lamellar stacks derived from the splating effect. Microscopic cracks are formed between each lamellar structure owing to the discontinuous deposition of molten droplets. Moreover, the elements in the prepared coatings also have a uniform distribution.

XRD patterns of the sprayed ZrO2 and 8YSZ coatings are depicted in Fig. 2. The experimental XRD results are identical to the theoretical XRD results, revealing that ZrO2 and 8YSZ crystallize into monoclinic and tetragonal phases with space groups of *P*21/*c*(14) and *P*42/*nmc*(137), respectively [57,58]. The refinement of the corresponding XRD pattern further demonstrated the phase structure of the prepared coating, as shown in Figs. 2(a) and 2(b). The arrangements of the lattice atoms of monoclinic ZrO2 and tetragonal 8YSZ are presented in Fig. 2(c). Compared with ZrO2, 8YSZ has greater lattice symmetry with the tetragonal phase because of the replacement of Y3+ ions with a portion of Zr4+ in the parent lattice of zirconia [59]. To maintain a smaller lattice energy, the structure of ZrO2 changes after a certain Y3+ concentration is introduced along with the movement of atoms. Specifically, oxygen atoms on the left and right sides move toward the center of the cell. The middle oxygen atoms move in the left diagonal direction toward the cation, and the cations on the right diagonal move up and down, respectively. A schematic diagram of the atomic movement is depicted in Fig. 2(c). Additionally, an oxygen vacancy is created inside the crystal structure of 8YSZ to maintain charge balance resulting from the disparity in valence between the atoms being substituted and the atoms being replaced, and the defect reaction equation of Y2O3-doped ZrO2 is expressed as Eq. (7) [60]:

The presence of an oxygen vacancy can be confirmed from the high-resolution O 1s spectra, as shown in Fig. S3 in the ESM. Both ZrO2 and 8YSZ coatings clearly have similar O 1s spectra, in which the peak can be fitted into three Gaussian peaks at approximately 529.9, 531.6, and 532.7 eV. The lowest BE peak at approximately 529.9 eV is assigned to lattice oxygen, BE peak at ~531.6 eV is assigned to oxygen vacancies, and the highest BE peak at ~532.6 eV is assigned to weakly adsorbed oxygen on the surface of samples [61]. In general, the XPS peak area mainly reflects the relative content of elements on the surface of a sample. According to the fitted data in Fig. S3 in the ESM, the relative percentage of oxygen vacancies is approximately 33% in the 8YSZ coating, which is greater than that in ZrO2 coating (approximately 17%). The increase in the relative ratio of oxygen vacancies in 8YSZ coating is attributed to the formation of additional oxygen vacancies introduced by the substitution of Y3+ for Zr4+ ions. Moreover, the existence of oxygen vacancies in the ZrO2 coating and the deviation of the oxygen vacancy ratio in the 8YSZ coating from the theoretical value may be related to defects in the surface of the coating, which is not a perfect lattice.

With the exception of the phase structure, the presence of defects greatly influences the physical properties of the original ZrO2 lattice [62], which will be further explored in the next discussion. Considering the outstanding microwave absorption and infrared stealth properties demonstrated by 8YSZ coating (described further below), it is imperative to analyze its microstructure, as depicted in Fig. 3. On the basis of the yttrium distribution depicted in Figs. 3(b)–3(e), the microzones of 8YSZ coating can be classified into three categories: regions with high yttrium content (Fig. 3(a1)), regions with low yttrium content (Fig. 3(a3)), and regions without any yttrium (Fig. 3(a2)). Combined with EDS data, the matching phases in Figs. 3(a1)–3(a3) are specified as 32YSZ, ZrO2, and 8YSZ, respectively, which is further identified by SAED and HRTEM results depicted in Fig. 3. The uneven micro-distribution of yttrium may be related to the annealing effect in the plasma spraying process. Notably, the characteristic diffraction peak of monoclinic ZrO2 is not detected in XRD patterns shown in Fig. 2(b), which may be related to limitations in the precision of X-ray diffraction techniques used and the presence of only a small quantity of ZrO2 phase.

Through these measurements, the temperature dependence of the ionic conductivity of ZrO2 and 8YSZ coatings was obtained and is plotted in Fig. 5(a). The ionic conductivity of ZrO2 coating is clearly almost independent of the temperature, whereas the behavior of 8YSZ coating is notably distinct. When the temperature reaches 900 °C, the ionic conductivity of 8YSZ reaches 0.02 S/cm, revealing that 8YSZ is a conductor at high temperatures. The ionic conductivity of YSZ increased with increasing the temperature, resulting in an increase in  εr″. Indeed, the ionic transport in the 8YSZ coating is attributed to oxygen vacancies introduced by nonequivalent ionic doping, as expressed in Eq. (7). At elevated temperatures, thermal activation can greatly reduce the transport energy barrier of oxygen vacancies (Fig. 5(b)), resulting in an improvement in ionic conductivity.

To quantify the microwave absorbing property for incident electromagnetic waves, the reflection loss (RL) of a sprayed coating is derived from its permittivity according to Eqs. (13) and (14) [65]:

(13) εr=εr′−iεr″

(14) μr=μr′−iμr″

(15) Zin=μrϵrtanh(i2πcμrεrfd)

(16) RL=20log⁡|Zin−1Zin+1|

where Z*in represents the input impedance of the absorber. *f*, *d*, *c* and i are the frequency, the thickness, the light velocity and imaginary unit respectively. * μr′ and  μr″ represent the real and imaginary parts of the permeability, respectively. Since ZrO2 and 8YSZ coatings are nonmagnetic,  μr′ and  μr″ are 1 and 0, respectively. A three-dimensional (3D) plot of RL vs. frequency and thickness of the samples is shown in Fig. S5 in the ESM. In contrast, the 8YSZ coating shows the best microwave absorbing property at 900 °C, and the result is plotted in Fig. 4(b), in which RLmin is −50 dB when the matching frequency and thickness are 10.5 GHz and 1.5 mm, respectively. Moreover, EAB reaches 2 GHz, ranging from 9.3 to 10.3 GHz, when the thickness is only 1.5 mm. Compared with traditional high-temperature MAMs [14,66−79], as depicted in Fig. 4(d), 8YSZ coating has better microwave absorbing properties, including strong absorption, ultrathin thickness, a large EAB, and a high operating temperature.

In general, the microwave absorption property is determined primarily by two factors: impedance matching and attenuation coefficient (α*). The former assesses the ability of electromagnetic waves to enter the absorber, whereas the latter evaluates the degree of dissipation of the incoming microwave. A delta function (Δ) was recently introduced to validate the impedance matching property, as described by Eq. (17) [80]:

(17) |Δ|=|sinh2(Kfd)−M|

where *K* and *M* are functions that depend on the relative complex permittivity and permeability (Eqs. (18) and (19)):

(18) K=4πμr′εr′×sin⁡(εr″/εr′+μr″/μr′2)c×cos⁡(εr″/εr′)×cos⁡(μr″/μr′)

(19) M=4μr′εr′cos⁡(εr″/εr′)×cos⁡(μr″/μr′)μr′εr′×sin⁡(εr″/εr′+μr″/μr′2)(μr′cos⁡(εr″/εr′)−εr′cos⁡(μr″/μr′))2+(tan⁡(εr″/εr′−μr″/μr′2))2×(μr′cos⁡(εr″/εr′)+εr′cos⁡(μr″/μr′))2

The smaller Δ is, the better the impedance matching properties. Usually, Δ less than 0.4 is used as the standard to judge the impedance-matching performance of MAM [80]. As shown in the contour map of Δ vs. the frequency and thickness (Fig. 4(c)), the 8YSZ coating presents superior impedance matching performance, which is a precondition for its good microwave absorbing ability. In addition, *α* of 8YSZ coating is calculated by Eq. (20):

(20) α=2πfc×(μr″εr″−μr′εr′)+(μr″εr″−μr′εr′)2+(μr′εr″+μr″εr′)2

As shown in Fig. 4(a), *α* varies between 110 and 145 as the frequency increases. In fact, *α* does not indicate a stronger dissipative effect for microwaves [81]. Too large *α* usually results in impedance mismatch, leading to poor reflection loss. Hence, the exceptional microwave absorbing ability is the result of achieving an equilibrium between impedance matching and *α*, according to Eqs. (13)–(16) and (20), the optimal attenuation coefficients can be derived, and the upper and lower limits are 276 and 57 Np/m when the thickness and reflection loss values are 1.5 mm and less than −10 dB, respectively, and the results are plotted in Fig. S6 in the ESM. The attenuation coefficients of 8YSZ coating at 900 °C fall within the optimal range, as shown in Fig. 4(a), resulting in good microwave absorbing properties. There is no doubt that the prepared 8YSZ coating in the present work achieves a balance between impedance matching and *α* and results in good microwave absorbing performance at elevated temperatures.

In addition to measuring RL, RCS serves as another metric for assessing the performance of radar stealth. HFSS is used to simulate the microwave absorption property of a perfect conductive plate (PEC) covered with an 8YSZ coating, and the results are graphically represented in Fig. 6. The simulations are conducted at a frequency of 11 GHz, with the incident direction aligned with the positive *z*-axis, and the detection angle is represented by the variable theta (*θ*). 3D RCSs of 8YSZ coatings at different temperatures are depicted in Fig. 6(a), and all the absorbers have similar RCS shapes but different intensities. Since PEC is a strong radar reflector, the negative direction of *z*-axis results in the greatest scattering signal, whereas the scattering signal of PEC covered with 8YSZ coating decreases as the temperature increases in the positive direction of *Z*-axis. As shown in Fig. 6(b), the 8YSZ coating has the minimum RCS value at 900 °C, which is less than −30 dB·m2 at a detection degree ranging from −90° to 90°, indicating good high-temperature microwave absorbing performance. To more intuitively observe the effect of the 8YSZ coating on reducing the RCS value, Fig. 6(c) shows the reduction values at different temperatures, which are obtained by subtracting RCS values of PEC from those of PEC coated with the 8YSZ coating. These results demonstrated that the 8YSZ coating has a beneficial effect on reducing RCS values and results in the most substantial reduction (27.8 dB·m2) in the reflected signal when the electromagnetic wave is vertically incident, and the temperature is 900 °C. Therefore, the 8YSZ coating has significant potential for practical applications, especially at high temperatures.

The main mechanism for the dissipation of electromagnetic waves is magnetic and dielectric losses [82]. Owing to the absence of magnetic properties, only dielectric loss occurs inside 8YSZ coating. The main components of dielectric loss are conductivity loss, interfacial polarization loss, and dipole polarization loss. Owing to thermal activation, the energy barrier for the migration of oxygen vacancies decreases, resulting in 8YSZ coating displaying conductive properties at high temperatures, as shown in Fig. 5. Compared with the ZrO2 coating, the increase in ionic conductivity facilitates the dissipation of the 8YSZ coating to the incoming electromagnetic wave.

In addition to the conductivity loss, interfacial polarization loss is also present in the 8YSZ coating. According to the results depicted in Fig. 3, heterogeneous interfaces of different phases originate from the segregation effect of the yttrium element. By constructing heterogeneous interfaces between the tetragonal 8YSZ and monoclinic ZrO2 phases, the electric field distributions inside the model at low (25 °C) and high (900 °C) temperatures were obtained, and the results are plotted in Fig. 7(a). There is an electric polarization effect at the interface of the two phases, especially at high temperatures. Interface polarization loss arises when the internal electric field lags behind the variation in the external electric field. Aside from the effects of ionic conductivity and space charge polarization, the presence of dipole polarization caused by variations in the local charge density generated by oxygen vacancies is also a significant factor in microwave dissipation. To theoretically explain the strengthening effect caused by the oxygen vacancy in 8YSZ, first-principles calculations of the charge density difference based on the perfect ZrO2 and oxygen-vacancy 8YSZ configurations are conducted, and the results are plotted in Fig. 7(b). The charge distribution around anions and cations is clearly symmetrical in the perfect ZrO2 lattice, as shown on the left in Fig. 7(b). However, with the O atom vanishing from the lattice introduced by the substitution of Zr4+ by Y3+ ions, the electrons delocalize at the oxygen vacancy site and flow into neighboring atoms, causing an asymmetric charge distribution, as depicted on the right in Fig. 7(b). Thus, increased polarization resonance could be induced by such charge separation in an external electromagnetic field, which will increase electromagnetic energy dissipation [83].

The infrared imaging detection system operates on the idea of detecting variations in thermal radiation energy between the target object and the surrounding background [34]. Therefore, infrared stealth technology is realized mainly by suppressing the thermal radiation energy of the target object. According to the Stefan–Boltzmann law Eq. (21) [23]:

(21) E=εσ0T4

where E*, *ε*, *σ*0, and *T* represent the thermal radiation energy, the infrared emissivity, the Stefan–Boltzmann constant (5.67×10−8 W/(m2·K4)), and the absolute temperature, respectively. Lowering the temperature can greatly reduce the radiation heat, but it also reduces the speed and maneuverability of the fighter aircraft. Thus, reducing the infrared emissivity of materials is an important means to realize infrared stealth. Reducing the infrared emissivity results in a decrease in the amount of energy radiated outward, making it more advantageous for achieving infrared stealth. By measuring the radiated energies of the standard black body and investigating samples at the same temperature, the infrared emissivities of ZrO2 and 8YSZ coatings as a function of temperature are shown in Figs. 8(a) and 8(b). The infrared emissivity of the prepared coating is lower than 0.3 in the window I (3–5 μm) from room temperature to 900 °C. However, its infrared emissivity is greater than 0.5 in the window II (8–14 μm). The increased infrared emissivity in the window II may be due to the resonance effect between the lattice vibration and LWIR bands. At elevated temperatures, the dimensionless contrast radiant intensity (CRI) of aircraft is mainly dominated by MWIR bands, as presented in Fig. 1(d). Compared with most materials [84−92], the 8YSZ coating has a low infrared emissivity, as shown in Fig. 8(b), indicating excellent infrared stealth performance, especially in the window I. Moreover, the infrared emissivity of the 8YSZ coating in the window I decreases with increasing the temperature. Derived from Hagen–Rubens law [93], the relationship between infrared emissivity and *σ* can be described by Eq. (22):

*

(22) ε=4(πε0Cλσ)1/2

where *ε*0 is the permittivity in vacuum, *c* is the velocity of light in vacuum, and *λ* is the wavelength. Because the infrared emissivity in Fig. 8(a) represents the average integrated values in the infrared band of 3–5 μm, the effect of *λ* on the infrared emissivity is negligible. Finally, the temperature dependence of the infrared emissivity is controlled by the temperature dependence of the conductivity. The ionic conductivity of the 8YSZ coating at different temperatures measured by the four-probe method is shown in Fig. 5(a), and the ionic conductivity of the 8YSZ coating increases gradually with increasing temperature. When the temperature reaches 900 °C, it reaches 0.02 S/cm. The ionic conductivity of the 8YSZ coating is positively correlated with temperature, leading to a decrease in its infrared emissivity as the temperature increases.

## 4 Conclusions

In this work, microwave-infrared compatible stealth was realized by a single 8YSZ coating prepared by APS. The main conclusions can be drawn as follows:

(1) Due to non-equivalent ion doping, the formation of oxygen vacancy in the sublattice of 8YSZ promotes its electric conductivity at high temperature, which results in the relative permittivity of the 8YSZ coating increasing with temperature.

(2) Originated from the synergy effect of conductive, interface polarization, and dipole polarization losses, 8YSZ coating presents good microwave absorbing property at 900 °C, which RLmin is as low as −50 dB, and the corresponding EAB is up to 2 GHz when the thickness of the coating is only 1.5 mm.

(3) Within the tested temperature range, the YSZ coating possesses low infrared emissivity (< 0.3), showing excellent infrared stealth performance.
